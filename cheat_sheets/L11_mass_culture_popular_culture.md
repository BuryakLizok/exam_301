## §1. Header / framing

This cheat sheet covers the mass society / mass culture debate as it runs from Matthew Arnold's mid-Victorian fears of "anarchy", through F. R. and Q. D. Leavis's interwar literary defence of cultural minority, into the post-war Americanisation panic (Hoggart, Orwell), and finally to the cultural-studies pushback by Raymond Williams ("culture is ordinary") and Stuart Hall (the popular as a contested terrain). The lecture stages the debate as a recurring problem rather than a settled question: each generation re-invents the worry that the wrong people, consuming the wrong things, will destroy moral order, cultural standards, and political stability. The 1976 punk panic over the Sex Pistols, the 2010s tabloid and Metropolitan-Police campaign against UK drill, the "brain rot" meme discourse about social media and smartphone use, the recurring rows about the teaching of English Literature and Latin in schools, the culture-wars framing of "high" culture being driven out by popular culture, and the wider fear of populism or "mob rule" are all live instances of the same template (Lecture 11 summary). The examinable task is to be able to (a) reconstruct the Arnoldian / Leavisite position with its strongest possible case, (b) explain why mass society theory rests on a particular sociology of industrialisation, urbanisation and atomisation, and (c) deploy Williams, Bourdieu and Hall to demolish or qualify it without flattening the seriousness of the original concern.

---

## §2. Key concepts and thinkers (≤200 words)

**Mass society:** atomised, urbanised, industrial society in which mediating institutions (village, family, church) have decayed and the individual stands exposed to centralised media power. **Mass culture:** popular culture produced industrially for a mass market, allegedly standardised, formulaic and homogenising. **Folk culture:** pre-industrial, autonomous culture made by the people for themselves. **The culture and civilisation tradition:** Arnold to Leavis lineage that treats culture as moral ideal and elite minority preserve. **Sweetness and light:** Arnold's pairing of beauty (sweetness) with cultivated intelligence (light). **Anarchy:** Arnold's term for erosion of shared moral standards under democratic disorder. **The cultural elite / conscious minority:** Q. D. Leavis's "armed and conscious minority" who must defend literary standards. **Brow culture:** the high / middle / low hierarchy. **Taste as class marker:** Bourdieu's claim that aesthetic judgement encodes class position. **Polysemic text, dominant / negotiated / oppositional readings, relative autonomy:** Hall's reception apparatus. **Moral panic:** disproportionate public alarm about a cultural practice as proxy for class anxiety. **Key thinkers:** Matthew Arnold, F. R. Leavis, Q. D. Leavis, Dwight MacDonald, George Orwell, Richard Hoggart, Raymond Williams, Pierre Bourdieu, Stuart Hall, Dick Hebdige, John Carey.

## §4. Information from the readings

### Strinati, ch. 1, "Mass culture and popular culture"

Strinati frames the chapter as a genealogy of the idea of mass culture, starting from Burke's claim that the modern concept of popular culture emerges with late-eighteenth-century national consciousness and Herder's distinction between popular and learned culture, then moving through Williams's reminder in *Keywords* that "popular" carries at least four overlapping senses: well-liked by many people; inferior kinds of work (popular press vs. quality press); deliberately setting out to win favour (popular journalism); and, most recently, "the culture actually made by people for themselves", often displaced to the past as folk culture. The chapter then isolates three recurrent themes that organise the literature: (1) where does popular culture come from, top-down or bottom-up? (2) what does industrialisation / commercialisation do to it, does the commodity form eat artistry? (3) what is its ideological role, indoctrination or resistance?

**Mass culture and mass society:** the major claim of mass society theory is that industrialisation and urbanisation destabilise the agrarian village community, the family, the church and religious life, producing "atomisation". The atomised individual, bound to others by purely contractual ties, is left without mediating institutions, becomes anomic and morally adrift, and is therefore peculiarly vulnerable to manipulation by mass media and mass markets. As Strinati puts it, "in a mass society, the individual is left more and more to his or her own devices, has fewer and fewer communities or institutions in which to find identity or values by which to live, and has less and less idea of the morally appropriate ways to live" (Strinati, 1995: 5). Religious certainties give way to "the amoral immediacy of rational individualism and secular anomie", and mass culture functions as "the moral placebos of a mass society" (Strinati, 1995: 6).

**The mass culture debate:** mass culture is "popular culture which is produced by the industrial techniques of mass production, and marketed for profit to a mass public of consumers" (Strinati, 1995: 10). MacDonald's formula stands at the centre: "Folk Art grew from below. It was a spontaneous, autochthonous expression of the people, shaped by themselves, pretty much without the benefit of High Culture, to suit their own needs. Mass Culture is imposed from above. It is fabricated by technicians hired by businessmen; its audiences are passive consumers, their participation limited to the choice between buying and not buying." (MacDonald, 1953: 60, cited in Strinati, 1995: 9). MacDonald goes further: mass culture "is a dynamic, revolutionary force, breaking down the old barriers of class, tradition, taste, and dissolving all cultural distinctions … producing what might be called homogenized culture", and is therefore "very, very democratic: it absolutely refuses to discriminate against, or between, anything or anybody" (MacDonald, 1953: 62, cited in Strinati, 1995: 15).

**Q. D. Leavis** appears as the empirical wing of the case. Strinati quotes her on the bifurcated reading public, the "lowbrow public" ignorant even of the names of highbrow writers, the highbrow public only knowing names like Ethel M. Dell and Tarzan "from hearsay rather than first-hand knowledge"; the critical minority "isolated, disowned by the general public and threatened with extinction" (Leavis, 1932: 35, cited in Strinati, 1995: 14). Her policy recommendation is unambiguous: "all that can be done, it must be realised, must take the form of resistance by an armed and conscious minority" (Leavis, 1932: 270, cited in Strinati, 1995: 18).

**Americanisation:** Strinati spends a long section showing that British mass-culture theory is bound up with anti-Americanism. Arnold already complained in 1869 that "in things of the mind, and in culture and totality, America, instead of surpassing us all, falls short … that chosen home of newspapers and politics" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 21). F. R. Leavis takes Americanisation as "an accomplished fact", "American conditions are the conditions of modern civilization" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 22). Orwell's essay on "the decline of the English murder" laments that the No Orchids type of Americanised crime fiction has no "depth of feeling" and that "Freud and Machiavelli have reached the outer suburbs" (Orwell, 1946, cited in Strinati, 1995: 24). Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy* (1957) finds in the milk-bar "juke-box boys", "with drape-suits, picture ties, and an American slouch" who "waggle one shoulder or stare, as desperately as Humphrey Bogart, across the tubular chairs", the symbol of "a shiny barbarism" replacing the dense moral fabric of the working-class community (Hoggart, 1957: 203–204, cited in Strinati, 1995: 27).

**Worpole and White** are introduced as oral-historical counter-evidence: working-class readers of the 1930s and 1940s actively preferred American crime fiction precisely because it offered "a realism about city life, an acknowledgement of big business corruption, and an unpatronising portrayal of working class experience and speech" missing from the corpse-in-the-library English crime novel (Worpole, 1983: 35, cited in Strinati, 1995: 30). White's history of Campbell Bunk in Finsbury Park shows young men using *Little Caesar*, *Public Enemy* and *Scarface* to "see themselves as heroes rather than bystanders, the subject of life rather than its object" (White, 1986: 166, cited in Strinati, 1995: 31) [VERIFY YEAR?]. Hebdige extends this into a subcultural-bricolage thesis: American iconography is "a set of symbols, objects and artefacts which can be assembled and re-assembled by different groups in a literally limitless number of combinations" (Hebdige, 1988: 74, cited in Strinati, 1995: 32) [VERIFY YEAR?].

**Critique of mass culture theory:** Strinati's final section catalogues seven problems, elitism; a "feminisation" of mass culture that lets critics dismiss anything weepy or romantic as feminine and therefore inferior (Modleski); a romanticised "golden age" with no clear historical referent; conflation of audience with passive dupe; inability to register genre or generic diversity within "mass" culture; failure to see how "art" / "popular" boundaries are themselves historical (Hitchcock as auteur, jazz as art, early rock 'n' roll as classic); and Ang's *Watching Dallas* (1989) [VERIFY YEAR?] finding that viewers articulate their pleasures and dislikes through a publicly available "ideology of mass culture" versus an "ideology of populism", deploying "ironic distance" to license enjoyment of supposedly bad objects.

### Arnold, *Culture and Anarchy* (1869)

Arnold's core move is the redefinition of culture as "the best that has been thought and said", a normative, hierarchical, perfectionist ideal rather than a description of how anyone actually lives. The book's polemical targets are (a) Hebraism (narrow Puritan moralism), (b) Philistinism (the propertied middle class's vulgar utilitarianism), (c) Barbarism (aristocratic complacency), and (d) the Populace (the riotous working class issuing from its "hiding place" and "doing as it likes"). Culture is offered as the corrective to all four. The state, properly conceived, embodies "our best self" and supplies the moral authority democratic society would otherwise lack. The slogan "sweetness and light" is the programme: cultivated intelligence to illuminate, refined beauty to humanise.

### F. R. Leavis, *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) and *Culture and Environment* (1933)

Leavis radicalises Arnold for the age of cinema, radio and tabloid press. The minority that "discerning appreciation of art and literature depends" upon now has to be deliberately trained, because the surrounding environment, advertising, pulp fiction, formulaic film, standardised journalism, actively de-skills the moral and aesthetic sensibility of the modern reader. *Culture and Environment*, co-written with Denys Thompson, is an English-classroom textbook designed to teach pupils to "read against" advertising and mass communication. The "organic community" of pre-industrial England functions as a regulative ideal (which Williams will later show is largely a literary fiction). The journal *Scrutiny* institutionalised the project.

### Q. D. Leavis, *Fiction and the Reading Public* (1932; expanded 1939)

Q. D. Leavis is the empirical sociologist of the Leavis circle. Working from publishers' lists, library borrowing data, questionnaires and best-seller surveys, she argues that the reading public has been stratified into highbrow, middlebrow and lowbrow strata served by different publishing infrastructures, and that the commercial market for fiction privileges formulaic genres (romance, adventure, detective) over the "serious" novel. Her tone is pessimistic but the method, market analysis, audience study, attention to the institutional shape of the cultural industries, anticipates exactly the sociology of culture that would later be turned against her conclusions.

### Hall, "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" (1981)

Hall rejects both poles of the Strinati dichotomy. Popular culture is neither MacDonald's debased imposition nor a pure expression of "the people". It is a contested terrain, a "battlefield", on which dominant institutions and subordinate groups continually rework cultural meanings. Two corollaries: (a) audiences are not passive; texts are polysemic and can be read in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways depending on the social position of the decoder; (b) culture has only relative autonomy, structural constraints (capital, race, class, gender) are real, but never wholly determining.

## §5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The Arnoldian formula, culture as "the best that has been thought and said" (Arnold, 1869: 6), looks, at first glance, like an innocent celebration of excellence. The work the formula does, though, is double. It simultaneously *defines* culture (as a curated, hierarchical canon) and *locates* the authority to police that definition (in a minority "with superior judgement"). Arnold's own description of the London working class, "raw and half developed … beginning to perplex us by marching where it likes, meeting where it likes, bawling what it likes, breaking what it likes", exposes the political reflex underneath the aesthetic argument. The fear of "anarchy" is in the first instance a fear of the franchise: *Culture and Anarchy* was written in the immediate aftermath of the 1867 Reform Act and the Hyde Park railings riots. I read Arnold, then, less as a literary critic than as a constitutional theorist of an emerging mass democracy who wants culture to do the disciplinary work that an established church and a deferential class structure can no longer do alone.

F. R. Leavis tightens this into a doctrine: "Upon this minority depends our power of profiting by the finest human experience of the past; they keep alive the subtlest and most perishable parts of tradition" (Leavis, 1930). What is striking here is that the minority is described in *custodial* rather than *creative* terms, its job is to keep tradition alive against contamination. Perry Anderson and Francis Mulhern's verdict that Leavisism is "petit-bourgeois moralism" and "radical conservatism" is, on this reading, fair. Yet Leavis is not simply reactionary: *Culture and Environment* shares with 1930s Marxist cultural critique a hostility to industrial capitalism, advertising, and the standardising machinery of the press, it just refuses the socialist conclusion. The Leavises offer the only sustained right-conservative critique of capitalism's cultural effects in the English tradition, and that is a position worth understanding rather than caricaturing.

Q. D. Leavis is the most interesting figure for sociological purposes, because her *method* contradicts her *conclusions*. In *Fiction and the Reading Public* she examines publishing markets, library lists and readership stratification with empirical seriousness, and concludes that "the critical minority to whose sole charge modern literature has now fallen is isolated, disowned by the general public and threatened with extinction" (Leavis, 1932: 35). Her remedy, "resistance by an armed and conscious minority" (Leavis, 1932: 270), is the manifesto of the Cambridge English faculty as it would constitute itself in the 1930s and 1940s, and via the post-war expansion of higher education would be exported across the English-speaking world as the curriculum of "English literature".

Stuart Hall's response is to refuse both the elitist and the populist pole. In "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'", he argues that popular culture is a "site of struggle", not an essence; that media texts are polysemic and yield dominant, negotiated or oppositional readings depending on the decoder's social position; that culture has "relative autonomy", it is shaped by structures of capital and race and gender, but is not reducible to them. This generates a research programme rather than a verdict. Take the *Daily Mirror*'s "Boris: Working class men are drunk, criminal & feckless" front page from 2019. A preferred reading takes it as straightforward exposure of an out-of-touch elite. A negotiated reading enjoys the spectacle of Johnson's hypocrisy without accepting the *Mirror*'s tribune-of-the-people self-image. An oppositional reading, Hall would say, notices that the framing repeats the very stereotype it claims to denounce, presenting working-class men as objects to be defended rather than political actors.

The contemporary instances trail off into the present. The 1976 *Daily Mirror* headline "WHO ARE THESE PUNKS?" and the later tabloid and Met-Police campaign against UK drill ("Top DJ profits from gangs", "'Drill' rap fuels the…", "Gangs award stabbing rivals") are formally identical: an old institution naming, photographing and policing a working-class youth subculture associated with a particular kind of urban music. UK punk and the Sex Pistols then; UK drill now. The "brain rot" meme discourse around social media and smartphone use reproduces Q. D. Leavis's argument almost verbatim: her phrase "a set of habits inimical to mental effort" (Leavis, 1932: 224) is what the contemporary panic is restating. The rows about the teaching of English Literature and Latin in schools, and the broader culture-wars claim that high culture is being driven out by popular culture, each repeats the template: a panic about who is consuming what, dressed up as a concern about democracy's moral and aesthetic health (Lecture 11 summary).

The most powerful critiques of the Arnoldian-Leavisite position are three. (1) Bourdieu shows that aesthetic taste is not objective but a class marker, rooted in class inequalities, so the disinterested judgement of the cultivated reader is itself an acquired competence whose acquisition tracks economic and educational capital (Lecture 11 summary, "Possible Critiques"). (2) Raymond Williams insists that "culture is ordinary", not the preserve of an elite but the lived, ordinary practice of meaning-making by which all communities sustain themselves. (3) Hall historicises the categories themselves: "the popular" is not a stable object that critics merely describe, it is a contested terrain whose boundaries cultural authorities re-draw in each generation, with consequences for who gets to count as a legitimate cultural actor. To these I would add Carey's blunter point from the lecture slide, that the mass culture debate "has always been haunted by anxieties about democracy itself", with hostility to mass culture coinciding with hostility to mass participation, mass literacy, and the erosion of elite authority. Ross adds that "contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety" (slide 14). The point of the lecture, I think, is not to choose a winner, Arnold and Hall both name something real, but to recognise that any account of culture which cannot specify *who is doing the defining* and *who benefits from the definition* is doing covert political work.

## §8. Essay answers

### M1. "Critically evaluate the claim that mass culture is a threat."

The statement "mass culture is a threat" is at best half-true, and the half it gets wrong matters more than the half it gets right. The phrase compresses two claims: a sociological one, that culture industries flatten variety and concentrate symbolic power; and a normative one, that the resulting culture is debased. The first is partly defensible. The second collapses under its own assumptions.

The Arnold-Leavis tradition supplies the strongest version. Arnold's *Culture and Anarchy* (1869) treats culture, "the best that has been thought and said", as the counterweight to mass democracy's tendency towards "anarchy". F. R. Leavis's *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) industrialises the worry: "the discerning appreciation of art and literature depends" on a deliberately trained minority, because advertising, pulp fiction and standardised journalism de-skill the modern sensibility. Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* (1932) adds the empirical apparatus, concluding that "the critical minority to whose sole charge modern literature has now fallen is isolated, disowned by the general public and threatened with extinction" (Leavis, 1932: 35). MacDonald completes the picture: mass culture "is a dynamic, revolutionary force, breaking down the old barriers of class, tradition, taste … producing what might be called homogenized culture" (MacDonald, 1953: 62, cited in Strinati, 1995: 15).

There is a genuine empirical core. The MacDonald homogenisation argument still describes the concentration of cultural production in a small number of commercial firms, with Netflix algorithms and streaming platforms a contemporary instance. The Carey-Ross point, that mass culture critique is haunted by anxieties about democracy and that "contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety", concedes that the *threat* discourse exists and matters.

The problem is everything that follows. Three failures stand out. First, the thesis rests on a "feminisation" move (Modleski, cited in Strinati, 1995: 42): anything sentimental or pleasurable is coded as feminine and therefore degraded. The dismissal of soap opera, romance fiction and reality TV like *Love Island* as inferior because they evoke "reactions associated with the feminine" tracks this gendered hierarchy with precision. Second, the thesis assumes a passive audience. Hall's encoding/decoding model in "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" shows texts are polysemic, yielding dominant, negotiated or oppositional readings depending on the decoder's social position. Ang's *Watching Dallas* shows viewers deploy "ironic distance" to license enjoyment (Strinati, 1995: 43). Third, the thesis rests on a romanticised "organic community", an idealised "golden age" whose historical referent Strinati shows is impossible to pin down.

Bourdieu's *Distinction* delivers the decisive blow. The Arnoldian "best that has been thought and said" is not an objective standard but the codified taste of a class fraction whose acquisition tracks inherited economic and educational capital. To say "mass culture is a threat" is, on Bourdieu's reading, to defend the symbolic monopoly of one class fraction against the consumption practices of another.

What survives of the threat thesis? Three things. (a) The political-economy worry about concentration of cultural production, now visible in streaming platforms and the dominance of figures like MrBeast on YouTube, is real. (b) Q. D. Leavis's insight that commercial pressures shape what gets published, played and recommended is correct and underpins the contemporary sociology of culture industries. (c) The anxiety about democracy that Carey identifies is itself a phenomenon worth studying.

What does not survive is the normative claim that mass culture is intrinsically debased. Strinati's own examples make the point: jazz, condemned as standardised mass culture, has been admitted to the canon; Hollywood cinema, dismissed by F. R. Leavis, has produced an auteur tradition; "early rock-'n'-roll records, once dismissed as mindless pap by music critics, are now accorded 'classic' status by changing critical standards" (Strinati, 1995: 41). The same trajectory runs through hip-hop, with Beyoncé and Stormzy now treated as canonical artists rather than threats. UK drill, currently policed as a threat on the lecture slide, is the latest entry in a sequence whose previous instalments have all been re-classified. To call this stuff a "threat" is to confuse one's discomfort with a sociological fact.

I conclude with Hall's reformulation: popular culture is a contested terrain, not an essence (Hall, 1981). The right question is not "is mass culture a threat?" but "to whom, and on whose behalf, is the threat being declared?" Answering converts a panicked aesthetic complaint into a political analysis, which is where the lecture wants us to end up.

### M2. "Is the notion of 'mass society' still meaningful?"

"Mass society" survives as a heuristic but fails as a description, and the contemporary media environment requires a different vocabulary, closer to Hall's "relative autonomy" and Bennett's structured-power formulation than to the atomisation narrative of MacDonald or Leavis.

The classical mass society concept rests on a specific sociology. Industrialisation and urbanisation eradicate agrarian community, decay the mediating institutions of village, family and church, and leave the individual "atomised", bound to others only by contractual ties, morally adrift, exposed to manipulation by centralised media. The atomised person "has fewer and fewer communities or institutions in which to find identity or values by which to live" (Strinati, 1995: 5). The mass media supply the "moral placebos" surrogate moralities require (Strinati, 1995: 6). On this model, the audience is a uniform mass and the cultural product a homogeneous good aimed at the lowest common denominator.

There are senses in which the contemporary environment vindicates parts of this story. The "brain rot" meme discourse around TikTok and smartphone use, signalled by the banana-monkey AI meme on the lecture slide, reproduces Q. D. Leavis's worry that the modern reader is trained into "a set of habits inimical to mental effort" (Leavis, 1932: 224) with eerie literalness. Her complaint that "life was not then a series of frivolous stimuli as it now is for the suburban dweller" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 16) reads as if written about the present.

But three features of the present make the classical framing a poor descriptor. First, the audience is no longer a uniform mass. Strinati concedes producers reach "sections of a market divided and stratified by tastes, values and preferences as well as money and power" (Strinati, 1995: 44). The atomised audience MacDonald imagined, "a solitary atom, uniform with and undifferentiated from thousands and millions of other atoms" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 12), is precisely what the contemporary, taste-stratified audience is not. Second, the relation of audience to producer is inverted. The MacDonald formula of "passive consumers, their participation limited to the choice between buying and not buying" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 9) is empirically thin once audiences are also producers, as YouTube creators from PewDiePie to MrBeast demonstrate at scale. Third, Hall's encoding/decoding apparatus shows that polysemic reading has always been the default.

What survives, in stronger form, is Tony Bennett's reformulation on the lecture slide: culture "is not simply imposed from above, nor is it freely chosen from below. It is produced, regulated, circulated, and interpreted within structured relations of power." This is less melodramatic but the right shape. Netflix algorithms structure visibility without dictating consumption; content-moderation regimes structure speech without controlling it; recommendation systems structure taste without imposing it. Hall's "relative autonomy" handles this better than atomisation: culture is shaped by capital, race, class and gender but not fully determined by them.

The LeMahieu point on the slide, the tension between "democratic inclusion and elite guidance", retains its bite. Every BBC licence-fee debate, every public-service-broadcasting row, every content-moderation argument is at root a fight over how much elite guidance is compatible with mass democratic participation. That is recognisably Arnold's 1869 fight. The GB News "Mickey Mouse degrees" panel on the Carey-Ross slide is the same fight in a populist register.

A final example. The drill rap panic the lecture slide pairs with the 1976 Sex Pistols panic is a perfect Arnoldian moment: a state agency, claiming to act for public safety, instructs press and platforms to construct a working-class musical tradition as a threat to civic order. The atomisation vocabulary cannot do justice to this; drill is intensely communal, locally rooted, decidedly not the product of a passive mass. The vocabulary that works is Hall's: a contested terrain, with a dominant institution and a subordinate group struggling over the meaning and visibility of cultural production. The same applies to the *Adolescence* and *Black Mirror* debates about youth and screen culture: structured by power, contested in reception, not reducible to atomised consumption.

So "mass society" still names the historical anxiety, and parts of its sociology, particularly Q. D. Leavis's account of commercial pressure, remain useful. As a description of where we now live, it has been overtaken. The work it was doing has been redistributed across Hall's relative autonomy, polysemic reception, and Bennett's structured-power formulation.

### M3. "To what extent does the BBC reflect the 'culture and civilisation tradition'?"

The BBC is the most consequential institutional embodiment of the Arnoldian-Leavisite culture and civilisation tradition in British life, but its contemporary identity is a compromise formation, partly Arnoldian, partly absorbing Williams and Hall, and the tensions within that compromise generate most of the political pressure on the Corporation.

The historical case for the BBC as Arnoldian is overwhelming. Arnold's slide sets the programme: culture is "the pursuit of our total perfection by means of getting to know … the best which has been thought and said in the world" (Arnold, 1869: 6), and the "Arnoldian Perspective" slide specifies that this position becomes "institutionalised as English literature teaching in the 1920s". The same decade gave Britain its national broadcaster, and the BBC's founding ethos is a near-verbatim translation of the Arnoldian formula: an institution charged with administering "sweetness and light" to "minister to the diseased spirit of our time". The national cultural authority Arnold could only gesture at, doing for democratic civic life what an established church could no longer do alone, is what the BBC undertook.

The Leavisite layer overlaid the Arnoldian one. *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) and *Culture and Environment* (1933) constructed literary education as a form of cultural defence against the standardising effects of mass communication. F. R. Leavis's sentence that "upon this minority depends our power of profiting by the finest human experience of the past; they keep alive the subtlest and most perishable parts of tradition" (Leavis, 1930) describes the production-and-commissioning class of a public-service broadcaster with uncomfortable precision.

Three things complicate the case. First, the BBC has always carried a parallel popular-entertainment tradition decidedly not Arnoldian. Hall's *Love Thy Neighbour* analysis is the cleanest example: a sitcom about a white working-class couple in Twickenham next door to a Black couple, read through Hall's "grammar of racism" framework, is precisely the kind of programming produced under commercial-and-democratic pressure rather than Arnoldian instruction. Long-running soap *Coronation Street* and competition shows like *Bake Off* operate in the same register. Second, the Williams critique, "culture is ordinary", was substantially absorbed: treating popular culture and ordinary experience as serious objects of analysis rather than as "degraded or secondary forms" became editorial practice. Third, Hall's reception-theory move, recognising plural audiences with different reading positions, has been institutionalised through audience research and targeted commissioning. The seminar exercise on the *Daily Mirror*'s "Boris: Working class men are drunk, criminal & feckless" headline (2019) is exactly what BBC editorial guidelines now anticipate.

The current position is a hybrid. The news-and-current-affairs operations, the schools-and-Open-University inheritance, and the arts commissioning are Arnoldian-Leavisite by design. The mainstream television commissioning and popular-music networks are post-Williams: they take the diversity of ordinary cultural life as their starting point. Recent drama like *Black Mirror* and *Adolescence* (commissioned across the public-service ecosystem) sits between the two: serious cultural ambition aimed at popular audiences. The structural tension between the two halves generates the recurring licence-fee crises.

Three contemporary illustrations sharpen the diagnosis. (a) The drill panic the lecture slide pairs with the 1976 Sex Pistols panic generated a predictable internal BBC question: present drill as a problem to be explained to a worried middle-class audience (Arnoldian) or commission the artists themselves (Williams-Hall). Practice has oscillated. (b) The GB News "Mickey Mouse degrees" debate on the Carey-Ross slide: the same Arnoldian reflex now occurs *outside* the BBC and is used to attack it, with right-wing media accusing the Corporation of populist concession. (c) The Working Class Movement Library, on the same slide, captures the longer history: institutional cultural authority does not only flow top-down, and the BBC's relationship to working-class-organised cultural memory has been awkward throughout its existence.

So the answer is "extensively, but unevenly, and decreasingly". The BBC remains the most important institutional carrier of the culture and civilisation tradition in the English-speaking world. But it has spent decades absorbing Williams, Hoggart, Hall and Bourdieu into its operating practice, and the unresolved tension between the Reithian DNA and the cultural-studies overlay is the central drama of its current crisis. Whether the BBC can sustain the compromise as audiences fragment further is, in effect, the question of whether the culture and civilisation tradition has any institutional future at all.

### M4. "'Contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety' (Ross). Discuss with reference to at least one contemporary moral panic."

I defend Ross's claim using the slide's two paired cases, the 1976 Sex Pistols panic and the contemporary UK drill panic, before generalising to the "brain rot" discourse and the school-curriculum rows. Every named "moral panic" in the recent British record turns out, on inspection, to be a panic about which class of person is doing which class of consuming, with the aesthetic complaint doing political work.

The Carey-Ross slide encapsulates the framework. Carey's claim is that the mass culture debate "has always been haunted by anxieties about democracy itself", and that "hostility toward mass culture often coincides with hostility toward mass participation, mass literacy, and the erosion of elite authority". Ross's complementary point is that "contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety". Intellectual disgust at popular cultural forms is displaced disgust at the form's typical consumer.

The UK drill case is the textbook contemporary instance. The lecture's opening slide pairs the *Sex Pistols Bulletin* of 1977 panic headlines ("Anarchy in the UK!", "THE FILTH AND THE FURY!", "Who are these punks?") with contemporary drill panic headlines ("'Drill' rap fuels the…", "Top DJ profits from gangs", "Gangs award stabbing rivals"). The form of the panic, folk-devil, tabloid, fear of urban youth, is structurally identical across half a century. The 1976 panic targeted white urban working-class men; the drill panic targets young Black Londoners. The official justifications shift (anarchism then, knife-crime now), but the demographic occupying the structural position is consistent: working-class culture-producers whose work circulates beyond the demographic that produced it.

The 1976 punk panic is the historical template. The Sex Pistols' "Anarchy in the UK" was treated as a literal anarchic threat to civic order, exactly the Arnoldian fear of "anarchy" the lecture's Arnold slide names. The pattern is clear: every two generations the British press and police discover a new working-class musical form, declare it the end of moral order, and use the resulting panic to discipline its producers. The earlier moral panics about video games like *Grand Theft Auto*, blamed for youth violence in the 2000s, are the same template displaced from music to interactive media.

The "brain rot" meme discourse, illustrated by the banana-monkey AI meme on the lecture slide, updates the same template. The objection that endless TikTok use produces "a set of habits inimical to mental effort" (Leavis, 1932: 224) is dressed in contemporary clothing but does familiar work. Notice who the panic targets: not the institutions that built the platforms, but the predominantly young, often working-class users who consume them. The Andrew Tate panic operates similarly: legitimate concerns about misogyny get displaced onto a class-coded anxiety about which young men are watching what. The English Literature and Latin teaching rows perform the same gesture: a panic about who is reading what, identifiably class-coded. The same logic underwrites contemporary book-banning campaigns: panic dressed as protection, targeting the consumption habits of a specific demographic. The Working Class Movement Library on the Carey-Ross slide stands as counter-emblem: working-class cultural organisation has its own institutional history the panic discourse refuses to recognise.

Ross's claim is not that aesthetic judgements are *only* class anxiety. Some criticisms are well-founded. Strinati preserves Q. D. Leavis's genuine empirical contributions on commercial pressure (Strinati, 1995: 14, 16) and concedes commercial mass production does shape genre and audience formation. The claim is that the *vehemence and selectivity* of the criticism, the choice to panic about *this* and not *that*, the political energy mobilised, all track class anxiety with very high reliability. Hall's encoding/decoding apparatus then explains why panics persist when their empirical basis is weak: the dominant reading is encoded into press/police/state apparatus and reproduced as common sense.

I conclude that Ross's claim is essentially correct, and that the drill panic is a particularly clean instance because the racial and class lines are unusually sharp, as the slide's juxtaposition with punk makes visible. The intellectual task, the one Carey, Hall, Bourdieu and Williams all licence, is not to defend every popular cultural form, but to refuse to grant the aesthetic complaint its claim of disinterestedness. Once the class location of the complainant becomes part of the analysis, most moral panics shrink to their proper size.

### M5. "Compare Arnold's defence of culture with F. R. Leavis's account of the 'minority'. What, if anything, is gained by the move from 1869 to 1930?"

Leavis radicalises Arnold's defence of culture along three axes: he industrialises the threat-diagnosis, professionalises the defending minority, and gives the project an institutional vehicle (the Cambridge English faculty and *Scrutiny*). What is gained is operational seriousness; what is lost is Arnold's residual liberalism.

Arnold's *Culture and Anarchy* (1869) is a political-philosophical essay before it is a literary-critical one. Its target is the constitutional crisis precipitated by the Second Reform Act: how can a democratic polity that has just enfranchised the urban working class maintain shared moral standards without an established church or a deferential class structure? Arnold's answer, culture as "the best that has been thought and said", "sweetness and light" administered by a cultivated minority for the moral benefit of all, is liberal in two senses. First, it is universalist: culture is in principle available to anyone willing to acquire it, and Arnold supports state-funded education as the delivery mechanism. Second, its targets are even-handed: Arnold criticises the working-class "Populace", the middle-class "Philistines", the aristocratic "Barbarians", and the puritan "Hebraists" with roughly equal vigour. The famous London-mob passage, "the working class … beginning to perplex us by marching where it likes, meeting where it likes, bawling what it likes, breaking what it likes", is alarmed but not hostile in the Leavisite sense; Arnold wants the Populace educated *into* culture, not walled off from it.

F. R. Leavis's *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) operates under different pressures. Mass media now exist in industrial form with audiences in the tens of millions. Q. D. Leavis's empirical work has demonstrated the bifurcation of readership into brow-stratified strata. The First World War has discredited nineteenth-century optimism about progress, and cultural Americanisation has become concrete (talkies, Hollywood distribution, the pulp magazine). Leavis's argument is consequently darker. The minority is no longer a regulative ideal but a beleaguered group whose task is custodial: "Upon this minority depends our power of profiting by the finest human experience of the past; they keep alive the subtlest and most perishable parts of tradition" (Leavis, 1930).

Three things are gained. (1) *Operational seriousness:* Leavis converts Arnold's philosophical sketch into a working pedagogy. *Culture and Environment* (1933) is a school textbook; the Cambridge English Tripos institutionalises the project; by 1955 the Leavisite curriculum has been exported across the English-speaking university system. (2) *A theory of mass communication:* Leavis takes seriously, as Arnold did not have to, the specific machinery of advertising, formula fiction and standardised journalism. *Culture and Environment* contains close readings of Northcliffe-press journalism that anticipate cultural studies' approach to media texts by thirty years. (3) *A professional vehicle:* the journal *Scrutiny* (1932-53) institutionalised the training of "armed and conscious" critics, as Q. D. Leavis put it.

What is lost is significant. (1) Arnold's *liberalism* erodes. The Arnoldian minority was a regulative ideal; the Leavisite minority is a sociological fact, recruited from a specific institutional base and explicitly hostile to "petit bourgeois" middle-class taste. Perry Anderson's and Francis Mulhern's reading of Leavisism as "radical conservatism", petit-bourgeois moralism organised around a national literary canon, is accurate. (2) Arnold's *universalism* erodes. Arnold believed culture could in principle be acquired by anyone; Leavis's "organic community" of pre-industrial England is a lost world that can be invoked but not recreated, and the working class disappears from Leavis's account except as a corrupted residue. (3) The *political* dimension thins. Arnold's culture was meant to do civic-religious work; Leavis's culture has narrowed to the technical training of a tiny critical elite. The democratic ambition of *Culture and Anarchy*, that the masses might be educated into culture, is replaced by the defensive ambition of *Mass Civilisation*, that culture might survive the masses.

The trade-off is real. Leavis's project worked institutionally; Arnold's did not, except via the BBC and the Penguin Classics imprint. But the price of the operational gain was the abandonment of the universalist horizon that made Arnold's vision liberal. The post-Leavisite cultural studies tradition, Hoggart, Williams, Hall, has spent sixty years trying to recover Arnold's democratic ambition without Leavis's cultural defensiveness. Whether they have succeeded is the subject matter of the rest of this paper.

### M6. "Using Stuart Hall's encoding / decoding model, analyse a recent media representation of working-class life."

I use Hall's encoding/decoding model, developed in his 1973 television-discourse paper and refined in "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" (1981), to analyse the *Daily Mirror* front page of 29 November 2019 reproduced on the lecture slide: "WHAT HE REALLY THINKS: Boris: Working class men are drunk, criminal & feckless." The image rewards close reading because it does several contradictory things at once.

Hall's model has three moves. (a) Texts are *encoded* by producers under specific institutional conditions: newsroom routines, editorial line, advertising imperatives, generic conventions. (b) The encoded text carries a *preferred reading* but is polysemic, decodable in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways. (c) Which reading a decoder produces depends on their social position, prior cultural competence and reception context. The model is not relativist, there is real structural power in the encoding moment, but it refuses the mass-culture-theory assumption that audiences passively absorb the preferred reading.

*Encoding analysis.* The *Mirror* is a left-leaning tabloid whose self-image is "tribune of the working class". The encoding work is the construction of Boris Johnson as a class enemy whose private prejudices have been exposed. The huge black-on-white headline isolates the offensive descriptors, "drunk, criminal & feckless", and attributes them to Johnson, while "WHAT HE REALLY THINKS" signals exposure. The accompanying photograph (Johnson mid-gesture, slight grimace) is the standard tabloid-villain shot. The "SNOOTY SPECIMEN" tagline beside the photograph reinforces the populist register.

*Preferred reading.* The reader is invited to be appalled at Johnson, to identify with the working-class men he disparages, and to recognise the *Mirror* as the paper that exposes elite contempt. This is supported by the headline's deictic distance ("HE", not "Boris") and the populist "Tory" rather than "Conservative".

*Dominant decoding.* A reader who shares the *Mirror*'s alignment, a left-leaning working-class or lower-middle-class adult, accepts the preferred reading without modification. Johnson's contempt is taken as evidence of the Conservative Party's underlying class hostility. The decoding produces anger at Johnson and reinforces identification with the *Mirror*.

*Negotiated decoding.* A reader sympathetic to the *Mirror*'s class politics but suspicious of its journalistic standards accepts the substantive claim while resisting the *Mirror*'s self-presentation. The negotiated reader notices that the *Mirror* itself routinely produces exactly the kind of class-stereotyping it accuses Johnson of, and that "tribune of the working class" is a marketing position, not a sociological fact. Agreement with the exposé, scepticism about the messenger.

*Oppositional decoding.* A reader from the political right treats the content as deliberate distortion by a hostile press. The *Mirror* is read as a partisan operation, the headline as smear journalism, the working-class-defender pose as cynicism.

What Hall's apparatus reveals, that mass-culture theory cannot, is the structural irony of the image. The *Mirror* claims to defend working-class men against Johnson's stereotype, but does so by *reproducing the stereotype on its own front page in 144-point type*. The phrase "drunk, criminal & feckless" is now part of the *Mirror*'s visual identity, regardless of who said it first. The polysemic instability means a working-class reader can plausibly read it as defence *or* as further insult. The seminar exercise on the slide ("How was your interpretation of these images reflective of your own social and cultural background?") is built precisely to surface this. The same apparatus handles the Vicky-Pollard "chav" image on the slide: encoded as comic working-class femininity, decoded variously as recognition, contempt or insult depending on the viewer's position. *Adolescence* and similar drama representations of working-class life invite the same triangulation.

Three generalisations follow. (1) Hall's relative-autonomy claim is vindicated: the *Mirror* operates within the structural constraints of the tabloid market but the choice to encode the page this way reflects editorial agency. (2) The encoding/decoding model handles class-coded imagery far better than mass-culture theory's encoded-and-absorbed model. (3) The very existence of contested decodings generates the political work: a univocal text does not divide the audience, and dividing the audience is the *Mirror*'s commercial and political function.

### M7. "'Culture is ordinary' (Williams). How damaging is this claim to the Arnoldian programme?"

Williams's "culture is ordinary" is more damaging to the Arnoldian programme than its three short words suggest, because it does not merely add a sociological footnote to Arnold, it rewrites the ontology on which Arnold's normative claims rested. The Arnoldian programme assumes culture is a particular kind of object (the best that has been thought and said), produced by a particular kind of process (creative work by a cultivated minority), and circulated through a particular kind of institution. Williams demolishes each assumption.

Williams formulated the slogan in his 1958 essay *Culture is Ordinary* and elaborated it across *Culture and Society* (1958), *The Long Revolution* (1961), and *Keywords* (1976). The argument has three components. (1) *Culture is everywhere.* It is not a special-purpose product of a special-purpose minority; it is the ordinary lived practice of meaning-making by which any community sustains itself. Culture is done in pubs, factories, kitchens, working men's clubs and football terraces as much as in the National Gallery. (2) *Culture is one of the most complicated words in the English language*, carrying three irreducible senses: a "tending of growth" (etymological), a "general process of intellectual, spiritual and aesthetic development" (Arnold's sense), and a "whole way of life" (anthropological). Arnold cashes the middle sense and treats the third as degraded; Williams reverses the priority. (3) *Culture is made, not given.* The canon Arnold treated as a found object is the precipitate of selection, transmission and institutional reproduction by specific class fractions.

The damage to Arnold is severe at each level. *Ontologically*, if culture is ordinary then Arnold's bounded object collapses into a continuous field of meaning-making within which gradations of value can still be drawn, but only locally. *Sociologically*, the working-class "Populace" Arnold worried about is not *outside* culture trying to break in, it is *inside* all along, producing its own forms which deserve description rather than disciplinary intervention. *Politically*, if the canon is a historical precipitate then the Arnoldian minority is not the disinterested custodian of value but an interested party in a continuing argument.

But Williams's claim has limits. First, "culture is ordinary" does not entail that all cultural products are equally good, Williams was an unembarrassed admirer of George Eliot, Hardy and Lawrence. The slogan refuses Arnold's ontology but not his evaluative practice. Second, the claim leaves under-theorised how cultural standards are produced in a particular community. Bourdieu would later supply the missing apparatus. Third, the institutional achievement of Arnoldian-Leavisite authority, the BBC, the Penguin Classic, the school English curriculum, remains, and even thinkers persuaded by Williams have benefited from it (Williams himself was a Cambridge English don, trained in the Leavis tradition he later attacked).

A further complication: Williams's "ordinary" is not Hall's "popular". Williams locates culture in the lived practice of a community; Hall locates it on a contested terrain between dominant and subordinate groups. Williams has a slightly romantic edge, treating the working-class community as authentic in a way that repeats some of Leavis's "organic community" gesture. Hall's account is grittier and is therefore the harder challenge over the long run.

Contemporary instances illustrate the practical force of the Williams move. The reclassification trajectories Strinati catalogues, jazz "now appreciated as art, though in the first half of the last century it was condemned as mass culture"; Hitchcock "defined as an auteur"; "early rock-'n'-roll records, once dismissed as mindless pap by music critics, are now accorded 'classic' status by changing critical standards" (Strinati, 1995: 41), are events the Leavisite framework cannot accommodate but Williams predicts as routine. The UK drill case is a Williams-shaped event in progress: practitioners reshaping the canon from within. The mainstream cultural status of Beyoncé and Stormzy is the same process completed: black music whose appropriation and reclassification has long been a contested cultural debate. Reality TV like *Love Island* or competition shows like *Bake Off* enter critical conversation alongside more prestigious drama like *Black Mirror* on the same terms. The "culture is ordinary" claim has been institutionally absorbed by the apparatus Arnold and Leavis built, which is the deepest evidence the slogan has done its work.

Williams's claim is highly damaging to the Arnoldian programme, but in a productive way: it has forced the programme to defend itself on different terrain. The contemporary culture-and-civilisation tradition is post-Williams whether it wants to be or not.

### M8. "Why has the mass culture debate so often taken the form of an Americanisation debate?"

The Americanisation framing is structurally constitutive of the mass culture debate for British and continental critics, because the United States supplied the most developed historical instance of every feature the critics wanted to attack. The Americanisation debate is the empirical leg of a theoretical project, not a separate anti-American animus.

Strinati lays out the case in chapter 1. American capitalism by the 1920s had produced the most developed culture industry, Hollywood studios, mass-circulation magazines, network radio, the recorded-music industry, and was exporting its products to Britain. For a Leavisite asking "what does mass culture look like at scale?", the only available answer was: it looks American. Arnold had prefigured the move in 1869: "in things of the mind, and in culture and totality, America, instead of surpassing us all, falls short … that chosen home of newspapers and politics", before Hollywood existed, because America already represented mass democracy and a popular press. F. R. Leavis updates the diagnosis: "American conditions are the conditions of modern civilization" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 22).

The framing serves three functions. (1) *Empirical concreteness.* Critics can point to specific Hollywood titles, magazines, bestsellers. The Hoggart juke-box-boys passage, "with drape-suits, picture ties, and an American slouch", waggling shoulders "as desperately as Humphrey Bogart, across the tubular chairs" (Hoggart, 1957: 203-204), is unimaginable without the American iconography it inventories. (2) *Theoretical leverage.* Identifying mass culture with America lets the critic associate it with democratic populism, commercial vulgarity, the absence of established cultural authority. (3) *National-cultural defence.* Anti-Americanism allowed British critics to mobilise patriotic sentiment in defence of a project, the preservation of national literary culture, that might otherwise look merely class-defensive. Q. D. Leavis quoting Gosse (1889) on "the spread of the democratic sentiment" producing "a revolt of the mob against our literary masters" does exactly this work.

The post-1945 phase intensifies the framing. Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy* (1957) is half-rural-elegy and half-Americanisation-panic: the milk bar and juke box stand in for everything that has been lost, the working men's club, the chapel, the lending library. Orwell's "Decline of the English Murder" (1946) makes the equation explicit: Americanised crime fiction has no "depth of feeling" because "Freud and Machiavelli have reached the outer suburbs". The Wenders line, "The Americans colonised our subconscious", gives the worry its starkest form.

The critique comes from three directions. *First*, Worpole, White and Hebdige show that British working-class consumers of American culture were not passive dupes but active appropriators, using Cagney and Bogart films to construct subcultural identities that resisted British class hierarchies. Worpole's oral history finds working-class readers preferred American crime fiction because it offered "a realism about city life, an acknowledgement of big business corruption, and an unpatronising portrayal of working class experience and speech" (Worpole, 1983: 35). *Second*, Hebdige shows that mid-1960s consumption of "American" iconography was simultaneously consumption of *continental* iconography (Italian scooters, Gauloises, espresso); the supposed Americanisation was polyglot. *Third*, Ang's *Watching Dallas* shows even paradigmatic Americanised product was decoded by European audiences through both an "ideology of mass culture" *and* an "ideology of populism", with "ironic distance" licensing enjoyment.

Three further considerations follow. (a) The Americanisation framing was always partly displacement: the "spectre of Americanisation could be used to stand in for any combination of the following ideological themes: the rebellion of youth, the 'feminisation' of British culture, the collapse of authority, the loss of Empire, the breakdown of the family, the growth in crime, the decline in attendance at places of worship" (Hebdige, cited in Strinati, 1995: 28). (b) Even paradigmatic Americanised products have never been received the way mass-culture critics assumed: contemporary streaming algorithms and YouTube creator phenomena like PewDiePie and MrBeast circulate globally, undercutting any clean Americanisation thesis. (c) Hall's contested-terrain reformulation displaces the question of national origin entirely: what matters is the struggle between dominant institutions and subordinate groups, not which side of the Atlantic the artefact came from.

The Americanisation framing was structurally constitutive of mid-twentieth-century mass culture critique, was always partly displacement of class anxiety, and its analytical utility has waned. The framing is dated. The underlying questions, about cultural homogenisation, audience agency and political economy, remain live.

### M9. "To what extent does the concept of 'ironic distance' enable us to understand the reception of contemporary cultural products?"

Ironic distance is a genuinely useful but limited reception-theoretic concept: it captures something real about how educated middle-class audiences manage their relationship to ostensibly low-status cultural products, but it does only part of the work more comprehensive frameworks (Hall, Bourdieu, Ang) provide.

The concept is developed in Ien Ang's *Watching Dallas* (1985). Ang interviewed Dutch viewers of the American soap and found three positions. (a) Viewers who hated *Dallas* articulated their hostility through an "ideology of mass culture", the show was American, vulgar, formulaic, manipulative. (b) Viewers who loved it without qualification used an "ideology of populism", taste was personal, intellectuals had no business legislating. (c) Viewers who loved it but were embarrassed deployed *ironic distance*, enjoying *Dallas* "as kitsch", "for the costumes", "as a guilty pleasure". The ironic-distance position is theoretically the most interesting: it lets viewers reconcile the two contradictory ideologies, simultaneously consuming the object and signalling recognition of its low-prestige status.

The concept transports cleanly to contemporary instances on the slide. The "brain rot" meme engagement the banana-monkey AI image stands for is conducted almost entirely through ironic distance. Strinati describes the same phenomenon: "those who watched but laughed at it from a carefully cultivated 'ironic distance', were confident and secure in the judgements they made" (Strinati, 1995: 43). Contemporary engagement with *Love Island* and tabloid celebrity coverage is the same irony-position at scale; *Black Mirror*'s audience consumes its takedowns of internet culture in exactly this register.

What ironic distance *enables* is significant. It refuses the simple binary of approval and disapproval. It registers the work consumers do to manage their social positioning while consuming objects that, taken straight, would compromise that positioning. It explains why "guilty pleasure" has become a stable category in everyday cultural talk. And it sits well alongside Hall's encoding/decoding apparatus: ironic-distance consumption is a particular form of negotiated reading.

But the concept has three limits. *First*, ironic distance is itself a class marker. Bourdieu's claim that taste is rooted in class inequalities applies directly: the capacity to consume a low-status product "ironically" is a competence that tracks educational and cultural capital. The middle-class deployment is a distinction strategy, a way of consuming the same object as the working-class neighbour while signalling that one's consumption is of a higher type. *Second*, the concept can obscure as much as it reveals. Some "ironic" consumption is straightforward enjoyment under a face-saving label. The seriousness with which audiences engage their objects, tracking, recapping, theorising, often outstrips mere irony. *Third*, the concept risks explaining everything and therefore nothing. If every consumption of an apparently low-status object can be redescribed as ironic, the category loses purchase.

What ironic distance does *not* enable us to see is the encoding moment. Ang's framework is fundamentally a reception framework, leaving in second place the question of why the producer encoded the object as they did. Hall's encoding/decoding model is more symmetrical: it analyses what the producer did and what the audience did, and treats the gap as the place where meaning gets fought over. The drill panic on the lecture slide is unhelpfully described in terms of ironic distance; it is well described in terms of contested encoding. The Andrew Tate phenomenon is similarly mishandled by an irony-only frame: his audience's engagement is rarely ironic, and reading it that way misses what is actually happening.

The seminar exercise on the Vicky-Pollard image, the *Daily Mirror* "Boris" front page, and the "chav" caricature explicitly asks how a polysemic image yields divergent readings depending on viewer position. Ironic-distance consumption is one of those positions but not the only one. A full description requires Hall's encoding analysis plus Bourdieu's distinction-strategy reading. Ironic distance is one tool in the kit, not the kit.

Ironic distance is a valuable concept, particularly for educated middle-class consumption of high-volume, low-status formulaic products. It substantially expands what we can see beyond mass culture theory's passive-audience model. But it is not self-sufficient. The richest analyses combine Ang's ideologies of mass culture and populism with Hall's polysemy and Bourdieu's distinction, and treat ironic distance as one move in a wider reception game.

### M10. "Assess Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* as a sociology of cultural industries."

Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* (1932) is, against its author's intention, the first developed sociology of cultural industries in the English tradition, and its empirical apparatus has long outlived the evaluative framework it was harnessed to. The book deserves reassessment as a methodological precursor to Bourdieu and the production-of-culture school, even as its conclusions are rejected.

The book's structure is itself an argument. Part I surveys the historical reading public from the eighteenth century forward, drawing on publishers' lists, bookseller records, library circulation data. Part II analyses the contemporary 1930s reading public, stratified into "highbrow", "middlebrow" and "lowbrow" tiers served by distinct publishing infrastructures. Part III offers the polemical conclusion, the "armed and conscious minority" passage, the book is mostly remembered for. The polemical conclusion is the weakest part; the methodological achievement of Parts I and II is what should be salvaged.

Three methodological innovations stand out. *First*, Q. D. Leavis takes the *publishing market* as her unit of analysis, not the individual reader. Best-seller lists, circulating-library subscription patterns, the inter-war boom in lending libraries, and the rise of the book club are the data points. This is unmistakably a sociology-of-production move, and Strinati credits her with anticipating "later sociological concerns with cultural industries, commodification, and audience formation". *Second*, she treats *genre* as a sociological category. Romance, detective, adventure, the "society novel", each is analysed as a distinct commercial formation with its own audience demographics and production routines. Strinati notes the empirical specifics: "Ethel M. Dell" or "Tarzan" as "convenient symbols" of lowbrow genres "drawn from hearsay rather than first-hand knowledge" by highbrow readers (Leavis, 1932: 35, cited in Strinati, 1995: 14). *Third*, she takes *audience formation* seriously: the reader is *produced* by a particular history of education, leisure provision, advertising and habit. Her passage on "the training of the reader who spends his leisure in cinemas, looking through magazines and newspapers, listening to jazz music … providing him with a set of habits inimical to mental effort" (Leavis, 1932: 224, cited in Strinati, 1995: 15) reads as an uncannily early version of contemporary worries about habit-formation through commercial media, the "brain rot" panic in 1932 vocabulary.

The empirical findings, on their own terms, are largely correct. The reading public *had* stratified. Commercial publishing *did* respond to mass-market demand with formulaic genres. The serious novel *was* losing market share. None of this depends on the Leavisite evaluative framework. Strinati notes that the apparatus of audience differentiation, "the historically shifting power relations between the groups involved, and the categories of taste at stake" (Strinati, 1995: 42), is what Q. D. Leavis's method foreshadows.

The evaluative framework is where the book fails. Three problems are serious. (a) *The hierarchical assumption.* Q. D. Leavis takes for granted that highbrow fiction is intrinsically more valuable and that her evaluative competence is reliable. Bourdieu's *Distinction* destroys this: the hierarchy is a class artefact, and highbrow competence is acquired through the same cultural-capital channels that produced Q. D. Leavis herself. (b) *The passive-audience assumption.* The book treats lowbrow readers as having been "trained" into bad habits by commercial publishing, with little attention to what those readers think they are doing. Worpole/White/Hebdige's later finding that working-class readers used American crime fiction actively is a direct challenge. (c) *The "organic community" assumption.* The pre-industrial reading public against which 1932 is measured is largely a literary construct. Williams's *The Country and the City* (1973) is a refutation of this gesture.

What rescues the book is that the empirical apparatus is separable from the polemical one. Contemporary sociology of cultural industries, political economy of communications, and post-Bourdieu work on distinction does recognisably Q. D. Leavis work in a contemporary register: the unit of analysis is the publishing-and-distribution market, now extended to Netflix and YouTube creator economies; genre is a sociological category; audience formation is taken seriously. Contemporary scholars simply do not assume the highbrow product is intrinsically more valuable.

*Fiction and the Reading Public* has been mis-shelved. Normally taught as part of the mass-culture-theory polemic, it is better understood as the founding empirical work of the sociology of cultural industries, accidentally clothed in a Leavisite polemic that its method does not require. The book deserves re-reading on its empirical merits, not just dismissal for its conclusions.

### M11. "Is moral panic a useful analytical category, or merely a way of dismissing legitimate cultural concerns?"

Moral panic is a useful analytical category when handled properly, with attention to how dominant institutions construct the panic and to the political work it performs, but it can degenerate in casual usage into a way of pre-emptively dismissing concerns that deserve case-by-case empirical assessment. The right answer is "both"; the interesting work is in saying when which.

The most powerful framing in the lecture's materials is the Hall-Gramsci tradition. Hall and colleagues' *Policing the Crisis* (1978) shows "how a relatively limited phenomenon, a moral panic, street robbery, labelled as 'mugging' became a major political and moral issue in 1970s Britain" and how "media, policing, and state power interact to produce ideological responses to wider social and economic crises". The "folk devil" of the young Black mugger was doing displaced work for a state under economic and political pressure. This is the apparatus for thinking about panic: not a checklist but a structural account of how press, police and state co-produce a threat-discourse whose function is to displace political anxiety onto a cultural object.

The category does serious work in the lecture's own examples. The 1976 punk panic, the *Sex Pistols Bulletin* of tabloid front pages ("THE FILTH AND THE FURY!", "WHO ARE THESE PUNKS?"), exhibits the Hall structure: tabloid amplification, police-and-state response, displacement of anxieties about youth unemployment onto a working-class musical form. The contemporary drill panic the slide pairs with it does the same work in a different demographic register: displacement of austerity-era youth-violence anxieties onto a Black urban musical form. The "brain rot" meme discourse and the English Literature/Latin teaching rows fit the template with adjusted demographics: working-class consumption-and-production patterns made into objects of cultural anxiety. The 1990s and 2000s panic about video games like *Grand Theft Auto* allegedly causing youth violence is the same structure displaced from music to interactive media; the Andrew Tate panic similarly displaces real concerns about misogyny onto a class-coded folk-devil figure.

The analytic utility is real. It lets the analyst (a) separate the *empirical* question of whether a cultural practice does the harm attributed to it from the *sociological* question of why the discourse takes the form it does; (b) attend to the *amplification mechanisms*, press, police, politicians, that convert local incidents into national panics; (c) track the *displaced political work* in Hall's *Policing the Crisis* sense; and (d) historicise the recurrence across generations, exactly what the slide's punk/drill juxtaposition asks the student to do.

But there are three dangers. *First*, "moral panic" can be used as a thought-terminating cliché: anything that looks like a panic gets labelled a panic, bypassing the empirical question of whether the underlying concern is well-founded. Strinati's caution applies: "some criticisms of popular cultural forms are well-founded" (Strinati, 1995: 38). *Second*, "moral panic" can be used as a class weapon, middle-class academics labelling working-class concerns as panics they should grow out of. The category then does exactly the Ross-class-anxiety work it was supposed to expose. *Third*, the category presupposes a non-panic baseline of proportionate response, but for many cases the empirical evidence is itself contested.

The right discipline is to use moral panic as a *descriptive* and *processual* category rather than a *verdictive* one. Asking "is this a moral panic?" should mean "does the Hall *Policing the Crisis* displacement-mechanism describe what is happening?", not "is this concern legitimate or illegitimate?". The two questions are separable. A concern can be both legitimate *and* the object of a panic; a concern can be illegitimate *and* not the object of a panic. The Carey-Ross slide quote, "contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety", is best read as a heuristic, not a verdict. The contemporary debate about *Adolescence* or *Black Mirror* representations of youth screen culture should be approached the same way: the question is what displacement work the panic discourse does, not whether the cultural objects are good or bad.

Moral panic is a useful analytical category handled carefully, and a degraded thought-terminator handled carelessly. The empirical task, case by case, is harder than the labelling task, and is what the lecture is actually inviting the student to do.

### M12. "'Popular culture is a site of struggle, not an essence' (Hall). Discuss."

Hall's reformulation is the single most important move in the post-war theorisation of popular culture, and its truth is most easily demonstrated by working through the historical record Strinati assembles: every claim of cultural essence has turned out to be the precipitate of a struggle whose dynamics the essence-claim was concealing.

Hall develops the claim across "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" (1981) and the encoding/decoding work of the 1970s. The argument has two negative moves and one positive. *Negative 1*: against the Leavisite essence-claim that popular culture is intrinsically degraded mass culture imposed from above. *Negative 2*: against the romantic essence-claim that popular culture is an authentic expression of "the people" rising from below. *Positive*: popular culture is the contested terrain on which dominant institutions and subordinate groups continually rework cultural meanings, a "battlefield", in Hall's metaphor, whose lines shift in each generation.

Three corollaries follow. (a) Texts are *polysemic*: media products can be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways depending on the decoder's social position. (b) Culture has *relative autonomy*: structural constraints (capital, race, class, gender) shape what is possible without fully determining what happens. (c) Cultural categories are *historically variable*: the boundary between "art" and "popular", "folk" and "mass", "high" and "low" is constantly being redrawn, and *that redrawing is itself what the struggle is about*.

The historical record vindicates the framework with embarrassing regularity. *Jazz* "is now appreciated as art, though in the first half of the last century it was condemned as mass culture by mass culture theory and the Frankfurt School" (Strinati, 1995: 41). Nothing in the music changed; the boundary moved. F. R. Leavis dismissed *cinema* as a serious cultural form, though MacDonald accepted Eisenstein. *Hitchcock* "made commercial films within the Hollywood system but has since been defined as an auteur, an original and creative genius". *Rock 'n' roll* records, "once dismissed as mindless pap by music critics, are now accorded 'classic' status by changing critical standards" (Strinati, 1995: 41). The broader reclassification of Black music, from jazz through rock'n'roll to hip-hop, with the appropriation debate as a constant subtext, runs the same arc. Beyoncé and Stormzy now sit inside the cultural establishment that condemned their predecessors. *Coronation Street* and *Bake Off*, once dismissed as soap and light entertainment, are taken seriously by cultural critics. Every trajectory falsifies the essence-claim and vindicates contested-terrain.

The contemporary instances on the lecture slide illustrate the dynamic in real time. *UK drill* occupies the same structural place jazz held in the 1920s: condemned as a threat by police and press, defended as a vital urban form by its practitioners, on its way to a reclassification the historical pattern says will look obvious in retrospect. The 1976 punk panic the slide pairs with it has completed the arc: "Anarchy in the UK" is now archive material, taught and curated rather than feared. The "brain rot" meme discourse, exemplified by the banana-monkey AI image, is at the earliest moral-panic stage of the same trajectory, condemned now, likely to be re-classified. The same pattern applies to *Black Mirror*'s ironic engagement with the same cultural material it satirises, already accepted as legitimate cultural commentary.

Three theoretical points follow. *First*, the contested-terrain claim is not relativism. Hall is not saying there is no difference in cultural value between high-canonical art and the most formulaic mass-produced product; he is saying the *machinery* by which that difference is constructed and reproduced is sociological, not metaphysical, and is itself part of what should be analysed. *Second*, the claim is not anti-evaluation. Williams, Hall and Hoggart were all unembarrassed about preferring some products to others; the framework is compatible with sustained critical evaluation, but requires the evaluator to declare their position rather than pretend to disinterestedness. *Third*, the claim is *empirically falsifiable*: if a cultural category boundary turned out never to have moved, the framework would be in trouble. The historical record provides no such case.

Hall's reformulation is essentially correct, has been vindicated by the historical and contemporary record, and remains the most powerful analytical framework available for the sociological study of popular culture. The remaining work is not to re-litigate the essence-claim but to extend the contested-terrain analysis into the platform-era cultural conflicts reshaping the field.

### M13. "What does the recurrence of moral panic across punk, drill, video games and short-form video reveal about the deep structure of the mass culture debate?"

The recurrence reveals a deep structure with three constants and one variable. The constants are the demographic of the targeted producer-consumers, the institutional choreography of the panic, and the displaced political work it performs. The variable is the specific cultural form. The deep-structural reading reorganises the mass culture debate around its sociological substrate rather than the procession of cultural objects. I work from the lecture instances: music genres (punk, drill), the "brain rot" meme discourse, the English Literature/Latin teaching debates, and the broader populism/"mob rule" anxiety.

The slide juxtaposes 1976 punk and contemporary drill, and the parallel is forensically exact. *Demographic*: urban working-class young people, predominantly white in 1976 and predominantly Black in the drill case, producing a fast, vernacular, locally-rooted form. *Institutional choreography*: the tabloid press generates initial amplification (the slide reproduces front pages from the *Sun*, *Daily Mail*, *Daily Mirror*, *Daily Express*); moral entrepreneurs emerge; the state apparatus follows; the cultural object is reclassified as a "threat" requiring response. *Displaced political work*: the panic mobilises around a cultural form anxieties whose underlying object is something else, youth unemployment, post-industrial decline, racial conflict, urban-policy failure. Hall's *Policing the Crisis* (1978) supplies the canonical statement: "media, policing, and state power interact to produce ideological responses to wider social and economic crises".

Extending the analysis. The *"brain rot"* panic about TikTok and smartphones fits the template with demographic shifted: teenagers and young adults consuming a medium older critics do not understand. The displaced political work is the underlying question of cultural concentration in a handful of platforms. The *English Literature/Latin teaching* rows are the same panic in a different register: whose children should read which canon, doing displaced work for anxieties about social mobility. The GB News "Mickey Mouse degrees" slide is the same gesture aimed at higher education. The *GTA* youth-violence panic and the Andrew Tate panic run the same template displaced respectively to interactive media and class-coded masculinity.

The deep-structural reading does three things. *First*, it explains the *predictability*: the same structural position gets targeted in each generation because the same position is what the panic exists to discipline (working-class culture-producers whose work circulates beyond the demographic that produced it). *Second*, it explains the *temporal pattern*: each form gets a moral-panic phase, then is reclassified as legitimate heritage, and the panic is redirected. Jazz, cinema, rock'n'roll, the Hitchcock-as-auteur trajectory (Strinati, 1995: 41) are the proof. Punk has completed the arc; drill is currently in panic phase. The same trajectory has played out with Black music more broadly, the appropriation debate being one of its constant features: Beyoncé and Stormzy now sit comfortably inside the cultural establishment that would have policed their forebears. *Third*, it explains the *displacement mechanism*: in each case the panic mobilises political energy around a cultural form rather than the underlying socio-economic conditions, allowing institutional response to feel adequate without addressing the conditions.

The recurrence is evidence for the Carey-Ross-Hall reading, not the Arnold-Leavis reading. The mass culture debate is *not* really about cultural quality, if it were, its objects would be the high-prestige cultural products whose mediocrity is well-attested, not the low-prestige working-class forms that consistently attract panic. It is, as Carey says, a debate "haunted by anxieties about democracy itself", about who is allowed to produce and consume culture. The Lecture 11 summary's list, with populism/"mob rule"/democratisation as a recurring instance, makes this explicit.

Three refinements close the argument. (a) The deep structure is *sociological*, not psychological: it does not depend on bad faith, and most moral entrepreneurs are sincere. (b) It is *historically variable in detail*: demographic specifics, institutional channels and political-economic context shift; analysis must be case-by-case. (c) It is *not deterministic*: panics can be resisted. The Worpole/White/Hebdige evidence that working-class consumers actively appropriated American iconography (Strinati, 1995: 29-32) is the lecture's own demonstration.

The recurrence reveals that the mass culture debate's surface preoccupation with cultural objects masks a stable sociological deep structure organised around the discipline of working-class cultural production and consumption. The debate's content changes; its function does not.
