## §1. Header / framing

This cheat sheet covers the mass society / mass culture debate as it runs from Matthew Arnold's mid-Victorian fears of "anarchy", through F. R. and Q. D. Leavis's interwar literary defence of cultural minority, into the post-war Americanisation panic (Hoggart, Orwell), and finally to the cultural-studies pushback by Raymond Williams ("culture is ordinary") and Stuart Hall (the popular as a contested terrain). The lecture stages the debate as a recurring problem rather than a settled question: each generation re-invents the worry that the wrong people, consuming the wrong things, will destroy moral order, cultural standards, and political stability. Punk in the 1970s, drill in the 2020s, the "brain rot" panic over short-form video, the culture-wars row over Florida book bans, the GTA-and-violence debate, and Andrew Tate's de-platforming are all live instances of the same template. The examinable task is to be able to (a) reconstruct the Arnoldian / Leavisite position with its strongest possible case, (b) explain why mass society theory rests on a particular sociology of industrialisation, urbanisation and atomisation, and (c) deploy Williams, Bourdieu and Hall to demolish or qualify it without flattening the seriousness of the original concern.

---

## §2. Key concepts and thinkers (≤200 words)

**Mass society:** atomised, urbanised, industrial society in which mediating institutions (village, family, church) have decayed and the individual stands exposed to centralised media power. **Mass culture:** popular culture produced industrially for a mass market, allegedly standardised, formulaic and homogenising. **Folk culture:** pre-industrial, autonomous culture made by the people for themselves. **The culture and civilisation tradition:** Arnold to Leavis lineage that treats culture as moral ideal and elite minority preserve. **Sweetness and light:** Arnold's pairing of beauty (sweetness) with cultivated intelligence (light). **Anarchy:** Arnold's term for erosion of shared moral standards under democratic disorder. **The cultural elite / conscious minority:** Q. D. Leavis's "armed and conscious minority" who must defend literary standards. **Brow culture:** the high / middle / low hierarchy. **Taste as class marker:** Bourdieu's claim that aesthetic judgement encodes class position. **Polysemic text, dominant / negotiated / oppositional readings, relative autonomy:** Hall's reception apparatus. **Moral panic:** disproportionate public alarm about a cultural practice as proxy for class anxiety. **Key thinkers:** Matthew Arnold, F. R. Leavis, Q. D. Leavis, Dwight MacDonald, George Orwell, Richard Hoggart, Raymond Williams, Pierre Bourdieu, Stuart Hall, Dick Hebdige, John Carey.

## §4. Information from the readings

### Strinati, ch. 1, "Mass culture and popular culture"

Strinati frames the chapter as a genealogy of the idea of mass culture, starting from Burke's claim that the modern concept of popular culture emerges with late-eighteenth-century national consciousness and Herder's distinction between popular and learned culture, then moving through Williams's reminder in *Keywords* that "popular" carries at least four overlapping senses: well-liked by many people; inferior kinds of work (popular press vs. quality press); deliberately setting out to win favour (popular journalism); and, most recently, "the culture actually made by people for themselves", often displaced to the past as folk culture. The chapter then isolates three recurrent themes that organise the literature: (1) where does popular culture come from, top-down or bottom-up? (2) what does industrialisation / commercialisation do to it, does the commodity form eat artistry? (3) what is its ideological role, indoctrination or resistance?

**Mass culture and mass society:** the major claim of mass society theory is that industrialisation and urbanisation destabilise the agrarian village community, the family, the church and religious life, producing "atomisation". The atomised individual, bound to others by purely contractual ties, is left without mediating institutions, becomes anomic and morally adrift, and is therefore peculiarly vulnerable to manipulation by mass media and mass markets. As Strinati puts it, "in a mass society, the individual is left more and more to his or her own devices, has fewer and fewer communities or institutions in which to find identity or values by which to live, and has less and less idea of the morally appropriate ways to live" (Strinati, 1995: 5). Religious certainties give way to "the amoral immediacy of rational individualism and secular anomie", and mass culture functions as "the moral placebos of a mass society" (Strinati, 1995: 6).

**The mass culture debate:** mass culture is "popular culture which is produced by the industrial techniques of mass production, and marketed for profit to a mass public of consumers" (Strinati, 1995: 10). MacDonald's formula stands at the centre: "Folk Art grew from below. It was a spontaneous, autochthonous expression of the people, shaped by themselves, pretty much without the benefit of High Culture, to suit their own needs. Mass Culture is imposed from above. It is fabricated by technicians hired by businessmen; its audiences are passive consumers, their participation limited to the choice between buying and not buying." (MacDonald, 1953: 60, cited in Strinati, 1995: 9). MacDonald goes further: mass culture "is a dynamic, revolutionary force, breaking down the old barriers of class, tradition, taste, and dissolving all cultural distinctions … producing what might be called homogenized culture", and is therefore "very, very democratic: it absolutely refuses to discriminate against, or between, anything or anybody" (MacDonald, 1953: 62, cited in Strinati, 1995: 15).

**Q. D. Leavis** appears as the empirical wing of the case. Strinati quotes her on the bifurcated reading public, the "lowbrow public" ignorant even of the names of highbrow writers, the highbrow public only knowing names like Ethel M. Dell and Tarzan "from hearsay rather than first-hand knowledge"; the critical minority "isolated, disowned by the general public and threatened with extinction" (Leavis, 1932: 35, cited in Strinati, 1995: 14). Her policy recommendation is unambiguous: "all that can be done, it must be realised, must take the form of resistance by an armed and conscious minority" (Leavis, 1932: 270, cited in Strinati, 1995: 18).

**Americanisation:** Strinati spends a long section showing that British mass-culture theory is bound up with anti-Americanism. Arnold already complained in 1869 that "in things of the mind, and in culture and totality, America, instead of surpassing us all, falls short … that chosen home of newspapers and politics" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 21). F. R. Leavis takes Americanisation as "an accomplished fact", "American conditions are the conditions of modern civilization" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 22). Orwell's essay on "the decline of the English murder" laments that the No Orchids type of Americanised crime fiction has no "depth of feeling" and that "Freud and Machiavelli have reached the outer suburbs" (Orwell, 1946, cited in Strinati, 1995: 24). Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy* (1957) finds in the milk-bar "juke-box boys", "with drape-suits, picture ties, and an American slouch" who "waggle one shoulder or stare, as desperately as Humphrey Bogart, across the tubular chairs", the symbol of "a shiny barbarism" replacing the dense moral fabric of the working-class community (Hoggart, 1957: 203–204, cited in Strinati, 1995: 27).

**Worpole and White** are introduced as oral-historical counter-evidence: working-class readers of the 1930s and 1940s actively preferred American crime fiction precisely because it offered "a realism about city life, an acknowledgement of big business corruption, and an unpatronising portrayal of working class experience and speech" missing from the corpse-in-the-library English crime novel (Worpole, 1983: 35, cited in Strinati, 1995: 30). White's history of Campbell Bunk in Finsbury Park shows young men using *Little Caesar*, *Public Enemy* and *Scarface* to "see themselves as heroes rather than bystanders, the subject of life rather than its object" (White, 1986: 166, cited in Strinati, 1995: 31) [VERIFY YEAR?]. Hebdige extends this into a subcultural-bricolage thesis: American iconography is "a set of symbols, objects and artefacts which can be assembled and re-assembled by different groups in a literally limitless number of combinations" (Hebdige, 1988: 74, cited in Strinati, 1995: 32) [VERIFY YEAR?].

**Critique of mass culture theory:** Strinati's final section catalogues seven problems, elitism; a "feminisation" of mass culture that lets critics dismiss anything weepy or romantic as feminine and therefore inferior (Modleski); a romanticised "golden age" with no clear historical referent; conflation of audience with passive dupe; inability to register genre or generic diversity within "mass" culture; failure to see how "art" / "popular" boundaries are themselves historical (Hitchcock as auteur, jazz as art, early rock 'n' roll as classic); and Ang's *Watching Dallas* (1989) [VERIFY YEAR?] finding that viewers articulate their pleasures and dislikes through a publicly available "ideology of mass culture" versus an "ideology of populism", deploying "ironic distance" to license enjoyment of supposedly bad objects.

### Arnold, *Culture and Anarchy* (1869)

Arnold's core move is the redefinition of culture as "the best that has been thought and said", a normative, hierarchical, perfectionist ideal rather than a description of how anyone actually lives. The book's polemical targets are (a) Hebraism (narrow Puritan moralism), (b) Philistinism (the propertied middle class's vulgar utilitarianism), (c) Barbarism (aristocratic complacency), and (d) the Populace (the riotous working class issuing from its "hiding place" and "doing as it likes"). Culture is offered as the corrective to all four. The state, properly conceived, embodies "our best self" and supplies the moral authority democratic society would otherwise lack. The slogan "sweetness and light" is the programme: cultivated intelligence to illuminate, refined beauty to humanise.

### F. R. Leavis, *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) and *Culture and Environment* (1933)

Leavis radicalises Arnold for the age of cinema, radio and tabloid press. The minority that "discerning appreciation of art and literature depends" upon now has to be deliberately trained, because the surrounding environment, advertising, pulp fiction, formulaic film, standardised journalism, actively de-skills the moral and aesthetic sensibility of the modern reader. *Culture and Environment*, co-written with Denys Thompson, is an English-classroom textbook designed to teach pupils to "read against" advertising and mass communication. The "organic community" of pre-industrial England functions as a regulative ideal (which Williams will later show is largely a literary fiction). The journal *Scrutiny* institutionalised the project.

### Q. D. Leavis, *Fiction and the Reading Public* (1932; expanded 1939)

Q. D. Leavis is the empirical sociologist of the Leavis circle. Working from publishers' lists, library borrowing data, questionnaires and best-seller surveys, she argues that the reading public has been stratified into highbrow, middlebrow and lowbrow strata served by different publishing infrastructures, and that the commercial market for fiction privileges formulaic genres (romance, adventure, detective) over the "serious" novel. Her tone is pessimistic but the method, market analysis, audience study, attention to the institutional shape of the cultural industries, anticipates exactly the sociology of culture that would later be turned against her conclusions.

### Hall, "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" (1981)

Hall rejects both poles of the Strinati dichotomy. Popular culture is neither MacDonald's debased imposition nor a pure expression of "the people". It is a contested terrain, a "battlefield", on which dominant institutions and subordinate groups continually rework cultural meanings. Two corollaries: (a) audiences are not passive; texts are polysemic and can be read in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways depending on the social position of the decoder; (b) culture has only relative autonomy, structural constraints (capital, race, class, gender) are real, but never wholly determining.

## §5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The Arnoldian formula, culture as "the best that has been thought and said" (Arnold, 1869: 6), looks, at first glance, like an innocent celebration of excellence. The work the formula does, though, is double. It simultaneously *defines* culture (as a curated, hierarchical canon) and *locates* the authority to police that definition (in a minority "with superior judgement"). Arnold's own description of the London working class, "raw and half developed … beginning to perplex us by marching where it likes, meeting where it likes, bawling what it likes, breaking what it likes", exposes the political reflex underneath the aesthetic argument. The fear of "anarchy" is in the first instance a fear of the franchise: *Culture and Anarchy* was written in the immediate aftermath of the 1867 Reform Act and the Hyde Park railings riots. I read Arnold, then, less as a literary critic than as a constitutional theorist of an emerging mass democracy who wants culture to do the disciplinary work that an established church and a deferential class structure can no longer do alone.

F. R. Leavis tightens this into a doctrine: "Upon this minority depends our power of profiting by the finest human experience of the past; they keep alive the subtlest and most perishable parts of tradition" (Leavis, 1930). What is striking here is that the minority is described in *custodial* rather than *creative* terms, its job is to keep tradition alive against contamination. Perry Anderson and Francis Mulhern's verdict that Leavisism is "petit-bourgeois moralism" and "radical conservatism" is, on this reading, fair. Yet Leavis is not simply reactionary: *Culture and Environment* shares with 1930s Marxist cultural critique a hostility to industrial capitalism, advertising, and the standardising machinery of the press, it just refuses the socialist conclusion. The Leavises offer the only sustained right-conservative critique of capitalism's cultural effects in the English tradition, and that is a position worth understanding rather than caricaturing.

Q. D. Leavis is the most interesting figure for sociological purposes, because her *method* contradicts her *conclusions*. In *Fiction and the Reading Public* she examines publishing markets, library lists and readership stratification with empirical seriousness, and concludes that "the critical minority to whose sole charge modern literature has now fallen is isolated, disowned by the general public and threatened with extinction" (Leavis, 1932: 35). Her remedy, "resistance by an armed and conscious minority" (Leavis, 1932: 270), is the manifesto of the Cambridge English faculty as it would constitute itself in the 1930s and 1940s, and via the post-war expansion of higher education would be exported across the English-speaking world as the curriculum of "English literature".

Stuart Hall's response is to refuse both the elitist and the populist pole. In "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'", he argues that popular culture is a "site of struggle", not an essence; that media texts are polysemic and yield dominant, negotiated or oppositional readings depending on the decoder's social position; that culture has "relative autonomy", it is shaped by structures of capital and race and gender, but is not reducible to them. This generates a research programme rather than a verdict. Take the *Daily Mirror*'s "Boris: Working class men are drunk, criminal & feckless" front page from 2019. A preferred reading takes it as straightforward exposure of an out-of-touch elite. A negotiated reading enjoys the spectacle of Johnson's hypocrisy without accepting the *Mirror*'s tribune-of-the-people self-image. An oppositional reading, Hall would say, notices that the framing repeats the very stereotype it claims to denounce, presenting working-class men as objects to be defended rather than political actors.

The contemporary instances trail off into the present. The 1976 *Daily Mirror* headline "WHO ARE THESE PUNKS?" and the 2018 Met Police list of drill videos to be removed from YouTube are formally identical: an old institution naming, photographing and policing a working-class youth subculture associated with a particular kind of urban music. UK punk and the Sex Pistols then; UK drill now. The "brain rot" discourse around TikTok scrolling and short-form video reproduces Q. D. Leavis's argument almost verbatim, except that "set of habits inimical to mental effort" now reads "dopamine loop". The Florida book bans, the Andrew Tate de-platforming row, the recurring GTA-violence-debates, the *Love Island* discourse, each repeats the template: a panic about who is consuming what, dressed up as a concern about democracy's moral and aesthetic health.

The most powerful critiques of the Arnoldian-Leavisite position are three. (1) Bourdieu shows that aesthetic taste is not objective but a class marker, the disinterested judgement of the "pure gaze" is itself an acquired competence whose acquisition tracks economic and educational capital. (2) Raymond Williams insists that "culture is ordinary", not the preserve of an elite but the lived, ordinary practice of meaning-making by which all communities sustain themselves. (3) Hall historicises the categories themselves: "the popular" is not a stable object that critics merely describe, it is a shifting boundary that cultural authorities re-draw in each generation, with consequences for who gets to count as a legitimate cultural actor. To these I would add John Carey's blunter point in *The Intellectuals and the Masses*, that the mass culture debate has "always been haunted by anxieties about democracy itself", and that contempt for popular culture often masks contempt for the people who make and consume it. The point of the lecture, I think, is not to choose a winner, Arnold and Hall both name something real, but to recognise that any account of culture which cannot specify *who is doing the defining* and *who benefits from the definition* is doing covert political work.

## §8. Essay answers

### M1. "Critically evaluate the claim that mass culture is a threat."

I want to argue that the statement "mass culture is a threat" is, at best, half-true, and that the half it gets wrong matters more than the half it gets right. The phrase compresses two distinct claims: a sociological one, that the products of culture industries flatten variety and concentrate symbolic power; and a normative one, that the resulting culture is debased and damaging. The first is partly defensible. The second collapses under the weight of its own assumptions.

The Arnold-Leavis tradition supplies the strongest version of the threat thesis. Arnold's *Culture and Anarchy* (1869) treats culture, "the best that has been thought and said", as the only available counterweight to mass democracy's tendency towards "anarchy", an erosion of shared moral standards. F. R. Leavis's *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) industrialises the worry: in the age of mass media, "the discerning appreciation of art and literature depends" on a deliberately trained minority, because the surrounding environment of advertising, pulp fiction and standardised journalism actively de-skills the modern sensibility. Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* (1939) adds the empirical apparatus, readership stratification, formulaic publishing markets, the eclipse of the serious novel, and concludes that "the critical minority to whose sole charge modern literature has now fallen is isolated, disowned by the general public and threatened with extinction" (Leavis, 1932: 35). Dwight MacDonald, on the American side, completes the picture: mass culture "is a dynamic, revolutionary force, breaking down the old barriers of class, tradition, taste, and dissolving all cultural distinctions … producing what might be called homogenized culture" (MacDonald, 1953: 62).

There is a genuine empirical core here. The Netflix-algorithm thesis, that recommendation systems homogenise content towards the centre of a global attention market, is a 2020s version of MacDonald's homogenisation argument. The platform infrastructure of TikTok, YouTube and Spotify does reward formulaic, short-form, engagement-maximising content. The Carey-Ross point on the slide, that mass culture critique is haunted by anxieties about democracy itself, and that "contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety", concedes that the *threat* discourse exists and matters. To this extent the threat thesis names something real.

The problem is everything that follows from naming it. Three failures stand out. First, the threat thesis rests on a "feminisation" move (Modleski), anything sentimental, romantic, emotional or pleasurable is coded as feminine and therefore degraded, while the supposedly muscular intellectual pleasures of high culture are coded as masculine and therefore valuable. The dismissal of *Love Island*, reality TV, romance fiction and soap opera tracks this gendered hierarchy with embarrassing precision. Second, the thesis assumes a passive audience. Hall's encoding / decoding model in "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" shows that media texts are polysemic and yield dominant, negotiated or oppositional readings depending on the decoder's social position. Ien Ang's *Watching Dallas* (1989) demonstrates this empirically: viewers articulate their relationship to American soap through a publicly available "ideology of mass culture" alongside an "ideology of populism", and many use "ironic distance" to license their enjoyment. Third, the thesis rests on a romanticised "organic community" that, as Williams showed in *The Country and the City*, is largely a literary fiction projected backwards from the present.

Bourdieu's *Distinction* delivers the decisive blow. The Arnoldian "best that has been thought and said" is not an objective standard discovered by a refined minority, it is the codified taste of a specific class fraction (the cultured bourgeoisie) whose acquisition of that taste tracks their inherited economic and educational capital. To say "mass culture is a threat" is therefore, on Bourdieu's reading, to defend the symbolic monopoly of one class fraction against the consumption practices of another.

What survives, then, of the threat thesis? Three things. (a) The political-economy worry about concentration of cultural production in a small number of platforms is real and has been borne out empirically. (b) The Q. D. Leavis insight that commercial pressures shape what gets published, played and recommended is correct and underpins the contemporary sociology of culture industries. (c) The anxiety about democracy that Carey identifies is itself a phenomenon worth studying, the threat discourse is data for sociologists, not just an opinion to agree or disagree with.

What does not survive is the normative claim that the products of mass culture are intrinsically debased. Beyoncé is a mass-cultural product and a major artist. Black music, continually appropriated and continually re-made, has been the engine of twentieth- and twenty-first-century popular form. UK drill, currently policed by the Met as a threat, is a complex urban poetic tradition. To call this stuff a "threat" is to confuse one's discomfort with a sociological fact.

I conclude with Hall's reformulation: popular culture is a contested terrain, not an essence. The right question is not "is mass culture a threat?" but "to whom, and on whose behalf, is the threat being declared?" Answering that question converts a panicked aesthetic complaint into a political analysis, which is where the lecture, properly understood, wants us to end up.

### M2. "Is the notion of 'mass society' still meaningful in the era of algorithmic media?"

I argue that "mass society" survives as a heuristic but fails as a description, and that the algorithmic-media environment of the 2020s requires a different vocabulary, one closer to Hall's "relative autonomy" and Bennett's structured-power formulation than to the atomisation narrative of MacDonald or Leavis.

The classical mass society concept rests on a specific sociology. Industrialisation and urbanisation eradicate agrarian community, decay the mediating institutions of village, family and church, and leave the individual "atomised", bound to others only by contractual ties, morally adrift, peculiarly exposed to manipulation by centralised media. As Strinati puts it, the atomised person "has fewer and fewer communities or institutions in which to find identity or values by which to live" (Strinati, 1995: 5). The mass media supply, in MacDonald's words, the "moral placebos" that ineffective surrogate moralities require (Strinati, 1995: 6). On this model, the audience is a uniform mass, a "lonely crowd", in David Riesman's phrase, and the cultural product is a homogeneous good aimed at the lowest common denominator.

There are obvious senses in which contemporary platform capitalism vindicates parts of this story. Concentration of attention in a small number of US-headquartered platforms is real. The Netflix algorithm's tendency to push content towards the predicted-engagement mean is the closest empirical analogue to MacDonald's homogenisation thesis. The "brain rot" discourse around short-form video reproduces Q. D. Leavis's worry, that the modern reader is being trained into "a set of habits inimical to mental effort", with eerie literalness. Smartphone-and-attention research at least gestures at an atomisation-like effect, with declines in face-to-face community involvement.

But three features of the present make "mass society" a poor descriptor. First, the audience is no longer a mass. Algorithmic personalisation means that two users of TikTok or YouTube in the same household see entirely different content libraries. The unifying "mass culture moment" of three-channel terrestrial television, everyone watching the same episode of *Coronation Street*, has been replaced by fragmentation into micro-publics. The horror that critics now express about "filter bubbles" is the *opposite* of the horror Arnold expressed about homogenisation; we are now panicking that there is too much variety, not too little. Second, the relation of audience to producer has been inverted. Platform users *are* the producers, PewDiePie, MrBeast, the millions of TikTok creators, and the line between professional and amateur production has been blurred to the point of meaninglessness. The MacDonald formula "fabricated by technicians hired by businessmen; its audiences are passive consumers" is empirically false for most of the cultural output of the 2020s. Third, the encoding / decoding work that Hall theorised in the 1970s is now visibly built into the platforms themselves: comment sections, duets, stitches, quote-tweets and remix tools institutionalise polysemic reading.

The notion that does survive, and survives in stronger form, is Tony Bennett's reformulation, captured on the lecture slide: culture "is not simply imposed from above, nor is it freely chosen from below. It is produced, regulated, circulated, and interpreted within structured relations of power." This is a less melodramatic claim than the mass society thesis but it is the right shape. Platform algorithms structure visibility without dictating consumption; content-moderation regimes structure speech without controlling speech; recommendation systems structure taste without imposing taste. Hall's "relative autonomy" handles this better than atomisation: culture is shaped by capital, race, class and gender but is not fully determined by them.

There is also the LeMahieu point on the slide, the historical tension between "democratic inclusion and elite guidance", which retains its analytical bite. Every public-service-broadcasting debate, every Wikipedia editorial dispute, every content-moderation row at Twitter / X or Meta is at root a fight over how much elite guidance, of what kind, is compatible with mass democratic participation. That is recognisably the same fight Arnold was having in 1869.

A final example. The 2018 Met Police request that YouTube remove UK drill videos is a perfect Arnoldian moment: a state agency, claiming to act on behalf of public safety, instructing a platform to suppress a working-class musical tradition because of its supposed effect on civic order. The atomisation vocabulary cannot do justice to this, drill is intensely communal, locally rooted, decidedly *not* the product of a passive mass. The vocabulary that works is Hall's: a contested terrain, with a dominant institution (the Met) and a subordinate group (young Black Londoners) struggling over the meaning and visibility of cultural production. Mass society as a phrase no longer captures what is happening. Cultural conflict, structured by relations of power but never fully determined by them, does.

So: "mass society" still names the historical anxiety, and parts of its sociology remain useful. As a description of where we now live, it has been overtaken. The work it was doing has been redistributed across the more granular concepts of platform power, algorithmic curation, polysemic reception and contested terrain.

### M3. "To what extent does the BBC reflect the 'culture and civilisation tradition'?"

I argue that the BBC is the most consequential institutional embodiment of the Arnoldian-Leavisite culture and civilisation tradition in twentieth- and twenty-first-century British life, but that its contemporary identity is a compromise formation, partly Arnoldian, partly populist, and that the tensions within that compromise generate most of the current political pressure on the Corporation.

The historical case for the BBC as Arnoldian is overwhelming. John Reith's founding mission, "to inform, to educate, to entertain" in that order, is a near-verbatim translation of Arnold's "sweetness and light", and Reith's explicit aim in the 1920s and 1930s was to "give the public something slightly better than it now thinks it likes". That sentence is the Arnoldian programme in operational form: cultural authority should "restrain and educate the masses". The institution-building task Arnold could only gesture at, a national cultural authority to do for democratic civic life what an established church and a deferential class structure could no longer do alone, was completed by the BBC between roughly 1922 and 1955. The single channel, the received-pronunciation continuity announcer, the Promenade Concerts, the Third Programme, the Reith Lectures, the *Sunday Service*, these were not stylistic choices, they were the cultural-and-civilisation tradition staffed and budgeted.

The Leavisite layer overlaid the Arnoldian one between roughly 1930 and 1980. F. R. Leavis's Cambridge English faculty trained the producers, controllers and commissioning editors who would run BBC radio and television drama in the post-war decades; the Leavisite formula that "studying English literature becomes a means of cultivating moral discrimination and resisting the standardising effects of mass communication" describes very accurately the editorial self-image of *The Wednesday Play*, *Play for Today*, and the BBC Schools and Open University output of the 1960s and 1970s. *Civilisation* (Kenneth Clark, 1969) and *The Ascent of Man* (Bronowski, 1973) are the genre's cathedral works, long-form, single-author, didactic, hierarchical, unembarrassed about the canonical privilege of European elite art.

Three things complicate the case. First, the BBC has always carried a parallel populist tradition, light entertainment, variety, comedy, sport, which is decidedly not Arnoldian. *Strictly Come Dancing*, *Saturday Kitchen*, *The Apprentice*, *EastEnders* and *Doctor Who* are unmistakably popular-cultural forms, and the Corporation's licence-fee defence has always required broad-audience reach. The 2002 *Great Britons* poll, in which the public voted Diana ahead of Darwin, was experienced inside the BBC as a problem precisely because it exposed the gap between the Arnoldian self-image and the populist remit. Second, the Williams critique, "culture is ordinary", was substantially absorbed by the BBC in the 1960s through the work of people like Stuart Hood, and *Cathy Come Home* (1966), *Boys from the Blackstuff* (1982) and *Our Friends in the North* (1996) are Williams-shaped programmes, not Arnoldian ones. Third, the Hall reception-theory move, the recognition of plural audiences with different reading positions, was institutionalised in the 1980s and 1990s through audience research, BBC Two's targeted commissioning, and ultimately the iPlayer-and-podcast strategy of the 2010s.

The current position, then, is a hybrid. The Reith Lectures, Radio 4 *In Our Time*, BBC Four arts commissioning, the Proms, and the news-and-current-affairs flagship operations are Arnoldian-Leavisite in design, they assume that broadcasting can and should "minister to the diseased spirit of our time" through cultivated intelligence. The mainstream television commissioning, the radio music networks (1, 1Xtra, 6Music), and the social-media operations are post-Williams in design, they take the diversity of ordinary cultural life as their starting point. The structural tension between these two halves is what generates the recurring crises of the licence-fee debate, the political-impartiality rows, and the Corporation's awkward dance with streaming competition.

Three contemporary illustrations sharpen the diagnosis. (a) The 2018 row about BBC documentaries on UK drill: the Corporation's instinctive Arnoldian impulse was to present drill as a problem to be explained to a worried middle-class audience, rather than a vital cultural form whose practitioners should be commissioning their own programmes. (b) The BBC's commissioning of Beyoncé-themed coverage around the *Renaissance* world tour: this is precisely the move Williams licensed, taking a mass-cultural artist seriously as an artist, and it generated the predictable backlash from Arnoldian critics. (c) The "Mickey Mouse degrees" debate the slide references via GB News: the same Arnoldian reflex now occurs *outside* the BBC and is used to attack it, with right-wing media accusing the Corporation of having abandoned cultural seriousness in favour of populist concession.

So the answer to the question is "extensively, but unevenly, and decreasingly". The BBC remains the most important institutional carrier of the culture and civilisation tradition in the English-speaking world. But it has spent forty years absorbing Williams, Hoggart, Hall and Bourdieu into its own operating practice, and the unresolved tension between the Reithian DNA and the cultural-studies overlay is the central drama of the institution's current crisis. Whether the BBC can sustain the compromise as audiences fragment further and political pressure mounts is, in effect, the question of whether the culture and civilisation tradition has any institutional future at all.

### M4. "'Contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety' (Ross). Discuss with reference to at least one contemporary moral panic."

I want to defend Ross's claim and use the UK drill / Metropolitan Police controversy of 2018–24 as my central case, before generalising to the brain-rot, *Love Island* and Andrew Tate panics. The argument runs: every named "moral panic" in the recent British cultural record turns out, on inspection, to be a panic about which class of person is doing which class of consuming, and the aesthetic complaint is doing political work.

The Carey-Ross slide encapsulates the framework. John Carey's *The Intellectuals and the Masses* (1992) is the long-form version: the mass culture debate "has always been haunted by anxieties about democracy itself", and "hostility toward mass culture often coincides with hostility toward mass participation, mass literacy, and the erosion of elite authority". Andrew Ross's *No Respect* sharpens this into the present claim, that the disgust intellectuals express at popular cultural forms is, on close inspection, displaced disgust at the social class of the form's typical consumer.

The UK drill case is the textbook instance. Between 2018 and 2024 the Metropolitan Police obtained at least 270 takedown requests against drill videos on YouTube, secured Criminal Behaviour Orders against named artists (1011, Skengdo & AM, Digga D) that *prohibited specific lyrics*, and lobbied platforms for proactive removal. The official justification is consequentialist: drill lyrics are alleged to incite gang violence and produce statistically measurable upticks in knife crime. The aesthetic-and-class logic underneath the justification is straight Arnold. The targeted artists are without exception young, Black, working-class men from specific London postcodes (Brixton, Tottenham, Peckham, Croydon). The lyrical content that is policed, fast, dense, codified, urban-vernacular, often involving boasts about violence, is structurally identical to the content that has been celebrated in white-bourgeois "high" cultural traditions for centuries (the warrior boast in Beowulf, the duelling code in Romantic poetry, the violent set-pieces of nineteenth-century opera). The decision that drill is uniquely "criminogenic" and Beowulf is uniquely "literary" is a class decision, not a content decision.

The 1976 punk panic is the historical template the lecture slide invites us to recognise. The same tabloid press (the *Sun*, the *Daily Mail*, the *Daily Mirror*) ran the same kind of front pages, "THE FILTH AND THE FURY!", "WHO ARE THESE PUNKS?", "ROCK GROUP START A 4-LETTER TV STORM", about the same demographic, the white urban working-class male. The Sex Pistols' "Anarchy in the UK" was treated as a literal anarchic threat to civic order rather than as the obvious piece of art-school pop-Situationism it actually was. McLaren and Westwood's strategic mobilisation of the panic for marketing purposes is exactly the kind of thing the mass-culture critics ought to have predicted but did not. The pattern is clear: every two generations the British press and police apparatus discover a new working-class musical form, declare it the end of moral order, and use the resulting panic to discipline the demographic that produced it.

The brain-rot panic about TikTok and short-form video updates the same template for the 2020s. The objection, that endless scrolling produces "a set of habits inimical to mental effort", to quote Q. D. Leavis's 1932 line almost verbatim, is dressed in neuroscientific clothing (dopamine loops, attention deficits, executive-function decay) but performs the familiar work. Notice who the panic targets: not the financiers and engineers who built the platforms, but the (predominantly young, predominantly female, often working-class) users who consume them. The Florida book-ban discourse around school libraries is the same move in different costume, the policed objects are overwhelmingly books by and about working-class, Black, immigrant or LGBTQ+ communities, and the policing class is overwhelmingly white-middle-class suburban. *Love Island* and the entire reality-TV / Towie / Geordie Shore ecology generates a continuous low-grade contempt discourse that is identifiably class-coded: the housemates' accents, taste in clothes, vocabulary and bodily presentation are the things being criticised, not anything substantive about the format. The Andrew Tate de-platforming row is interestingly half-different, Tate's audience is largely working-class male teenagers, but Tate himself is wealthy, and the disgust is more straightforwardly political (misogyny) than class-based, though the brittleness of the consensus around his removal traces the usual class lines.

The Ross claim is not that aesthetic judgements are *only* class anxiety. Some criticisms of popular cultural forms are well-founded, drill does sometimes incite, TikTok algorithms are sometimes harmful, Tate is sometimes monstrous. The claim is that the *vehemence and selectivity* of the criticism, the choice to panic about *this* and not *that*, the political energy that gets mobilised, all track class anxiety with very high reliability. Hall's encoding / decoding apparatus then explains why panics persist even when their empirical basis is weak: the dominant reading is encoded into the press / police / state apparatus, and is therefore reproduced as common sense.

I conclude that Ross's claim is essentially correct, and that the drill panic is a particularly clean instance because the racial and class lines are unusually sharp. The intellectual task, the one Carey, Hall, Bourdieu and Williams all licence, is not to defend every popular cultural form, but to refuse to grant the aesthetic complaint its claim of disinterestedness. Once the class location of the complainant becomes part of the analysis, most moral panics shrink to their proper size.

### M5. "Compare Arnold's defence of culture with F. R. Leavis's account of the 'minority'. What, if anything, is gained by the move from 1869 to 1930?"

I argue that Leavis radicalises Arnold's defence of culture along three axes, he industrialises the threat-diagnosis, he professionalises the defending minority, and he gives the project an institutional vehicle (the Cambridge English faculty and *Scrutiny*), and that what is gained is operational seriousness, while what is lost is Arnold's residual liberalism.

Arnold's *Culture and Anarchy* (1869) is a political-philosophical essay before it is a literary-critical one. Its target is the constitutional crisis precipitated by the Second Reform Act: how can a democratic polity that has just enfranchised the urban working class maintain shared moral standards without an established church, a deferential class structure, or an effective state apparatus? Arnold's answer, culture as "the best that has been thought and said", "sweetness and light" administered by a cultivated minority for the moral benefit of all, is genuinely liberal in two senses. First, it is universalist: the culture is in principle available to anyone willing to acquire it, and Arnold supports state-funded education as the delivery mechanism. Second, its targets are even-handed: Arnold criticises the working-class "Populace", the middle-class "Philistines", the aristocratic "Barbarians", and the puritan "Hebraists" with roughly equal vigour. His prose treats the cultivated minority as a regulative ideal rather than a fixed sociological group. The famous London-mob passage, "the working class … beginning to perplex us by marching where it likes, meeting where it likes, bawling what it likes, breaking what it likes", is alarmed but not hostile in the Leavisite sense; Arnold genuinely wants the Populace to be educated *into* culture, not walled off from it.

F. R. Leavis's *Mass Civilisation and Minority Culture* (1930) operates under different historical pressures. The mass media, radio, cinema, the popular press, now exist in industrial form and have audiences in the tens of millions. Q. D. Leavis's empirical work on the reading public has demonstrated the bifurcation of readership into brow-stratified strata that no longer share a common literary culture. The First World War has discredited the nineteenth-century optimism about progress, and the threat of cultural Americanisation has become concrete (talkies, Hollywood distribution, the pulp magazine). Leavis's argument is consequently darker and more defensive. The minority is now not a regulative ideal but a beleaguered specific group whose task is custodial: "Upon this minority depends our power of profiting by the finest human experience of the past; they keep alive the subtlest and most perishable parts of tradition. Upon them depend the implicit standards that order the finer living of an age" (Leavis, 1930).

The three things gained by the move are real. (1) *Operational seriousness:* Leavis converts Arnold's philosophical sketch into a working pedagogy, *Culture and Environment* (1933) is a school textbook, and the Cambridge English Tripos becomes the institutional embodiment of the project. By 1955 the Leavisite curriculum has been exported across the English-speaking university system. (2) *A theory of mass communication:* Leavis takes seriously, in a way Arnold did not have to, the specific machinery of advertising, formula fiction and standardised journalism. *Culture and Environment* contains close readings of Northcliffe-press journalism and inter-war advertising that anticipate cultural studies' approach to media texts by thirty years. (3) *A professional vehicle:* the journal *Scrutiny* (1932–53) institutionalised the project of training "armed and conscious" critics, as Q. D. Leavis put it. The institutional achievement is substantial.

What is lost is significant. (1) Arnold's *liberalism* erodes. The Arnoldian minority was a regulative ideal; the Leavisite minority is a sociological fact, recruited from a specific institutional base (Cambridge English, *Scrutiny*) and explicitly hostile to "petit bourgeois" middle-class taste. Perry Anderson's and Francis Mulhern's reading of Leavisism as "radical conservatism", petit-bourgeois moralism organised around a national literary canon, is, on this point, accurate. (2) Arnold's *universalism* erodes. Arnold believed culture could in principle be acquired by anyone; Leavis's "organic community" of pre-industrial England is a lost world that can be invoked but not recreated, and the working class disappears from Leavis's account except as a corrupted residue. (3) The *political* dimension thins. Arnold's culture was meant to do the work of civic religion in a democratic society; Leavis's culture has narrowed to the technical training of a tiny critical elite. The democratic ambition of *Culture and Anarchy*, that the masses might one day be educated into culture, is replaced by the defensive ambition of *Mass Civilisation*, that culture might survive the masses.

The trade-off is real. Leavis's project worked institutionally; Arnold's did not, except via the BBC and the Penguin Classics imprint. But the price of the operational gain was the abandonment of the universalist horizon that made Arnold's vision liberal in the first place. The post-Leavisite cultural studies tradition, Hoggart, Williams, Hall, has spent sixty years trying to recover the democratic ambition of Arnold without the cultural defensiveness of Leavis. Whether they have succeeded is the subject matter of the rest of this paper.

### M6. "Using Stuart Hall's encoding / decoding model, analyse a recent media representation of working-class life."

I will use Hall's encoding / decoding model, developed in *Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse* (1973) and refined in "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" (1981), to analyse the *Daily Mirror* front page of 29 November 2019 reproduced on the lecture slide: "WHAT HE REALLY THINKS: Boris: Working class men are drunk, criminal & feckless. Tory also slammed single mums in magazine rant." The image rewards close reading because it is doing several contradictory things at once, and Hall's apparatus is built for exactly this kind of polysemic object.

Hall's model has three moves. (a) Texts are *encoded* by producers under specific institutional conditions, newsroom routines, editorial line, advertising imperatives, generic conventions. (b) The encoded text carries a *preferred reading*, but is polysemic, it can in principle be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways. (c) Which reading a decoder produces depends on the decoder's social position, prior cultural competence and immediate context of reception. The model is not relativist, there is real structural power in the encoding moment, but it refuses the mass-culture-theory assumption that audiences passively absorb the preferred reading.

*Encoding analysis.* The *Mirror* is a left-leaning tabloid whose self-image is "tribune of the working class". The encoding work being done on this front page is therefore the construction of Boris Johnson as a class enemy whose private prejudices have been exposed. The huge black-on-white headline isolates the offensive descriptors, "drunk, criminal & feckless", and attributes them to Johnson, while the "WHAT HE REALLY THINKS" framing signals exposure. The accompanying photograph (cropped tightly, Johnson mid-gesture, slight grimace) is the standard tabloid-villain shot. The placement of "E.T. is back" in the top corner with a snowman image performs the tabloid's parallel job, light entertainment alongside political indignation. The "80% OFF" subscription flash bottom-left and the "SNOOTY SPECIMEN" tagline beside the Johnson photograph reinforce the populist register.

*Preferred reading.* The reader is invited to be appalled at Johnson, to identify with the working-class men he disparages, and to recognise the *Mirror* as the paper that exposes such elite contempt. This reading is supported by the headline's deictic distance ("HE", not "Boris") and by the placement of "Tory" rather than "Conservative", the populist abbreviation rather than the formal party name.

*Dominant decoding.* A reader who shares the *Mirror*'s political alignment, a Labour-voting working-class or lower-middle-class adult, perhaps in a Red Wall constituency, accepts the preferred reading without modification. Johnson's privately expressed contempt is taken as evidence of the Conservative Party's underlying class hostility. The decoding produces anger at Johnson and reinforces identification with the *Mirror*. Note that this decoding is the one the lecture slide labels "preferred", Hall uses both terms interchangeably.

*Negotiated decoding.* A reader who is sympathetic to the *Mirror*'s class politics but suspicious of its journalistic standards, perhaps a Guardian-reading Labour activist, accepts the substantive claim (Johnson did say these things in his 1995 *Spectator* magazine column) while resisting the *Mirror*'s self-presentation. The negotiated reader notices that the *Mirror* itself routinely produces exactly the kind of class-stereotyping content it accuses Johnson of, and that "tribune of the working class" is itself a marketing position rather than a sociological fact. The decoding produces a more layered response, agreement with the exposé, scepticism about the messenger.

*Oppositional decoding.* A reader from the political right, a Conservative voter, a *Telegraph* subscriber, produces a different reading entirely. The substantive content (Johnson's private remarks, made twenty-four years earlier in a satirical context) is treated as a deliberate distortion by a hostile press in the middle of a general election campaign. The *Mirror* is read as a partisan operation, the headline as smear journalism, and the working-class-defender pose as electoral cynicism. The decoding is fully oppositional, none of the preferred reading is accepted.

What Hall's apparatus reveals that the mass-culture-theory apparatus cannot is the structural irony of the image. The *Mirror* claims to defend working-class men against Johnson's stereotype, but does so by *reproducing the stereotype on its own front page in 144-point type*. The phrase "drunk, criminal & feckless" is now part of the *Mirror*'s visual identity, regardless of who originally said it. The polysemic instability of the image means that a working-class reader can plausibly read it as defence *or* as further insult, and the lecture seminar exercise on the slide ("How was your interpretation of these images reflective of your own social and cultural background?") is built precisely to surface this instability.

Three further generalisations follow. (1) Hall's relative-autonomy claim is vindicated: the *Mirror* operates within the structural constraints of the tabloid press market but is not fully determined by them, the choice to encode the front page this way reflects editorial agency. (2) The encoding / decoding model handles class-coded media imagery far better than the encoded-and-absorbed model of mass culture theory. (3) The very existence of contested decodings is what generates the political work of the image, a univocal text does not divide the audience, and dividing the audience is the *Mirror*'s commercial and political function.

### M7. "'Culture is ordinary' (Williams). How damaging is this claim to the Arnoldian programme?"

I argue that Williams's "culture is ordinary" is more damaging to the Arnoldian programme than its three short words suggest, because it does not merely add a sociological footnote to Arnold, it rewrites the ontology on which Arnold's normative claims rested. The Arnoldian programme assumes that *culture* is a particular kind of object (the best that has been thought and said), produced by a particular kind of process (creative work by a cultivated minority), and circulated through a particular kind of institution (education by the same minority). Williams demolishes each of the three assumptions in turn.

Williams first formulated the slogan in his 1958 essay *Culture is Ordinary* and elaborated it across *Culture and Society* (1958), *The Long Revolution* (1961), and *Keywords* (1976). The argument has three components. (1) *Culture is everywhere.* It is not a special-purpose product of a special-purpose minority; it is the ordinary lived practice of meaning-making by which any community sustains itself. The work of culture is done in pubs, factories, kitchens, working men's clubs and football terraces as much as in the National Gallery and Glyndebourne, and the same person typically participates in many cultural sites simultaneously. (2) *Culture is one of the most complicated words in the English language*, as Williams notes in *Keywords*: it carries at least three irreducible senses, a "tending of growth" (etymological, from agriculture), a "general process of intellectual, spiritual and aesthetic development" (Arnold's sense), and a "whole way of life, material, intellectual and spiritual" (the anthropological sense). Arnold's programme cashes the middle sense and treats the third as a degraded extension; Williams cashes the third and treats the middle as a sub-class of the third. (3) *Culture is made, not given.* The canon that Arnold treated as a found object, "the best", is in fact the precipitate of a long process of selection, transmission and institutional reproduction by specific class fractions in specific historical conditions.

The damage to Arnold is severe at each level. *Ontologically*, if culture is ordinary then Arnold's bounded object, "the best that has been thought and said", collapses into a continuous field of human meaning-making within which gradations of value can still be drawn, but only locally and provisionally. *Sociologically*, if culture is the lived practice of any community then the working-class "Populace" Arnold worried about is not *outside* culture trying to break in, it is *inside* culture all along, producing its own forms, which deserve description rather than disciplinary intervention. *Politically*, if the canon is a historical precipitate rather than a found object then the Arnoldian minority is not the disinterested custodian of value but an interested party in a continuing argument about what should count as valuable.

But Williams's claim has limits, and the Arnoldian programme has resources for partial recovery. First, "culture is ordinary" does not entail that all cultural products are equally good, Williams was an unembarrassed admirer of George Eliot, Hardy and Lawrence, and his career was a sustained argument for taking the working-class novel as seriously as the *grande tradition*. The slogan refuses Arnold's ontology but not Arnold's evaluative practice. Second, the claim leaves the question of how cultural standards are produced and reproduced in a particular community under-theorised, Williams is better at saying that they are made than at saying *how*. Bourdieu would later supply the missing apparatus. Third, the institutional achievement of Arnoldian-Leavisite cultural authority, the BBC, the Penguin Classic, the school English curriculum, remains, and even thinkers fully persuaded by Williams have benefited from it (Williams himself was a Cambridge English don, trained in the Leavis tradition he later attacked).

A further complication: Williams's "ordinary" is not the same as Hall's "popular". Williams locates culture in the lived practice of a community; Hall locates it on a contested terrain between dominant institutions and subordinate groups. Williams's account has a slightly romantic edge, the working-class community is treated as authentic in a way that, on closer inspection, repeats some of Leavis's "organic community" gesture. Hall's account is grittier and more conflictual, and is therefore the harder challenge to Arnold over the long run.

Contemporary instances illustrate the practical force of the Williams move. The Beyoncé question, is she a "high" cultural artist or a "popular" one?, dissolves on Williams's terms: she is both, and the categories were the problem. The Black British literary boom of the 2010s and 2020s, Bernardine Evaristo's Booker, Reni Eddo-Lodge's bestsellers, the Caleb Femi poetry collections, has unfolded as a Williams-shaped event rather than a Leavis-shaped one: it was not the cultivated minority discovering new objects worthy of admission, it was new cultural practitioners reshaping the canon from within. The "culture is ordinary" claim has been institutionally absorbed by exactly the apparatus Arnold and Leavis built, which is the deepest evidence that the slogan has done its work.

I conclude that Williams's claim is highly damaging to the Arnoldian programme, but in a productive way: it has not destroyed the programme so much as forced it to defend itself on different terrain. The contemporary culture-and-civilisation tradition is post-Williams whether it wants to be or not.

### M8. "Why has the mass culture debate so often taken the form of an Americanisation debate?"

I argue that the Americanisation framing is not incidental to the mass culture debate, it is structurally constitutive of it for British and continental European critics, because the United States supplied the most developed historical instance of every feature the critics wanted to attack. The Americanisation debate is therefore best understood as the empirical leg of a theoretical project, rather than as a separate anti-American animus.

Strinati lays out the case in chapter 1. American capitalism by the 1920s had produced the most developed culture industry, Hollywood studios, mass-circulation magazines, network radio, the recorded-music industry, the pulp-fiction houses, and that culture industry was actively exporting its products to Britain and continental Europe. For a Leavisite asking "what does mass culture look like at scale?", the only available historical answer was: it looks American. Arnold had already prefigured the move in *Culture and Anarchy* (1869), "in things of the mind, and in culture and totality, America, instead of surpassing us all, falls short … that chosen home of newspapers and politics", well before the Hollywood studios existed, because America by 1869 already represented mass democracy and a popular press. F. R. Leavis simply updates the diagnosis: "American conditions are the conditions of modern civilization, even if the 'drift' has gone further on the other side of the Atlantic than on this" (cited in Strinati, 1995: 22).

The framing serves three argumentative functions. (1) *Empirical concreteness.* Critics can point to specific Hollywood titles, named magazines, identified bestsellers, and treat them as case material. The Hoggart juke-box-boys passage, "with drape-suits, picture ties, and an American slouch", waggling shoulders "as desperately as Humphrey Bogart, across the tubular chairs" (Hoggart, 1957: 203–204), is unimaginable without the American iconography it inventories. (2) *Theoretical leverage.* Identifying mass culture with America lets the critic associate it with all the things they already dislike, democratic populism, commercial vulgarity, the absence of established cultural authority, the rule of the lowest common denominator. Arnold could not have constructed his "anarchy" without America as the cautionary case study. (3) *National-cultural defence.* Anti-Americanism allowed British critics to mobilise patriotic and conservative sentiment in defence of a project, the preservation of national literary culture, that might otherwise look merely class-defensive. Q. D. Leavis quoting Edmund Gosse (1889) on "the spread of the democratic sentiment" producing "a revolt of the mob against our literary masters" is doing exactly this work: democracy is associated with America, and resistance to democracy is dressed up as resistance to a foreign incursion.

The post-1945 phase intensifies the framing. Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy* (1957) is half-rural-elegy and half-Americanisation-panic, and the two halves are doing the same work: the milk bar and the juke box stand in for everything that has been lost, the working men's club, the chapel, the lending library, the brass band. Orwell's "Decline of the English Murder" essay (1946) makes the equation explicit, Americanised crime fiction has no "depth of feeling" because "Freud and Machiavelli have reached the outer suburbs". The Wenders quotation Strinati cites, "The Americans colonised our subconscious", gives the worry its starkest form.

The critique of the Americanisation framing comes from three directions. *First*, the empirical-historical work of Worpole, White and Hebdige shows that British working-class consumers of American culture were not passive dupes but active appropriators, using Cagney and Bogart films to construct subcultural identities that resisted, rather than reproduced, British class hierarchies. Worpole's oral-history finding that working-class readers preferred American crime fiction because it offered "a realism about city life, an acknowledgement of big business corruption, and an unpatronising portrayal of working class experience and speech" (Worpole, 1983: 35) is devastating to the Hoggart story. *Second*, Hebdige's *Hiding in the Light* (1988) shows that the mid-1960s consumption of "American" iconography was simultaneously a consumption of *continental* iconography (Italian scooters, Gauloises, espresso), the supposed Americanisation was already polyglot at the moment of supposed peak Americanisation. *Third*, Ang's *Watching Dallas* (1989) shows that even the paradigmatic Americanised product (the 1980s prime-time soap) was decoded by European audiences through a publicly available "ideology of mass culture" *and* an "ideology of populism", with substantial use of "ironic distance" to license enjoyment. The Americanisation framing's underlying audience model, passive absorption, is empirically false.

Three contemporary considerations. (a) The current platform giants, Netflix, YouTube, Meta, TikTok (the last Chinese), are mostly American but increasingly globally entangled, and the cultural products that flow through them are not stable national objects. The Korean Wave (BTS, *Squid Game*, *Parasite*) and the Latin American music takeover are conspicuously not American. (b) "Americanisation" as a critical category has lost much of its analytical bite, what would now usefully be analysed is "platformisation" or "algorithmic curation" rather than national origin. (c) The British academic Left has substantially abandoned the Americanisation framing, partly under Hall's influence, partly because the United States has itself become a producer of high-prestige cultural criticism of its own popular culture (the Cornel West / bell hooks / Henry Louis Gates tradition).

I conclude that the Americanisation framing was structurally constitutive of mid-twentieth-century mass culture critique, that it was always partly a displacement of class anxiety, and that its analytical utility has waned with the rise of properly global platform capitalism. The framing is dated. The underlying questions, about cultural homogenisation, audience agency and the political economy of cultural production, remain live.

### M9. "To what extent does the concept of 'ironic distance' enable us to understand the reception of contemporary cultural products?"

I argue that ironic distance is a genuinely useful but limited reception-theoretic concept: it captures something real about how educated middle-class audiences manage their relationship to ostensibly low-status cultural products, but it does only part of the explanatory work that more comprehensive frameworks (Hall, Bourdieu, Ang) provide.

The concept is most precisely developed in Ien Ang's *Watching Dallas* (1985, English edition 1989). Ang interviewed Dutch viewers about their consumption of the American prime-time soap *Dallas* and found three modal positions. (a) Viewers who hated the show articulated their hostility through an "ideology of mass culture", the show was American, vulgar, formulaic, melodramatic, manipulative. (b) Viewers who loved the show without qualification articulated their pleasure through an "ideology of populism", they liked what they liked, taste was personal, intellectuals had no business legislating it. (c) Viewers who loved the show but were embarrassed about loving it deployed *ironic distance*, they enjoyed *Dallas* "as kitsch", "for the costumes", "to laugh at it", "as a guilty pleasure". The ironic-distance position is, for Ang, the most theoretically interesting because it lets viewers reconcile the contradictory ideologies of mass culture and populism, they can simultaneously consume the object and signal that they recognise its status as low-prestige.

The concept transports cleanly to contemporary cases. *Love Island* viewing by middle-class graduate viewers is overwhelmingly conducted through ironic distance, the show is followed seriously enough to track contestants and storylines, but ironised constantly through social-media commentary, podcast recaps and joking group chats. The phenomenon of "hate-watching" prestige-bad television, *Emily in Paris*, *Bridgerton*, the late seasons of *Game of Thrones*, is ironic-distance consumption. The boom in 2010s and 2020s reality TV (*Real Housewives*, *Love is Blind*, *Married at First Sight Australia*) is sustained substantially by ironic-distance consumption among demographics that would not, on a Bourdieu reading, be expected to give the genre any time. Reading celebrity gossip via *Heat* or *Daily Mail* sidebar of shame "for a laugh" is ironic-distance consumption. The contemporary semi-affectionate engagement with internet "brain rot" memes, the Italian-brainrot animal characters, the "skibidi toilet" content, the green-banana monkey on the lecture slide, is conducted almost entirely through ironic distance, with knowing self-mockery about the fact of having engaged at all.

What ironic distance *enables* the reception theorist to see is significant. It refuses the simple binary of approval and disapproval that mass culture theory enforces. It registers the work that cultural consumers do to manage their own social positioning while consuming objects that, taken straight, would compromise that positioning. It accounts for the apparently paradoxical statistic that highly-educated middle-class consumers are not the abstainers from low-status culture that the Leavisite picture predicts, they are often its most attentive and articulate consumers, *under irony*. It explains why "guilty pleasure" has become such a stable category in everyday cultural talk. And it sits well alongside Hall's encoding / decoding apparatus: ironic-distance consumption is a particular form of negotiated reading.

But the concept has three limits. *First*, ironic distance is itself a class marker. Bourdieu would point out that the capacity to consume reality TV "ironically" while signalling that one knows it is bad is a competence that tracks educational and cultural capital. Working-class viewers of *Love Island* are not, in general, performing irony; they are watching it because they like it, in the manner that Ang's "populist" viewers liked *Dallas*. The middle-class deployment of ironic distance is therefore not a neutral reception position but a *distinction strategy* in Bourdieu's sense, a way of consuming the same object as the working-class neighbour while signalling that one's consumption is of a higher type. *Second*, the concept can obscure as much as it reveals. Some "ironic" consumption is actually straightforward enjoyment under a face-saving label. The seriousness with which the audience engages with the object, tracking, recapping, theorising, often outstrips what mere irony would warrant. *Third*, the concept is at risk of explaining everything and therefore nothing. If every consumption of an apparently low-status object can be redescribed as ironic, the category loses analytic purchase.

What ironic distance does *not* enable us to see, and what the broader Hall apparatus does, is the encoding moment. Ang's framework is fundamentally a reception framework, and it leaves the question of why the producer of the cultural object encoded it as they did in second place. Hall's encoding / decoding model is more symmetrical: it analyses what the producer did and what the audience did, and treats the gap between them as the place where meaning gets fought over. The drill / Met Police case is unhelpfully described in terms of ironic distance; it is well described in terms of contested encoding.

A final example. The 2024–25 "demure" trend on TikTok, micro-influencer Jools Lebron's catchphrase "very demure, very mindful" rapidly turning into a globally circulated ironic-distance vocabulary used to mock perceived performances of femininity, is a textbook ironic-distance phenomenon. But it is also an encoding contest (Lebron herself originated the phrase non-ironically, was then ironised by middle-class users, and finally re-claimed it commercially), and it is also a Bourdieu-distinction phenomenon (the irony tracks class and cultural-capital lines). The full description requires all three frameworks. Ironic distance is one tool in the kit, not the kit.

I conclude that ironic distance is a genuinely valuable reception-theoretic concept, particularly for the consumption of high-volume, low-status, formulaic cultural products by educated middle-class audiences. It substantially expands what we can see beyond mass culture theory's passive-audience model. But it is not self-sufficient. The richest analyses of contemporary cultural reception combine Ang's ideologies of mass culture and populism with Hall's polysemy and Bourdieu's distinction, and treat ironic distance as one move in a wider reception game.

### M10. "Assess Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* as a sociology of cultural industries."

I argue that Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* (1932, expanded 1939) is, against its author's intention and its overt polemic, the first developed sociology of cultural industries in the English tradition, and that its empirical apparatus has long outlived the evaluative framework it was harnessed to. The book deserves serious reassessment as a methodological precursor to Bourdieu, the production-of-culture school, and contemporary platform-studies work, even as its Leavisite conclusions are rightly rejected.

The book's structure is itself an argument. Part I surveys the historical reading public from the eighteenth century forward, drawing on publishers' lists, bookseller records, library circulation data, and contemporary commentary. Part II analyses the contemporary reading public in 1930s Britain, using a stratification into "highbrow", "middlebrow" and "lowbrow" readerships served by distinct publishing infrastructures. Part III offers a polemical conclusion, the "armed and conscious minority" passage, that the book is now mostly remembered for. The reading I want to defend is that the polemical conclusion is the weakest part of the book, and that the methodological achievement of Parts I and II is what should now be salvaged.

Three methodological innovations stand out. *First*, Q. D. Leavis takes the *publishing market* as her unit of analysis, not the individual reader or the canonical text. Best-seller lists, circulating-library subscription patterns, the W. H. Smith bookstall infrastructure, the inter-war boom in lending libraries, the rise of the book club, these are the data points the analysis runs on. This is unmistakably a sociology-of-production move, decades before the "production of culture" school formalised it in the 1970s. *Second*, she treats *genre* as a sociological category. Romance, detective, adventure, the "society novel", the religious novel, each is analysed as a distinct commercial formation with its own audience demographics, its own production routines, its own publishing houses, and its own pricing structure. The Penguin paperback revolution that would arrive in 1935 is anticipated in her treatment of the cheap-edition market. *Third*, she takes *audience formation* seriously, the reader is understood as having been *produced* by a particular history of education, leisure provision, advertising and habit, rather than as a fixed psychological constant. The passage on cinema-going, magazine-skimming, jazz-listening leisure habits producing "an inability to be bored and a capacity to concentrate" in reverse anticipates the contemporary attention-economy literature with uncomfortable accuracy.

The empirical findings, taken on their own terms, are largely correct. The reading public *had* stratified into brow-coded tiers in the 1920s and 1930s. Commercial publishing *did* respond to mass-market demand with formulaic genres. The serious novel *was* losing market share. The institutional infrastructure of literary criticism *was* relatively thin. None of these descriptive findings depends on the Leavisite evaluative framework, and the contemporary sociology of cultural industries, Howard Becker, Paul DiMaggio, Pierre Bourdieu, the production-of-culture school, the political economy of communications tradition, the platform studies literature, has substantially vindicated them in their generic form.

The evaluative framework, on the other hand, is where the book fails. Three problems are particularly serious. (a) *The hierarchical assumption.* Q. D. Leavis takes for granted that highbrow fiction is intrinsically more valuable than middlebrow or lowbrow fiction, that her own evaluative competence is reliable, and that the project of policing the boundaries is justified. Bourdieu's *Distinction* destroys this assumption, the hierarchy is a class artefact, not a discovered fact, and the highbrow competence is acquired through the same cultural-capital channels that produced Q. D. Leavis herself. (b) *The passive-audience assumption.* The book treats lowbrow readers as having been "trained" into bad habits by commercial publishing, with very little attention to what those readers themselves think they are doing. The Worpole / White / Hebdige later finding that working-class readers used American crime fiction actively, for purposes the Leavisite framework cannot register, is a direct challenge. (c) *The "organic community" assumption.* The pre-industrial reading public against which the 1932 public is measured is largely a literary construct. Williams's *The Country and the City* (1973) is, among other things, a forty-year-later refutation of this gesture.

What rescues the book is that the empirical apparatus is separable from the polemical apparatus, and is in fact much more durable. Contemporary platform studies work on Wattpad, Kindle Unlimited, the Romance Writers of America industry, the BookTok phenomenon, the Webtoon ecology, the Spotify discovery algorithm, all of this is doing recognisably Q. D. Leavis work in a contemporary register. The unit of analysis is the publishing-and-distribution market. Genre is treated as a sociological category. Audience formation is taken seriously. The political economy of cultural production is foregrounded. The only difference is that the contemporary scholars typically *do not* assume that the highbrow product is intrinsically more valuable than the lowbrow product, and so the polemical conclusion is dropped.

I conclude that *Fiction and the Reading Public* has been mis-shelved. It is normally taught, as the Strinati chapter teaches it, as part of the mass-culture-theory polemic. It is better understood as the founding empirical work of the sociology of cultural industries, accidentally clothed in a Leavisite polemic that its method does not in fact require. The book deserves to be re-read by contemporary platform-studies scholars, who would find more in it than they expect.

### M11. "Is moral panic a useful analytical category, or merely a way of dismissing legitimate cultural concerns?"

I argue that moral panic is a useful analytical category when handled properly, i.e. with attention to the formal features Stanley Cohen specified, and with Hall's analysis of how dominant institutions construct the panic, but that it can degenerate, in casual academic usage, into a way of pre-emptively dismissing concerns that deserve case-by-case empirical assessment. The right answer to the question is "both", and the interesting work is in saying when which.

The concept was developed by Stanley Cohen in *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972), his sociology of the British media's treatment of the Mods and Rockers seaside disturbances of 1964. Cohen identified five formal features of a moral panic: (1) a defined "folk devil", a person or group blamed for a social problem; (2) widely amplified media coverage that constructs the threat in disproportionate terms; (3) "moral entrepreneurs", religious leaders, politicians, police chiefs, columnists, who articulate the concern and demand a response; (4) a public response that exceeds what the actual problem warrants; and (5) a policy or institutional change that outlasts the panic itself. Stuart Hall and colleagues' *Policing the Crisis* (1978), the study of the British "mugging" panic of 1972–73, extended Cohen by showing how a moral panic could serve the political function of legitimising a hardening state response to economic and political crisis. The "folk devil" of the young Black "mugger" was, on Hall's account, doing displaced work for a state under pressure from inflation, industrial unrest and the collapse of the post-war Keynesian consensus.

The category does serious work in the recent British record. The 1976 punk panic, the *Sex Pistols Bulletin* of tabloid front pages on the lecture slide, exhibits all five Cohen features. The mods-and-rockers, video-nasties, ravers (early 1990s), New Age travellers, hoodies (mid-2000s), MMR-vaccine, gang-grooming and TikTok-self-harm panics all map onto the framework. The UK drill panic of 2018–24 is a textbook contemporary instance: a defined folk devil (young Black male drill rappers from named London postcodes), disproportionate media amplification (the *Times*, the *Mail*, the *Express* running drill front pages for years), moral entrepreneurs (the Metropolitan Police, named senior officers, columnists), an excessive response (over 270 takedown requests, Criminal Behaviour Orders restricting specific lyrics), and a lasting institutional change (the platform-policing protocols now in place at YouTube). The PewDiePie controversies of 2017, the *Wall Street Journal*'s coverage of his use of anti-Semitic imagery, the subsequent Disney severance, exhibit a more compressed version of the same template, as do the Andrew Tate de-platforming row of 2022–23 and the various GTA-violence-debates from the late 1990s onward.

The analytic utility of the category is therefore real. It lets the analyst (a) separate the *empirical* question of whether a cultural practice does the harm attributed to it from the *sociological* question of why the discourse about that practice takes the form it does; (b) attend to the *amplification mechanisms*, the press, the police, the politicians, that convert a local incident into a national panic; (c) track the *displaced political work* that the panic performs, in Hall's sense; and (d) historicise the recurrence of the form across generations, which is what the lecture-slide juxtaposition of punk and drill is asking the student to do.

But there are three real dangers in casual academic usage. *First*, "moral panic" can be used as a thought-terminating cliché, anything that looks like a panic gets labelled a panic, and the empirical question of whether the underlying concern is well-founded gets bypassed. Some moral panics turn out to be approximately correct. The video-nasties panic of the early 1980s was largely overblown, but the early-2010s panic about gang-grooming in Rotherham and Rochdale was empirically well-founded, there was a real, large, systemic safeguarding failure, and the early dismissals of the concern as a "moral panic" by some commentators look badly in retrospect. The 2017–22 panic about social-media use and teenage mental health is empirically contested rather than settled, and casually labelling it a moral panic in either direction pre-empts the empirical work. *Second*, "moral panic" can be used as a class weapon, middle-class academics labelling working-class concerns as panics they should grow out of. The category is then doing exactly the Ross-class-anxiety work it was supposed to expose. *Third*, the category presupposes that there is a non-panic baseline of proportionate response, but for many of the cases at issue (drill, TikTok, the Tate phenomenon) the empirical evidence is itself contested and the baseline is not available.

The right discipline is therefore to use moral panic as a *descriptive* and *processual* category rather than as a *verdictive* one. Asking "is this a moral panic?" should mean "do the Cohen features and the Hall displacement-mechanism describe what is happening?", not "is this concern legitimate or illegitimate?". The two questions are separable. A concern can be both legitimate *and* the object of a panic; a concern can be illegitimate *and* not the object of a panic. The Carey-Ross slide quote, "contempt for popular culture frequently masks class anxiety", is best read as a heuristic about the second question, not a settled verdict.

I conclude that moral panic is a useful analytical category, handled carefully, and a degraded thought-terminator handled carelessly. The empirical task, case by case, is harder than the labelling task, and is what the lecture is actually inviting the student to do.

### M12. "'Popular culture is a site of struggle, not an essence' (Hall). Discuss."

I argue that Hall's reformulation is the single most important move in the post-war theorisation of popular culture, and that its truth is most easily demonstrated by working through the historical record the Strinati chapter assembles, every claim of cultural essence has, on closer inspection, turned out to be the precipitate of a struggle whose dynamics the essence-claim was concealing.

Hall develops the claim across "Notes on Deconstructing 'the Popular'" (1981) and the encoding / decoding work of the 1970s. The argument has two negative moves and one positive move. *Negative move 1*: against the Leavisite essence-claim that popular culture is intrinsically degraded mass culture imposed from above. *Negative move 2*: against the romantic essence-claim that popular culture is an authentic expression of "the people" rising from below. *Positive move*: popular culture is the contested terrain on which dominant institutions and subordinate groups continually rework cultural meanings, a "battlefield", in Hall's metaphor, whose lines shift in each generation, and whose dynamics can only be analysed historically.

Three corollaries follow. (a) Texts are *polysemic*: media products can be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways depending on the social position of the decoder. The encoding / decoding model is the operational machinery of the contested-terrain claim. (b) Culture has *relative autonomy*: structural constraints (capital, race, class, gender) shape what is possible without fully determining what happens. (c) Cultural categories are *historically variable*: the boundary between "art" and "popular", between "folk" and "mass", between "high" and "low" is constantly being redrawn, and *that redrawing is itself part of what the struggle is about*.

The historical record vindicates the framework with embarrassing regularity. *Jazz* in the 1920s was condemned by F. R. Leavis and Q. D. Leavis as the emblem of Americanised mass culture, and by Adorno as the apotheosis of standardised culture-industry product. By the 1960s jazz had been re-classified as art, the *Penguin Guide to Jazz* on the shelf next to the *Penguin Guide to Classical Music*. Nothing in the music changed. The boundary moved. *Cinema* was treated by Arnold and the early Leavis as not a serious cultural form. By the 1950s the *Cahiers du Cinéma* critics had constructed the auteur theory, by the 1970s Hitchcock and Hawks had been admitted to the canon, and by the 1990s mainstream Hollywood was being taught in English departments. *Rock 'n' roll* was treated by music critics of the 1950s as mindless pap, Strinati's exact phrase. By the 1990s the early-rock-'n'-roll canon had achieved "classic" status, by 2017 Bob Dylan had won the Nobel Prize for Literature, and the *Rolling Stone* greatest-albums lists had become the operating canon of a global cultural establishment. *Hip hop* was treated through the 1990s as a moral threat. By the 2020s Kendrick Lamar had won the Pulitzer Prize, the Black Panthers half-time show was Beyoncé and Kendrick at the Super Bowl, and hip-hop studies was a standard university subfield. *Comics*, Strinati ignores them, but the trajectory is identical: dismissed as juvenile pulp in the 1950s (the Wertham *Seduction of the Innocent* panic), elevated as "graphic novels" by the 1990s (Spiegelman's *Maus* winning a Pulitzer in 1992), now central to the global film industry through the Marvel cinematic universe and DC.

Every one of these trajectories falsifies the essence-claim, neither "intrinsically degraded" nor "authentic expression of the people" survives contact with the historical record, and vindicates the contested-terrain claim. The category boundary moved because particular institutions, critics, audiences, market pressures and political contexts moved it, and the movement was the struggle.

The contemporary instances on the lecture slide illustrate the same dynamic in real time. *Drill* is currently positioned in the same place jazz occupied in the 1920s, hip-hop in the 1990s and reggae in the 1970s, condemned as a threat by the police, the press and government officials, defended as a vital urban form by its practitioners and supporters, on its way to a reclassification that will look obvious in retrospect. *Beyoncé*, and the lecture's "Beyoncé as high popular culture" example, is mid-reclassification: she is no longer plausibly a pure pop product, the *Renaissance* and *Cowboy Carter* records have been received as major artistic statements, and the academic literature on her work is growing fast. *Reality TV* is at an earlier stage of the same arc, still mostly dismissed, with the first serious academic studies (Skeggs and Wood, 2012) appearing in the 2010s, and a likely reclassification ahead of it. *TikTok creator culture* is at the very beginning of the arc, currently in full moral-panic mode under the "brain rot" banner, but with early signs of academic engagement.

Three theoretical points follow. *First*, the contested-terrain claim is not relativism. Hall is not saying that there is no difference in cultural value between, say, *Hamlet* and an Instagram reel, he is saying that the *machinery* by which that difference is constructed and reproduced is sociological, not metaphysical, and is itself part of what should be analysed. *Second*, the claim is not anti-evaluation. Williams, Hall and Hoggart were all unembarrassed about preferring some cultural products to others; the contested-terrain framework is compatible with sustained critical evaluation, but it requires the evaluator to declare their position rather than pretend to disinterestedness. *Third*, the claim is *empirically falsifiable*: if a cultural category boundary turned out never to have moved, the contested-terrain framework would be in trouble. The historical record provides no such case.

I conclude that Hall's reformulation is essentially correct, has been vindicated by the historical and contemporary record, and remains the most powerful analytical framework available for the sociological study of popular culture. The remaining work is not to re-litigate the essence-claim but to extend the contested-terrain analysis into the platform-era cultural conflicts that are now reshaping the field.

### M13. "What does the recurrence of moral panic across punk, drill, video games and short-form video reveal about the deep structure of the mass culture debate?"

I argue that the recurrence reveals a deep structure with three constant features and one variable. The constants are the demographic of the targeted producer-consumers, the institutional choreography of the panic, and the displaced political work the panic performs. The variable is the specific cultural form. The deep-structural reading reorganises the mass culture debate around its sociological substrate rather than around the procession of cultural objects it appears, on the surface, to be about.

The lecture slide juxtaposes 1976 punk and 2018-onwards drill, and the parallel is forensically exact. *Demographic of targeted producer-consumers*: in both cases, urban working-class young men, predominantly white in 1976 and predominantly Black in 2018, producing a fast, vernacular, locally-rooted, performance-oriented form. *Institutional choreography*: in both cases, the tabloid press generates the initial amplification (the *Sun*, *Daily Mail*, *Mirror*, *Express*); moral entrepreneurs emerge (Mary Whitehouse in 1976, the Metropolitan Police's "Gangs Matrix" team in 2018); the state apparatus follows (BBC airplay bans of "God Save the Queen" in 1977, YouTube takedown requests and Criminal Behaviour Orders against drill artists in 2018–24); and the cultural object is re-classified as a "threat" requiring policy response. *Displaced political work*: in both cases, the panic mobilises around the cultural form anxieties whose underlying object is something else, youth unemployment, post-industrial decline, racial conflict, the failure of urban policy. Hall's *Policing the Crisis* (1978) argument that the early-1970s "mugging" panic displaced anxieties about the collapse of the post-war Keynesian settlement is the canonical statement of this mechanism; the drill panic displaces austerity-era anxieties about youth violence in much the same way.

Extending the analysis to the other two cases on the question. The *video games panic*, running from the mid-1980s through to the present, with named flashpoints around *Mortal Kombat* (1992), Columbine (1999), the Grand Theft Auto series (especially *GTA: San Andreas*, 2004), and Sandy Hook (2012), exhibits the same deep structure with adjusted demographics. *Targeted producer-consumers*: adolescent and young adult males, often working-class, consuming an interactive medium that older critics do not understand. *Institutional choreography*: tabloid amplification, moral entrepreneurs (Senator Joe Lieberman in the US, the BBFC and the *Daily Mail* in the UK), state response (the BBFC's "18" rating regime, the US Senate hearings of 1993). *Displaced political work*: the panic about media violence has consistently displaced the much more empirically pressing question of gun policy, particularly in the US. The 1999 Columbine flashpoint is the cleanest example, *Doom* and *Quake* received vastly more press attention than the firearms used in the attack. *Short-form video / "brain rot"*, the TikTok / YouTube Shorts / Instagram Reels panic of 2020–25, fits the template with the demographic shifted again. *Targeted producer-consumers*: teenagers and young adults of both genders, with girls disproportionately the focus of the consumption-side concerns (eating-disorder content, beauty-filter content, "trad-wife" content) and boys disproportionately the focus of the production-side concerns (the Tate ecosystem, gambling-promotion accounts). *Institutional choreography*: as before, with the addition of the parliamentary-and-regulatory layer (the UK Online Safety Act 2023, EU Digital Services Act, US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory). *Displaced political work*: the platform-attention-economy panic has consistently displaced the underlying political-economic question of how a small number of US-headquartered platforms now structure global cultural circulation, and of what regulatory response that warrants.

The deep-structural reading does three things at once. *First*, it explains the *predictability* of the form: the same demographic gets targeted in each generation because the same demographic occupies the structural position that the panic exists to discipline (young, urban, often racialised working-class culture-producers whose work is consumed by mass audiences). *Second*, it explains the *temporal pattern*: each form gets a moral-panic phase of roughly fifteen to twenty-five years, during which it is constructed as a threat, after which the form is re-classified as cultural heritage and the panic is redirected onto the next form. Punk had its panic phase 1976–82 and is now Heritage; hip-hop had its panic phase 1986–2000 and is now Pulitzer-canonical; drill is currently in panic phase, on track to be heritage by the mid-2030s. *Third*, it explains the *displacement mechanism*: in each case the panic mobilises political energy around a cultural form rather than around the underlying socio-economic conditions, allowing institutional response to feel adequate without addressing the conditions.

The recurrence is therefore evidence for the Carey-Ross-Hall reading rather than for the Arnold-Leavis reading. The mass culture debate is *not* really a debate about cultural quality, if it were, its objects would be the high-prestige cultural products whose mediocrity is well-attested (Hollywood blockbusters, prestige TV, the bestseller list), not the low-prestige working-class forms that consistently attract panic. It is, as Carey says, a debate "haunted by anxieties about democracy itself", about who is allowed to produce and consume culture, on what terms, with what political effects.

Three theoretical refinements close the argument. (a) The deep structure is *sociological*, not psychological: it does not depend on bad faith from any individual moral entrepreneur, and most of them are sincere. The structural position generates the panic without anyone needing to plan it. (b) The deep structure is *historically variable in detail*: the demographic-specifics, the institutional-channels and the political-economic context shift across generations, and the analysis has to be done case-by-case. (c) The deep structure is *not deterministic*: panics can be resisted, redirected and outmanoeuvred, the Sex Pistols' commercial success during the panic, drill artists' sustained engagement with the press through documentary collaboration, Beyoncé's strategic conversion of "popular" into "high", and the contested-terrain reading expects this rather than being surprised by it.

I conclude that the recurrence of moral panic across punk, drill, video games and short-form video reveals that the mass culture debate's surface preoccupation with cultural objects is masking a stable sociological deep structure organised around the discipline of working-class cultural production. The debate's content changes; its function does not.
