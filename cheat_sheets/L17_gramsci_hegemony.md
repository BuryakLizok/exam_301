# L17. Gramsci and the Turn to Hegemony

---

## §1. Lecture Overview & Core Argument

This lecture rebuilds the conceptual armoury of the module by turning to Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937), the Italian Marxist whose *Prison Notebooks* (1929–35) reframed the central problem of how ruling groups maintain power in capitalist societies. Where the Frankfurt School treats culture as a near-totalising "culture industry" of manipulation from above, and where structuralism treats popular culture as an "ideological machine" that dictates thoughts "with the same law-like regularity as, in Saussure's conspectus … the system of *langue* dictated the events of *parole*" (Bennett, 1986: xii), Gramsci opens a third way. Bennett's foundational claim (and the organising claim of this lecture) is that the turn to Gramsci displaces the deadlocked polarity between "structuralism and culturalism" by viewing popular culture "as a force field of relations shaped, precisely, by these contradictory pressures and tendencies" (Bennett, 1986: xiii).

The lecture's core argument: power in advanced capitalist societies is maintained less through coercion than through hegemony, the cultural and moral leadership exercised over civil society. As Gramsci puts it, "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'" (Gramsci, 1971: 57–8). The slides distil this with characteristic clarity: "Power = coercion + hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides). Hegemony is therefore not a synonym for dominant ideology. The dominant ideology thesis is "one of exclusion", imposing a monolithic ruling worldview "as an alien force, on the subordinate classes" (Bennett, 1986: xiii). Hegemony, by contrast, "involves processes of inclusion": it is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides), generated as much from below as from above.

Five conceptual moves frame the lecture. (1) The shift from coercion to consent: "the power of the ruling groups and the reproduction of the status quo are maintained less through the State's direct exercise of force than through the management of consent and through the exercise of moral and intellectual authority" (Gramsci, 1971, slide quote). (2) The redefinition of the state into political society (coercion: law, police, courts, military) plus civil society (consent: media, education, religion, family, culture), Gramsci's "integral state." (3) The reconceptualisation of culture: media, schools, churches and families are not afterthoughts to economic struggle; they are the very terrain on which struggle is fought. As Bennett insists, hegemony enables analysis of popular culture "without adopting a position that is either opposed to it or uncritically for it" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). (4) The shift in revolutionary strategy from the war of manoeuvre ("direct revolutionary confrontation", Lecture 17 slides, suited to societies where civil society is thin and the state is the whole field) to the war of position, the long, sustained ideological struggle within civil society that advanced capitalist societies require. (5) The reframing of intellectuals: every social group produces its own organic intellectuals, while traditional intellectuals merely *appear* neutral.

This Gramscian turn is the hinge between the modernist canon (Marx, Frankfurt School, structuralism) and the cultural-studies tradition (Hall, Hebdige, the CCCS). As Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now* demonstrates, it remains the indispensable framework for analysing twenty-first-century power, from the techno-financial bloc that "got the world that they wanted" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3) to the "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), platform capitalism, and the counter-hegemonic possibilities of contemporary movements. My argument throughout these essays will be that Gramsci's framework remains uniquely able to register both the stability and the fragility of capitalist power because it treats common sense as a contradictory, contested terrain, never a finished achievement and never irredeemable.

## §2. Key Concepts (Named, Defined, Located)

- **Hegemony**: not dominant ideology, but the process by which a leading social group secures cultural and moral leadership and the active consent of subaltern groups. "Hegemony involves the more or less active consent of large sections of the population … a process of management and administration" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **The integral state**: state = political society + civil society. Political society (coercion: law, police, courts, military). Civil society (consent: media, education, religion, family, culture). "Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Civil society (Gramsci sense)**: the "social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State" (Lecture 17 slides), where "ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together."

- **Common sense**: the everyday, taken-for-granted, often contradictory beliefs through which people understand the world; "historically produced … and shaped by dominant groups" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Good sense**: the critical, reflective kernels embedded *within* common sense that can be mobilised against it. "Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Organic intellectuals**: emerge from and articulate the interests of particular social groups (journalists, editors, cultural producers, podcasters, comic-book writers). Counter-hegemonic example: Alan Moore, Garth Ennis as working-class organic intellectuals re-writing the superhero genre.

- **Traditional intellectuals**: present themselves as neutral, independent, free-floating (priests, professors, judges) but in fact serve historically constituted blocs.

- **War of manoeuvre**: "direct revolutionary confrontation" (Lecture 17 slides), viable where civil society is thin and the state is the whole field.

- **War of position**: long-term struggle within civil society to reshape "culture, values, and common sense" (Lecture 17 slides), "particularly through media, education, and popular culture."

- **The historical bloc**: alliance of class fractions, ideological positions and institutional forces that sustain a given hegemonic project.

- **Passive revolution**: change from above that absorbs and neutralises pressure from below; a hegemonic move that pre-empts and incorporates challenges rather than confronting them.

- **Popular-democratic struggle**: struggles articulated around "the people" vs "the power bloc" rather than narrowly class-vs-class.

- **Articulation**: cultural elements have no fixed class-belongingness; they are "movable": "a practice which is articulated to bourgeois values today may be disconnected from those values and connected to socialist ones tomorrow" (Bennett, 1986: xvi).

- **Counter-hegemony**: organised cultural, ideological and political resistance that contests dominant common sense, visible in subcultures (Hebdige), fandoms (Jenkins's "textual poachers"), oppositional readings (Hall) and re-articulations of popular forms.

- **Grammar of race (Hall)**: in 'The Whites of Their Eyes' (1981) Hall shows how "racial (white / colonial) hegemony is encoded in media representations" and "can be decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides), through a deeper syntactic structure of representations, framings and narrative roles rather than explicit slurs.

- **Mugging panic (Hall et al., *Policing the Crisis*, 1978)**: Hall and CCCS colleagues show how "capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging') but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides). Built around a 1972 Birmingham street robbery and the racialised media-police-court ecology that followed.

- **Encoding/decoding (Hall, 1973)**: media texts are encoded with hegemonic preferred meanings but can be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways, the moment counter-hegemony enters reception.

- **Structure of feeling** (Williams, via *Hegemony Now*): the affective texture through which "passive consent" is secured, a "cynical resignation … with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5).

## §3. Key Notes from the Presentation (Slide by Slide)

- **Slide 1. Title.** SC301, Lecture 17: The turn to Gramsci and Hegemony. Frames the lecture's pivot from earlier ideology-as-imposition models to hegemony as negotiated leadership.

- **Slide 2. Gramsci's life.** Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937), Italian Marxist writing in early 20th-century Europe, marked by mass politics, failed socialist revolutions in Western Europe, and the rise of fascism. His central problem: why capitalist societies secured popular stability despite deep inequality. He wrote most of his theory in prison under Mussolini, producing the *Prison Notebooks*, which focus on culture, ideology, and power rather than economic determinism alone.

- **Slide 3. Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937) / *Prison Notebooks* (1929–35).** Pull-quote: "The power of the ruling groups and the reproduction of the status quo are maintained less through the State's direct exercise of force than through the management of consent and through the exercise of moral and intellectual authority."

- **Slide 4. Hegemony and civil society / Hegemony.** Hegemony refers to the way a dominant social group maintains power primarily through consent rather than coercion. For Gramsci, ruling groups succeed when their values, beliefs, and interests are accepted as common sense by society as a whole. Hegemony is cultural and moral leadership, not just political control, and it must be continuously produced and renewed.

- **Slide 5. General Idea of Hegemony.** Long Gramsci quotation: "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'. A social group dominates antagonistic groups … perhaps even by armed force, it leads kindred and allied groups. A social group can, and indeed must, exercise 'leadership' before winning government power … It subsequently becomes dominant when it exercises power, but even if it holds it firmly in its grasp, it must continue to 'lead' as well" (Gramsci, 1971: 57–8).

- **Slide 6. Hegemony and Consent.** Bullet checklist: hegemony involves "more or less active consent" of large sections of the population; it is a process of management and administration, not an ideology; it is "not something imposed on society from above so much as generated from below"; the dominant ideology thesis is one of exclusion, hegemony involves processes of inclusion; "Hegemony = a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites"; "Power = coercion + hegemony".

- **Slide 7. Hegemony and the Media / Are the Media the Principal Instruments of Hegemony?** Marx quote on the class that owns material production controlling mental production. "The media mobilise and apply frameworks of thought, ideologies if you like, which are largely generated and maintained outside them." The "means of mental production" are far wider than the national media. "The nuts and bolts of social knowledge are put together and adjusted by people in ordinary practical activities, or what Gramsci called 'civil society'."

- **Slide 8. Political society and civil society.** Gramsci distinguishes political society (state, law, police, courts, military, operating mainly through coercion) from civil society (media, education, religion, family, culture, operating through persuasion and consent). Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony.

- **Slide 9. Hegemony and Civil Society.** "Civil society is where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests." "Civil society is the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together." "Civil society is the social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State."

- **Slide 10. Hegemony as a Political Process of Social Management and Administration.** "All the public institutions of civil society are engaged in developing and managing the part of world with which they're concerned." The social group who carry out these activities are organic intellectuals. Unlike the dominant ideology thesis, hegemony describes a complex process of codification and management, not a system of ideas. Hegemony "treats ideas as the codification of practical experience."

- **Slide 11. Counter-hegemony and organic intellectuals.** Section title slide introducing the second half of the lecture.

- **Slide 12. Common Sense and Good Sense.** Common sense is the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world: historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups. Good sense refers to the critical and reflective elements within common sense that can challenge dominant ideas. Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense.

- **Slide 13. Intellectuals and Cultural Power.** Gramsci defines intellectuals broadly as those who organise ideas and meaning in society. Traditional intellectuals see themselves as neutral or independent. Organic intellectuals emerge from specific social groups and articulate their interests. Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms.

- **Slide 14. Media as a Site of Hegemonic Struggle.** From a Gramscian perspective, media does not simply transmit information; it constructs reality through framing, representation, and repetition. Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits.

- **Slide 15. War of Position.** A war of position refers to a long-term struggle within civil society to reshape culture, values, and common sense. Unlike a war of manoeuvre (direct revolutionary confrontation), Gramsci argues that social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture.

- **Slide 16. Hall, 'Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse' (1973): hegemony and counter-hegemony.** Diagrammatic slide: production encodes preferred hegemonic meanings; visual/aural form mediates; decoding can be dominant, negotiated or oppositional, leaving possibility of counter-hegemonic representations and counter-hegemonic readings.

- **Slide 17. Hall and Gramsci.** Hall extends Gramsci's concept of hegemony to race and other categorisations. In 'The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media' (1981) he employs the term 'grammar of race' to show how racial (white / colonial) hegemony is encoded in media representations, and can be decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways. In *Policing the Crisis* he shows how capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging'), but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate.

- **Slide 18. Examples of counter-hegemony / Media, hegemony and counter-hegemony.** Stuart Hall incorporated Gramscian ideas to show how hegemony was encoded in media-messages, but also how counter-hegemony could inform oppositional readings in decoding, and how sub-cultures could act counter-hegemonically. "The study of media in order to assess the impact of ideology was largely abandoned in the 1980s. This was a legacy of the Frankfurt School but also post-modernism." Scholars such as John Fiske argued that audiences were so empowered that ideology and effect had become irrelevant. For Fiske, the role of media consumption in the production of personal and group identities was most important. Other scholars have disregarded the encoding component of Hall's model, focusing only on the reception of media content.

- **Slide 19. Subcultures as counter-hegemonic.** Subculture is a minority culture within a primary culture that goes against the accepted norms and habits of the majority society. Often youth subcultures are closely associated with certain media. According to Hebdige, subculture is "consumers acting against the expectation of passivity." They resist cooption into society through ritual and style. Subcultures can be counter-hegemonic. Subcultures use media objects as part of this construction. However, this may result in the generation of new commodities and industries, eventually coopting the subculture into the majority capitalist culture. Market research firms engage in trend spotting, selling their insights to the market and accelerating this process.

- **Slide 20. Fandom as counter-hegemonic.** Fandom is similar to subculture, though much more focused around one particular media. Members of fandoms set themselves apart from "casual" consumers through their devotion and knowledge. Tulloch describes them as a "powerless elite": distinguished by knowledge of and engagement with the media they consume, but often powerless to influence its content. Jenkins describes fandoms as "textual poachers": they claim back power by appropriating the characters and worlds of media and creating their own interpretations, storylines and artefacts. This can act to resist hegemonic interpretations. They trespass on the grounds of the powerful content producers. Fandoms are indicative of many changes in cultural consumption that have come hand in hand with New Media.

- **Slide 21. Contemporary Relevance and Critique.** Gramsci remains relevant for analysing digital media, platforms, and culture wars, where power operates through visibility, algorithms, and moral framing rather than force. Critiques argue that Gramscian analysis can overemphasise culture at the expense of material structures or treat hegemony as too totalising. Nonetheless, his framework remains central for understanding media, consent, and cultural power.

- **Slide 22. SC301 / Class 16: The turn to Gramsci and Hegemony.** Second-half title slide flagging the superhero case study.

- **Slide 23. Superheroes: hegemony (see Engstrom and Beliveau: *Gramsci and Media Literacy*).** Superheroes, from Superman onwards, have been used to portray ideas about freedom, race, and masculinity. More recently there have been attempts to portray superheroes as embodying more liberal / new masculine values (in the MCU, for example Captain America runs a support group). "Hegemony in practice…"

- **Slide 24. Superheroes: counter-hegemony (see Engstrom and Beliveau: *Gramsci and Media Literacy*).** "'Organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics such as Alan Moore (*Watchmen*) and Garth Ennis (*The Boys*) have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism."

- **Slide 25. Film / television adaptations.** Film and television adaptations have introduced audiences to these counter-hegemonic representations and even subverted the original comics to allow for counter-hegemony. For example, in *The Boys* (TV) subverting the myth of heroism, or in *Watchmen* (TV) whose main character is a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists.

- **Slide 26. Essential reading.** Closing reading-list slide pointing toward Bennett's 'Popular culture and the turn to Gramsci' and adjacent texts.

## §4. Worked Examples (Concrete, Datable)

- ***Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978)**: a 1972 Birmingham street robbery becomes the seed of a national moral panic about "mugging" that, through the joint work of police press briefings, court reporting and tabloid framing, displaces the structural crisis of 1970s British capitalism onto a racialised criminal figure. The lecture's paradigm of "capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging'), but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Hall, 'The Whites of Their Eyes' (1981)**: the "grammar of race" encoded in media representations, working through framing and narrative role rather than explicit slur. Hall reads *Love Thy Neighbour*, a 1970s ITV sitcom built around a white working-class couple (Eddie and Joan Booth) in Twickenham and their Black neighbours (Bill and Barbie Reynolds), as a text in which racial common sense is reproduced and held up for ambivalent decoding at once.

- **Encoding/decoding (Hall, 1973)**: the diagrammatic slide in the lecture maps the trajectory from production-encoding of preferred hegemonic meanings, through visual/aural form, to reception in dominant, negotiated or oppositional decodings, leaving the door open to "counter-hegemonic representations" and "counter-hegemonic readings" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **The Wall Street-Silicon Valley historical bloc (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "the people who got the world that they wanted were the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and those sections of finance capital closely aligned with them" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). The combination of "social liberalisation, anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets and digitisation of media and information" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3) reads as the platform of this bloc.

- **The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: the neoliberal hegemony "fully established itself during the administrations of Bill Clinton and Tony Blair in the 1990s" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4) and held until 2016, a paradigm of how a hegemonic settlement secures cultural stasis even amid intensive technological innovation.

- **2016 as conjunctural opening (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "From the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, to the UK's vote to leave the European Union" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1), running into COVID-19 and accelerating climate collapse, marks "a moment of grand realignment … a rare instant where more or less anything could be possible" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). The contemporary "interregnum" of the Gramscian epigraph.

- **Apple, Facebook, Google as platform monopolies (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "the methods that digital technology corporations such as Apple, Facebook and Google used to establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). "Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5).

- **The "structure of feeling" of late neoliberalism (Williams & Gilbert via R. Williams)**: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The textbook of passive consent.

- **Reality television and internet influencer culture (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Entrepreneurial common sense produced through everyday entertainment.

- **MCU's Captain America support group**: "More recently there have been attempts to portray superheroes as embodying more liberal / new masculine values (in the MCU, for example Captain America runs a support group). Hegemony in practice…" (Lecture 17 slides). The hegemonic bloc absorbing partial recognition of progressive masculine vulnerability.

- **Alan Moore's *Watchmen*, Garth Ennis's *The Boys***: "'Organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics … have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **TV adaptations as counter-hegemonic re-encoding**: *The Boys* (TV) "subverting the myth of heroism"; *Watchmen* (TV) whose "main character is a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Civil-society everyday life**: "Civil society is where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests" (Lecture 17 slides). The Millennium street party is the slide's chosen image of the ordinary texture in which hegemony is reproduced and contested.

## §5. Critiques & Counter-Arguments

**Class-essentialism (residual)**: Critics argue Gramsci, despite his sophistication, still tethers cultural analysis to "the antagonistic relations between the bourgeoisie and the working class as the two fundamental classes of capitalist society" (Bennett, 1986: xiv). Bennett notes that the Gramscian response, articulation theory, "enables due account to be taken of the relative separation of different regions of cultural struggle (class, race, gender) as well as of the complex and changing ways in which these may be overlapped on to one another" (Bennett, 1986: xvi), but the post-Marxist drift toward identity-as-primary-political-object is itself critiqued by Williams and Gilbert as having been "analytically and strategically disastrous" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6).

**Over-totalisation**: the lecture itself notes "Gramscian analysis can overemphasise culture at the expense of material structures or treat hegemony as too totalising" (Lecture 17 slides). Bennett concedes the framework "is liable to the criticism that it is too accommodating and expansive a framework, over-totalising in its analytical claims and ambit" (Bennett, 1986: xvii).

**Fiske and the populist drift**: by the 1980s "scholars such as John Fiske argued that audiences were so empowered that ideology and effect had become irrelevant" (Lecture 17 slides); celebrating resistance everywhere risks dissolving the very concept of hegemony.

**The Foucauldian alternative**: Foucault disperses power across capillaries; Gramsci's framework, by contrast, "served as an important check on the Foucauldian tendency to view power and the struggle against it as equally diffuse and unrelated" (Bennett, 1986: xvi).

**Abandonment in the 1980s–90s**: "The study of media in order to assess the impact of ideology was largely abandoned in the 1980s. This was a legacy of the Frankfurt School but also post-modernism" (Lecture 17 slides). *Hegemony Now* is partly a recovery operation.

**Subculture co-optation**: Hebdige already anticipated that subcultural resistance "may result in the generation of new commodities and industries, eventually coopting the subculture into the majority capitalist culture" (Lecture 17 slides). "Market research firms engage in trend spotting, selling their insights to the market and accelerating this process" (Lecture 17 slides).

**Williams & Gilbert's update**: traditional Gramscian theory assumes "majority active consent." They argue most actual consent is passive, "cynical resignation … combined with … ineffectual critique" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Hegemony now operates through platforms, infrastructures and structures of feeling, not just through active ideological assent.

**Identity vs interests**: *Hegemony Now* warns that the post-Marxist celebration of identity "always risks complicity with a liberal and idealist conception of politics" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Hegemony must be re-grounded in material interests.

### Essay M1. "A social group can, and indeed must, exercise 'leadership' before winning government power … even if it holds it firmly in its grasp, it must continue to 'lead' as well" (Gramsci, *Prison Notebooks*: 57–8). Discuss Gramsci's distinction between domination and intellectual and moral leadership, and explain why a ruling group must lead before, during and after taking power.

Gramsci's claim in the General Idea of Hegemony section of the *Prison Notebooks* compresses the conceptual revolution that defines L17. Power, in advanced capitalist societies, is composed of two interlocking modalities: domination and intellectual and moral leadership. The slide quotation makes the temporal sequence explicit. Leadership is required before government power can be won, leadership is sustained while power is held, and the failure of leadership precipitates the unravelling of domination itself. The argument transforms classical Marxism by inserting cultural and ethical work into the very definition of class rule.

The opening move is the redefinition of supremacy. Gramsci writes that "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'" (Gramsci, 1971: 57). Domination is exercised over antagonistic groups, sometimes by armed force, while leadership is exercised over kindred and allied groups. These are not alternatives but two faces of one apparatus. The slide on Hegemony and Consent crystallises this as the equation "Power = coercion + hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides). Coercion remains real: police, courts and the military operate. But under settled conditions they recede into the background, activated only when leadership falters.

The temporal claim is the most striking part. A group "can, and indeed must, exercise 'leadership' before winning government power" (Gramsci, 1971: 57). Hegemony is therefore a condition for state power, not merely a consequence of it. The bourgeoisie did not seize the modern state by frontal assault on a feudal apparatus; it first organised cultural, moral and intellectual leadership across the institutions of civil society, in literary academies, newspapers, professional associations, religious reform, and only then assumed political command. The teacher's summary captures this when it notes that hegemony "must be continuously produced and renewed" (GramsciandHegemony teacher summary). Renewal is not optional maintenance; it is the work that keeps the bloc intact.

The second temporal move is just as important. Once a group "becomes dominant", Gramsci insists, "it must continue to 'lead' as well" (Gramsci, 1971: 58). The ruling class does not get to stop leading once it has won. This is why the lecturer pairs the slide quotation with the equation "Power = coercion + hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides) rather than "Power = coercion or hegemony". The two terms operate simultaneously and in proportion to the conjuncture. Where leadership is strong, coercion is muted. Where leadership weakens, coercion comes forward, and visible coercion is itself a sign of hegemonic crisis.

Bennett's reading sharpens the analytical payoff. For Gramsci, the cultural and ideological relations between ruling and subordinate classes "consist less in the *domination* of the latter by the former than in the struggle for *hegemony*, that is, for moral, cultural, intellectual and, thereby, political leadership over the whole of society" (Bennett, 1986: xiv). The thereby is the key word. Political leadership *follows* moral, cultural and intellectual leadership. The terrain on which politics is decided is the terrain of common sense.

What kinds of practices count as leadership? The lecture lists schools, churches, families, newspapers, advertising and broadcasting. The teacher's summary adds that "newspapers, films, television, education, and everyday language all play a role in shaping how people understand the world" (GramsciandHegemony teacher summary). These are not afterthoughts to economic struggle but the texture in which class projects either secure consent or lose it. Reality television's celebration of entrepreneurial selfhood, the cultural prestige of business-influencer formats and the everyday idiom of self-help and aspiration are the daily reproduction of a leadership claim by the dominant bloc.

The model has cross-lecture links. Where the Frankfurt School (L12) presents the culture industry as imposing dominant ideology from above, Gramsci foregrounds active consent generated from below. Where Habermas (L13) idealises the bourgeois public sphere as a domain of rational-critical debate, Gramsci sees the same institutional terrain as already saturated with leadership work. Where Williams (L14) describes culture as ordinary, Gramsci shows why that ordinariness is politically loaded: it is the medium of hegemonic articulation.

The strategic implication is significant. Counter-hegemonic projects cannot simply seek state power; they must first do the long work of moral, cultural and intellectual leadership in civil society. Hall's later reading of the political right's hegemonic success in the 1980s confirms the inverse case: a project that does this work patiently wins durably (Hall, 1988: 6). Leadership is the longest game, but the only game that secures durable transformation.

### Essay M2. "Hegemony involves the more or less active consent of large sections of the population" and is "not something imposed on society from above so much as generated from below" (Lecture 17 slides). To what extent does this Gramscian account of consent depart from the dominant ideology thesis, and what does it mean to say that hegemony "must be continuously produced and renewed"?

The Hegemony and Consent slide compresses two claims that, taken together, mark the most decisive break in twentieth-century Marxist theory of culture. First, hegemony is not the imposition of a coherent ideology on a passive population but a process of negotiation that requires the active consent of large social blocs. Second, this consent is generated from below as much as from above, drawn from materials already circulating in everyday life. The slide's bullet that hegemony "must be continuously produced and renewed" (Lecture 17 slides; teacher summary) makes the point dynamic: hegemony is always a project in motion, never a finished settlement.

The departure from the dominant ideology thesis is sharp. As Bennett summarises the orthodoxy Gramsci breaks with, "the sphere of cultural and ideological practices [is] governed by a dominant ideology, essentially and monolithically bourgeois in its characteristics, which, albeit with varying degrees of success, is imposed from without, as an alien force, on the subordinate classes" (Bennett, 1986: xiii). On that older model, ruling ideas descend from a unified ruling class onto a recipient population. The role of cultural analysis is to expose the obfuscation. The political subject of the model is the analyst.

Gramsci dismantles this picture in three steps. First, hegemony is not an ideology. The slide is precise: hegemony "does not designate a particular ideology or even the ruling ideas in society but rather a process of management and administration" (Lecture 17 slides). The shift from system of ideas to process of administration is critical. Hegemonic work happens in routines, institutional practices, classroom drills, news bulletins, family dinners and now in algorithmic feeds. It is not a doctrine; it is a daily craft.

Second, hegemony works through inclusion. The slide contrasts the two logics: "The idea of the dominant ideology is one of exclusion (it excludes and suppresses all opposition). Hegemony, on the other hand, involves processes of inclusion" (Lecture 17 slides). Hegemonic projects must offer something. Bennett puts the implication directly: bourgeois hegemony "is secured not via the obliteration of working class culture, but via its *articulation to* bourgeois culture and ideology so that, in being associated with and expressed in the forms of the latter, its political affiliations are altered in the process" (Bennett, 1986: xiv–xv). Welfare provisions, anti-discrimination law, partial cultural recognition: these are hegemonic instruments, not capitulations.

Third, hegemony is generated from below as much as from above. The slide formulates this as a polemic against top-down imposition: "Hegemony is not something imposed on society from above so much as generated from below" (Lecture 17 slides). This must be read carefully. It does not mean hegemonic projects are spontaneous expressions of popular will. It means they are *assembled* out of materials already circulating in popular common sense. Williams and Gilbert give the contemporary specification: a hegemonic bloc forms when shared material interests are organised "across different sectors of society", drawing on dispositions encouraged across schools, reality television and influencer cultures (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These are good-sense kernels recoded for political ends.

What does it mean to say hegemony "must be continuously produced and renewed" (Lecture 17 slides)? Three things. First, that hegemonic settlements are temporally vulnerable. The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair held together because the everyday cultural work of consolidation went on continuously; the post-2016 conjuncture, what Gramsci would call an interregnum (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1), is the moment when renewal falters. Second, that hegemony is performative. The bloc must keep performing leadership through media, education and culture; the moment performance stops, consent erodes. The slide's bullet that hegemony is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides) makes the performance two-sided. Third, that opposition is permanent. Active consent is "more or less active" (Lecture 17 slides), which is to say it always shades into half-consent, withdrawal and oppositional articulation. Williams and Gilbert's account of "passive consent" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5) registers the lower end of this spectrum, where consent becomes cynical resignation.

Cross-links. The continuous-renewal claim contrasts with Adorno's culture-industry thesis (L12), which presents consent as already accomplished by the industrial form. It pairs with Hall's encoding/decoding (L15), where the preferred reading must be continuously encoded across texts. The dominant ideology thesis treated culture as a finished product. Gramsci treats it as a daily practice.

### Essay M3. "Are the Media the Principal Instruments of Hegemony?" (Lecture 17 slides). Discuss with reference to Gramsci's account of the means of mental production, the role of media in civil society, and the claim that "the nuts and bolts of social knowledge are put together and adjusted by people in ordinary practical activities".

The slide question, "Are the Media the Principal Instruments of Hegemony?" (Lecture 17 slides), is deliberately framed as a question rather than a thesis. The lecturer's answer, structured across the same slide, is a qualified yes that immediately widens the field. Media matter, but they are nodes within a far wider ecology of civil-society institutions and ordinary practical activities, and their work is precisely to mobilise frameworks of thought that are largely generated elsewhere. The Gramscian payoff is that media analysis must be embedded in an analysis of civil society as a whole.

The slide marshals three propositions in sequence. The first is the Marxist starting point: "the class which has the means of material production at its disposal, has control at the same time over the means of mental production" (Lecture 17 slides). Ownership of the means of mental production (publishing houses, broadcasters, platforms) gives the dominant bloc structural advantage in shaping what circulates as common sense. The second is a corrective: "The media mobilise and apply frameworks of thought, ideologies if you like, which are largely generated and maintained outside them" (Lecture 17 slides). Newsrooms, editors and producers do not invent the frameworks they apply. They draw on a wider ideological field formed in education, family life, popular memory and political discourse. The third widens the conceptual field: "What Marx calls the 'means of mental production' are a far wider and more varied set of institutions and activities than those of the national media" (Lecture 17 slides). The slide closes by relocating the central work in everyday life: "The nuts and bolts of social knowledge are put together and adjusted by people in ordinary practical activities, or what Gramsci called 'civil society'" (Lecture 17 slides).

Several conclusions follow. First, the answer to the slide's question is no when read narrowly. Media are not the principal instruments of hegemony if "principal" means sole or even predominant. The teacher's summary makes the point in slightly different language: "Newspapers, films, television, education, and everyday language all play a role in shaping how people understand the world" (GramsciandHegemony teacher summary). Education and everyday language are co-equal sites with media. The dinner table, the playground and the workplace canteen are sites of hegemonic work. The schoolroom is the textbook example.

Second, the answer is a qualified yes in a more important sense. Media institutions are the central *mobilisers* and *applicators* of frameworks generated across civil society. They aggregate and circulate hegemonic common sense. The lecturer's slide on Media as a Site of Hegemonic Struggle makes the mechanism clear: "media does not simply transmit information; it constructs reality through framing, representation, and repetition" (Lecture 17 slides). Framing selects which structural causes are admitted, representation determines who appears as agent or problem, and repetition embeds the framing as second nature. The slide gives a paradigm example: "Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides).

Third, the formulation respects civil-society plurality. The slide on Hegemony as a Political Process of Social Management adds the institutional point: "All the public institutions of civil society are engaged in developing and managing the part of world with which they're concerned" (Lecture 17 slides). Schools manage curricular common sense, religious institutions manage moral common sense, professional bodies manage occupational common sense, and broadcasters manage news common sense. The job of hegemonic analysis is to map the joints between these.

The Bennett reading adds a methodological caution. The Gramscian framework is at its best when it shows that "cultural elements have no fixed class-belongingness" and that articulations are "movable" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). A media artefact is not hegemonic in itself; it becomes hegemonic when articulated to a particular bloc's interests, and can be re-articulated by counter-hegemonic projects. Hall's encoding/decoding model (L15) supplies the operational diagram: producers encode preferred meanings, but reception admits "negotiated" and "oppositional" decodings (Hall, 1980: 134), and the diagram in the L17 slides explicitly flags both "counter-hegemonic representations" and "counter-hegemonic readings" (Lecture 17 slides).

The cross-lecture register matters. Where Adorno and Horkheimer (L12) treat the culture industry as the principal instrument of consent, Gramsci redistributes that work across civil society. The L14 reading of Williams's "culture is ordinary" (Williams, 1958: 4) maps onto Gramsci's claim about ordinary practical activities directly. Media matter, but Gramsci insists on a wider angle.

### Essay M4. "Civil society is the social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State … the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together" (Lecture 17 slides). Using Gramsci's distinction between political society and civil society, discuss why he treats civil society as the principal terrain of hegemonic struggle in advanced capitalist societies.

The Hegemony and Civil Society slide gives a definition that is at once topographical and operational. Civil society is a space (it has a location) and a process (it is where ideas and practical behaviours are knitted together). The double character is the reason Gramsci's distinction between political society and civil society reorganises Marxist theory of the state, and it is also the reason advanced capitalist societies cannot be transformed by frontal assault on state power alone. The seat of hegemony is in the texture of everyday life.

The slide on Political society and civil society sets the structural distinction. "Political society includes the state, law, police, courts, and military, and operates mainly through coercion. Civil society includes media, education, religion, family, and culture, and operates through persuasion and consent" (Lecture 17 slides). Together they form what Gramsci calls the integral state. Crucially the slide adds: "Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides). Civil society is therefore not the apolitical opposite of the state, as in liberal theory, but the medium through which the state, broadly understood, lives.

What kind of space is civil society? The lecturer's slide is unusually evocative: "Civil society is where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests" (Lecture 17 slides). The texture is everyday. The Millennium street party is no accident: it stands for the ordinary social rituals through which hegemony is reproduced and, occasionally, contested. The slide goes further: civil society is "the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the operational definition. Ideas are not pure mental contents; they are embedded in routines, in greetings, in work practices, in queues, in commutes. Hegemony lives in these knots.

The third bullet on the slide is the structural one: civil society is "the social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State" (Lecture 17 slides). The double function matters. Civil society creates an apparent autonomy from the state (we are not at the office of government when we watch television) and at the same time integrates us into its broader regime of legitimation (the broadcaster's framings carry the state's project into the living room). This is precisely why frontal assault on state power leaves hegemony intact. Tear down a government and the broadcasters, schools, churches and family idioms continue to reproduce the bloc's leadership.

Why is civil society the principal terrain of hegemonic struggle in advanced capitalist societies? Gramsci's historical argument is comparative. In societies where civil society is thin, the state is everything; the war of manoeuvre, the frontal assault, can decide things. In advanced capitalist societies civil society is dense: schools, professional bodies, churches, broadcasters, publishing houses, sporting institutions, charitable foundations, platform companies. This density absorbs shocks. Hegemonic settlements survive electoral defeats because the institutional ecology continues to ventriloquise the bloc's common sense. The lecturer's slide on the War of Position draws the strategic conclusion explicitly: "social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides).

Three implications follow. First, civil society is administrative. "All the public institutions of civil society are engaged in developing and managing the part of world with which they're concerned" (Lecture 17 slides). Hegemony is the sum of these managements. Second, civil society is the home of organic intellectuals who codify practical experience. The slide notes that "the social group who carry out these types of activities are organic intellectuals" (Lecture 17 slides). Civil society's leadership is performed by teachers, journalists, podcasters, religious figures and now influencers. Third, civil society is the privileged site of counter-hegemony. Subcultures, fandoms and oppositional movements all operate from within civil society, not against it from outside. The slide on Subcultures notes that they "resist cooption into society through ritual and style" (Lecture 17 slides), an internal opposition.

Cross-links. The framing differs from Habermas's idealised public sphere (L13), which treats civil society as a rational-critical space external to power; Gramsci sees it as already saturated with hegemonic struggle. It pairs with Williams's structures of feeling (L14): the affective texture of civil society is part of how hegemony is felt. And it sets up the *Hegemony Now* claim that platforms have become a new civil-society layer (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5).

### Essay M5. "Common sense is the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world … historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups" (Lecture 17 slides; teacher summary). Discuss the conceptual pair of common sense and good sense, and explain how it helps a Gramscian analyst read contemporary popular culture.

The Common Sense and Good Sense slide presents a conceptual pair that does more analytical work than almost any other in Gramsci's repertoire. Its power lies in refusing both elite condescension and populist celebration. People are neither dupes nor sovereign critics. They carry sedimented, contradictory common sense within which good-sense kernels live as latent resources, awaiting articulation. For a Gramscian reading of popular culture, this opens the contested interior of the consenting subject rather than treating consent as a black box.

The slide's definition is precise. "Common sense is the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world. It is historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups" (Lecture 17 slides). The teacher's summary repeats the formulation almost verbatim (GramsciandHegemony teacher summary), an indication of how central the concept is. Three features matter. First, common sense is everyday and taken for granted; it is not a doctrine that subjects could refute, because they hardly notice they hold it. Second, it is historically produced; what passes for common sense in one conjuncture was hard-won leadership in an earlier one. Third, it is contradictory; common sense does not need to be internally consistent because it is the sediment of multiple past struggles.

The pair's other term saves the framework from cultural pessimism. "Good sense refers to the critical and reflective elements within common sense that can challenge dominant ideas. Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense" (Lecture 17 slides). Good sense is not outside common sense and not the analyst's higher knowledge. It lives within the same reservoir that holds dominant framings. Hegemonic work is not the conjuring of false consciousness but the daily activity of foregrounding some common-sense fragments and burying others.

What does this mean for reading popular culture? Three operations follow.

First, the analyst reads texts for the common-sense materials they assemble. A reality-television show celebrating entrepreneurial selfhood does not invent the value of hard work; it activates a long-sedimented folk-wisdom about effort and discipline and articulates it to the bloc's preferred political programme. The lecturer's Media as a Site of Hegemonic Struggle slide gives the paradigmatic example: "Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides). The good-sense kernel that personal responsibility matters in immediate life is real; the hegemonic move splices it to a framing that obscures structural causation.

Second, the analyst reads for good-sense kernels available for re-articulation. The same reservoir that holds "individual responsibility" also holds "we look after each other" and "the system is rigged". Williams and Gilbert's diagnosis of the contemporary structure of feeling identifies the suppressed good sense explicitly: it includes "a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Most people already know the system is unfair; the question is whether a counter-hegemonic project can articulate that good sense into a hegemonising horizon. Cross-link to L13: Fraser's account of "subaltern counterpublics" (Fraser, 1992: 67) supplies the institutional setting in which submerged good-sense kernels can be circulated and re-articulated outside hegemonic broadcast.

Third, the analyst reads polysemically. Hall's encoding/decoding model (L15) is essentially a methodology for good sense. Television texts "carry the institutional/political/ideological order imprinted in them" (Hall, 1980: 134) as preferred reading, but the polysemic structure of the sign means oppositional decoding remains possible. What makes oppositional reading sociologically possible, rather than wishful, is the good-sense kernel held within the same common sense the text addresses. *Coronation Street*'s long-running depiction of working-class community life, for example, encodes dominant common sense about social mobility and individual virtue, but its sedimented archive of solidarity, mutual aid and shared trouble can be decoded as a source of good sense about collective life. The text is contradictory because common sense is contradictory.

The cross-lecture mapping is rich. Williams's "culture is ordinary" (Williams, 1958: 4) supplies the social ontology under the common-sense thesis: hegemony works on materials already produced by people in the texture of everyday life (L14). Hoggart's account of the hollowing-out of "the older, more vigorous popular culture" by a flattering commercial idiom (Hoggart, 1957: 246) anticipates the slide's diagnosis of how good sense is suppressed. Hall's reading of *Love Thy Neighbour* in 'The Whites of Their Eyes' shows the same common-sense work on the racial axis. The Common Sense and Good Sense pair is Gramsci's single most useful analytic for everything that follows in the L13–L16 sequence.

### Essay M6. "Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides; teacher summary). Discuss Gramsci's distinction between traditional and organic intellectuals, with reference to media institutions and cultural production.

The Intellectuals and Cultural Power slide redefines intellectual work in a way that has organised cultural studies for decades. Gramsci's claim that journalists, editors and cultural producers can function as organic intellectuals is not an honorific but a structural analysis. Anyone who organises ideas and meaning in society performs intellectual work; the question is for whom. The distinction between traditional and organic intellectuals turns this into a tool for analysing media institutions, the institutional ecology of cultural production, and the historical bloc.

The slide sets out the conceptual triangle. "Gramsci defines intellectuals broadly as those who organise ideas and meaning in society. Traditional intellectuals see themselves as neutral or independent. Organic intellectuals emerge from specific social groups and articulate their interests. Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). The teacher's summary reproduces the same definitions (GramsciandHegemony teacher summary). The implicit polemic is against the romantic image of the free-floating intellectual.

The traditional intellectual is recognisable: priests, professors, judges, leading journalists who present themselves as standing above the fray. The slide is precise that they "see themselves as neutral or independent" (Lecture 17 slides). They are not necessarily dishonest about this; the claim to neutrality is a real institutional position. But the Gramscian analyst reads neutrality as itself a hegemonic posture. Traditional intellectuals appear neutral because the historical bloc whose interests they articulate has secured its leadership so completely that those interests have become the unmarked default. To stand outside is to stand within.

The organic intellectual is the corrective concept. The slide notes that organic intellectuals "emerge from specific social groups and articulate their interests" (Lecture 17 slides). They are organic in the sense that they emerge organically from a social group, and they remain organic by continuing to articulate that group's project. A trade-union educator, a journalist who articulates the worldview of a specific class fraction, a creator who voices a community: all are organic intellectuals. The slide's important addition is that media professionals "can function as" organic intellectuals, with the modal verb signalling that not all do, that the function is conditional on the work performed.

What does this analysis disclose about media institutions? Three things. First, that media work is always already political work. The BBC newsroom's craft of impartiality is not the absence of position but a particular way of taking position by refusing visible alignment. Hall's reading of the 1970s mugging panic in *Policing the Crisis* (1978) shows the mechanism: the racialised moral panic was produced not by individual racist journalists but by the routine cooperation of police press briefings, court reporting and tabloid framings (Lecture 17 slides). Organic intellectual work happens at institutional rather than individual level.

Second, that the historical bloc has its own organic-intellectual ecology. Williams and Gilbert identify the contemporary version: "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Influencers, business-success podcasters, reality-television presenters and the tabloid press perform organic-intellectual work for the techno-financial bloc, translating its position into the idiom of everyday aspiration. The slide on Hegemony as a Political Process makes the institutional claim explicit: "the social group who carry out these types of activities are organic intellectuals" (Lecture 17 slides).

Third, that counter-hegemony has its own organic-intellectual lineage. The Counter-hegemony and organic intellectuals section of the lecture takes Alan Moore and Garth Ennis as exemplars, "'organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics" who "have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides). Moore's *Watchmen* and Ennis's *The Boys* (and their HBO and Prime Video television adaptations) perform organic-intellectual work for a constituency that the corporate superhero canon had hegemonically defined out of the genre. The TV *Watchmen* placing a Black, female superhero, Sister Night, at its centre, and *The Boys* on television "subverting the myth of heroism" (Lecture 17 slides), are organic-intellectual interventions inside the corporate distribution system.

Cross-links. The framework pairs with Hall's encoding/decoding (L15): organic intellectuals shape the preferred reading at the encoding moment, while counter-hegemonic intellectuals work on the same texts at decoding. It also marks the distance from postmodern (L16) dispersed authorship: Gramsci insists that intellectual work is always attached to a project. The journalist who claims neutrality is the exemplar of the traditional position the framework was designed to unmask.

### Essay M7. "A war of position refers to a long-term struggle within civil society to reshape culture, values, and common sense" (Lecture 17 slides). Drawing on Hall's reading of encoding/decoding as "hegemony and counter-hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides), discuss the war of position as the dominant strategic horizon for cultural politics in advanced capitalist societies.

The slide on the War of Position offers the most strategically consequential paragraph in the lecture. The point of the distinction between war of manoeuvre and war of position is to assert that in advanced capitalist societies, frontal assault on state power is neither viable nor decisive. Power is too thickly distributed across civil society for that. The slide adds the institutional sites: media, education, popular culture. Hall's encoding/decoding diagram, which the lecturer draws under the explicit heading "hegemony and counter-hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides), turns the positional war into an operational programme.

The slide's formulation is precise. "A war of position refers to a long-term struggle within civil society to reshape culture, values, and common sense. Unlike a war of manoeuvre (direct revolutionary confrontation), Gramsci argues that social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). Three features stand out. First, the war is long-term; settlements take decades to build. Second, it is conducted within civil society; the institutional terrain is dense and distributed, not a single citadel. Third, its target is culture, values and common sense; this is the level at which hegemony is held, and therefore the level at which counter-hegemony must be built.

The contrast with the war of manoeuvre is historical and structural. In societies where civil society is thin, the state is the whole field. A successful frontal assault decides things. In advanced capitalism the state sits inside a thick institutional ecology, the integral state of political society plus civil society (Lecture 17 slides). Tearing down the political society leaves the civil-society apparatus intact, and the civil-society apparatus will reconstitute the bloc's leadership. The slide on Hegemony and Civil Society makes the consequence visible: civil society is "the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together" (Lecture 17 slides). Knit such things together long enough and they survive almost any government change.

What does the positional war look like in practice? The slide names the three principal fronts: media, education, popular culture. Hall's encoding/decoding model gives the diagram for the first and third. The lecturer's slide reproduces it with explicit annotations: at the production end, "Possibility of counter-hegemonic representations"; at the reception end, "Possibility of counter-hegemonic readings (negotiated / oppositional)" (Lecture 17 slides). Hall's original framing is that television's preferred meaning "carries the institutional/political/ideological order imprinted in them" (Hall, 1980: 134), but the polysemic structure of the sign keeps both counter-hegemonic production and counter-hegemonic decoding alive. A positional war therefore proceeds on both ends of the diagram. At encoding, it builds counter-hegemonic media institutions and supports counter-hegemonic creators (the slide's Alan Moore, Garth Ennis, Sister Night, *The Boys* examples). At decoding, it builds the interpretive communities, fandoms and counterpublics that can read dominantly produced texts oppositionally.

The strategic implication is that cultural politics is the substance, not the ornament, of political struggle. As Bennett's reading of Gramsci insists, popular culture is "a force field of relations shaped, precisely, by these contradictory pressures and tendencies" (Bennett, 1986: xiii), and the political project is therefore to articulate cultural elements differently. The hegemonic right has done this exercise repeatedly. The Thatcherite project Hall analysed in the 1980s, the long 1990s consolidation under Clinton and Blair, and the post-2016 articulation of nationalist populism (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4–7) were all positional victories before they were electoral ones. The left has, by Williams and Gilbert's account, lacked comparable positional infrastructure for decades.

Cross-links. The war-of-position framework pairs naturally with Williams's "culture is ordinary" thesis (L14) and Hall's encoding/decoding (L15) because both insist that ordinary cultural practice is where politics actually happens. It contrasts sharply with Habermas's rational-critical public sphere (L13): Habermas locates politics in a special discursive arena, Gramsci locates it across everyday cultural practice. And the post-2016 conjuncture, what Gramsci called an "interregnum" of "morbid symptoms" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1), is the moment when positional war must intensify rather than wait. The question is who will articulate the materials of late-neoliberal common sense, with its real grievances and submerged good-sense kernels, into a hegemonising horizon.

The war of position is therefore not patience as withdrawal but patience as strategy. It is the only strategy whose temporal arc matches the depth of the structures it seeks to transform. Counter-hegemony is slow, distributed and continuous, or it is nothing.

### Essay M8. "Power = coercion + hegemony." Critically discuss with reference to Gramsci.

The slide equation "Power = coercion + hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides) compresses the central theoretical revolution of the *Prison Notebooks*. I read it not as additive but as redistribution: Gramsci argues that the visible apparatus of force (political society) is the smaller part of the story, and that the larger work of reproducing capitalist order happens through hegemony, the cultural and moral leadership exercised through civil society.

The starting puzzle is empirical. Gramsci, writing in a Fascist prison after the defeat of the Italian workers' movement, asked why Western European capitalism had not collapsed. The classical Marxist account (base determines superstructure, immiseration produces revolution) could not explain the resilience of bourgeois rule, working-class consent to its own subordination, or fascism with mass support. Something else held the system together. Gramsci named it hegemony: "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'" (Gramsci, 1971: 57–8). The ruling class dominates by force when necessary, but it *leads* by consent, and must "exercise 'leadership' before winning government power" (1971: 57).

So coercion is not abolished. The police, the army, the courts remain: they are political society. But in mature capitalist societies they are activated as a last resort. The everyday work of reproducing the social order is done elsewhere: in schools, churches, families, newspapers, broadcast media, advertising and now algorithmic platforms. As the lecture puts it, "the power of the ruling groups and the reproduction of the status quo are maintained less through the State's direct exercise of force than through the management of consent and through the exercise of moral and intellectual authority" (Gramsci, 1971, slide quotation).

What does this redistribution mean analytically? Three things. First, it dissolves the dominant ideology thesis. Hegemony is not a single coherent worldview imposed top-down on a passive population. That account is "one of exclusion" (Lecture 17 slides). Hegemony is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides), absorbing and partially satisfying subaltern demands in order to neutralise them. Welfare states, anti-discrimination law, even certain forms of identity recognition are not capitulations of hegemonic power; they are exactly how it works.

Second, it makes consent the central problem. "More or less active consent of large sections of the population" (Lecture 17 slides) is a continuum from full endorsement to cynical resignation. Williams and Gilbert call the lower end of this continuum passive consent: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4) was the high water mark of exactly this passive-consent settlement.

Third, the equation politicises culture. If power is largely cultural, then culture is largely political. The lecture's example is the diet of "entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These are not entertainment plus instruction; they are the daily production of hegemonic common sense. Conversely, Alan Moore and Garth Ennis "reinventing the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides), and Hall's counter-hegemonic readings of media racial grammar, are interventions on the same terrain.

A critical caveat. The equation can over-rotate towards culture and underweight coercion. Neoliberalism "in practice has always been enabled and supported by authoritarian, racist, and outright antidemocratic practices of government" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Coercion *plus* hegemony, then, with the balance shifting depending on the conjuncture: in crisis, coercion comes forward; in stability, hegemony does the quiet work. The contrast with Foucault sharpens the equation. Foucault disperses power across capillary "disciplinary" techniques in barracks, clinics, schools and prisons (Foucault, 1977: 215), and through biopolitical management of populations (Foucault, 1978: 140). Where Foucault's power has no centre and no class subject, Gramsci's hegemony retains both a strategic agent (the historical bloc) and a determinate site (civil society), giving counter-hegemonic projects something definite to target. Hall's *Policing the Crisis* (1978) is the lecture's paradigm of how the two interlock, a racialised moral panic about "mugging" licensing the intensification of police powers while reorganising popular common sense (Lecture 17 slides). Gramsci's contribution was to show that cultural and coercive are mutually constitutive, and that the cultural is more effective.

### Essay M9. Why did the turn to Gramsci displace both Frankfurt School pessimism and structuralist analysis of popular culture? Answer with reference to Bennett.

Tony Bennett's introduction to *Popular Culture and Social Relations* maps why, in the late 1970s and early 1980s, cultural studies turned from Frankfurt School and structuralist inheritances and reorganised itself around Gramsci. I argue that the Gramscian turn was not a refinement of either tradition but a rejection of a shared assumption: that culture is a vehicle for a monolithic dominant ideology imposed from without.

Bennett's diagnostic is precise. "Politically speaking, both the structuralist and culturalist paradigms subscribe to a rather similar conception of the structure and organisation of the cultural and ideological spheres … both paradigms regard the sphere of cultural and ideological practices as being governed by a dominant ideology, essentially and monolithically bourgeois in its characteristics, which, albeit with varying degrees of success, is imposed from without, as an alien force, on the subordinate classes" (Bennett, 1986: xiii). On this reading, Frankfurt's culture industry and Saussurean structuralism are political twins. The former condemns popular culture as the deformation of the masses; the latter dissects it as the mechanical reproduction of bourgeois codes. Both leave the analyst standing outside, exposing the obfuscating mechanism. Adorno and Horkheimer's verdict that under the culture industry "the deceived masses … insist on the very ideology which enslaves them" (Adorno & Horkheimer, 1944: 133) is the high-water mark of this pessimism: domination is total, consumers are dupes, the analyst is the only one awake. Gramsci's hegemony cancels the asymmetry by treating popular consent as historically produced and partially contested rather than as ontological gullibility, and that is precisely the conceptual shift that makes a Birmingham programme of research possible.

The alternative (culturalism, the home-grown British tradition of Hoggart, Williams and Thompson) fell into the opposite trap. It was "uncritically romantic in its celebration of popular culture as expressing the authentic interests and values of subordinate social groups and classes" (Bennett, 1986: xii). On this reading popular culture is the people's voice, awaiting only amplification. Both positions, Bennett notes, share "a conception of the cultural and ideological field as being divided between two opposing cultural and ideological camps, bourgeois and working class, locked in a zero-sum game" (Bennett, 1986: xiii).

Gramsci's hegemony cuts this knot. The cultural and ideological relations between ruling and subordinate classes "consist less in the *domination* of the latter by the former than in the struggle for *hegemony*, that is, for moral, cultural, intellectual and, thereby, political leadership over the whole of society" (Bennett, 1986: xiv). Hegemony is not imposition; it is negotiation. Bourgeois hegemony "is secured not via the obliteration of working class culture, but via its *articulation to* bourgeois culture and ideology so that, in being associated with and expressed in the forms of the latter, its political affiliations are altered in the process" (Bennett, 1986: xiv–xv).

This single move transforms three things at once. First, it dissolves analytical paralysis: the analyst need not choose between condemning popular culture as dupery or celebrating it as authentic resistance. Popular culture becomes "an area of negotiation between the two within which … dominant, subordinate and oppositional cultural and ideological values and elements are 'mixed' in different permutations" (Bennett, 1986: xv). Reality TV is neither pure ideology nor pure people's voice; it is a contested field.

Second, it makes cultural practices *movable*. "The political and ideological articulations of cultural practices are *movable*: a practice which is articulated to bourgeois values today may be disconnected from those values and connected to socialist ones tomorrow" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). Nationalism has no fixed class belonging; it can be articulated to fascism or socialism depending on the conjuncture.

Third, it allows analysis of race, gender and nation alongside class without subsuming them. The framework "enables due account to be taken of the relative separation of different regions of cultural struggle (class, race, gender) as well as of the complex and changing ways in which these may be overlapped on to one another" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). This is what Hall does when he extends Gramsci to the "grammar of race" in *Policing the Crisis* and 'The Whites of Their Eyes'.

Bennett concedes the framework's risk: "it is liable to the criticism that it is too accommodating and expansive a framework, over-totalising in its analytical claims and ambit" (Bennett, 1986: xvii). But against Frankfurt's gloom and structuralism's mechanism, Gramsci gives popular-culture analysis its maturity: the ability to take popular culture seriously as a site of struggle rather than as either symptom or salvation.

### Essay M10. What does Gramsci mean by civil society, and why does he treat it as the principal terrain of struggle in advanced capitalist societies?

Gramsci's distinction between political society and civil society is the structural backbone of his theory of hegemony, and it makes his analysis applicable to advanced capitalist societies where seizure of state power has proven impossible. Civil society for Gramsci is not, as in liberal theory, the realm of voluntary association set against the state, but the institutional terrain where consent to ruling-class leadership is manufactured, contested and renewed. Recognising this redirects revolutionary strategy from frontal assault to long, sustained cultural work.

The lecture's slide diagram captures the basic distinction. "Political society includes the state, law, police, courts, and military, and operates mainly through coercion" while "Civil society includes media, education, religion, family, and culture, and operates through persuasion and consent" (Lecture 17 slides). Together they form what Gramsci calls the integral state. Crucially, "Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides). Civil society is not a buffer between citizen and state; it is the *medium* through which the state, broadly understood, exists.

What kind of space is it? "Civil society is the social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State … the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together … where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the texture of everyday life, and what makes it politically decisive. As Marx via Gramsci put it, "the nuts and bolts of social knowledge are put together and adjusted by people in ordinary practical activities" (Lecture 17 slides). It is worth marking the contrast with Habermas's bourgeois public sphere, "a category of bourgeois society" mediating between state and private life (Habermas, 1989: 27). Where Habermas idealises this space as the site of rational-critical debate, Gramsci sees the same terrain as already saturated with hegemonic struggle. Fraser's "subaltern counterpublics", in which "members of subordinated social groups invent and circulate counterdiscourses" (Fraser, 1992: 67), translate directly into Gramscian terms as counter-hegemonic articulation within civil society.

Why is this the principal terrain in advanced capitalism? Gramsci's answer turns on a contrast between societies where civil society is thin (a frontal assault could topple the regime) and where it is densely institutionalised. In the latter, churches, parties, unions, newspapers, schools and families absorb shocks and reproduce consent independently of state action. A frontal assault would leave bourgeois hegemony intact because hegemony lives in the press, the school and the family dinner table. Hence: "social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the war of position.

Civil society has three crucial features. First, it operates through inclusion rather than exclusion. Hegemony works by absorbing dissident demands and re-articulating them. The post-war welfare state did not destroy working-class consciousness; it accommodated it within a settlement combining social-democratic concessions with capitalist property relations.

Second, civil society is administrative. "All the public institutions of civil society are engaged in developing and managing the part of world with which they're concerned" (Lecture 17 slides). The BBC, schools, professional bodies are not propaganda machines but managers of consent, embedding common-sense framings in routine practice.

Third, civil society is the home of organic intellectuals who "shape narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). Hall's *Policing the Crisis* shows the institutional ecology in which this works: police press briefings, court reporting and tabloid framings produced the racialised "mugging" panic not by individual conspiracy but by the routine cooperation of civil-society sites.

Contemporary illustrations bear this out. The alliance of "the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and those sections of finance capital closely aligned with them" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3) came to lead through civil society long before any election. Big Tech platforms (Apple, Facebook, Google) now hold "virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). The same logic illuminates Thatcherism's TINA and Brexit's "take back control": hegemonic victories first won in civil society, in tabloid framings and everyday talk, long before any ballot.

This is why, for Gramsci, the seizure of state power without prior victory in civil society is doomed. A counter-hegemonic bloc must lead before it can rule. There is no shortcut. The war of position is long.

### Essay M11. How does Gramsci's distinction between common sense and good sense help us analyse contemporary media?

Gramsci's distinction between common sense and good sense is the single most analytically powerful tool in his repertoire for analysing contemporary media, more powerful even than hegemony itself, because it opens the contradictory interior of the consenting subject rather than treating consent as a black box.

The lecture's definitions are stark. "Common sense is the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world. It is historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups. Good sense refers to the critical and reflective elements within common sense that can challenge dominant ideas. Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense" (Lecture 17 slides). Two things are crucial here. Common sense is not stupid: it is historically produced, the sediment of past struggles. And good sense is not external to common sense: it lives inside it, available to be activated by the right kind of articulation.

For media analysis this changes everything. Audiences are not dupes carrying false consciousness, nor sovereign decoders plucking whatever meanings they fancy from texts. They are carriers of contradictory common sense, in which hegemonic framings coexist with submerged good-sense kernels, and media work is the daily struggle to amplify the former and bury the latter.

Take the slide's own example: "Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides). The hegemonic move is precisely to activate a deeply sedimented folk wisdom about personal responsibility (good sense in some contexts: people really do shape their immediate lives) and to splice it to a framing that obscures structural causation, legitimising cuts and inaction. The brilliance of the framing is that it does not contradict ordinary experience; it builds on it. The good-sense kernel that might challenge it (recognition that the crisis is one of capital accumulation, not personal thrift) exists in the same common-sense reservoir, but it requires articulation to surface.

Williams and Gilbert give the contemporary version of this hegemonic work. Neoliberal common sense circulates through "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These sites do not impose a doctrine; they ventriloquise existing folk-wisdom about hard work and aspiration, and articulate it to a political programme of "anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). The good-sense kernels (cooperation, mutual aid, structural critique) are present but unmobilised.

The dominant late-neoliberal "structure of feeling" that Williams and Gilbert anatomise is itself a perfect specimen of good sense suppressed. "A conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5) is good sense that *exists*: most people know the system is unfair. But it goes nowhere because no political force articulates it into action; instead it sits alongside "an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5) as a stable, passive consent. The challenge for any counter-hegemonic project is to take that submerged critique and articulate it into a hegemonic horizon.

The methodological implication for media studies is significant. Texts must be read not for a single ideological message but for the *contradictory* common sense they assemble and the good-sense kernels they suppress or activate. This is exactly the methodological payoff of Hall's encoding/decoding model. Television discourse is encoded with a "preferred reading" but remains "polysemic", admitting "dominant", "negotiated" and "oppositional" decodings (Hall, 1980: 134). The good-sense kernel embedded in the same common-sense reservoir is what makes oppositional decoding sociologically possible rather than merely wishful. *Policing the Crisis* models this exactly: Hall and his collaborators show how the "mugging" moral panic activates a long-sedimented racial common sense while suppressing the good-sense recognition that the underlying crisis is one of capital accumulation (Lecture 17 slides). 'The Whites of Their Eyes' extends the analysis to sitcom: *Love Thy Neighbour*'s comic juxtaposition of the Booth and Reynolds households trades on a shared common sense about neighbourly racial conflict that can be decoded "in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides), with good-sense recognition of the absurdity of racism running alongside its naturalisation.

Gramsci thus offers media analysis a non-condescending materialism. People are not stupid; their common sense is the sediment of history. The political question is which fragments of that history get articulated, by whom, in whose interest. That is the daily work of hegemonic and counter-hegemonic media.

### Essay M12. "Hegemony is generated from below, not imposed from above." Discuss with concrete examples.

The Gramscian claim that "hegemony is not something imposed on society from above so much as generated from below" (Lecture 17 slides) is the most counter-intuitive and the most powerful element of his framework. It is correct in two senses (hegemony requires consent from below, and its ideological materials are drawn from below) but should not be confused with the populist claim that hegemonic projects are spontaneous expressions of the people. They are constructed, by organic intellectuals, out of materials already circulating in popular common sense.

The thesis rests on Gramsci's distinction between hegemony and dominant ideology. The latter is "one of exclusion" while hegemony "involves processes of inclusion … a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides). Bourgeois hegemony "is secured not via the obliteration of working class culture, but via its *articulation to* bourgeois culture and ideology" (Bennett, 1986: xiv–xv). The hegemonic bloc must offer something (partial recognition, partial concession) to generate the consent it requires. That something must come from below.

The paradigmatic case is the neoliberal hegemony anatomised by Williams and Gilbert. Neoliberalism did not impose itself on a recalcitrant population; it constructed a new common sense by articulating elements already circulating in popular life. It works through "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These folk-wisdoms about aspiration and self-reliance were re-articulated to a programme of "anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets and digitisation of media and information" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). Thatcherism is the British paradigm: Hall reads it as a hegemonic project that took popular discontent with the post-war settlement and articulated it into a market-authoritarian common sense. The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4) was its consolidation.

What 2016 reveals is that this generation-from-below is reversible. "From the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, to the UK's vote to leave the European Union" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1) marks the moment a "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) began articulating the same popular materials into a counter-bloc. Brexit's "take back control" slogan, the cost-of-living crisis, MAGA, Reform UK, the tabloid press circulating culture-wars framing: these mobilise real grievances from below. What changed was the political force willing to articulate them. "What holds together political collectives … is not primarily a shared 'identity' so much as a common set of material interests" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6).

Yet "generated from below" cuts against any spontaneist reading. Hegemonic projects require organic intellectuals. "Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). Raymond Williams's cultural-materialist insistence that "culture is ordinary: that is the first fact" (Williams, 1958: 4) supplies the social ontology under this claim: hegemony works on materials already produced by people in the texture of everyday life, materials that are neither false nor pre-ideological but the raw stuff of meaning-making itself. The infrastructure Williams and Gilbert describe (schools, reality TV, influencer culture, podcasters such as Joe Rogan, the manosphere around figures like Andrew Tate, platforms) is the organic-intellectual ecology through which sentiments from below are codified into common sense from above. Generation from below means assembly from below, not creation from below.

A counter-hegemonic version of the same logic operates. *Policing the Crisis* shows that within the racialised "mugging" panic of 1970s Britain, "counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest" was operating (Lecture 17 slides), articulating long-sedimented experience into oppositional forms. Black Lives Matter and climate movements such as Just Stop Oil are contemporary cases: good-sense kernels articulated by counter-intellectuals into oppositional public forms.

Williams and Gilbert's update adds a complication. Contemporary hegemony often does not require active consent. The dominant structure of feeling is passive: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency … combined with … ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Late-neoliberal hegemony does not need to win hearts and minds; it only needs to keep them disengaged.

The strategic implication is that counter-hegemony must do the same articulation work. It must identify good-sense kernels (solidarity, fairness, recognition of structural injustice) and articulate them into a hegemonising project. Without that, popular discontent stays passive, and the right captures the materials the left fails to mobilise.

### Essay M13. Evaluate the claim that media professionals are organic intellectuals. Use specific contemporary examples.

Gramsci's redefinition of "intellectual" as anyone who organises ideas and meaning in society opens a productive way of thinking about journalists, editors, podcasters, comic-book writers and influencers as functioning organic intellectuals. The claim is largely correct, but must be qualified in three ways: by recognising the limits of individual agency within media institutions, by distinguishing types of organic-intellectual work, and by acknowledging platform-mediated intellectuals whose relation to class structures is diffuse.

The lecture's definition is broad. "Gramsci defines intellectuals broadly as those who organise ideas and meaning in society. Traditional intellectuals see themselves as neutral or independent. Organic intellectuals emerge from specific social groups and articulate their interests. Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). The crucial move is to politicise the apparent neutrality of professional media work. The BBC newsroom's claim to impartiality, the broadsheet leader-writer's air of judicious balance, the academic talking-head's expertise: all these are positions within hegemonic struggle, not above it.

Contemporary illustrations bear this out. *Hegemony Now* identifies the organic-intellectual ecology as "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Reality TV presenters, business-influencer YouTubers, podcasters such as Joe Rogan, and the tabloid press (the *Sun*, *Mail*, *Mirror*) do organic-intellectual work in Gramsci's sense: translating the position of the techno-financial bloc into the idiom of everyday aspiration, until the bloc's interests appear as common sense.

On the counter-hegemonic side, the slides give us Alan Moore and Garth Ennis as "'organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics" who "have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides). Moore's *Watchmen* and Ennis's *The Boys*, especially in their TV adaptations, take a hegemonic American myth (the heroic superhero embodying patriotic American power) and subvert it from within. Sister Night in HBO's *Watchmen*, "a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists" (Lecture 17 slides), performs this articulation; *The Boys* on television enacts "subverting the myth of heroism" (Lecture 17 slides).

Hall models media as organic-intellectual infrastructure. *Policing the Crisis* shows that the "mugging" panic was produced not by individual racist journalists but by an institutional ecology of police briefings, court reporting routines and tabloid framings that produced racialised crime coverage almost automatically (Lecture 17 slides). 'The Whites of Their Eyes' shows the same work in primetime sitcoms: *Love Thy Neighbour*'s scriptwriters codified a "grammar of race" (Lecture 17 slides) that organised popular understanding of the multiracial neighbourhood (Eddie and Joan Booth, Bill and Barbie Reynolds). The mechanism is what Hall's encoding/decoding paper calls the routinised production of a "preferred reading", embedded in the "professional code" that newsroom staff perform precisely *as* impartial craft (Hall, 1980: 136). Hall's later reading of Thatcherism extends this to political journalism: the right-wing press performed the organic-intellectual work of articulating "authoritarian populism" into a winning common-sense bloc (Hall, 1988: 6). Organic intellectuals work through institutions, not in spite of them.

The claim must be qualified. First, individual journalists operate within institutional and economic constraints. The institutional ecology Hall describes is itself the organic intellectual; particular reporters are nodes within it. Second, Williams and Gilbert add a further dimension: the platforms themselves. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power … Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Big Tech (Apple, Facebook, Google) "establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6), shaping in advance which organic-intellectual voices can find audiences. Third, "media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides). The routine practice of news selection, performed by professionals who understand themselves as neutral, is the daily reproduction of the bloc's preferred common sense.

The Gramscian framework remains uniquely capable of registering all this. To say a journalist is an organic intellectual is not an accusation but an analytical claim: media work is always already political work, and pretending otherwise is itself a hegemonic move.

### Essay M14. "Common sense is the central battleground of contemporary politics." Discuss with reference to the conjunctural crisis described in Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now*.

The proposition that common sense is the central battleground of contemporary politics is exactly right, and one of Gramsci's most important practical contributions to political analysis. I defend it through Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now*, reading the neoliberal "long 1990s" as the paradigm of common-sense victory and the post-2016 "interregnum" as the moment of common-sense disturbance.

Common sense is "the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world. It is historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups" (Lecture 17 slides). It is a contested settlement, the precipitate of past hegemonic and counter-hegemonic struggles. To change politics fundamentally is to change common sense. Winning an election is one thing; making a project unchallengeable is another, and the latter is what hegemonic victory means.

Williams and Gilbert provide the paradigm case. The "leading tendencies of global culture" since the late twentieth century are "more or less direct expressions of the interests and values" of Silicon Valley and aligned finance capital (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). What made this hegemony succeed was not any specific election (though Clinton and Blair consolidated it) but that it changed what could be said in everyday life. During the "long 1990s … social and political relations remained remarkably stable" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4): the achieved hegemonic condition where individualist, market-conforming common sense had become difficult to dispute.

How did neoliberal hegemony win the common-sense battle? Not by inventing a new worldview but by articulating elements already circulating in popular life. Williams and Gilbert identify the everyday infrastructure: "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). The aspirations to upward mobility, the valorisation of self-reliance, the suspicion of state inefficiency: these were good-sense kernels (real responses to real experiences) that were re-articulated into a coherent project. The lecture's gloss is exact: "media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides).

The 2016 conjuncture marks the moment when this settlement begins to crack. "From the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, to the UK's vote to leave the European Union, and from the global COVID-19 pandemic to the early signs of catastrophic climate collapse" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1), the "rules" that had governed common sense were "rapidly discarded" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). Williams and Gilbert read this not as the death of hegemony but as the emergence of a competing articulation: a "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) that mobilises real grievances and rearticulates them into a counter-bloc. The battle over common sense is what is being fought.

Why is common sense the central battleground? Because elections, policies and institutions follow common sense; they rarely lead it. The Wall Street-Silicon Valley bloc could only secure decades of deregulation, financialisation and platform monopolisation because the common-sense framing of markets as efficient and platforms as neutral had already been built. The lecture's framework gives the analytic backbone: hegemony is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides) generated as much from below as from above; the work that matters is upstream of policy.

The Williams and Gilbert qualification sharpens the diagnosis. Late-neoliberal common sense is held passively: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency … combined with … ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The diagnosis borrows its conceptual machinery from the British Cultural Studies lineage. Raymond Williams's "structure of feeling" registers the affective texture in which "culture is ordinary" (Williams, 1958: 4) shades into political quiescence, and Hoggart's account of how "the older, more vigorous popular culture" is hollowed out by a commercial idiom that flatters its consumers (Hoggart, 1957: 246) anticipates the lecture's image of neoliberal common sense as resignation dressed as pleasure. This is vulnerable hegemony. Counter-hegemonic projects have an opening but must do the articulation work, identifying good-sense kernels and mobilising them. From Hall's reading of Black resistance within the *Policing the Crisis* conjuncture to Moore's and Ennis's superhero re-encodings, counter-articulation is possible but slow. The successful left projects of the future will be those that win the common-sense battle before they win the electoral one (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7).

### Essay M15. How does Stuart Hall extend Gramsci's framework to race? Answer with reference to *Policing the Crisis* and 'The Whites of Their Eyes'.

Stuart Hall's extension of Gramsci to race is one of the foundational moves of British cultural studies, transforming media analysis by adding race as a constitutive axis of hegemonic struggle alongside class. Hall's two key interventions (*Policing the Crisis* and 'The Whites of Their Eyes') demonstrate how the Gramscian framework can register racial hegemony without becoming class-essentialist, and how media institutions function as prime sites where racial common sense is encoded, contested and decoded oppositionally.

The lecture summarises Hall's contribution: he "extends Gramsci's concept of hegemony to race and other categorisations" and employs the term "grammar of race" to show how racial hegemony is encoded in media representations and "can be decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides). In *Policing the Crisis* he shows how capitalist hegemony "displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging'), but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides).

*Policing the Crisis* (1978), authored by Hall and colleagues at the Birmingham CCCS, is the masterwork of Gramscian media analysis. Built around a 1972 Birmingham street robbery, the book tracks how "mugging" became a major political issue in 1970s Britain through the interaction of media, policing and state power. Capitalist hegemony "displaces the crisis onto a moral panic" (Lecture 17 slides): the crisis of capital became, in tabloid hands, a crisis of order; the crisis of order became a crisis of race.

This extends Gramsci to race in three ways. First, hegemony operates by *displacement*: when the contradictions of capital accumulation cannot be resolved economically, they are managed ideologically through racialised folk-devils. Race becomes a sutured surface across which class contradiction is reorganised. Second, organic intellectuals of the right (police press officers, court reporters, tabloid editors) collaborated, without conspiracy, in producing racialised common sense. Hall shows the institutional ecology of moral panic. Third, "counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest" (Lecture 17 slides) operates inside the conjuncture, not outside it.

'The Whites of Their Eyes' (1981) deepens the apparatus with the "grammar of race." Hall's claim is that racism in British media operates not through explicit slurs but through a deeper syntactic structure of representations, framings, juxtapositions and narrative roles. This grammar is "encoded in media representations" and can be "decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides). Hall's concrete example is the 1970s ITV sitcom *Love Thy Neighbour*, built around the white working-class Booths (Eddie and Joan) in Twickenham and their Black neighbours the Reynoldses (Bill and Barbie). The series encodes a racial grammar through casting, dialogue, comic timing and narrative role that constructs Black neighbours as a problem to be managed by white anxiety, even when the surface "lesson" appears to chastise overt racism. The preferred meaning is the grammar, not the moral.

This extends Gramsci because racial hegemony is not a separate sphere alongside class hegemony but woven into the same media practices. Bennett notes that the framework "enables due account to be taken of the relative separation of different regions of cultural struggle (class, race, gender) as well as of the complex and changing ways in which these may be overlapped on to one another" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). Hall is the theorist of this overlap. Race in Britain is not separable from class formation; class is not separable from racial categorisation.

The encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1973), diagrammed in the lecture slides, gives the methodological backbone: production encodes preferred hegemonic meanings, visual/aural form mediates, and audiences read dominantly, negotiate, or decode oppositionally. Hall's later restatement makes the politics explicit: television's "preferred reading" carries "the institutional/political/ideological order imprinted in them", but the polysemic structure of the sign means oppositional decoding remains possible (Hall, 1980: 134). The British Cultural Studies lineage matters here. From Williams's insistence that "culture is ordinary" (Williams, 1958: 4) to Hall's Birmingham programme, the move is to take popular media seriously as the medium in which racial common sense is both reproduced and contested. Counter-hegemony is generated in part at the moment of decoding, but counter-hegemonic decoding requires resources that must be built (alternative media, organised communities, counter-publics).

The contemporary purchase remains undiminished. Williams and Gilbert's analysis of the rise of a "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), the culture-wars framing in contemporary tabloid press, and the racialisation of Black Lives Matter coverage all work on the same terrain: displacing structural-economic contradiction onto racialised figures of threat. The Gramscian-Hallian framework remains the most powerful tool we have, because it refuses both colour-blind class reductionism and a culturalist abandonment of structural analysis.

### Essay M16. Compare the war of manoeuvre and the war of position. Which better captures contemporary left strategy, and why?

Gramsci's distinction between war of manoeuvre and war of position is one of the most strategically consequential in twentieth-century Marxism, and the indispensable starting point for thinking about left politics today. The war of position is overwhelmingly the right framework, but pure positional warfare risks complacency in moments of conjunctural crisis where decisive interventions are possible. The best strategy combines long-term position with readiness for moments of accelerated articulation.

The lecture defines the terms with characteristic precision. "A war of position refers to a long-term struggle within civil society to reshape culture, values, and common sense. Unlike a war of manoeuvre (direct revolutionary confrontation), Gramsci argues that social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides).

Gramsci's contrast was historical. The war of manoeuvre, "direct revolutionary confrontation" (Lecture 17 slides), suited societies where the state was almost everything and civil society thin. In the advanced capitalist West, civil society was dense. Hegemonic power lived not in the state alone but in newspapers, churches, schools and families. A frontal assault could not displace it. The conclusion: "social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the war of position.

For contemporary left strategy, this framework is overwhelmingly correct. There is no single institution whose seizure would reorganise power. Power lives in a distributed civil society and, increasingly, in the platforms and infrastructures of finance and technology. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power … Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The dominant bloc is "finance capital and big tech" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6); Big Tech (Apple, Facebook, Google) has established "virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Only a long, multi-front war of position has any chance.

What does that look like in practice? Williams and Gilbert sketch a strategy that is "socialist … populist and democratic" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), one that asks what alliances might cohere to "challenge both the hegemony of the techno-financial elite and the emerging power of the nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7). The terrain is sustained struggle "through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). The neoliberal bloc (Thatcherism's TINA, the long 1990s of Clinton and Blair) won by precisely this long war; the left has no comparable infrastructure.

Yet pure positionalism risks two problems. First, it can become indistinguishable from political withdrawal: doing "the long work" while immediate crises demand decisive action. Second, it can miss moments of organic crisis when "the old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear" (Gramsci, quoted in Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). Williams and Gilbert argue that since 2016 we have been living through such a moment: "a moment of grand realignment … a rare instant where more or less anything could be possible" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1).

In such moments, the war of position must intensify rather than wait. The strategic question is what can be articulated *now* into a counter-hegemonic project. The post-2016 conjuncture (Trump's MAGA, Brexit and "take back control", Reform UK, COVID-19, the cost-of-living crisis, climate collapse) is the interregnum the epigraph describes. Ground has been opened. The question is by whom new common sense gets articulated, with movements such as Black Lives Matter and Just Stop Oil pulling at the materials. The institutional vehicles for such articulation map closely onto Fraser's "subaltern counterpublics", "parallel discursive arenas where members of subordinated social groups invent and circulate counterdiscourses" (Fraser, 1992: 67). A serious war of position must build counterpublics across media, education and platform infrastructure simultaneously, rather than treating any one front as decisive.

Williams and Gilbert prescribe: "a left political strategy that can productively combine populist and democratic aspects might have the capacity to supplant the hegemony of technology and finance sectors, and present a credible bulwark against the nationalist authoritarians rising to take their place" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7). Populist, because it must articulate popular interests into a hegemonic horizon; democratic, because it must build new practices of democracy throughout civil society.

The war of position is correct. But it must be a *fighting* position, ready to seize the moments when crisis opens the ground.

### Essay M17. To what extent does Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now* update Gramsci for the era of platform capitalism?

Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now* presents itself as a recovery and update of Gramscian analysis for platform capitalism, financialisation and the crisis of neoliberalism. The book makes three substantive updates: a theory of passive consent, a re-grounding in material interests, and the addition of platforms as a structural mode of hegemony. The updates extend Gramsci for a conjuncture he could not have imagined.

The book opens with the Gramscian epigraph: "The old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear" (quoted in Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). The crisis is that of neoliberalism, "the political system that has ruled almost the entirety of the planet since the 1990s, is everywhere in decline, if not ruination" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1).

The central empirical claim is that the hegemonic bloc is the alliance between Silicon Valley and Wall Street. "The people who got the world that they wanted were the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and those sections of finance capital closely aligned with them" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3), and the precise combination of "social liberalisation, anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets and digitisation of media and information" expresses the interests of this coalition of class fractions. This is the historical bloc of the contemporary conjuncture.

The first update is *passive consent*. Classical theory assumes hegemony requires "the more or less active consent of large sections of the population" (Lecture 17 slides). Williams and Gilbert argue "the crude assumption that 'hegemony' can only be achieved through majority active consent to particular sets of governing norms is simply untrue" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4–5). Most consent under late neoliberalism is passive. They borrow Raymond Williams's "structure of feeling": "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). This is vulnerable hegemony, but hegemony.

The second update is *material interests*. Williams and Gilbert push back against post-Marxist culturalism's drift toward identity: "the consequent abandonment of any understanding of politics as centrally concerned with the expression of material interests has been analytically and strategically disastrous" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5–6). The point is not class reductionism but to insist that political collectives are held together by shared material interests rather than identity (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). This corrects post-Marxist excesses while preserving the Gramscian insight that hegemonic blocs are alliances of fractions.

The third update is *platforms as infrastructural hegemony*. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power … Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Gramsci theorised hegemony as ideological, cultural and moral. Williams and Gilbert add a fourth dimension: infrastructural. Hegemony today is instantiated in the architecture of Big Tech (Apple, Facebook, Google), which "establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). The infrastructural register is where Gramsci edges closest to Foucault. Foucault's account of disciplinary apparatuses that produce subjects through "a whole micro-penality of time" (Foucault, 1977: 178), and of biopolitical management as power "to make live" through population-level optimisation (Foucault, 1978: 138), captures something real about platform regimes that Gramsci's older ideological vocabulary does not. Yet Williams and Gilbert resist a wholesale switch: platforms remain hegemonic, that is, governed by an identifiable bloc and contestable, rather than dispersed beyond strategic intervention.

This third update is the most genuinely new. The first two refine Gramscian categories; the third adds one he could not have anticipated. Contemporary counter-hegemony must operate on all three planes: contesting structures of feeling, articulating material interests, and building alternative infrastructures.

The limits are worth marking. The book is more diagnostic than prescriptive on the third front: it identifies platforms as the key infrastructural mode but is less explicit on what counter-hegemonic platforms would look like. The "future war of position" is sketched as "socialist … populist and democratic" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), but the institutional vehicles remain underspecified.

Nonetheless, *Hegemony Now* is the most significant update of Gramscian theory in decades, and it confirms rather than refutes the claim that Gramsci's framework remains the most powerful tool for analysing twenty-first-century power.

### Essay M18. "Subcultures and fandoms are counter-hegemonic only until they are commodified." Discuss.

The claim that subcultures and fandoms are counter-hegemonic only until commodified compresses a real tension in cultural studies between celebratory accounts of resistance and pessimistic accounts of co-optation. The claim is partially correct (commodification dilutes counter-hegemonic charge) but too binary. The Gramscian framework allows us to see that counter-hegemonic moments can persist within commodified forms, that the line between resistance and incorporation is a continuum rather than a threshold, and that commodification can itself be the medium through which counter-hegemonic ideas reach mass publics.

The lecture sets out the classical framing. Hebdige's analysis of subcultures presents them as "consumers acting against the expectation of passivity. They resist cooption into society through ritual and style. Subcultures can be counter-hegemonic" (Lecture 17 slides). Subcultures use "media objects as part of this construction. However, this may result in the generation of new commodities and industries, eventually coopting the subculture into the majority capitalist culture. Market research firms engage in trend spotting, selling their insights to the market and accelerating this process" (Lecture 17 slides). The slide's logic is precise: insurgent style is recoded as commodity, the commodity is sold back as fashion, and the subcultural energy is partially neutralised.

Fandom is similar but more focused. Tulloch's "powerless elite", Jenkins's "textual poachers": fans claim back power by appropriating "the characters and worlds of media and create their own interpretations, storylines and artefacts. This can act to resist hegemonic interpretations" (Lecture 17 slides). The slide flags fan-creative practice (re-narration, alternative storylines, derivative artefacts) as the recognisable counter-hegemonic moment.

So the basic empirical observation is correct. Insurgent forms become merchandise; ritual-aesthetic disruption is recoded as style; corporate distribution absorbs critique and resells it. The corporate apparatus has a remarkable capacity to absorb its critics and resell their critique back to them. Adorno and Horkheimer's culture-industry thesis (Adorno & Horkheimer, 1944: 121) anticipates this trajectory in its starkest form: "amusement under late capitalism is the prolongation of work" (1944: 137), and dissent itself becomes another product line. Gramsci's framework, however, refuses Adorno's totalising verdict. Where culture industry sees only co-optation, hegemony sees a "force field" (Bennett, 1986: xiii) in which absorption is partial, contested and reversible.

But the claim that this absorption *exhausts* the counter-hegemonic charge is too strong, for three reasons.

First, the Gramscian principle of articulation. Cultural elements have no fixed political belonging. As Bennett puts it, "the political and ideological articulations of cultural practices are *movable*: a practice which is articulated to bourgeois values today may be disconnected from those values and connected to socialist ones tomorrow" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). A commodified element can be re-articulated.

Second, commodification can be the means of mass reach. *Watchmen* and *The Boys* are corporate products; they are also the means by which Moore's and Ennis's "themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides) reach audiences who would never read political theory. HBO's *Watchmen* placed "a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists" (Lecture 17 slides) in primetime corporate distribution. The MCU's gestures toward more "liberal / new masculine values" (Captain America's support group, in the lecture's example) are obviously partial and obviously commercial, but they are not nothing. The slides flag this as "Hegemony in practice…" (Lecture 17 slides), and it is hegemonic struggle as practice, where the bloc accommodates and is partially shifted.

Third, hegemony today operates infrastructurally as well as ideologically. The platforms on which subcultures and fandoms now exist are themselves part of the hegemonic apparatus: Apple, Facebook and Google hold "virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Counter-hegemonic subcultures using these platforms use infrastructure controlled by the capital they oppose. This is a deeper commodification than t-shirts: it is the commodification of subcultural space itself. Yet platforms remain sites where "counter-hegemonic readings (negotiated / oppositional)" (Lecture 17 slides) emerge. Platform commodification is real but not totalising.

The Gramscian conclusion is therefore not that counter-hegemony fails the moment it is commodified, but that counter-hegemonic struggle is permanent. There is no purity outside commodity capitalism in which authentic resistance lives; there is only continual struggle within and against commodified forms. The question is not whether a movement has been commodified (almost everything in capitalist society has been or will be) but whether, within and against commodification, it continues to do counter-hegemonic articulation work. Some do; some don't. The Gramscian framework gives us the tools to tell which is which.

### Essay M19. Is Gramsci's framework still useful for analysing digital media, or does the algorithmic platform require a different theory of power?

Digital platforms differ from broadcast media in important ways: they operate through algorithmic personalisation rather than mass address, monetise behavioural data rather than audience attention, and function as infrastructures of everyday life rather than discrete content channels. Gramsci's framework remains substantially useful for digital media (perhaps more useful than for broadcast) but requires the update Williams and Gilbert provide in *Hegemony Now*: a theory of passive consent, emphasis on material interests, and platforms as infrastructural hegemony alongside the ideological and cultural.

The lecture is honest about the question. "Gramsci remains relevant for analysing digital media, platforms, and culture wars, where power operates through visibility, algorithms, and moral framing rather than force. Critiques argue that Gramscian analysis can overemphasise culture at the expense of material structures or treat hegemony as too totalising. Nonetheless, his framework remains central for understanding media, consent, and cultural power" (Lecture 17 slides). The "remains central" claim is the one I want to defend.

What in Gramsci still works? Five things. First, the claim that power in advanced capitalism operates through consent in civil society rather than coercion holds entirely. Big Tech platforms are not coercive apparatuses; they are sites of civil society where consent is manufactured. "Civil society includes media, education, religion, family, and culture, and operates through persuasion and consent" (Lecture 17 slides). The platform belongs squarely in this register.

Second, common sense maps directly onto algorithmic culture. Algorithms do not impose new beliefs; they amplify, sort and recombine already-circulating common sense. "Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides) is even more apt for algorithmic curation than for broadcast.

Third, the organic-intellectual concept extends to digital media. Williams and Gilbert identify "internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6) as a central site where the bloc's entrepreneurial common sense is reproduced. Influencers, podcasters such as Joe Rogan, the manosphere around figures like Andrew Tate, and creators of all kinds are organic intellectuals of identifiable constituencies. The counter-hegemonic example of Alan Moore and Garth Ennis as working-class "organic intellectuals" (Lecture 17 slides) extends naturally to contemporary counter-platform-creators.

Fourth, encoding/decoding is more applicable in an environment of fragmented audiences and active production. Decoding now includes commenting, remixing, sharing: audiences not only make meaning but redistribute it. Hall's original three-position scheme (dominant, negotiated, oppositional) was already designed for "polysemic" texts whose preferred reading "carries the institutional/political/ideological order imprinted in them" (Hall, 1980: 134); platform circulation amplifies the negotiated and oppositional channels without dissolving the preferred reading. "Counter-hegemonic readings (negotiated / oppositional)" (Lecture 17 slides) become counter-hegemonic re-encoding.

Fifth, the war of position is exactly the right strategic framework. The long struggle to shape platform regulation, alternative platforms, public-service digital infrastructure, and cultural common sense around digital life is the actual terrain.

What needs updating? Williams and Gilbert's three points. First, *passive consent*. Digital platforms exemplify hegemony that does not require active assent. The dominant structure of feeling is "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5).

Second, *material interests*. Identity-focused readings of platform politics miss that the project is centrally about who gets paid. "What holds together political collectives … is not primarily a shared 'identity' so much as a common set of material interests" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). The extraction of attention-as-data is a material interest, not a cultural style.

Third, *platforms as infrastructural hegemony*. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power … Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The platform shapes what can be said, by whom, with what reach, under what data extraction. This is not just ideological work; it is infrastructural work, requiring its own vocabulary alongside Gramsci's older categories.

So: Gramsci's framework remains uniquely useful for digital media, but it needs the updates *Hegemony Now* supplies. A different theory of power is not required; an extended Gramscian theory is. The crisis of neoliberalism, platform capitalism and the "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) are conditions for which Gramsci was writing, made strange by a century of capitalist mutation.
