# L17. Gramsci and the Turn to Hegemony

---

## §1. Lecture Overview & Core Argument

This lecture rebuilds the conceptual armoury of the module by turning to Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937), the Italian Marxist whose *Prison Notebooks* (1929–35) reframed the central problem of how ruling groups maintain power in capitalist societies. Where the Frankfurt School treats culture as a near-totalising "culture industry" of manipulation from above, and where structuralism treats popular culture as an "ideological machine" that dictates thoughts "with the same law-like regularity as, in Saussure's conspectus … the system of *langue* dictated the events of *parole*" (Bennett, 1986: xii), Gramsci opens a third way. Bennett's foundational claim (and the organising claim of this lecture) is that the turn to Gramsci displaces the deadlocked polarity between "structuralism and culturalism" by viewing popular culture "as a force field of relations shaped, precisely, by these contradictory pressures and tendencies" (Bennett, 1986: xiii).

The lecture's core argument: power in advanced capitalist societies is maintained less through coercion than through hegemony, the cultural and moral leadership exercised over civil society. As Gramsci puts it, "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'" (Gramsci, 1971: 57–8). The slides distil this with characteristic clarity: "Power = coercion + hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides). Hegemony is therefore not a synonym for dominant ideology. The dominant ideology thesis is "one of exclusion", imposing a monolithic ruling worldview "as an alien force, on the subordinate classes" (Bennett, 1986: xiii). Hegemony, by contrast, "involves processes of inclusion": it is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides), generated as much from below as from above.

Five conceptual moves frame the lecture. (1) The shift from coercion to consent: "the power of the ruling groups and the reproduction of the status quo are maintained less through the State's direct exercise of force than through the management of consent and through the exercise of moral and intellectual authority" (Gramsci, 1971, slide quote). (2) The redefinition of the state into political society (coercion: law, police, courts, military) plus civil society (consent: media, education, religion, family, culture), Gramsci's "integral state." (3) The reconceptualisation of culture: media, schools, churches and families are not afterthoughts to economic struggle; they are the very terrain on which struggle is fought. As Bennett insists, hegemony enables analysis of popular culture "without adopting a position that is either opposed to it or uncritically for it" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). (4) The shift in revolutionary strategy from the war of manoeuvre ("direct revolutionary confrontation", Lecture 17 slides, suited to societies where civil society is thin and the state is the whole field) to the war of position, the long, sustained ideological struggle within civil society that advanced capitalist societies require. (5) The reframing of intellectuals: every social group produces its own organic intellectuals, while traditional intellectuals merely *appear* neutral.

This Gramscian turn is the hinge between the modernist canon (Marx, Frankfurt School, structuralism) and the cultural-studies tradition (Hall, Hebdige, the CCCS). As Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now* demonstrates, it remains the indispensable framework for analysing twenty-first-century power, from the techno-financial bloc that "got the world that they wanted" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3) to the "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), platform capitalism, and the counter-hegemonic possibilities of contemporary movements. My argument throughout these essays will be that Gramsci's framework remains uniquely able to register both the stability and the fragility of capitalist power because it treats common sense as a contradictory, contested terrain, never a finished achievement and never irredeemable.

## §2. Key Concepts (Named, Defined, Located)

- **Hegemony**: not dominant ideology, but the process by which a leading social group secures cultural and moral leadership and the active consent of subaltern groups. "Hegemony involves the more or less active consent of large sections of the population … a process of management and administration" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **The integral state**: state = political society + civil society. Political society (coercion: law, police, courts, military). Civil society (consent: media, education, religion, family, culture). "Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Civil society (Gramsci sense)**: the "social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State" (Lecture 17 slides), where "ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together."

- **Common sense**: the everyday, taken-for-granted, often contradictory beliefs through which people understand the world; "historically produced … and shaped by dominant groups" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Good sense**: the critical, reflective kernels embedded *within* common sense that can be mobilised against it. "Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Organic intellectuals**: emerge from and articulate the interests of particular social groups (journalists, editors, cultural producers, podcasters, comic-book writers). Counter-hegemonic example: Alan Moore, Garth Ennis as working-class organic intellectuals re-writing the superhero genre.

- **Traditional intellectuals**: present themselves as neutral, independent, free-floating (priests, professors, judges) but in fact serve historically constituted blocs.

- **War of manoeuvre**: "direct revolutionary confrontation" (Lecture 17 slides), viable where civil society is thin and the state is the whole field.

- **War of position**: long-term struggle within civil society to reshape "culture, values, and common sense" (Lecture 17 slides), "particularly through media, education, and popular culture."

- **The historical bloc**: alliance of class fractions, ideological positions and institutional forces that sustain a given hegemonic project.

- **Passive revolution**: change from above that absorbs and neutralises pressure from below; a hegemonic move that pre-empts and incorporates challenges rather than confronting them.

- **Popular-democratic struggle**: struggles articulated around "the people" vs "the power bloc" rather than narrowly class-vs-class.

- **Articulation**: cultural elements have no fixed class-belongingness; they are "movable": "a practice which is articulated to bourgeois values today may be disconnected from those values and connected to socialist ones tomorrow" (Bennett, 1986: xvi).

- **Counter-hegemony**: organised cultural, ideological and political resistance that contests dominant common sense, visible in subcultures (Hebdige), fandoms (Jenkins's "textual poachers"), oppositional readings (Hall) and re-articulations of popular forms.

- **Grammar of race (Hall)**: in 'The Whites of Their Eyes' (1981) Hall shows how "racial (white / colonial) hegemony is encoded in media representations" and "can be decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides), through a deeper syntactic structure of representations, framings and narrative roles rather than explicit slurs.

- **Mugging panic (Hall et al., *Policing the Crisis*, 1978)**: Hall and CCCS colleagues show how "capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging') but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides). Built around a 1972 Birmingham street robbery and the racialised media-police-court ecology that followed.

- **Encoding/decoding (Hall, 1973)**: media texts are encoded with hegemonic preferred meanings but can be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways, the moment counter-hegemony enters reception.

- **Structure of feeling** (Williams, via *Hegemony Now*): the affective texture through which "passive consent" is secured, a "cynical resignation … with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5).

## §3. Key Notes from the Presentation (Slide by Slide)

- **Slide 1. Title.** SC301, Lecture 17: The turn to Gramsci and Hegemony. Frames the lecture's pivot from earlier ideology-as-imposition models to hegemony as negotiated leadership.

- **Slide 2. Gramsci's life.** Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937), Italian Marxist writing in early 20th-century Europe, marked by mass politics, failed socialist revolutions in Western Europe, and the rise of fascism. His central problem: why capitalist societies secured popular stability despite deep inequality. He wrote most of his theory in prison under Mussolini, producing the *Prison Notebooks*, which focus on culture, ideology, and power rather than economic determinism alone.

- **Slide 3. Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937) / *Prison Notebooks* (1929–35).** Pull-quote: "The power of the ruling groups and the reproduction of the status quo are maintained less through the State's direct exercise of force than through the management of consent and through the exercise of moral and intellectual authority."

- **Slide 4. Hegemony and civil society / Hegemony.** Hegemony refers to the way a dominant social group maintains power primarily through consent rather than coercion. For Gramsci, ruling groups succeed when their values, beliefs, and interests are accepted as common sense by society as a whole. Hegemony is cultural and moral leadership, not just political control, and it must be continuously produced and renewed.

- **Slide 5. General Idea of Hegemony.** Long Gramsci quotation: "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'. A social group dominates antagonistic groups … perhaps even by armed force, it leads kindred and allied groups. A social group can, and indeed must, exercise 'leadership' before winning government power … It subsequently becomes dominant when it exercises power, but even if it holds it firmly in its grasp, it must continue to 'lead' as well" (Gramsci, 1971: 57–8).

- **Slide 6. Hegemony and Consent.** Bullet checklist: hegemony involves "more or less active consent" of large sections of the population; it is a process of management and administration, not an ideology; it is "not something imposed on society from above so much as generated from below"; the dominant ideology thesis is one of exclusion, hegemony involves processes of inclusion; "Hegemony = a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites"; "Power = coercion + hegemony".

- **Slide 7. Hegemony and the Media / Are the Media the Principal Instruments of Hegemony?** Marx quote on the class that owns material production controlling mental production. "The media mobilise and apply frameworks of thought, ideologies if you like, which are largely generated and maintained outside them." The "means of mental production" are far wider than the national media. "The nuts and bolts of social knowledge are put together and adjusted by people in ordinary practical activities, or what Gramsci called 'civil society'."

- **Slide 8. Political society and civil society.** Gramsci distinguishes political society (state, law, police, courts, military, operating mainly through coercion) from civil society (media, education, religion, family, culture, operating through persuasion and consent). Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony.

- **Slide 9. Hegemony and Civil Society.** "Civil society is where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests." "Civil society is the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together." "Civil society is the social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State."

- **Slide 10. Hegemony as a Political Process of Social Management and Administration.** "All the public institutions of civil society are engaged in developing and managing the part of world with which they're concerned." The social group who carry out these activities are organic intellectuals. Unlike the dominant ideology thesis, hegemony describes a complex process of codification and management, not a system of ideas. Hegemony "treats ideas as the codification of practical experience."

- **Slide 11. Counter-hegemony and organic intellectuals.** Section title slide introducing the second half of the lecture.

- **Slide 12. Common Sense and Good Sense.** Common sense is the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world: historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups. Good sense refers to the critical and reflective elements within common sense that can challenge dominant ideas. Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense.

- **Slide 13. Intellectuals and Cultural Power.** Gramsci defines intellectuals broadly as those who organise ideas and meaning in society. Traditional intellectuals see themselves as neutral or independent. Organic intellectuals emerge from specific social groups and articulate their interests. Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms.

- **Slide 14. Media as a Site of Hegemonic Struggle.** From a Gramscian perspective, media does not simply transmit information; it constructs reality through framing, representation, and repetition. Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits.

- **Slide 15. War of Position.** A war of position refers to a long-term struggle within civil society to reshape culture, values, and common sense. Unlike a war of manoeuvre (direct revolutionary confrontation), Gramsci argues that social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture.

- **Slide 16. Hall, 'Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse' (1973): hegemony and counter-hegemony.** Diagrammatic slide: production encodes preferred hegemonic meanings; visual/aural form mediates; decoding can be dominant, negotiated or oppositional, leaving possibility of counter-hegemonic representations and counter-hegemonic readings.

- **Slide 17. Hall and Gramsci.** Hall extends Gramsci's concept of hegemony to race and other categorisations. In 'The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media' (1981) he employs the term 'grammar of race' to show how racial (white / colonial) hegemony is encoded in media representations, and can be decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways. In *Policing the Crisis* he shows how capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging'), but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate.

- **Slide 18. Examples of counter-hegemony / Media, hegemony and counter-hegemony.** Stuart Hall incorporated Gramscian ideas to show how hegemony was encoded in media-messages, but also how counter-hegemony could inform oppositional readings in decoding, and how sub-cultures could act counter-hegemonically. "The study of media in order to assess the impact of ideology was largely abandoned in the 1980s. This was a legacy of the Frankfurt School but also post-modernism." Scholars such as John Fiske argued that audiences were so empowered that ideology and effect had become irrelevant. For Fiske, the role of media consumption in the production of personal and group identities was most important. Other scholars have disregarded the encoding component of Hall's model, focusing only on the reception of media content.

- **Slide 19. Subcultures as counter-hegemonic.** Subculture is a minority culture within a primary culture that goes against the accepted norms and habits of the majority society. Often youth subcultures are closely associated with certain media. According to Hebdige, subculture is "consumers acting against the expectation of passivity." They resist cooption into society through ritual and style. Subcultures can be counter-hegemonic. Subcultures use media objects as part of this construction. However, this may result in the generation of new commodities and industries, eventually coopting the subculture into the majority capitalist culture. Market research firms engage in trend spotting, selling their insights to the market and accelerating this process.

- **Slide 20. Fandom as counter-hegemonic.** Fandom is similar to subculture, though much more focused around one particular media. Members of fandoms set themselves apart from "casual" consumers through their devotion and knowledge. Tulloch describes them as a "powerless elite": distinguished by knowledge of and engagement with the media they consume, but often powerless to influence its content. Jenkins describes fandoms as "textual poachers": they claim back power by appropriating the characters and worlds of media and creating their own interpretations, storylines and artefacts. This can act to resist hegemonic interpretations. They trespass on the grounds of the powerful content producers. Fandoms are indicative of many changes in cultural consumption that have come hand in hand with New Media.

- **Slide 21. Contemporary Relevance and Critique.** Gramsci remains relevant for analysing digital media, platforms, and culture wars, where power operates through visibility, algorithms, and moral framing rather than force. Critiques argue that Gramscian analysis can overemphasise culture at the expense of material structures or treat hegemony as too totalising. Nonetheless, his framework remains central for understanding media, consent, and cultural power.

- **Slide 22. SC301 / Class 16: The turn to Gramsci and Hegemony.** Second-half title slide flagging the superhero case study.

- **Slide 23. Superheroes: hegemony (see Engstrom and Beliveau: *Gramsci and Media Literacy*).** Superheroes, from Superman onwards, have been used to portray ideas about freedom, race, and masculinity. More recently there have been attempts to portray superheroes as embodying more liberal / new masculine values (in the MCU, for example Captain America runs a support group). "Hegemony in practice…"

- **Slide 24. Superheroes: counter-hegemony (see Engstrom and Beliveau: *Gramsci and Media Literacy*).** "'Organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics such as Alan Moore (*Watchmen*) and Garth Ennis (*The Boys*) have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism."

- **Slide 25. Film / television adaptations.** Film and television adaptations have introduced audiences to these counter-hegemonic representations and even subverted the original comics to allow for counter-hegemony. For example, in *The Boys* (TV) subverting the myth of heroism, or in *Watchmen* (TV) whose main character is a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists.

- **Slide 26. Essential reading.** Closing reading-list slide pointing toward Bennett's 'Popular culture and the turn to Gramsci' and adjacent texts.

## §4. Worked Examples (Concrete, Datable)

- ***Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978)**: a 1972 Birmingham street robbery becomes the seed of a national moral panic about "mugging" that, through the joint work of police press briefings, court reporting and tabloid framing, displaces the structural crisis of 1970s British capitalism onto a racialised criminal figure. The lecture's paradigm of "capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging'), but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Hall, 'The Whites of Their Eyes' (1981)**: the "grammar of race" encoded in media representations, working through framing and narrative role rather than explicit slur. Hall reads *Love Thy Neighbour*, a 1970s ITV sitcom built around a white working-class couple (Eddie and Joan Booth) in Twickenham and their Black neighbours (Bill and Barbie Reynolds), as a text in which racial common sense is reproduced and held up for ambivalent decoding at once.

- **Encoding/decoding (Hall, 1973)**: the diagrammatic slide in the lecture maps the trajectory from production-encoding of preferred hegemonic meanings, through visual/aural form, to reception in dominant, negotiated or oppositional decodings, leaving the door open to "counter-hegemonic representations" and "counter-hegemonic readings" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **The Wall Street-Silicon Valley historical bloc (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "the people who got the world that they wanted were the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and those sections of finance capital closely aligned with them" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). The combination of "social liberalisation, anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets and digitisation of media and information" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3) reads as the platform of this bloc.

- **The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: the neoliberal hegemony "fully established itself during the administrations of Bill Clinton and Tony Blair in the 1990s" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4) and held until 2016, a paradigm of how a hegemonic settlement secures cultural stasis even amid intensive technological innovation.

- **2016 as conjunctural opening (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "From the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, to the UK's vote to leave the European Union" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1), running into COVID-19 and accelerating climate collapse, marks "a moment of grand realignment … a rare instant where more or less anything could be possible" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). The contemporary "interregnum" of the Gramscian epigraph.

- **Apple, Facebook, Google as platform monopolies (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "the methods that digital technology corporations such as Apple, Facebook and Google used to establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). "Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5).

- **The "structure of feeling" of late neoliberalism (Williams & Gilbert via R. Williams)**: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The textbook of passive consent.

- **Reality television and internet influencer culture (Williams & Gilbert, 2022)**: "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Entrepreneurial common sense produced through everyday entertainment.

- **MCU's Captain America support group**: "More recently there have been attempts to portray superheroes as embodying more liberal / new masculine values (in the MCU, for example Captain America runs a support group). Hegemony in practice…" (Lecture 17 slides). The hegemonic bloc absorbing partial recognition of progressive masculine vulnerability.

- **Alan Moore's *Watchmen*, Garth Ennis's *The Boys***: "'Organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics … have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **TV adaptations as counter-hegemonic re-encoding**: *The Boys* (TV) "subverting the myth of heroism"; *Watchmen* (TV) whose "main character is a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists" (Lecture 17 slides).

- **Civil-society everyday life**: "Civil society is where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests" (Lecture 17 slides). The Millennium street party is the slide's chosen image of the ordinary texture in which hegemony is reproduced and contested.

## §5. Critiques & Counter-Arguments

**Class-essentialism (residual)**: Critics argue Gramsci, despite his sophistication, still tethers cultural analysis to "the antagonistic relations between the bourgeoisie and the working class as the two fundamental classes of capitalist society" (Bennett, 1986: xiv). Bennett notes that the Gramscian response, articulation theory, "enables due account to be taken of the relative separation of different regions of cultural struggle (class, race, gender) as well as of the complex and changing ways in which these may be overlapped on to one another" (Bennett, 1986: xvi), but the post-Marxist drift toward identity-as-primary-political-object is itself critiqued by Williams and Gilbert as having been "analytically and strategically disastrous" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6).

**Over-totalisation**: the lecture itself notes "Gramscian analysis can overemphasise culture at the expense of material structures or treat hegemony as too totalising" (Lecture 17 slides). Bennett concedes the framework "is liable to the criticism that it is too accommodating and expansive a framework, over-totalising in its analytical claims and ambit" (Bennett, 1986: xvii).

**Fiske and the populist drift**: by the 1980s "scholars such as John Fiske argued that audiences were so empowered that ideology and effect had become irrelevant" (Lecture 17 slides); celebrating resistance everywhere risks dissolving the very concept of hegemony.

**The Foucauldian alternative**: Foucault disperses power across capillaries; Gramsci's framework, by contrast, "served as an important check on the Foucauldian tendency to view power and the struggle against it as equally diffuse and unrelated" (Bennett, 1986: xvi).

**Abandonment in the 1980s–90s**: "The study of media in order to assess the impact of ideology was largely abandoned in the 1980s. This was a legacy of the Frankfurt School but also post-modernism" (Lecture 17 slides). *Hegemony Now* is partly a recovery operation.

**Subculture co-optation**: Hebdige already anticipated that subcultural resistance "may result in the generation of new commodities and industries, eventually coopting the subculture into the majority capitalist culture" (Lecture 17 slides). "Market research firms engage in trend spotting, selling their insights to the market and accelerating this process" (Lecture 17 slides).

**Williams & Gilbert's update**: traditional Gramscian theory assumes "majority active consent." They argue most actual consent is passive, "cynical resignation … combined with … ineffectual critique" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Hegemony now operates through platforms, infrastructures and structures of feeling, not just through active ideological assent.

**Identity vs interests**: *Hegemony Now* warns that the post-Marxist celebration of identity "always risks complicity with a liberal and idealist conception of politics" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Hegemony must be re-grounded in material interests.

### Essay 1. "Power = coercion + hegemony." Critically discuss with reference to Gramsci.

The slide equation "Power = coercion + hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides) compresses the central theoretical revolution of the *Prison Notebooks*. I read it not as an additive equation but as a redistribution: Gramsci is arguing that the visible apparatus of force (what he calls political society) is the smaller, less interesting part of the story, and that the larger work of reproducing capitalist order happens through hegemony, the cultural and moral leadership exercised through civil society.

The starting puzzle is empirical. Gramsci, writing in a Fascist prison after the catastrophic defeat of the Italian workers' movement, asked why Western European capitalism had not collapsed under its own contradictions. The classical Marxist account (base determines superstructure, immiseration produces revolution) could not explain the resilience of bourgeois rule, the consent of the working class to its own subordination, or the rise of fascism with mass support. Something else was holding the system together. Gramsci named that something hegemony: "the supremacy of a social group manifests itself in two ways, as 'domination' and as 'intellectual and moral leadership'" (Gramsci, 1971: 57–8). The ruling class dominates by force when necessary, but it *leads* by consent, and it must, indeed, "exercise 'leadership' before winning government power" (1971: 57).

So coercion is not abolished. The police, the army, the courts remain: they are political society. But in mature capitalist societies they are activated as a last resort. The everyday work of reproducing the social order is done elsewhere: in schools, churches, families, newspapers, broadcast media, advertising and now algorithmic platforms. As the lecture puts it, "the power of the ruling groups and the reproduction of the status quo are maintained less through the State's direct exercise of force than through the management of consent and through the exercise of moral and intellectual authority" (Gramsci, 1971, slide quotation).

What does this redistribution mean analytically? Three things. First, it dissolves the dominant ideology thesis. Hegemony is not a single coherent worldview imposed top-down on a passive population. That account is "one of exclusion" (Lecture 17 slides). Hegemony is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides), absorbing and partially satisfying subaltern demands in order to neutralise them. Welfare states, anti-discrimination law, even certain forms of identity recognition are not capitulations of hegemonic power; they are exactly how it works.

Second, it makes consent the central problem. "More or less active consent of large sections of the population" (Lecture 17 slides) is a continuum from full endorsement to cynical resignation. Williams and Gilbert call the lower end of this continuum passive consent: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4) was the high water mark of exactly this passive-consent settlement.

Third, the equation politicises culture. If power is largely cultural, then culture is largely political. The lecture's example is the diet of "entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These are not entertainment plus instruction; they are the daily production of hegemonic common sense. Conversely, Alan Moore and Garth Ennis "reinventing the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides), and Hall's counter-hegemonic readings of media racial grammar, are interventions on the same terrain.

A critical caveat. The equation can over-rotate towards culture and underweight coercion. Neoliberalism "in practice has always been enabled and supported by authoritarian, racist, and outright antidemocratic practices of government" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Coercion *plus* hegemony, then, with the balance shifting depending on the conjuncture: in crisis, coercion comes forward; in stability, hegemony does the quiet work. Hall's *Policing the Crisis* (1978) is the lecture's paradigm of how the two interlock, a racialised moral panic about "mugging" licensing the intensification of police powers while reorganising popular common sense (Lecture 17 slides). Gramsci's contribution was to show that the cultural and the coercive are mutually constitutive, and that the cultural is the more effective.

### Essay 2. Why did the turn to Gramsci displace both Frankfurt School pessimism and structuralist analysis of popular culture? Answer with reference to Bennett.

Tony Bennett's introduction to *Popular Culture and Social Relations* is the clearest mapping of why, in the late 1970s and early 1980s, cultural studies turned away from both its Frankfurt School and structuralist inheritances and reorganised itself around Antonio Gramsci. I argue that the Gramscian turn was not a refinement of either tradition but a rejection of a shared assumption that both had inherited from classical Marxism: that culture was essentially a vehicle for a monolithic dominant ideology imposed from without.

Bennett's diagnostic is precise. "Politically speaking, both the structuralist and culturalist paradigms subscribe to a rather similar conception of the structure and organisation of the cultural and ideological spheres … both paradigms regard the sphere of cultural and ideological practices as being governed by a dominant ideology, essentially and monolithically bourgeois in its characteristics, which, albeit with varying degrees of success, is imposed from without, as an alien force, on the subordinate classes" (Bennett, 1986: xiii). On this reading, Frankfurt's culture industry and Saussurean structuralism are political twins. The former condemns popular culture as the deformation of the masses; the latter dissects it as the mechanical reproduction of bourgeois codes. Both leave the analyst standing outside, exposing the obfuscating mechanism.

The alternative (culturalism, the home-grown British tradition of Hoggart, Williams and Thompson) fell into the opposite trap. It was "uncritically romantic in its celebration of popular culture as expressing the authentic interests and values of subordinate social groups and classes" (Bennett, 1986: xii). On this reading popular culture is the people's voice, awaiting only amplification. Both positions, Bennett notes, share "a conception of the cultural and ideological field as being divided between two opposing cultural and ideological camps, bourgeois and working class, locked in a zero-sum game in which one side gains only at the expense of the other" (Bennett, 1986: xiii).

Gramsci's hegemony cuts this knot. For Gramsci, "the cultural and ideological relations between ruling and subordinate classes in capitalist societies consist less in the *domination* of the latter by the former than in the struggle for *hegemony*, that is, for moral, cultural, intellectual and, thereby, political leadership over the whole of society" (Bennett, 1986: xiv). Hegemony is not imposition; it is negotiation. The bourgeoisie becomes hegemonic "only to the degree that bourgeois ideology is able to accommodate, to find some space for, opposing class cultures and values. A bourgeois hegemony is secured not via the obliteration of working class culture, but via its *articulation to* bourgeois culture and ideology so that, in being associated with and expressed in the forms of the latter, its political affiliations are altered in the process" (Bennett, 1986: xiv–xv).

This single move transforms three things at once. First, it dissolves the analytical paralysis: the analyst no longer has to choose between condemning popular culture as dupery or celebrating it as authentic resistance. Popular culture becomes "an area of negotiation between the two within which … dominant, subordinate and oppositional cultural and ideological values and elements are 'mixed' in different permutations" (Bennett, 1986: xv). Reality TV is neither pure ideology nor pure people's voice; it is a contested field.

Second, it makes cultural practices *movable*. "The political and ideological articulations of cultural practices are *movable*: a practice which is articulated to bourgeois values today may be disconnected from those values and connected to socialist ones tomorrow" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). Nationalism, for instance, has no fixed class belonging; it can be articulated to fascism or to socialism depending on the conjuncture. This opens popular culture "as one of enormous political possibilities" (Bennett, 1986: xvi).

Third, it allows analysis of race, gender and nation alongside class without subsuming them. "The Gramscian critique of class essentialist conceptions of culture and ideology … enables due account to be taken of the relative separation of different regions of cultural struggle (class, race, gender) as well as of the complex and changing ways in which these may be overlapped on to one another" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). This is precisely what Hall does when he extends Gramsci to the "grammar of race" in *Policing the Crisis* and 'The Whites of Their Eyes'.

Bennett concedes the framework's risk: "it is liable to the criticism that it is too accommodating and expansive a framework, over-totalising in its analytical claims and ambit" (Bennett, 1986: xvii). But against the Frankfurt School's gloom and structuralism's textual mechanism, Gramsci gives popular-culture analysis its theoretical maturity: the ability to take popular culture seriously as a site of struggle rather than as either symptom or salvation.

### Essay 3. What does Gramsci mean by civil society, and why does he treat it as the principal terrain of struggle in advanced capitalist societies?

Gramsci's distinction between political society and civil society is the structural backbone of his theory of hegemony, and it is what makes his analysis applicable to advanced capitalist societies where straightforward seizure of state power has proven impossible. I argue that civil society for Gramsci is not, as in liberal theory, the realm of voluntary association set against the state, but rather the institutional terrain where consent to ruling-class leadership is manufactured, contested and renewed. Recognising this redirects revolutionary strategy from frontal assault to long, sustained cultural work.

The lecture's slide diagram captures the basic distinction. "Political society includes the state, law, police, courts, and military, and operates mainly through coercion" while "Civil society includes media, education, religion, family, and culture, and operates through persuasion and consent" (Lecture 17 slides). Together they form what Gramsci calls the integral state. Crucially, "Media is located primarily in civil society and plays a key role in sustaining hegemony" (Lecture 17 slides). Civil society is not a buffer between citizen and state; it is the *medium* through which the state, broadly understood, exists.

What kind of space is it? The lecture is precise: "Civil society is the social space which both distances us from and connects us to the State. Civil society is the key place where ideas and practical behaviours are directly knitted together. Civil society is where we live: it's where we behave as members of families and of local communities, where we work and relax, where we engage in social celebrations like a Millennium street party or social protests" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the texture of everyday life, and that is what makes it politically decisive. As Gramsci put it via Marx, "the nuts and bolts of social knowledge are put together and adjusted by people in ordinary practical activities, or what Gramsci called 'civil society'" (Lecture 17 slides).

Why is this the principal terrain in advanced capitalism? Gramsci's answer turns on a contrast between societies where civil society is thin (the state is almost everything, and a frontal assault on the state apparatus could topple the regime) and societies where civil society is densely institutionalised. In the latter, churches, parties, unions, newspapers, schools and families absorb shocks and reproduce consent independently of any direct state action. A frontal political assault would leave bourgeois hegemony intact because hegemony lives in the press, the school, the church and the family dinner table. Hence the strategic implication: "social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the war of position.

Civil society as the Gramscian terrain has three crucial features. First, it operates through inclusion rather than exclusion. Unlike the dominant ideology thesis where bourgeois culture imposes itself by displacing working-class culture, hegemony in civil society works by absorbing dissident demands and re-articulating them. The post-war welfare state did not destroy working-class consciousness; it accommodated it within a hegemonic settlement that combined social-democratic concessions with capitalist property relations.

Second, civil society is administrative rather than ideological in the narrow sense. "All the public institutions of civil society are engaged in developing and managing the part of world with which they're concerned" (Lecture 17 slides). The BBC, the NHS, schools, professional bodies: these are not propaganda machines; they are managers of consent, embedding particular common-sense framings in routine practice. Hegemony "treats ideas as the codification of practical experience" (Lecture 17 slides).

Third, civil society is the home of organic intellectuals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) who, in the lecture's formulation, "can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). They articulate the interests of particular blocs while presenting themselves as neutral. Hall's *Policing the Crisis* shows the institutional ecology in which this works: police press briefings, court reporting and editorial framings produced the racialised "mugging" panic of 1970s Britain not by individual conspiracy but by the routine cooperation of civil-society sites.

Contemporary illustrations bear this out. Williams and Gilbert track how a particular alliance of class fractions, "the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and those sections of finance capital closely aligned with them" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3), came to lead through civil society long before any specific election was won. Their account of the platform emphasises that "the platform" has become a key institution of contemporary civil society, "instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The platforms of Apple, Facebook and Google, which establish "virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6), are now where common sense is made.

This is why, for Gramsci, the seizure of state power without prior victory in civil society is doomed. A counter-hegemonic bloc must first lead before it can rule. The implication for the left is sobering: there is no shortcut. The war of position is long.

### Essay 4. How does Gramsci's distinction between common sense and good sense help us analyse contemporary media?

Gramsci's distinction between common sense and good sense is, I argue, the single most analytically powerful tool in his conceptual repertoire for analysing contemporary media, more powerful even than hegemony itself, because it opens the contradictory interior of the consenting subject rather than treating consent as a black box.

The lecture's definitions are stark. "Common sense is the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world. It is historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups. Good sense refers to the critical and reflective elements within common sense that can challenge dominant ideas. Hegemonic struggle involves shaping common sense while suppressing or marginalising good sense" (Lecture 17 slides). Two things are crucial here. Common sense is not stupid: it is historically produced, the sediment of past struggles. And good sense is not external to common sense: it lives inside it, available to be activated by the right kind of articulation.

For media analysis this changes everything. Audiences are not dupes carrying false consciousness from which they need to be liberated by enlightened theorists. Nor are they sovereign decoders who pluck whatever meanings they fancy from texts. They are carriers of contradictory common sense, in which hegemonic framings coexist with submerged good-sense kernels, and media work is the daily struggle to amplify the former and bury the latter.

Take the slide's own example: "Media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides). The hegemonic move is precisely to activate a deeply sedimented folk wisdom about personal responsibility (good sense in some contexts: people really do shape their immediate lives) and to splice it to a framing that obscures structural causation, legitimising cuts and inaction. The brilliance of the framing is that it does not contradict ordinary experience; it builds on it. The good-sense kernel that might challenge it (recognition that the crisis is one of capital accumulation, not personal thrift) exists in the same common-sense reservoir, but it requires articulation to surface.

Williams and Gilbert give the contemporary version of this hegemonic work. Neoliberal common sense circulates through "the didactic and symbolic elements of neoliberal ideology … a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These cultural sites do not impose a doctrine; they ventriloquise an existing folk-wisdom about hard work, self-reliance and aspiration, and articulate it to a political programme of "anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). The good-sense kernels (people's real capacities for cooperation, mutual aid, structural critique) are present but unmobilised.

The dominant late-neoliberal "structure of feeling" that Williams and Gilbert anatomise is itself a perfect specimen of good sense suppressed. "A conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5) is good sense that *exists*: most people know the system is unfair. But it goes nowhere because no political force articulates it into action; instead it sits alongside "an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5) as a stable, passive consent. The challenge for any counter-hegemonic project is to take that submerged critique and articulate it into a hegemonic horizon.

The methodological implication for media studies is significant. Texts must be read not for a single ideological message but for the *contradictory* common sense they assemble and the good-sense kernels they suppress or activate. *Policing the Crisis* models this exactly: Hall and his collaborators show how the "mugging" moral panic activates a long-sedimented racial common sense while suppressing the good-sense recognition that the underlying crisis is one of capital accumulation (Lecture 17 slides). 'The Whites of Their Eyes' extends the analysis to sitcom: *Love Thy Neighbour*'s comic juxtaposition of the Booth and Reynolds households trades on a shared common sense about neighbourly racial conflict that can be decoded "in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides), with good-sense recognition of the absurdity of racism running alongside its naturalisation.

Gramsci thus offers media analysis a non-condescending materialism. People are not stupid; their common sense is the sediment of history. The political question is which fragments of that history get articulated, by whom, in whose interest. That is the daily work of hegemonic and counter-hegemonic media.

### Essay 5. "Hegemony is generated from below, not imposed from above." Discuss with concrete examples.

The Gramscian claim that "hegemony is not something imposed on society from above so much as generated from below" (Lecture 17 slides) is the most counter-intuitive and the most powerful element of his framework. I argue that it is correct in two senses (hegemony requires consent from below to be hegemony at all, and the ideological materials of hegemonic projects are themselves drawn from below) but that this should not be confused with the populist claim that hegemonic projects are spontaneous expressions of the people. They are constructed, by organic intellectuals, out of materials already circulating in popular common sense.

The "from below" thesis rests on Gramsci's distinction between hegemony and the dominant ideology thesis. The latter, as Bennett notes, is "one of exclusion (it excludes and suppresses all opposition)" while hegemony "involves processes of inclusion … a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides). Bourgeois hegemony "is secured not via the obliteration of working class culture, but via its *articulation to* bourgeois culture and ideology" (Bennett, 1986: xiv–xv). The hegemonic bloc must offer something (partial recognition, partial concession, partial inclusion) to generate the active consent it requires. That something must come from below.

The paradigmatic case in the L17 materials is the neoliberal hegemony anatomised by Williams and Gilbert. Neoliberalism, they argue, did not impose itself on a recalcitrant population; it constructed a new common sense by articulating elements already circulating in popular life. "Actually existing neoliberalism" works through "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). These norms are folk-wisdoms about aspiration and self-reliance that have been re-articulated to a programme of "anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets and digitisation of media and information" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). Hegemony was generated from below by assembling popular materials, then re-articulating them to the interests of the dominant class fractions. The "long 1990s" of Clinton and Blair (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4) was its high water mark: a settlement that secured "remarkably stable" social and political relations until 2016 (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4).

What 2016 reveals, on Williams and Gilbert's account, is that this generation-from-below is reversible. "From the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, to the UK's vote to leave the European Union" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1) marks a moment in which a "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) begins to articulate the same popular materials into a counter-bloc. The grievances were real and circulating from below; what changed was the political force willing to articulate them. As Williams and Gilbert put it, "what holds together political collectives … is not primarily a shared 'identity' so much as a common set of material interests" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Hegemonic projects work when they crystallise interests felt from below into a programme led from above.

Yet "generated from below" cuts against any spontaneist reading. Hegemonic projects require organic intellectuals to do the articulating work. As the lecture notes, "media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). The didactic and symbolic infrastructure that Williams and Gilbert describe (schools, reality TV, influencer culture, platforms) is precisely the organic-intellectual ecology through which sentiments from below are codified into common sense from above. Generation from below means assembly from below, not creation from below.

There is a counter-hegemonic version of the same logic. Hall's *Policing the Crisis* shows that even within the racialised "mugging" panic of 1970s Britain, "counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest" was operating (Lecture 17 slides), articulating long-sedimented experience into oppositional public forms. The encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1973) generalises the point: "counter-hegemonic readings (negotiated / oppositional)" (Lecture 17 slides) are themselves materials drawn from below, organised by counter-intellectuals into oppositional articulations. These are hegemonic struggles fought from below because the materials of hegemony are always, finally, popular materials.

The Williams and Gilbert update adds a crucial complication. Contemporary hegemony often does not require active consent at all. The dominant structure of feeling is "passive consent": "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency … combined with … ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). This is still generated from below in the sense that the affect comes from below, but it is generated as quiet acquiescence rather than enthusiastic endorsement. Late-neoliberal hegemony does not need to win hearts and minds; it only needs to keep them disengaged.

The strategic implication is that counter-hegemony must do the same kind of articulation work. It must identify good-sense kernels in popular life (solidarity, fairness, recognition of structural injustice) and articulate them into a project that can hegemonise. Without that articulation, popular discontent stays passive, and the right captures the materials that the left fails to mobilise.

### Essay 6. Evaluate the claim that media professionals are organic intellectuals. Use specific contemporary examples.

Gramsci's redefinition of "intellectual" as anyone who organises ideas and meaning in society (as opposed to the educated specialist with a degree and a tweed jacket) opens a productive way of thinking about journalists, editors, podcasters, comic-book writers, influencers and tabloid columnists as functioning organic intellectuals. I argue that the claim is largely correct and analytically indispensable, but that it must be qualified in three ways: by recognising the limits of individual agency within media institutions, by distinguishing types of organic intellectual work, and by acknowledging the rise of platform-mediated organic intellectuals whose relation to traditional class structures is more diffuse.

The lecture's definition is broad. "Gramsci defines intellectuals broadly as those who organise ideas and meaning in society. Traditional intellectuals see themselves as neutral or independent. Organic intellectuals emerge from specific social groups and articulate their interests. Media professionals (journalists, editors, cultural producers) can function as organic intellectuals by shaping narratives, values, and social norms" (Lecture 17 slides). The crucial move is to politicise the apparent neutrality of professional media work. The BBC newsroom's claim to impartiality, the broadsheet leader-writer's air of judicious balance, the academic talking-head's expertise: all these are positions within hegemonic struggle, not above it.

Specific contemporary illustrations bear this out. *Hegemony Now*'s account of "actually existing neoliberalism" identifies the organic-intellectual ecology as "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Reality TV presenters, business-influencer YouTubers, and the magazine columnists of aspiration are doing organic-intellectual work in Gramsci's sense: translating the position of a particular class fraction (the techno-financial bloc) into the idiom of everyday aspiration and self-help, until the bloc's interests appear as universal common sense.

On the counter-hegemonic side, the slides give us Alan Moore and Garth Ennis as "'organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics" who "have reinvented the superhero genre to explore themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides). Moore's *Watchmen* and Ennis's *The Boys*, especially in their TV adaptations, take a hegemonic American myth (the heroic superhero embodying patriotic American power) and subvert it from within: counter-hegemony as cultural work. Sister Night in HBO's *Watchmen*, "a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists" (Lecture 17 slides), performs precisely this kind of counter-hegemonic articulation, while *The Boys* on television enacts the slide's gloss of "subverting the myth of heroism" (Lecture 17 slides).

Hall provides the further model of media as organic-intellectual infrastructure. *Policing the Crisis* shows that the "mugging" panic was not produced by individual racist journalists but by an institutional ecology of police briefings, court reporting routines and editorial framings that produced racialised crime coverage almost automatically (Lecture 17 slides). 'The Whites of Their Eyes' shows how the same kind of organic-intellectual work is carried out in primetime sitcoms: *Love Thy Neighbour*'s scriptwriters, producers and broadcasters codified a "grammar of race" (Lecture 17 slides) that organised popular understanding of the multiracial neighbourhood. Organic intellectuals work *through* institutions, not in spite of them.

The claim must be qualified. First, individual journalists and producers operate within institutional and economic constraints that limit their organic-intellectual agency. The institutional ecology Hall describes in *Policing the Crisis* is itself the organic intellectual; particular reporters are nodes within it. Second, there are different types of organic intellectual work. Gramsci distinguished between technical-economic functions and political-cultural ones. Williams and Gilbert add a third dimension: the platforms themselves. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power … Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). Apple, Facebook and Google "establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6), shaping in advance which organic-intellectual voices can find audiences at all.

Third, the lecture's note that "media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides) is in itself an account of organic-intellectual work without conspiracy: the routine practice of news selection and framing, performed by professionals who often understand themselves as neutral, is the daily reproduction of the bloc's preferred common sense.

The Gramscian framework remains uniquely capable of registering all this. To say a journalist is an organic intellectual is not an accusation but an analytical claim: media work is always already political work, and pretending otherwise is itself a hegemonic move.

### Essay 7. "Common sense is the central battleground of contemporary politics." Discuss with reference to the conjunctural crisis described in Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now*.

The proposition that common sense is the central battleground of contemporary politics is, I think, exactly right, and it is one of the most important practical contributions Gramsci makes to political analysis. I will defend it through the conjunctural analysis offered by Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now*, reading the neoliberal "long 1990s" as the paradigm of common-sense victory, and the post-2016 "interregnum" as the moment of common-sense disturbance.

Common sense, the lecture reminds us, is "the everyday, taken-for-granted set of beliefs through which people understand the world. It is historically produced, often contradictory, and shaped by dominant groups" (Lecture 17 slides). It is not a fixed thing: it is a contested settlement, the precipitate of past hegemonic and counter-hegemonic struggles. To change politics fundamentally is to change common sense. To win an election is one thing; to make a political project unchallengeable is another, and the latter is what hegemonic victory means.

Williams and Gilbert provide the paradigm case for the contemporary moment. The "leading tendencies of global culture" since the late twentieth century, they argue, are the "more or less direct expressions of the interests and values" of the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and the sections of finance capital aligned with them (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). What made this neoliberal hegemony succeed was not that any specific election was won (although Clinton and Blair in the 1990s are named as its consolidating administrations) but that it changed what could be said and thought in everyday life. During the "long 1990s … social and political relations remained remarkably stable" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4): the achieved hegemonic condition where individualist, market-conforming common sense had become difficult to dispute.

How did neoliberal hegemony win the common-sense battle? Not by inventing a new worldview but by articulating elements already circulating in popular life. Williams and Gilbert identify the everyday infrastructure: "a set of entrepreneurial, competitive, individualist norms that are explicitly encouraged across a range of social sites, from schools to reality television shows and internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). The aspirations to upward mobility, the valorisation of self-reliance, the suspicion of state inefficiency: these were good-sense kernels (real responses to real experiences) that were re-articulated into a coherent project. The lecture's gloss is exact: "media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides).

The 2016 conjuncture marks the moment when this settlement begins to crack. "From the election of Donald Trump to the presidency of the United States, to the UK's vote to leave the European Union, and from the global COVID-19 pandemic to the early signs of catastrophic climate collapse" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1), the "rules" that had governed common sense were "rapidly discarded" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). Williams and Gilbert read this not as the death of hegemony but as the emergence of a competing articulation: a "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) that mobilises real grievances and rearticulates them into a counter-bloc. The battle over common sense is what is being fought.

Why is common sense the central battleground? Because elections, policies and institutions follow common sense; they rarely lead it. The Wall Street-Silicon Valley bloc could only secure decades of deregulation, financialisation and platform monopolisation because the common-sense framing of markets as efficient and platforms as neutral had already been built. The lecture's framework gives the analytic backbone: hegemony is "a process of negotiation or compromise on the part of the ruling elites" (Lecture 17 slides) generated as much from below as from above; the work that matters is upstream of policy.

The Williams and Gilbert qualification sharpens the diagnosis. Late-neoliberal common sense is increasingly held passively: "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency … combined with … ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). This is a vulnerable form of hegemony, because it is not enthusiastic. Counter-hegemonic projects have an opening, but they must do the articulation work, identifying good-sense kernels in current common sense and mobilising them. The lecture's models of counter-hegemonic articulation, from Hall's reading of Black resistance within the *Policing the Crisis* conjuncture to Moore's and Ennis's superhero re-encodings, demonstrate that counter-articulation is possible, but it is slow, cumulative work. The successful left projects of the future, on Williams and Gilbert's reading, will be those that win the common-sense battle before they win the electoral one (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7).

### Essay 8. How does Stuart Hall extend Gramsci's framework to race? Answer with reference to *Policing the Crisis* and 'The Whites of Their Eyes'.

Stuart Hall's extension of Gramsci to race is one of the foundational moves of British cultural studies, and it transformed the analysis of media not by abandoning the framework of hegemony but by adding race as a constitutive axis of hegemonic struggle alongside class. I argue that Hall's two key interventions (*Policing the Crisis* and 'The Whites of Their Eyes') together demonstrate how the Gramscian framework can be extended to register racial hegemony without becoming class-essentialist, and how media institutions function as the prime sites where racial common sense is encoded, contested and (sometimes) decoded oppositionally.

The lecture summarises Hall's contribution: "Hall extends Gramsci's concept of hegemony to race and other categorisations. In 'The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media' (1981) he employs the term 'grammar of race' to show how racial (white / colonial) hegemony is encoded in media representations: can be decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways. In 'Policing the Crisis' he shows how capitalist hegemony displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging'), but also how counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides).

*Policing the Crisis* (1978), authored by Stuart Hall and colleagues at the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS), is the masterwork of Gramscian media analysis. As the revision summary puts it, the book tracks "how a relatively limited phenomenon, a moral panic, street robbery, labelled as 'mugging' became a major political and moral issue in 1970s Britain" and how "media, policing, and state power interact to produce ideological responses to wider social and economic crises". The slide's gloss is the operative one: capitalist hegemony "displaces the crisis onto a moral panic ('mugging')" (Lecture 17 slides), reconstructing around a 1972 Birmingham robbery the entire conjuncture of 1970s Britain. The crisis of capital became, in media hands, a crisis of order; the crisis of order became a crisis of race.

This is Gramsci extended to race in three precise ways. First, hegemony is shown to operate by *displacement*: when the contradictions of capital accumulation cannot be resolved economically, they are managed ideologically through the construction of racialised folk-devils. Race becomes a sutured surface across which class contradiction is reorganised. Second, the analysis demonstrates how organic intellectuals of the right (police press officers, court reporters, editorial framings) collaborated, often without conspiracy, in the production of the racialised common sense. Hall shows the *institutional ecology* of moral panic. Third, the slides also flag the other side of the conjuncture: how "counter-hegemonic Black resistance / protest might operate" (Lecture 17 slides). The counter-hegemonic moment is internal to the conjuncture, not external to it.

'The Whites of Their Eyes' (1981) deepens the theoretical apparatus with the concept of the "grammar of race." Hall's claim is that racism in contemporary British media operates not through explicit racial slurs but through a deeper syntactic structure: a "grammar" of representations, framings, juxtapositions, repetitions and narrative roles. This grammar is "encoded in media representations" and can be "decoded in ambivalent and contradictory ways" (Lecture 17 slides). The revision summary records Hall's concrete example: the 1970s ITV sitcom *Love Thy Neighbour*, "based around a white working class couple (Eddie and Joan Booth) living in Twickenham, in the outer London suburbs, and a black couple (Bill and Barbie Reynolds) as their next-door neighbours". The series, on Hall's reading, encodes a racial grammar through casting, dialogue, comic timing and narrative role that constructs Black neighbours as a problem to be managed by white anxiety, even when the surface "lesson" of any given episode appears to chastise overt racism. The preferred encoded meaning is the grammar, not the moral; the explicit denotative content is not racist while the structuring grammar is.

This extends Gramsci because it shows that racial hegemony is not a separate sphere alongside class hegemony but woven into the same media practices. Bennett notes that the Gramscian framework "enables due account to be taken of the relative separation of different regions of cultural struggle (class, race, gender) as well as of the complex and changing ways in which these may be overlapped on to one another" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). Hall is the great theorist of this overlap. Race in Britain is not separable from the class formation of late capitalism; class is not separable from racial categorisation. The hegemonic project must articulate both, and so must its opposition.

The encoding/decoding model gives this its methodological backbone. The 'Encoding/Decoding in the Television Discourse' (1973) model, diagrammed in the lecture slides, maps the trajectory from the production of preferred hegemonic meanings, through the visual/aural form, to the moment of reception, where audiences may read dominantly, negotiate, or oppositionally decode. Counter-hegemony, in Hall's framework, is generated in part at the moment of decoding, but counter-hegemonic decoding requires resources that themselves must be built (alternative media, organised communities, counter-publics).

The contemporary purchase of the framework remains undiminished. Williams and Gilbert's analysis of the rise of a "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) within the crisis of neoliberalism is a project on the same terrain: the displacement of structural-economic contradiction onto racialised and culturalised figures of threat, and the consolidation of new racialised common sense within the platform-mediated civil society. The Gramscian-Hallian framework remains the most powerful tool we have for analysing this work, precisely because it refuses both colour-blind class reductionism and a culturalist abandonment of structural analysis.

### Essay 9. Compare the war of manoeuvre and the war of position. Which better captures contemporary left strategy, and why?

Gramsci's distinction between the war of manoeuvre and the war of position is one of the most strategically consequential in twentieth-century Marxism, and it remains the indispensable starting point for any serious thinking about left politics today. I argue that the war of position is overwhelmingly the right framework for contemporary left strategy, but that pure positional warfare risks complacency in moments of acute conjunctural crisis where decisive interventions are possible. The best contemporary left strategy combines a long-term war of position with readiness for what one might call moments of accelerated articulation.

The lecture defines the terms with characteristic precision. "A war of position refers to a long-term struggle within civil society to reshape culture, values, and common sense. Unlike a war of manoeuvre (direct revolutionary confrontation), Gramsci argues that social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides).

Gramsci's original contrast was historical. The war of manoeuvre, "direct revolutionary confrontation" (Lecture 17 slides), suited societies where the state was almost everything and civil society thin: a frontal political assault could topple the regime. In the societies Gramsci was actually writing about, the advanced capitalist West where he had seen revolutionary attempts fail, civil society was dense. Hegemonic power lived not in the state apparatus alone but in newspapers, churches, schools and families, and a frontal political assault could not displace it. The strategic conclusion was that "social change in advanced capitalist societies requires sustained cultural and ideological struggle, particularly through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). This is the war of position.

For contemporary left strategy, this framework is overwhelmingly correct. There is no single institution whose seizure would reorganise power. Power lives in a distributed civil society and, increasingly, as *Hegemony Now* argues, in the platforms and infrastructures of finance and technology. Williams and Gilbert make this concrete: "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power, and it is through their control of finance and technology platforms that these groups have come to predominate. Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The dominant bloc is the alliance "of finance capital and big tech" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6); the platforms of Apple, Facebook and Google have established "virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Only a long, multi-front war of position has any chance.

What does that war of position look like in practice? Williams and Gilbert sketch the shape: a strategy that is "socialist … populist and democratic" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), one that addresses "what specific class alliances and other types of social coalitions might plausibly have the potential to cohere and challenge both the hegemony of the techno-financial elite and the emerging power of the nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), and one that asks "what types of technical and institutional infrastructures would they need to build in order to realise their political potential" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7). The lecture's framework gives the underlying terrain: sustained struggle "through media, education, and popular culture" (Lecture 17 slides). The neoliberal bloc won its hegemony by precisely this kind of long war of position; the left has no comparable infrastructure.

Yet pure positionalism risks two problems. First, it can become indistinguishable from political withdrawal, the comforting belief that one is "doing the long work" while immediate crises demand decisive action. Second, it can miss moments of organic crisis when "the old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear" (Gramsci, quoted in Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1, opening epigraph), moments when accelerated articulation is possible. Williams and Gilbert open *Hegemony Now* with precisely this Gramscian observation, arguing that since 2016 we have been living through such a moment: "We are in a moment of grand realignment, where different cycles of world history have clicked together to produce a rare instant where more or less anything could be possible" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1).

In such moments, the war of position must intensify rather than wait. The strategic question is what can be articulated *now* into a counter-hegemonic project. As Williams and Gilbert put it, the central concern is whether a coalition can be built that can "supplant the hegemony of technology and finance sectors, and present a credible bulwark against the nationalist authoritarians rising to take their place" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7). The post-2016 conjuncture (Trump, Brexit, COVID-19, climate collapse) is precisely the interregnum the epigraph describes: ground has been opened in which new common sense will be articulated. The strategic question is by whom.

Williams and Gilbert's prescription is precise: "a left political strategy that can productively combine populist and democratic aspects might have the capacity to supplant the hegemony of technology and finance sectors, and present a credible bulwark against the nationalist authoritarians rising to take their place" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7). Populist, because it must articulate popular interests into a hegemonic horizon; democratic, because it must build new practices of democracy throughout civil society.

The war of position, in short, is correct. But it must be a *fighting* position, ready to seize the moments when crisis opens the ground for new common sense to be articulated.

### Essay 10. To what extent does Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now* update Gramsci for the era of platform capitalism?

Williams and Gilbert's *Hegemony Now* presents itself as a recovery and update of Gramscian analysis for the era of platform capitalism, financialisation and the crisis of neoliberalism. I argue that the book makes three substantive updates to the Gramscian framework, the theory of passive consent, the re-grounding of analysis in material interests, and the addition of platforms as a structural mode of hegemony, and that these updates together constitute a significant extension of hegemony theory. Williams and Gilbert themselves frame the move modestly: "we need to update and upgrade Gramsci's account (and those of his most notable successors). This book therefore presents a number of developments on the modern and postmodern theories of hegemony" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). The updates do not abandon Gramsci; they extend him for a conjuncture he could not have imagined.

The book opens with a Gramscian epigraph that frames the whole project: "The old is dying and the new cannot be born; in this interregnum a great variety of morbid symptoms appear" (Gramsci, quoted in Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). The crisis Gramsci was describing was the inter-war crisis of liberal capitalism; the crisis Williams and Gilbert are describing is the crisis of neoliberalism in the 2020s: "the global crisis of neoliberalism … Neoliberalism, which is the political system that has ruled almost the entirety of the planet since the 1990s, is everywhere in decline, if not ruination" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 1). Their diagnostic question is Gramsci's: how does power actually work in such a moment?

The central empirical claim is that the hegemonic bloc of the present is the alliance between Silicon Valley and Wall Street. "The people who got the world that they wanted were the tech entrepreneurs of Silicon Valley and those sections of finance capital closely aligned with them. The precise combination of social liberalisation, anti-egalitarianism, globalisation, deregulation of markets, financialisation of assets and digitisation of media and information that has characterised the leading tendencies of global culture … can be seen as more or less direct expressions of the interests and values of this particular coalition of class fractions" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 3). This identifies, in Gramscian terms, the historical bloc of the contemporary conjuncture.

The first update is the theory of passive consent. Classical Gramscian theory assumes hegemony requires "the more or less active consent of large sections of the population" (Lecture 17 slides). Williams and Gilbert argue this is too strong: "the crude assumption that 'hegemony' can only be achieved through majority active consent to particular sets of governing norms is simply untrue" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 4–5). Most actual consent under late neoliberalism is passive: cynical resignation combined with ineffectual critique. They borrow Raymond Williams's "structure of feeling" to capture this: the dominant late-neoliberal structure of feeling combines "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). This is a vulnerable form of hegemony, but it is hegemony.

The second update is the re-grounding in material interests. Williams and Gilbert push back against post-Marxist culturalism's drift toward identity and recognition: "Contemporary radical theory desperately needs a coherent model of politics as primarily a contest between interests, in order to escape from the debilitating limitations of theoretical models which assume that 'identities' or 'values' are the real objects and subjects of contemporary political struggle … the consequent abandonment of any understanding of politics as centrally concerned with the expression of material interests has been analytically and strategically disastrous" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5–6). The point is not to retreat to class reductionism ("it is true that not all interests can be understood simply or primarily as class interests") but to insist that political collectives are held together by shared material interests rather than identity (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). This is a corrective to certain post-Marxist excesses while preserving the Gramscian insight that hegemonic blocs are alliances of fractions, not pure class essences.

The third update is the addition of platforms and infrastructures as a structural mode of hegemony. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power, and it is through their control of finance and technology platforms that these groups have come to predominate. Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). This is significant. Gramsci theorised hegemony as ideological, cultural, moral and intellectual. Williams and Gilbert add a fourth dimension: infrastructural. Hegemony today is also instantiated in the technical architecture of "digital technology corporations such as Apple, Facebook and Google" which "establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6).

This third update is the most genuinely new. The first two refine Gramscian categories; the third adds a category Gramsci could not have anticipated. Together they constitute a substantial extension. The strategic conclusion is that contemporary counter-hegemony must operate on all three planes: contesting structures of feeling, articulating material interests, and building alternative platforms and infrastructures.

The limits of the update are also worth marking. The book is more diagnostic than prescriptive on the third front: it identifies platforms as the key infrastructural mode of hegemony but is less explicit on what counter-hegemonic platforms would look like. The "future war of position" is sketched as "socialist … populist and democratic" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), with the requirement to "build new practices of democracy throughout society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) and to ask "what types of technical and institutional infrastructures would they need to build in order to realise their political potential" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7), but the institutional vehicles remain underspecified. This is perhaps unavoidable: the situation is still in motion.

Nonetheless, *Hegemony Now* is the most significant update of Gramscian theory in decades, and it confirms rather than refutes the underlying claim that Gramsci's framework remains the most powerful tool for analysing how power works in the early twenty-first century.

### Essay 11. "Subcultures and fandoms are counter-hegemonic only until they are commodified." Discuss.

The claim that subcultures and fandoms are counter-hegemonic only until they are commodified compresses a real tension in cultural studies between celebratory accounts of resistance and pessimistic accounts of co-optation. I argue that the claim is partially correct (commodification does typically dilute counter-hegemonic charge) but that it is too binary. The Gramscian framework allows us to see that counter-hegemonic moments can persist within commodified forms, that the line between resistance and incorporation is not a sharp threshold but a continuum, and that commodification itself can be the medium through which counter-hegemonic ideas reach mass publics they could not otherwise have reached.

The lecture sets out the classical framing. Hebdige's analysis of subcultures presents them as "consumers acting against the expectation of passivity. They resist cooption into society through ritual and style. Subcultures can be counter-hegemonic" (Lecture 17 slides). Subcultures use "media objects as part of this construction. However, this may result in the generation of new commodities and industries, eventually coopting the subculture into the majority capitalist culture. Market research firms engage in trend spotting, selling their insights to the market and accelerating this process" (Lecture 17 slides). The slide's logic is precise: insurgent style is recoded as commodity, the commodity is sold back as fashion, and the subcultural energy is partially neutralised.

Fandom is similar but more focused. Tulloch's "powerless elite", Jenkins's "textual poachers": fans claim back power by appropriating "the characters and worlds of media and create their own interpretations, storylines and artefacts. This can act to resist hegemonic interpretations" (Lecture 17 slides). The slide flags fan-creative practice (re-narration, alternative storylines, derivative artefacts) as the recognisable counter-hegemonic moment.

So the basic empirical observation is correct. Insurgent forms become merchandise; ritual-aesthetic disruption is recoded as style; corporate distribution absorbs critique and resells it. The corporate apparatus has a remarkable capacity to absorb its critics and resell their critique back to them.

But the claim that this absorption *exhausts* the counter-hegemonic charge is too strong, for three reasons.

First, the Gramscian principle of articulation. Cultural elements have no fixed political belonging. As Bennett puts it, "the political and ideological articulations of cultural practices are *movable*: a practice which is articulated to bourgeois values today may be disconnected from those values and connected to socialist ones tomorrow" (Bennett, 1986: xvi). A commodified element can be re-articulated.

Second, commodification can be the means of mass reach. *Watchmen* and *The Boys* are corporate products; they are also the means by which Moore's and Ennis's "themes of corruption, ego cowardice, commodification, fascism, racism and sexism" (Lecture 17 slides) reach audiences who would never read political theory. HBO's *Watchmen* placed "a Black, female superhero (Sister Night) who fights fascists" (Lecture 17 slides) in primetime corporate distribution. The MCU's gestures toward more "liberal / new masculine values" (Captain America's support group, in the lecture's example) are obviously partial and obviously commercial, but they are not nothing. The slides flag this as "Hegemony in practice…" (Lecture 17 slides), and it is hegemonic struggle as practice, where the bloc accommodates and is partially shifted.

Third, the Williams and Gilbert update reminds us that hegemony today operates infrastructurally as well as ideologically. The platforms on which subcultures and fandoms now exist are themselves part of the hegemonic apparatus: Apple, Facebook and Google "establish virtual monopolies both on the distribution of information and on key infrastructures of everyday life, communication, and entertainment" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6). Counter-hegemonic subcultures using these platforms are using infrastructure controlled by the very capital they may oppose. This is a deeper form of commodification than t-shirts: it is the commodification of the very space of subcultural existence. Yet platforms remain, in the slides' encoding/decoding terms, sites where "counter-hegemonic readings (negotiated / oppositional)" (Lecture 17 slides) can still emerge. Platform commodification is real but not totalising.

The Gramscian conclusion is therefore not that counter-hegemony fails the moment it is commodified, but that counter-hegemonic struggle is permanent. There is no purity outside commodity capitalism in which authentic resistance lives; there is only continual struggle within and against commodified forms. The question is not whether a movement has been commodified (almost everything in capitalist society has been or will be) but whether, within and against commodification, it continues to do counter-hegemonic articulation work. Some do; some don't. The Gramscian framework gives us the tools to tell which is which.

### Essay 12. Is Gramsci's framework still useful for analysing digital media, or does the algorithmic platform require a different theory of power?

The question is a serious one because digital platforms differ from broadcast media in important ways: they operate through algorithmic personalisation rather than mass address, they collect and monetise behavioural data rather than selling audience attention, and they function as infrastructures of everyday life rather than discrete content channels. I argue that Gramsci's framework remains substantially useful for analysing digital media (perhaps even more useful than for broadcast) but that it requires the kind of update Williams and Gilbert provide in *Hegemony Now*: a theory of passive consent, an emphasis on material interests, and a recognition of platforms as a distinct mode of infrastructural hegemony alongside the ideological and the cultural.

The lecture is honest about the question. "Gramsci remains relevant for analysing digital media, platforms, and culture wars, where power operates through visibility, algorithms, and moral framing rather than force. Critiques argue that Gramscian analysis can overemphasise culture at the expense of material structures or treat hegemony as too totalising. Nonetheless, his framework remains central for understanding media, consent, and cultural power" (Lecture 17 slides). The "remains central" claim is the one I want to defend.

What in Gramsci still works for digital media? Five things. First, the fundamental claim that power in advanced capitalist societies operates through consent in civil society rather than coercion in political society holds entirely. The platforms of "Apple, Facebook and Google" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6) are not coercive apparatuses; they are sites of civil society where consent is manufactured. As the lecture's distinction has it, "Civil society includes media, education, religion, family, and culture, and operates through persuasion and consent" (Lecture 17 slides). The platform belongs squarely in this register.

Second, the concept of common sense maps directly onto algorithmic culture. Algorithms do not impose new beliefs; they amplify, sort and recombine already-circulating common sense. The lecture's point that "media normalises particular explanations of social issues (e.g. individual responsibility over structural causes), helping to stabilise hegemonic common sense while managing dissent within acceptable limits" (Lecture 17 slides) is even more apt for algorithmic curation than for broadcast: the platform builds its preferred framings out of folk-wisdoms that are already there.

Third, the organic-intellectual concept extends to digital media. Williams and Gilbert identify "internet influencer culture" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6) as one of the central sites where the neoliberal bloc's entrepreneurial common sense is reproduced. Influencers, podcasters and platform-creators of all kinds are organic intellectuals of identifiable constituencies. The slides' counter-hegemonic example of Alan Moore and Garth Ennis as "'organic intellectuals' from working class backgrounds with radical politics" (Lecture 17 slides) extends naturally to contemporary counter-hegemonic platform-creators.

Fourth, the encoding/decoding tradition is even more applicable in an environment of fragmented audiences and active production. Decoding now includes commenting, remixing, sharing: audiences are not only making meaning but redistributing it. "Counter-hegemonic readings (negotiated / oppositional)" (Lecture 17 slides) can become counter-hegemonic re-encoding.

Fifth, the war of position is exactly the right strategic framework. The long war of position to shape platform regulation, alternative platforms, public-service digital infrastructure, and the cultural common sense around digital life is the actual terrain of struggle.

What in Gramsci needs updating? Williams and Gilbert's three points. First, *passive consent*. Digital platforms exemplify the form of hegemony that does not require active assent. The dominant structure of feeling is precisely the "cynical resignation at the lack of political agency enjoyed by most citizens with a conscious but ineffectual critique of capitalist selfishness and an embrace of the everyday pleasures of an advanced consumer society" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5) that produces continued platform participation.

Second, *material interests*. Identity-focused readings of platform politics miss that the hegemonic project is centrally about who gets paid. Williams and Gilbert insist that "what holds together political collectives … is not primarily a shared 'identity' so much as a common set of material interests" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 6), and the recovery of material interests as the spine of hegemonic analysis is necessary for serious platform critique. The extraction of attention-as-data is a material interest, not just a cultural style.

Third, and most importantly, *platforms as infrastructural hegemony*. This is the genuinely new category. "It is the platform that is today a key mechanism of infrastructural power … Platforms can be understood as instantiations of hegemonic social relations, enabling a range of forms of participation, while always setting subtle limits to the freedom of action enjoyed by their participants" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 5). The platform shapes what can be said, by whom, with what reach, under what affective conditions, with what data extraction. This is not just ideological work; it is infrastructural work, and it requires its own analytical vocabulary alongside Gramsci's older categories.

So my answer is: Gramsci's framework remains uniquely useful for digital media, but it needs the updates *Hegemony Now* supplies. A different theory of power is not required; an extended Gramscian theory is. The crisis of neoliberalism, the rise of platform capitalism and the emergence of the "nationalist authoritarian alternative" (Williams & Gilbert, 2022: 7) are precisely the conditions for which Gramsci was writing, made strange by a century of capitalist mutation.
