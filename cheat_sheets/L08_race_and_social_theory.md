# L8. A Question of Experience (2): Race and Social Theory

## 1. Topic Overview

This lecture moves the question of "experience" from gender (L7) to race, showing how sociology has had to confront its own complicity in racial classification while also drawing on Black theorists who treat race as a constitutive feature of modernity rather than a side issue. The argument unfolds in three parts. First, the lecturer traces scientific racism from Linnaeus (1735) and Blumenbach (1775) through nineteenth-century craniometry to twentieth-century eugenics, showing that race had no biological referent before this period. The word's etymology (French "racine," Arabic "ras") simply meant root or beginning. Only when classification was tied to power did "race" produce the pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy that justified slavery and colonialism. Following Foucault, the lecture frames this as power-knowledge: science "observed" what its own categories had already produced.

Second, W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963) is presented as the indispensable Black sociologist of modernity. Born five years before the Emancipation Proclamation's promise was anchored in the Fifteenth Amendment, the first Black PhD from Harvard, Du Bois asks in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) what it means that legal emancipation produced no substantive freedom. His answer reorganises the sociology of selfhood. The veil separates Black from white worlds; double consciousness names the "peculiar sensation" of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others"; twoness ("an American, a Negro") refuses the unitary actor presupposed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. In *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) Du Bois refines this by insisting that race is not biology but social fact: the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia." The revision document notes that this anticipates the "racial realism" of Critical Race Theory.

Third, the lecture turns to contemporary social theory through Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy. Morrison's *The Origin of Others* (2017) treats race as "an effect of power" and "Othering" as a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal. Americanness, she argues, "remains color for many people." Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) and 'Race Ends Here' (1998) refuse to bracket Africa as marginal to modernity: the slave trade is constitutive of Western modernity, racial terror is "cheerfully complicit" with occidental rationality, and we must rewrite history from the slaves' standpoint rather than merely include Black voices. The lecturer concludes: races do not exist, but the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of race do, and they saturate institutions, media, art and ways of seeing.

The pay-off for SC301 is methodological. The lecture argues that mainstream social theory (Weber, Durkheim, Goffman, Garfinkel) built its account of the modern self on a tacit white subject, treating racialised experience as a deviation or a special case. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy reverse this: the Black experience of modernity is the most revealing standpoint because modernity itself was forged through slavery, racial classification and colonial domination. The colour line is, in Du Bois's Forethought, "the problem of the Twentieth Century" (Du Bois, 1903: 1) and, given the persistence of the school-to-prison pipeline, of mass incarceration and the army as what the revision document calls "a modern form of Black slavery," equally a problem of the twenty-first.

## 2. Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Scientific racism**: The nineteenth-century project of tying "race" to biology, producing pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy. Originates with Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) and Blumenbach's five-race skull typology.
- **Power-knowledge** (Foucault, applied by Gilroy): Classification "produced" the very races it claimed to "observe." Race was "a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).
- **Eugenics**: Pseudo-science of "improving" racial stock; the lecture connects it to the 1933 photograph of Nazi racial measurements and to the "eugenic ravings of Francis Crick" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **The colour line**: Du Bois's diagnostic of the twentieth century: "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line" (Du Bois, 1903: 1).
- **The veil**: The social barrier separating Black from white worlds; Black people are "shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4).
- **Second sight**: The compensatory gift of those born "with a veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4), a critical perspective on the dominant world they are excluded from yet forced to inhabit.
- **Double consciousness**: "This sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **Twoness**: "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **The talented tenth**: Du Bois's later concept (mentioned in the revision document) for the educated Black elite whose role is to lead racial uplift.
- **Racial realism**: Du Bois's view (later taken up by CRT, as the revision document notes) that race is not biology but social fact, defined by unequal treatment, Jim Crow, segregation.
- **Othering** (Morrison): The construction of racial outsiders to stabilise the dominant group's self-image; "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29).
- **Whiteness** (Morrison): Analysis of whiteness as a form of unmarked power; Americanness "remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17).
- **The Black Atlantic**: Gilroy's name for the transnational cultural and political space formed by slavery's middle passage; Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Caribbean as one circuit.
- **Epidermalisation** (Fanon, via Gilroy): The colonial process of making bodies meaningful by endowing them with "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254).
- **Interest convergence** (CRT, via revision document): Racial progress occurs only when it aligns with white interests.
- **Counter-storytelling** (CRT, via revision document): Narrative methods that reveal Black epistemologies erased by dominant historiography.
- **Primal history of modernity** (Gilroy after Walter Benjamin): Modernity rewritten "from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).
- **Tertium quid**: Du Bois's term for the "intermediate category between animal and human" into which Black people were forced (cited in Gilroy, 1993: 55).
- **Chemical gaze** (Gilroy after Foucault): The perceptual regime within which the modern idea of race "leaked out" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).

## 3. Slide-by-Slide Walkthrough

**Slides 1-3: Defining race and racism.** The lecture opens by noting that the word "race" is similar to French "racine" (root) and possibly Arabic "ras" (head, beginning). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology and seemed to refer to a particular biological group or type or category" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Racism is then defined as the belief that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3).

**Slides 4-5: Provocations.** The lecturer asks: who believes there are different races? How many races are there? What criteria identify them? The everyday question "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) is flagged as the contemporary trace of racial classification.

**Slides 6-7: Linnaeus.** Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) divided Homo Sapiens into four sub-species: Homo Europaeus, Homo Afer (Sub-Saharan Africans), Homo Asiaticus and Homo Americanus (Native Americans). Classification rested on morphology but Linnaeus also claimed differences in "temperament and psychology" (slide 6). The lecturer notes finer subdivisions ("Northern Europeans"/"Southern Europeans").

**Slide 8: Blumenbach.** Johann Friedrich Blumenbach (1752-1840) proposed five races, supposedly evidenced by skull shape: Caucasian (white), Mongolian (yellow), Malayan (brown), Negroid (black) and American (red). Again, supposed psychological differences track morphology.

**Slide 9: Power-knowledge.** "Science claimed to 'observe' racial differences. The classifications they produced appeared to give them knowledge … This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 9). The lecturer ties this to Foucault (L7) and asks whether the question of "colour" runs through race talk because there "needs to be something to be 'observed' in order to classify." This is vision and power. The accompanying image juxtaposes the 1868 "Apollo Belvidere" / "Creole Negro" / "Young chimpanzee" comparison with the 1933 photograph of Nazi racial measurements.

**Slides 10-11: Du Bois on race as social fact.** "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). In a "fictional" dialogue Du Bois insists race is "a cultural, sometimes a historical fact" and concludes: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153).

**Slide 12: Du Bois biography.** Born Massachusetts 1868, died 1963. Studied in Berlin in the 1890s; first Black PhD from Harvard; polymath; first generation of African Americans after Emancipation (1863).

**Slide 13: Post-Emancipation.** The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared all slaves free; the post-Civil-War amendments made it unconstitutional to deny the vote on the basis of "race" or "color" or prior slave status. Yet "writing a generation after all this, in the 1890s, Du Bois asked why nothing had really changed for African Americans" (slide 13).

**Slide 14: "Being a problem".** Du Bois's opening: "being a problem is a strange experience" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). The visiting cards anecdote: a girl refuses his card and "it dawned on me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others … shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). Gibson's editorial gloss: "one's sense of race and racial terms comes only from experience" (Gibson, 1996: xxv).

**Slide 15: Double consciousness.** "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The veiled "Negro" gets "no true self-consciousness but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

**Slide 16: Twoness vs the unitary actor.** "For Du Bois, being double is the basis of the experience of the world. This differs from the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel" (slide 16). "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). "The history of the American Negro is the history of this strife … this longing to attain self-conscious manhood, to merge his double self into a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

**Slide 17: The waste of double aims.** "This waste of double aims, this seeking to satisfy two unreconciled ideals, has wrought sad havoc with the courage and faith and deeds of ten thousand thousand people" (Du Bois, 1903: 6).

**Slide 18: Substantive freedom.** Despite the Emancipation Proclamation and the 15th Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found freedom in his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 7). "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11).

**Slide 19: Neither Africanise nor bleach.** "he wishes neither of the older selves to be lost. He would not Africanize America, for America has much to teach the world … He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The lecturer underlines the link of modernity and slavery.

**Slide 20: Modernity built race as a labour fix.** Long Du Bois quotation: "In the latter part of the seventeenth and early in the eighteenth centuries, the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4). "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). Source: Du Bois, W.E.B. 1898. 'The Study of the Negro Problems', *Annals of the American Academy*, Jan 1898: 1-23.

**Slide 21: Morrison biography.** Toni Morrison: novelist, writer, thinker. The lecturer flags the 2017 Harvard lectures published as *The Origin of Others* (Harvard University Press).

**Slide 22: Morrison's central question.** "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter? … Race is the classification of a species, and we are the human race, period [full-stop]. Then what is this other thing, the hostility, the social racism, the Othering" (Morrison, 2017: 15). Citing Bruce Baum: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29).

**Slide 23: Americanness as colour.** "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). The lecturer adds: "[Think also about Trump and what it means nowadays to be a 'Mexican-American']."

**Slide 24: Media and Othering.** "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like (or ought to look like) and what in fact we are like" (Morrison, 2017: 37). Suggested novels: *Beloved*, *Paradise*, *The Bluest Eye*; also Octavia Butler's *Dawn* and Angela Carter's *The Magic Toyshop*.

**Slide 25: Gilroy biography.** Paul Gilroy, currently at UCL, previously LSE, Harvard, Goldsmiths and Essex. Most famous books: *There Ain't No Black in the Union Jack*, *The Black Atlantic: Modernity and Double Consciousness*, *Between Camps* (published in the US as *Against Race*).

**Slide 26: Race as primary object of knowledge and power.** Gilroy is influenced by Foucault but does not fully agree. "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).

**Slide 27: Africa as constitutive, not peripheral.** Africa "was not on the side-lines of the West and the development of modernity." The transnational, evidenced in the slave trade, was always part of Western modernity; hence the "Black Atlantic." Ethnic/"racial" identities are outcomes of political, economic and cultural processes, not fixed essences. "We need to rethink history and modernity and not just 'include' the marginal positions that have been ignored. But to rewrite history and modernity from these positions" (slide 27).

**Slide 28: Racial terror and reason.** "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Annotated "(Think of Dussel)" by the lecturer. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

**Slide 29: Standpoint, not unmediated experience.** "This unstable standpoint is to be understood in a different way from the clarion calls to … unmediated experience which sometimes appear in association with the term." "In terms of contemporary politics and social theory, the value of this project lies in its promise to uncover both an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood … that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Annotated "(Think of Du Bois)."

**Slide 30: Conclusion.** "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do. This is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist. It is about how the modern social world is constituted on forms of racism which we might not be able to recognise immediately but are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 30).

## 5. Debates & Tensions

- **Biological vs social conceptions of race**: Scientific racism insists race is biologically real; Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all reject this but disagree on what to put in its place. Du Bois retains "race" as a cultural and historical fact; Gilroy presses towards "race ends here" and the abolition of raciology; Morrison occupies a middle ground, naming race as power-effect.
- **Inclusion vs rewriting**: Should we "add" Black voices to existing modernity narratives or rewrite modernity from the standpoint of the enslaved? Gilroy explicitly demands the latter, citing Walter Benjamin's "primal history."
- **Unitary vs split self**: Du Bois's double consciousness explicitly contests the unified actor of Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. Can sociology accommodate a self that is constitutively riven?
- **Racism as individual prejudice vs structural condition**: Lecture conclusion stresses that this "is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist" but how the social world is constituted on unrecognised forms of racism in institutions, art, music and ways of seeing.
- **Pessimism vs progress**: The revision document notes that CRT treats racial progress as a "myth" reversed by "interest convergence." Du Bois oscillates: Emancipation has not delivered, yet *Souls* ends on the possibility that two world-races may give each to each.
- **Universalism vs particularism**: Gilroy critiques bourgeois humanism for repairing itself by adding the figures Du Bois called "tertium quid"; he wants conceptions of selfhood that are "forever disassociated from the psychological and epistemic correlates of racial subordination" (Gilroy, 1993: 56).
- **Visibility and the body**: Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' asks whether nanoscale biology and digital imaging make race "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Yet Fanon's epidermalisation still organises everyday life (Gilroy, 1998: 254-255).
- **Africa as periphery vs Africa as constitutive**: Gilroy explicitly contests the view that Africa was on the side-lines of Western modernity (slide 27).

## 6. Examples & Applications

- **Jim Crow segregation**: Du Bois's empirical anchor for racial realism; the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The revision document names Jim Crow as Du Bois's foremost example of race as social fact.
- **The visiting cards refusal**: Du Bois's autobiographical scene in the New England schoolhouse (Du Bois, 1903: 2-3) as the everyday mechanism by which the veil is communicated.
- **The Ku Klux Klan and carpet-baggers**: Du Bois's catalogue of the Reconstruction failure: "the holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry" left the freedman with no substantive freedom (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **The Massachusetts-to-Georgia slave statutes**: Du Bois's (1898: 3-4) demonstration that racial law was a labour-supply fix; "such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color."
- **Indented servants vs life-slaves**: Du Bois shows how legal categories diverged once "the economic superiority of the slave system" combined with religious and linguistic difference to harden a racial line (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4).
- **The 1868 "Apollo Belvidere / Creole Negro / Young Chimpanzee" plate**: Slide 9's example of scientific racism's visual rhetoric, placing the Greek ideal at the top of a hierarchy that descends through the Black body to the chimpanzee.
- **The 1933 Nazi racial measurements photograph**: Slide 9's example of scientific racism institutionalised in twentieth-century state machinery.
- **The "Where are you really from?" question** (slide 4): The everyday racialised question through which the colour line still operates.
- **Sartje Baartman and Michael Jordan / Bugs Bunny**: Gilroy's paired example of nineteenth-century display of the racialised body and contemporary "pas de deux" of "the super-human, godly Michael Jordan and Bugs Bunny" as "the reductio ad absurdum of hybridized African trixter tale-telling" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).
- **The Linnaeus frontispiece (orangutan and Negro girl)**: Gilroy reads it as showing the "suggested kinship between these sub- and anti-human species" and the entanglement of race with sex and gender (Gilroy, 1998: 254).
- **Robert Lincoln Watkins and the "micromotoscope"**: Gilroy's example of an early-twentieth-century shift from comparative anatomy to microscopy in producing racial knowledge (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **Francis Crick's "eugenic ravings"**: Gilroy's example of how the founding of molecular biology was implicated in the "redefinition of life in terms of information, messages and code" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **NMR/MRI and PET imaging**: Gilroy notes that these technologies have "remade the relationship between the seeable and the unseen … status of bio-racial differences that vanish at these levels of resolution" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **Kant's *Physische Geographie* and the split bamboo cane**: Gilroy cites Eze and Neugebauer on Kant's writing about "the qualities of Negro skin and the practical problems it presented when pain had to be inflicted with a split bamboo cane" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). Anchor case for epidermalisation avant la lettre.
- **Hegel on "the African's capacity for historical action"**: Gilroy (1998: 254-255) cites Hegel's "limitations he placed upon the African's capacity for historical action" as exemplifying "epidermal thinking in its emergent form."
- **Toussaint to the devil**: Du Bois names Toussaint Louverture in a catalogue of how white prejudice extends "from Toussaint to the devil" (Du Bois, 1903: 6); the Haitian revolutionary as the disavowed object of white contempt.
- **The Sorrow Songs**: Du Bois's Forethought announces that each chapter begins with "some echo of haunting melody from the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past" (Du Bois, 1903: Forethought).
- **Ethiopia the Shadowy and Egypt the Sphinx**: Du Bois names the long African past whose "powers of single black men flash here and there like falling stars" (Du Bois, 1903: 4).
- **The school-to-prison pipeline**: The revision document names this as a CRT example of how the US prison and military system functions as "a modern form of Black slavery."
- **Prisons and the army as modern slavery**: The revision document explicitly cites these as the CRT example of how "racial progress is always pushed back, and only occurs through 'interest convergence'."
- **American identity as whiteness**: Morrison's example of immigrants "embrac[ing] their whiteness" to become "real Americans" (Morrison, 2017: 17); the revision document highlights that "American identity is tied to whiteness, reinforced by media and culture / law and order / religion."
- **Trump-era "Mexican-American"** (slide 23): Lecturer's contemporary annotation on Morrison's whiteness-as-Americanness thesis.

## 9. Past-Q Essay Answers

### Past Q 2025. What is meant by 'scientific racism'? How have sociologists tried to counter this concept?

In this essay I define scientific racism as the nineteenth-century project of grounding racial classification in supposedly biological fact, and show how sociologists from Du Bois through Morrison to Gilroy have countered it by treating race as a social, historical and political construction.

"Race" had no biological referent before the nineteenth century. Its etymology is benign: French "racine" (root) or Arabic "ras" (head). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Scientific racism is the doctrine that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3). Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) divided Homo sapiens into Europaeus, Afer, Asiaticus and Americanus, claiming temperamental as well as morphological differences. ### Past Q 2024. How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to two theorists.

In this essay I argue that European modernity was constituted by unequal race relations, not merely accompanied by them, through Du Bois and Gilroy. The wealth, philosophy and self-image of European modernity were materially and conceptually subsidised by the racial subordination of African peoples.

Du Bois's economic and legal foundation. In 'The Study of the Negro Problems' (1898), "the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4). The supply problem was solved by importing Africans and creating a racial category through statute: "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). "Dozens of enactments, from Massachusetts to Georgia, were made 'for the proper regulation of slaves and servants'" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4), and the racial line hardened when "the economic superiority of the slave system" combined with linguistic, religious and morphological difference. Race emerged as a legal-economic device because slavery was more profitable than indenture; it congealed afterwards into the natural fact scientific racism would later "discover." Modernity benefited twice: from unpaid labour and from the ideological asset of being the racially enlightened standard. As Du Bois says in *Dusk of Dawn*: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136).

Gilroy radicalises this. In *The Black Atlantic* he abandons the comforting story that slavery was a regrettable side-business. "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" (Gilroy, 1993: 54); it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). The Atlantic triangle of European masters, Caribbean plantations and African slaves was the laboratory of modern bureaucratic management, industrial finance and consumption. Gilroy's polemic cuts to the benefit question: "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Modernity did not benefit despite slavery; rationality and slavery rose together.

The cultural and epistemic dividend follows. European science gained authority by classifying non-European peoples as inferior. As Gilroy puts it, "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). Knowledge production was an arm of empire. Linnaeus's orangutan-and-Negro frontispiece, in which "the suggested kinship between these sub- and anti-human species" anchors the European self-image (Gilroy, 1998: 254), is one example. Slide 9's 1868 plate placing "Apollo Belvidere" above the "Creole Negro" and the "Young Chimpanzee" is another. Even bourgeois humanism, Gilroy notes, relied on excluding those Du Bois called a "tertium quid", an "intermediate category between animal and human" (cited in Gilroy, 1993: 55).

A counter-argument holds that modernity was progressive and slavery a pre-modern residue. I reject this. The legal innovations that produced racial slavery were modern inventions, as Du Bois (1898: 3-4) documents in the statutes that hardened the slave/servant boundary. Emancipation was incomplete: "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The revision document specifies the continuity: the prison and the army as "a modern form of Black slavery," and CRT's interest convergence thesis. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor makes the continuity contemporary.

If modernity benefited from slavery, then a sociology that simply adds Black voices to a fundamentally white narrative is inadequate. We must, with Gilroy, attempt "the primal history of modernity reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). The slaves' standpoint reveals what the masters' standpoint conceals. "We need to rethink history and modernity and not just 'include' the marginal positions that have been ignored" (slide 27). The L2 framing of colonial modernity sharpens this: Howard French (2021) argues African labour and African gold financed the European centuries we call "modern," while Dussel (1993) holds that "European modernity is based on violence" against its colonial underside. Gilroy's Black Atlantic is the African contribution to modernity that L2 names.

European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in three reinforcing ways: economically through unpaid labour (Du Bois, 1898), culturally through "scientific" racial classification (Linnaeus, Blumenbach, slide 9), and politically through the self-image of a free Europe defined against unfree others.

### Past Q 2023. What is meant by "scientific racism"? How have sociologists tried to counter this concept?

In this essay I define scientific racism as the nineteenth-century project of grounding racial classification in supposedly biological fact, and show how sociologists from Du Bois through Morrison to Gilroy have countered it by treating race as social construction.

"Race" had no biological referent before the nineteenth century. Etymology: French "racine" (root) or Arabic "ras" (head). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Scientific racism is the doctrine that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3). The lineage is Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735), with "temperament and psychology" (slide 6); Blumenbach's (1752-1840) five-race skull typology (slide 8); the 1868 "Apollo Belvidere / Creole Negro / Young Chimpanzee" plate (slide 9); the 1933 photograph of Nazi racial measurements (slide 9).

Following Foucault: "Science claimed to 'observe' racial differences. This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 9). Gilroy concurs: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252); "truths of race were produced 'performatively' from the hat that biological science provided" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Scientific racism is the manufacture of racial reality by classificatory institutions tied to colonial and slaveholding power.

The first counter-move is Du Bois's. In *Dusk of Dawn* he concedes that biology cannot ground race: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136); but refuses the conclusion that race is therefore unreal. He relocates its reality: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact. The black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). This is the first sociological anti-essentialism about race: its reality lies in legal and social practices, not in skulls or genes. The revision document notes this is the source of CRT's "racial realism": race is real as social fact, fictive as biology.

The second counter-move is Morrison's. In *The Origin of Others* she presses: "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter?" (Morrison, 2017: 15). Citing Baum: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). Race functions through Othering: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). The dominant group needs racial others to stabilise its own normality. The L9 postcolonial extension is Said (1978), who shows that the same operation works at the level of discourse: scholarship, novels and journalism produce "the Orient" as a body of knowledge that confirms European normality, the discursive sibling of scientific racism's biological classification. Whiteness is not the absence of race but the unmarked form of racial power.

The third counter-move is Gilroy's. In 'Race Ends Here' (1998) he argues that scientific racism rested on the "Euclidean anatomy" of comparative racial measurement, which has been outflanked by molecular biology and digital imaging. "Scientific and biological, historical and cultural, rational and irrational, skin, bone and even blood are no longer primary referents of racial discourse" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). At molecular resolution, "'race' might best be approached as an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). The "eugenic ravings of Francis Crick" (Gilroy, 1998: 253) show that molecular biology was itself contaminated by inherited race-thinking; the broader trajectory has nonetheless undermined the perceptual regime that produced race. The science that produced scientific racism has superseded its own object.

Counter-arguing scientific racism in the lab does not abolish racism in the street. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all stress that racism persists as institutional and cultural fact even after biology repudiates it. "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do" (slide 30). Black Lives Matter mobilisations after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor show the persistence. The revision document specifies CRT's institutional sites: the school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as "modern form of Black slavery"; "racial progress is always pushed back, and only occurs through 'interest convergence'."

I conclude that scientific racism is the modern project of dressing racial hierarchy in biological clothing, and that sociologists have countered it by relocating race's reality to social structure (Du Bois), to the affective work of othering (Morrison) and to the changing technologies of vision (Gilroy). The counter is unfinished because the legacy of scientific racism is sedimented in institutions and habits of perception that survive the discrediting of its biological premises.

### Past Q 2022. Drawing from at least two of W.E.B Du Bois, Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy, discuss the implications of "race" for contemporary society.

In this essay I draw on Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy to argue that race, though biologically empty, has profound implications for contemporary society in three registers: the constitution of the self, the production of national belonging, and the structure of global modernity.

The first implication is for selfhood. In *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) Du Bois introduces double consciousness as the experiential consequence of living within the colour line: "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The self is constitutively split: "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The unitary actor presupposed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel is unavailable to racialised subjects (slide 16). L6 standpoint epistemology develops this directly: Hill Collins (1990) names Du Bois as an explicit ancestor of Black feminist standpoint, and Mohanty (1984) shows how Western feminism's construction of the "Third World Woman" reproduces Morrison's Othering at a global scale.

The second implication is for national belonging. In *The Origin of Others* Morrison observes that "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). National identity is not just a passport but a racialised performance. The lecturer adds: "[Think also about Trump and what it means nowadays to be a 'Mexican-American']" (slide 23). The revision document echoes: "American identity is tied to whiteness, reinforced by media and culture / law and order / religion." Morrison's othering thesis names the mechanism: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). Brexit-era hostile-environment debates and Trump-era policy framings show the same dynamic in contemporary form.

The third implication is structural. In *The Black Atlantic* and 'Race Ends Here', Gilroy refuses to treat slavery and colonialism as side-issues. "The order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" (Gilroy, 1993: 54); it built modernity through the Atlantic slave system. The modern social world is built on these racialised foundations, and Morrison's "routine media presentations" (Morrison, 2017: 37) continue to "narrow our views of what humans look like." Gilroy's normative claim follows: we must reconstruct modernity from the slaves' point of view (Gilroy, 1993: 55), not merely add Black voices.

Examples across all three thinkers give contemporary content. The revision document's CRT catalogue (school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as "modern form of Black slavery"; interest convergence; counter-storytelling) operationalises all three implications. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor makes the structural diagnosis newsworthy; cultural references like *Get Out* and *Selma* stage the racialised perception for critique.

The objection that race is a declining force in a globalising multicultural world fails on each thinker's evidence. Du Bois warned that equality in law is not enough: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). Morrison's evidence: "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like" (Morrison, 2017: 37). Gilroy's evidence: the chemical gaze (Gilroy, 1998: 253) and Fanon's epidermalisation (Gilroy, 1998: 254-255) still shape how bodies are read. Generic affirmative-action debates and cost-of-living racialised wealth gaps show that formal anti-discrimination has not dissolved the structure.

Race has three deep implications for contemporary society. Through Du Bois, it shapes the structure of racialised selfhood, producing the doubled, second-sighted experience that mainstream sociology must learn from. Through Morrison, it shapes national belonging by making whiteness the unmarked currency of full citizenship. Through Gilroy, it shapes global modernity by reproducing the asymmetries laid down by the Atlantic slave system. Race is biologically empty and sociologically full.

### Past Q 2021. Du Bois asked: "How does it feel to be a problem?" Outline and assess his response to this question.

In this essay I outline Du Bois's response and assess it on three criteria: descriptive accuracy, conceptual originality, political adequacy.

Du Bois opens *Souls* with the question and defers it: "To the real question, How does it feel to be a problem? I answer seldom a word" (Du Bois, 1903: 2). The question is a polite circumlocution for the white interlocutor's racialised gaze. The response unfolds in three moves: an autobiographical scene, a conceptual formulation of double consciousness, and a programmatic call for substantive equality.

The autobiographical scene is the visiting cards incident. As a New England schoolboy, Du Bois recalls a newcomer who "refused my card, refused it peremptorily, with a glance. Then it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). Being a problem feels like the unwilled recognition that one is on the wrong side of an invisible barrier. The veil names this barrier; second sight names the compensatory gift: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world" (Du Bois, 1903: 3).

The conceptual formulation is double consciousness: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). This doubling is structural, not a deficit to be repaired by assimilation. "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The aim is not to abandon either pole: "He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

The programmatic move is the demand for substantive rather than formal equality. Despite the Emancipation Proclamation and the Fifteenth Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Du Bois catalogues the failures: "the holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). He ends: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). Being a problem feels like being formally free but substantively unfree.

On descriptive accuracy, Du Bois's response is remarkable. The visiting cards scene captures the everyday mechanism by which racial difference is communicated, not by lynching or Jim Crow law but by a glance and a refused card. "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) is the visiting-card refusal at the front door of citizenship. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor gives institutional content.

On conceptual originality, double consciousness is one of the most important contributions of American social theory. It differs from "the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel" (slide 16). Gilroy reads it as anticipating "the new conceptions of selfhood and individuation that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). L10 extends Du Bois's "second-sight" into visual culture: hooks (1992) names the "oppositional gaze" of the Black spectator who looks back, refusing to be the passive object of the white world's "tape." Second sight becomes a politics of looking.

On political adequacy, the response is more ambivalent. The demand for merger of African and American selves can sound assimilationist; Du Bois insists he "would not Africanize America" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Gilroy worries the demand to merge may understate the rupture slavery represents; his "primal history of modernity reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55) implies some doublings should not be merged. CRT's myth-of-racial-progress thesis gives Du Bois's optimism a sober check.

Limitations. Du Bois's frame is gendered ("self-conscious manhood"), obscuring gendered racial experience. His American focus underplays the transnational frame Gilroy's Black Atlantic recovers. The analytical core survives these critiques.

Du Bois's response is descriptively acute, conceptually transformative, and politically incomplete but generative. Being a problem feels like always seeing yourself through someone else's eyes; the response is to fight for a world in which that doubling becomes optional rather than imposed.

### Mock 1. "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro." Discuss this passage from Du Bois.

In this essay I discuss Du Bois's twoness passage, "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). It should be read as a sociological diagnosis of what the colour line does to selfhood under formal equality, neither identity-confusion complaint nor assimilation programme.

The passage closes 'Of Our Spiritual Strivings', which has already laid down the question "How does it feel to be a problem?" (Du Bois, 1903: 2), the refused visiting card and the veil ("shut out from their world by a vast veil," Du Bois, 1903: 3), the gift of second sight ("born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world," Du Bois, 1903: 3), and double consciousness ("this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others," Du Bois, 1903: 5). The twoness sentence is the experiential apex of this chain.

I read four claims in the passage. First, the doubling is involuntary ("One ever feels"); it is an imposed perceptual condition, not chosen identity politics. Second, the two poles are non-negotiable: not "a Negro or an American" but "an American, a Negro." Each is constitutive. Third, the conflict is structural, not psychological: "two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals." Fourth, the body is the site of strain: "in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder." Du Bois locates racial experience in the dark body that bears it, not in the soul abstracted from physiology.

The passage closes off two alternatives Du Bois rejects. The first is racial separation, "Africanise America": "He would not Africanize America, for America has too much to teach the world and Africa" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The second is racial assimilation: "He would not bleach his Negro blood in a flood of white Americanism, for he knows that Negro blood has a message for the world" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The twoness passage is the only available alternative: hold the two strivings together, not by reconciling them but by refusing to surrender either.

This is Du Bois's central contribution to a social theory of the self. Mainstream classical sociology assumed a unitary subject. Du Bois insists that the racialised subject in a white-majority polity cannot be unitary because the polity itself is double: nominally inclusive, structurally exclusive. This contrasts with "the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel" (slide 16). Twoness is the experiential consequence of the colour line as a social fact.

The passage has contemporary purchase. "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) performs the same operation Du Bois names: it invites a doubling by treating the racialised subject as not-quite-here. The revision document's note that "American identity is tied to whiteness" extends the diagnosis to twenty-first-century citizenship debates. Morrison's "the definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17) generalises Du Bois's American/Negro doubling: any non-white claim on Americanness produces twoness. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor makes the diagnosis newly audible.

Objections. The first is that twoness over-essentialises a binary that fragments into many. The right reply, supplied by Crenshaw's intersectionality, is that the Black woman experiences more than two strivings but the structural logic still applies. The second is that twoness pathologises racialised subjectivity. Du Bois's coupling of double consciousness with second sight is the answer: the doubled subject sees what the unitary subject cannot. CRT's counter-storytelling formalises this perceptual advantage.

Gilroy extends the passage diasporically. In *The Black Atlantic* he argues that the slaves' standpoint demands "the new conceptions of selfhood and individuation that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). The twoness passage is one such conception in embryo. L6 standpoint theory makes the same move at higher generality: Hill Collins (1990) explicitly names Du Bois as ancestor of Black feminist standpoint, treating twoness as the foundational racial standpoint from which the unitary white subject's self-image can be seen as such.

The twoness passage is Du Bois's most concentrated diagnosis of the racialised self under formal equality. It names an imposed doubling, refuses the false choices of assimilation and separation, and grounds Black critical perception in the body that endures the strain. It anticipates the standpoint epistemology Gilroy will globalise and CRT will translate into counter-storytelling.

### Mock 2. Critically assess Gilroy's argument in *The Black Atlantic* with reference to the role of slavery in modernity.

Gilroy's thesis. "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination"; it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, 1993: 54). Morrison adds the affective component. Does treating slavery as "modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) obscure the heterogeneity of modern experiences? Gilroy's reply, in his treatment of the standpoint as "unstable" (Gilroy, 1993: 56), is that the slaves' standpoint is an analytical orientation, not an unmediated experience: it reveals features the masters' position conceals, without claiming to be the only valid one.

The second critique asks whether Gilroy underplays resistance and creativity. His frame includes "creative, communicative" cultural work, the "vernacular variety of unhappy consciousness" of *The Black Atlantic* (Gilroy, 1993: 56). The Sorrow Songs that close Du Bois's *Souls* are one Black Atlantic instance; the Forethought announces that each chapter begins with "some echo of haunting melody from the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past."

The third asks: what does Gilroy's frame demand? The revision document's CRT examples (school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as "a modern form of Black slavery"; the myth of racial progress reversed by interest convergence; counter-storytelling) give concrete normative content. Each is a contemporary site at which the Black Atlantic's structural legacy is reproduced. Black Lives Matter as a movement, mobilising after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor, is the latest visible instance of counter-storytelling against that legacy.

The three thinkers together show that slavery was not modernity's regrettable side-business but its foundation and enduring grammar. Gilroy gives us the structural picture; Du Bois (1898) the legal-economic mechanism; Morrison the affective machinery. The revision document's framing applies: "Modernity / world is shaped by African people through resistance, slavery and cultural exchange. Challenge to ethnic / nationalistic models of history. Africa is central to modernity."

Gilroy's argument in *The Black Atlantic* is analytically powerful and substantially correct. Slavery built modernity economically, legally, culturally and epistemically. Critique refines but does not undo the thesis. The corollary is that any sociology that treats slavery as an exotic past underestimates the modern present.

### Mock 3. What does Du Bois mean by the "veil", and why does he treat it as constitutive of African American experience?

In this essay I reconstruct what Du Bois means by the veil and show why he treats it as constitutive of African American experience.

The veil enters *Souls* in the visiting card scene. As a New England schoolboy, Du Bois recalls that "it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). The veil is a metaphor for social exclusion that operates without overt segregation: no wall, no law required, just the refused card, the averted glance. It is invisible from the white side and palpable from the Black.

Three properties follow. First, the veil is perceptual: it organises how the two sides see each other. Black people see themselves "through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). Second, it bestows second sight: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). Third, it generates double consciousness: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

Why is the veil constitutive? Three reasons.

First, historical-structural. The veil is the lingering presence of slavery and post-Emancipation failure. "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Du Bois catalogues the failures: "the holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). In *Dusk of Dawn*: "The black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The veil is what makes Jim Crow durable.

Second, sociological-relational. The racialised self is constituted in relation to the white world's perception. There is no Black selfhood "behind" the veil independent of it; the experience of being on the wrong side produces the doubled selfhood. "How does it feel to be a problem?" (Du Bois, 1903: 2) is constitutive, not occasional. The Du Boisian self "differs from the sense of unity, of being a single actor" presumed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel (slide 16).

Third, phenomenological. The veil organises every encounter, every glance. Du Bois's doubled literary method communicates the texture of veiled experience. The Forethought announces that each chapter begins with "some echo of haunting melody from the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past"; veiled experience speaks through Black musical tradition.

Three alternatives fail. The individualist ("any Black person can transcend the veil by talent") is rebutted by Du Bois personally: the first Black Harvard PhD still describes the visiting cards moment as definitive. The assimilationist is rejected: "He would not bleach his Negro blood in a flood of white Americanism" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The separatist is rejected too: "He would not Africanize America, for America has too much to teach the world and Africa" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The veil is constitutive because it cannot be exited by individual, assimilationist or separatist means.

Contemporary purchase. "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) is the contemporary refused card. The revision document's CRT note that "racial progress is always pushed back" generalises the veil's persistence. Morrison's "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like" (Morrison, 2017: 37) shows the veil in contemporary media; L10 develops the perceptual side directly through hooks (1992) on the "oppositional gaze," the Black spectator's refusal to be passively seen through the veil and active recovery of second sight as critical practice.

Gilroy worries the veil might encase Black experience in fixity. In 'Race Ends Here' he is wary of "the absolute sovereignty of unmediated experience" (Gilroy, 1993: 56) and seeks "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). Du Bois has a Gilroyan resource: second sight is a critical capacity political action can deploy, not a permanent metaphysical condition.

The veil names the invisible-from-one-side social barrier that produces double consciousness, second sight and doubled selfhood under formal equality. Du Bois treats it as constitutive because it is historical-structural, sociological-relational and phenomenological. The veil is what the colour line feels like from the wrong side.

Morrison. The 1898 essay is the empirical and legal anchor that grounds the lecture's argument that race is socially produced, not biologically discovered.

### Mock 10. Answer with reference to Gilroy's 'Race Ends Here': what does it mean to say that race has become an "after-image"?

In this essay I explain what Gilroy means by saying race has become "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). The metaphor combines a thesis about technological-perceptual change with a normative project of overcoming raciology; I assess its purchase against the lecture's wider analysis.

Gilroy writes that "'race' might best be approached as an after-image, a lingering symptom of looking too intently or too casually into the damaging glare emanating from colonial conflicts at home and abroad" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). The image is optical: an after-image is the visual trace left on the retina after the source of light is removed. It is real to the perceiver but has no current external referent. Race is the after-image of scientific racism: a perceptual reality whose biological cause is gone.

Three claims.

First, technological-perceptual change. The "Euclidean anatomy" of nineteenth-century scientific racism, measuring skulls and classifying physiognomies, produced race as an observable scientific object. Linnaeus (1735) and Blumenbach's five-race typology belong to this regime. But contemporary techniques (molecular biology, MRI, digital imaging) have outflanked it: "Scientific and biological, historical and cultural, rational and irrational, skin, bone and even blood are no longer primary referents of racial discourse" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). The technical apparatus that supported scientific racism has been replaced by one that does not see race.

Second, the persistence of perception. Racial perception continues despite the dissolution of the biological referent: "raciological ways of organizing and classifying the world have retained that special baggage of perspectival inclinations, perceptual habits and scalar assumptions" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Race exists out of step with the scientific moment that produced it; it survives its own refutation.

Third, the normative claim. Gilroy hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). 'Race Ends Here' is programmatic.

The metaphor does important work. First, it captures something true about scientific racism's afterlife: Du Bois's insight that "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136) was powerless on its own to dissolve race because race operates as perception. Second, it locates the political problem in perceptual training. Third, it suggests race may yet end, since after-images fade.

But the metaphor has limits.

First, it underplays institutional reproduction. After-images fade because no new light reinforces them; race does not fade because institutions reproduce it. The revision document: "racial progress is always pushed back" through interest convergence (§8). The school-to-prison pipeline and the carceral-military continuation of slavery are sources of fresh "light"; Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor makes the recharging visible.

Second, the metaphor risks suggesting scientific argument can dissolve race. Decades of argument have not. Du Bois's racial realism explains the persistence more directly.

Third, it underplays the agential dimension Morrison emphasises. Race is actively produced by power: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25); "rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). The metaphor risks freezing race in the past while it is still being made.

Gilroy has a reply. The metaphor displaces biological scientific racism specifically; it does not deny race's institutional life. He acknowledges racial differentiation "may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) and persists where biology no longer underwrites it.

Other Gilroy concepts extend the analysis. Fanon's "epidermalization" names the colonial process by which bodies are made meaningful through "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254) and continues even where the biological theory has been retired. The "chemical gaze" of contemporary biomedical technologies (Gilroy, 1998: 253) produces new perceptual regimes that can reinscribe race. L10 supplies the counter-pressure: hooks (1992) on the "oppositional gaze" shows that the Black spectator can look back at the after-image, refusing the passive position scientific racism's visual training prescribed.

Du Bois tests the metaphor. *Dusk of Dawn*: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The after-image needs addition: institutions continue to use it as current reality. The metaphor gives the perceptual diagnosis; Du Bois the institutional.

Gilroy's after-image captures something important (race persists as perceptual reality after biological supports have collapsed) but is incomplete on its own. Race is reproduced as well as remembered; institutions, media and political mobilisations actively manufacture racial categorisation. 'Race Ends Here' is best read as a normative call rather than a descriptive claim; the fade requires the political and institutional work the metaphor hopes to provoke.

### Mock 11. Using at least two authors, explain why "race ends here" is harder than it sounds.

In this essay I use Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy himself to explain why Gilroy's slogan that race ends here (1998) is harder than it sounds. The slogan is right biologically and admirable normatively, but the social, institutional and affective infrastructure of race is far harder to dismantle than the doctrine that has been refuted.

Gilroy hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) and treats race as "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Scientifically, molecular biology and digital imaging have outflanked the "Euclidean anatomy" of scientific racism. Politically, cultural and technological shifts may enable the end of raciology.

But Gilroy names the difficulty. Racial differentiation "may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252); it is deeply embedded. Raciological habits "retained that special baggage of perspectival inclinations, perceptual habits and scalar assumptions" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).

Du Bois specifies the social difficulty. *Dusk of Dawn* anticipates Gilroy by separating biology from social reality: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). The "and yet" is the problem. Even where biology fails to ground race, the practice remains binding: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). Ending race requires ending the practices that constitute it.

Morrison specifies the affective difficulty. "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) names the mechanism. "Rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29) names the affective payoff. Until the affective economy of othering is dismantled, race will keep being recreated. The L9 postcolonial extension is Said (1978), whose account of Orientalism shows discursive racism (the East as essentialised body of knowledge) outliving its biological cousin: scientific racism ends, discursive racism continues.

Five reasons follow.

First, institutional inertia. Du Bois's colonial labour statutes (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4) show the depth of embedding. The revision document's CRT examples (school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as "modern form of Black slavery") name the carceral, educational and military institutions that continue to operate with racial categorisation. Ending these requires reformatting data infrastructures, retraining personnel, changing statutory frameworks.

Second, perceptual habit. Fanon's epidermalisation, bodies endowed with "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254), names the deep perceptual training. The 1868 "Apollo Belvidere / Creole Negro / Young Chimpanzee" plate (slide 9) is one visual technology that did the original training; its descendants continue to operate.

Third, the political utility of race. Morrison's account explains why political actors keep recreating the category. The slide 23 annotation on Trump-era rhetoric about "Mexican-Americans" and Brexit-era hostile-environment debates show contemporary instances. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor shows race re-emerging as the medium of counter-mobilisation.

Fourth, the symmetric problem of anti-racist mobilisation. Anti-racism uses racial categories to measure disparities and organise solidarity. CRT operationalises this through counter-storytelling; Gilroy in *Against Race* (2000) acknowledges the tension and wants a transitional anti-racism that uses racial categories to dismantle racialisation. Generic affirmative-action debates illustrate the dilemma.

Fifth, the historical depth of the Black Atlantic. Gilroy treats slavery as constitutive: "Plantation slavery provided the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, 1993: 54). The wealth and institutions of European modernity were forged through the Black Atlantic. Ending race requires rewriting modernity itself. Gilroy's normative demand: "the primal history of modernity [must be] reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

The Gilroyan reply. 'Race Ends Here' is a normative target. I accept this, but the programme has to overcome institutional inertia, perceptual habit, political utility, the symmetric problem of anti-racist measurement and the historical depth of the Black Atlantic.

Du Bois shows race is real because racism is real; biological refutation is necessary but not sufficient. Morrison shows race is an effect of power; refuting the doctrine does not deprive power of the affective work race performs. Gilroy's slogan is correct in principle but requires institutional and affective work the slogan cannot do.

Race ends here is harder than it sounds because race is reproduced by institutions, perceptual habits, political utility, anti-racist measurement and the historical depth of modernity. Gilroy is right about the direction; Du Bois and Morrison show why the journey is longer.

### Mock 12. Can sociology abolish race without losing the analytical category?

In this essay I argue that sociology cannot abolish race in the present without losing analytical and political traction, but that it can and should work towards a future in which the category becomes unnecessary. The argument rests on the distinction between abolishing race as biological doctrine (already done in principle) and abolishing race as analytical category (premature, but worth aspiring to). I make the case through Du Bois, Morrison, Gilroy and CRT.

The question can mean several things: should sociology stop using the category? Can its use be reformed without dropping it? What is the timeline of category-loss? I take all three readings seriously.

The strongest abolitionist case is Gilroy's. In 'Race Ends Here' (1998) and *Against Race* (2000) he hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) and worries that sociological use of racial categories can reinscribe what it tries to dismantle. The categorising practices of sociology are continuous with the classificatory practices that originally produced race. Each measurement reinforces the category's salience. L9 supplies a sharper version of the dilemma: Fanon (1967) sees that the "white gaze" produces the Black subject as racial, so any anti-racism that simply names the racial subject risks repeating the gaze it opposes. The abolitionist programme would have sociology stop using race at all.

The case is ethically attractive but practically premature. Three reasons.

First, race remains real as social fact. Du Bois's mature position in *Dusk of Dawn* is that race is biologically trivial but socially decisive: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The revision document's CRT examples (school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as a "modern form of Black slavery") name contemporary institutional sites where racial categorisation operates. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor, racialised cost-of-living and wealth gaps, generic policing concerns all require racial measurement to be named. Stopping measurement would make these disparities harder to see and address.

Second, race operates as power-effect that continues to be reproduced. Morrison's "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) names the mechanism. Sociology has to track the production to analyse it. An abolitionist sociology would describe institutional racism without naming the categorising operation institutional racism performs.

Third, anti-racist politics requires racial measurement. CRT's diagnostic work depends on the racial category: interest convergence requires data showing differential treatment; counter-storytelling requires identifying the subjugated standpoint. Removing the category does not remove the inequality but does remove the political tools for naming it.

Four reform moves.

First, treat the category as historical and constructed. "Races do not exist" biologically (slide 30); Du Bois's "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact" (Du Bois, 1940: 153) is the right framing.

Second, distinguish use from reification. Sociology can use racial categories without claiming they track natural kinds; the same logic applies to class and gender.

Third, design categorisation to be self-undermining. The category exists because the disparity exists; dismantle the disparity, and the category becomes unnecessary. Gilroy's "anti-toxins" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) is the right horizon.

Fourth, integrate Morrison and Du Bois. Track the affective work (Morrison) alongside the structural reproduction (Du Bois, CRT).

Three objections. First, Gilroy: any use of race entrenches it. Reply: not measuring race makes it invisible, not absent. Reflexive use, not abolition.

Second, colour-blindness: race-conscious sociology contributes to the divisions it studies. CRT's reply: colour-blindness in a racially structured society protects the structure. Generic affirmative-action debates show the trade-off.

Third, methodological: race in survey data conflates self-identification, ascription and ancestry. The right response is refinement, not abolition.

Du Bois's "Equality in law is not enough" applies. Formal abolition of racial categorisation in law does not abolish the social fact. Cultural references like *Get Out* and *Selma*, and Brexit-era and Trump-era policy framings, show the gap between formal de-categorisation and substantive structure.

Sociology should work towards a future where race is no longer needed analytically, because the infrastructure has been dismantled. The work is generational. In the meantime, use the category reflexively, with the aim of dissolving the conditions that require it.

Sociology cannot abolish race in the present without losing the traction needed to measure and contest disparities, but it can work towards a future where the category becomes unnecessary. The intermediate path is reflexive use: race as historical and constructed, held analytically rather than reified. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy support this. The end of race is a horizon, not a starting move.

### Mock 13. Discuss the role of the Sorrow Songs and Black cultural production in Du Bois's *Souls*.

In this essay I discuss the role of the Sorrow Songs and Black cultural production in Du Bois's *Souls of Black Folk* (1903). I argue they function as the affirmative counterpoint to the diagnostic of the veil and double consciousness. Where double consciousness names the doubling imposed by the colour line, the Sorrow Songs name the cultural production by which the doubled subject creates something irreducible to the dominant world.

The Forethought announces the architectural role of the Sorrow Songs: "Before each chapter, as now printed, stands a bar of the Sorrow Songs, some echo of haunting melody from the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past" (Du Bois, 1903: Forethought). Each chapter opens with two epigraphs: a fragment of European poetry and a bar of musical notation from a Black spiritual. Du Bois's editorial decision is programmatic: the European canon and the Black spiritual share the page.

The Sorrow Songs return at chapter ends: "there is no true American music but the wild sweet melodies of the Negro slave; the American fairy tales and folk-lore are Indian and African" (Du Bois, 1903: 7). The Sorrow Songs are evidence that the Black presence in America is constitutive, not residual.

Three functions follow.

First, evidential. The Sorrow Songs are Du Bois's evidence that Black life under the veil produces something distinctive. America's defining music is Black; America's self-image as white obscures this. The cultural production rebuts the white world's "amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

Second, methodological. *Souls* is doubled. Du Bois writes as sociologist (the 1898 labour-statute analysis) and as man of letters (chapter epigraphs, lyrical passages, closing chapter on the Sorrow Songs). The doubling of method matches the doubling of consciousness. Black cultural production is the medium through which Du Bois's analysis becomes possible.

Third, normative. The Sorrow Songs anchor Du Bois's hope. *Souls* ends with the demand that "two world-races may give each to each those characteristics both so sadly lack" (Du Bois, 1903: 7). They are "the soul-beauty of a race which his larger audience despised" (Du Bois, 1903: 4); they make the merger into "a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) worth pursuing.

The lecture presents Du Bois as the indispensable Black sociologist of modernity; the Sorrow Songs show he is also a Black artist of modernity. The "creative, communicative use of the memory of slavery" Gilroy (1993: 55) names as central to the Black Atlantic is exemplified here: a musical practice born under slavery becomes the cultural matter of post-Emancipation Black life.

Gilroy. *The Black Atlantic* treats Black music as the bar of the Sorrow Songs writ large, extended to a transnational Black cultural circuit. Du Bois's textual practice prefigures Gilroy's theorisation: Black culture is constitutive of, not derivative from, modernity. The polemic that "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56) sharpens Du Bois's musical evidence: the music welled up from souls held under that terror.

Morrison. Her observation that "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like" (Morrison, 2017: 37) provides the contemporary contrast. Du Bois's epigraphs widen the view; Morrison's complaint is that contemporary media narrow. L10 develops the widening into a politics of looking: hooks (1992) on the "oppositional gaze" treats Black visual and cultural production as active counter-seeing, the heir of Du Bois's Sorrow Songs in a media-saturated present. Cultural references like *Get Out* and *Selma* operate in this tradition.

The objection that the Sorrow Songs risk romanticising Black suffering misfires. Du Bois keeps suffering and cultural production tightly coupled. The songs "welled up from black souls in the dark past" (Du Bois, 1903: Forethought); they are not separable from the conditions that produced them.

A second objection, that Black gifts to America assimilate the political demand, Du Bois resists. The demand is substantive equality: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). The cultural offering is part of the case for equality, not a substitute.

The Sorrow Songs serve three roles: evidential, methodological, and normative. Du Bois's text prefigures the Black Atlantic; Morrison's worry about media narrowing finds in Du Bois's editorial widening one of its remedies. The Sorrow Songs are the affirmative half of *Souls*: not denial of the veil, but proof that the veiled subject creates.

### Mock 14. What does Gilroy mean by saying race is modernity's "most pernicious signature"?

In this essay I explain what Gilroy means by race as "modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). The phrase carries three claims: racial categorisation is constitutive of modernity, integrally damaging, and articulated in a grammar linking science, nationality and political power.

Gilroy writes: "Though it preceded the distinctive quality of sociality we distinguish as modern, the order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature. It articulated reason and unreason. It knitted together science and superstition. Raciology required that enlightenment and myth be intertwined. 'Race' and nationality supplied the logic and mechanism of their interconnections" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).

Three components.

First, the "signature" metaphor. A signature is both authorship and authenticity. Modernity authored race; race authenticates modernity. Race is not a by-product but a defining mark.

Second, "most pernicious." Modernity has many signatures: capitalism, industrial production, bureaucratic administration. Among these, race is the most damaging. Scientific racism authorised slavery, colonial extraction and genocidal violence; the lecture's lineage from Linnaeus (1735) through Blumenbach to the 1933 photograph traces the perniciousness.

Third, articulation. Race "articulates reason and unreason." Modernity presents itself as reason over myth; race shows modern reason produced scientific racism and modern enlightenment produced racial myths.

The geopolitical dimension. "'race' and nationality supplied the logic and mechanism of their interconnections" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). The European nation-state defined itself racially (against the colonised, against the slaves); the racial science of Blumenbach and his successors supplied the categorisation; colonial bureaucracy enforced it. Modernity's national-rational form has race built into its joints.

Du Bois supplies legal-economic evidence. The slave statutes "from Massachusetts to Georgia" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4) were the original signing. The racial line was inscribed in colonial law to organise labour; modernity then read it as natural. *Dusk of Dawn* exposes the trick: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136); the signature was signed by power, not nature.

Morrison supplies the affective dimension. "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). The signature is not just legal-administrative but identarian. The affective work of othering ("a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal," Morrison, 2017: 29) maintains the signature even when the legal ink has faded.

Three implications.

First, periodisation. If race is modernity's signature, then modernity cannot be dated from rational achievements alone without dating it from racial atrocities. Slide 28: "Africa was not on the side-lines of the West and the development of modernity."

Second, critique. "Racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56) follows. Rationality and racial terror rose together. The L2 decolonial pairing is direct: Dussel (1993) treats "European modernity" as "based on violence" against its colonial underside, while Howard French (2021) shows African labour and African gold subsidising the so-called rational European centuries. Gilroy's "signature" is the racial form of the violence Dussel names and the labour-extraction French quantifies.

Third, normative. Ending race requires changing modernity itself. Gilroy's demand to "rewrite history and modernity from these positions" (slide 27) follows. Adding Black voices does not change the signature; the signature has to be erased.

Two objections. First, the framing is too totalising. Reply: race "may be" the most pernicious signature, not the only one. But any aspect that does not contend with the racial signature is not yet fully theorised.

Second, the metaphor essentialises modernity. Reply: Gilroy treats modernity as a project with characteristic signatures, not an essence. The signature is a historical product, not an essential property.

Contemporary purchase. The revision document's CRT framing of "racial progress is always pushed back" (§8) gives the signature claim contemporary content: where formal racial inclusion is granted, the signature reappears in the next cycle. Slide 9's 1868 plate and 1933 photograph are visual versions; Morrison's "routine media presentations" (Morrison, 2017: 37) is its quieter form. Black Lives Matter after George Floyd and Breonna Taylor, Brexit-era hostile-environment debates, and Trump-era policy framings show the signature re-signed today.

Gilroy's claim carries constitution (race is integral to modernity), perniciousness (race is the most damaging defining mark), and articulation (race is the seam where reason and unreason meet). The implications are periodisation, critique and normative direction. Du Bois supplies legal-economic evidence; Morrison supplies affective machinery; CRT supplies institutional specification. Race is modernity's most pernicious signature because modernity could not have been what it became without it.
