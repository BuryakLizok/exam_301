# L8 — A Question of Experience (2): Race and Social Theory

## 1. Topic Overview

This lecture moves the question of "experience" from gender (L7) to race, showing how sociology has had to confront its own complicity in racial classification while also drawing on Black theorists who treat race as a constitutive feature of modernity rather than a side issue. The argument unfolds in three parts. First, the lecturer traces scientific racism from Linnaeus (1735) and Blumenbach (1752-1840) through nineteenth-century craniometry to twentieth-century eugenics, showing that race had no biological referent before this period — the word's etymology (French "racine," Arabic "ras") simply meant root or beginning. Only when classification was tied to power did "race" produce the pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy that justified slavery and colonialism. Following Foucault, the lecture frames this as power-knowledge: science "observed" what its own categories had already produced.

Second, W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963) is presented as the indispensable Black sociologist of modernity. Born five years after the Emancipation Proclamation, the first Black PhD from Harvard, Du Bois asks in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) what it means that legal emancipation produced no substantive freedom. His answer reorganises the sociology of selfhood. The veil separates Black from white worlds; double consciousness names the "peculiar sensation" of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others"; twoness — "an American, a Negro" — refuses the unitary actor presupposed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. In *Dusk of Dawn* (1940/1968) Du Bois refines this by insisting that race is not biology but social fact: the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia." This anticipates the racial realism of Critical Race Theory.

Third, the lecture turns to contemporary social theory through Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy. Morrison's *The Origin of Others* (2017) treats race as "an effect of power" and "Othering" as a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal — Americanness, she argues, "remains color for many people." Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) and *Race Ends Here* (1998) refuse to bracket Africa as marginal to modernity: the slave trade is constitutive of Western modernity, racial terror is "cheerfully complicit" with occidental rationality, and we must rewrite history from the slaves' standpoint rather than merely include Black voices. The lecturer concludes: races do not exist, but the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of race do, and they saturate institutions, media, art and ways of seeing.

The pay-off for SC301 is methodological. The lecture argues that mainstream social theory — Weber, Durkheim, Goffman — built its account of the modern self on a tacit white subject, treating racialised experience as a deviation or a special case. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy reverse this: the Black experience of modernity is the most revealing standpoint because modernity itself was forged through slavery, racial classification and colonial domination. The colour line is, in Du Bois's famous Forethought, "the problem of the Twentieth Century" — and, given Windrush, Black Lives Matter and persistent racial inequalities, of the twenty-first.

## 2. Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Scientific racism**: The nineteenth-century project of tying "race" to biology, producing pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy. Originates with Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) and Blumenbach's five-race skull typology (1775).
- **Power-knowledge** (Foucault, applied by Gilroy): Classification "produced" the very races it claimed to "observe." Race was "a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252).
- **Eugenics**: Early twentieth-century pseudo-science of "improving" racial stock, basis of Nazi racial policy and twentieth-century sterilisation programmes.
- **The colour line**: Du Bois's diagnostic of the twentieth century — "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line" (*Souls*, Forethought, p. 1).
- **The veil**: The social barrier separating Black from white worlds; Black people are "shut out from their world by a vast veil" (*Souls*, p. 4).
- **Second sight**: The compensatory gift of those born "with a veil" — a critical perspective on the dominant world they are excluded from yet forced to inhabit.
- **Double consciousness**: "This sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (*Souls*, p. 5).
- **Twoness**: "One ever feels his twoness, — an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (*Souls*, p. 5).
- **The talented tenth**: Du Bois's later concept — the educated Black elite whose role is to lead racial uplift (referenced in his broader programme).
- **Racial realism**: Du Bois's view (later taken up by CRT, especially Derrick Bell) that race is not biology but social fact — defined by unequal treatment, Jim Crow, segregation.
- **Othering** (Morrison): The construction of racial outsiders to stabilise the dominant group's self-image; "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (*Origin of Others*, p. 29).
- **Whiteness studies**: Analysis of whiteness as a form of unmarked power; Americanness "remains color for many people" (Morrison, p. 17).
- **The Black Atlantic**: Gilroy's name for the transnational cultural and political space formed by slavery's middle passage — Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Caribbean as one circuit.
- **Epidermalization** (Fanon, via Gilroy): The colonial process of making bodies meaningful by endowing them with "qualities of 'colour'" (*Race Ends Here*, p. 254).
- **Interest convergence** (Derrick Bell, CRT): Racial progress occurs only when it aligns with white interests.
- **Counter-storytelling** (CRT): Narrative methods that reveal subjugated Black epistemologies erased by dominant historiography.
- **Primal history of modernity** (Gilroy after Benjamin): Modernity rewritten "from the slaves' point of view" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 55).
- **Tertium quid**: Du Bois's term for the "intermediate category between animal and human" into which Black people were forced (*Souls*, cited Gilroy, p. 55).

## 4. Debates & Tensions

- **Biological vs social conceptions of race**: Scientific racism insists race is biologically real; Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all reject this but disagree on what to put in its place. Du Bois retains "race" as a cultural and historical fact; Gilroy presses towards "race ends here" and the abolition of raciology; Morrison occupies a middle ground, naming race as power-effect.
- **Inclusion vs rewriting**: Should we "add" Black voices to existing modernity narratives or rewrite modernity from the standpoint of the enslaved? Gilroy explicitly demands the latter, citing Walter Benjamin's "primal history."
- **Unitary vs split self**: Du Bois's double consciousness explicitly contests the unified actor of Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. Can sociology accommodate a self that is constitutively riven?
- **Racism as individual prejudice vs structural condition**: Lecture conclusion stresses that this "is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist" but how the social world is constituted on unrecognised forms of racism in institutions, art, music and ways of seeing.
- **Pessimism vs progress**: CRT (after Bell) treats the USA as still white-supremacist; the myth of racial progress masks "interest convergence." Du Bois oscillates — Emancipation has not delivered, yet *Souls* ends on the possibility that two world-races may give each to each.
- **Universalism vs particularism**: Gilroy critiques bourgeois humanism for repairing itself by adding the figures Du Bois called "tertium quid"; he wants conceptions of selfhood that are "forever disassociated from the psychological and epistemic correlates of racial subordination" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 56).
- **Visibility and the body**: Gilroy in *Race Ends Here* asks whether nano-biology and digital imaging make race "an after-image" — vestigial. Yet the lecture insists epidermal experience (Fanon) still organises everyday life.
- **Africa as periphery vs Africa as constitutive**: Gilroy explicitly contests the view that Africa was on the side-lines of Western modernity.

## 5. Examples & Applications

- **Jim Crow segregation (1877-1965)** as Du Bois's empirical anchor for racial realism — the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 153).
- **Redlining and US housing discrimination**: HOLC maps from the 1930s denying mortgage credit to Black neighbourhoods; structural wealth gap persists.
- **British Empire's "civilising mission"**: rhetoric of cultural uplift justifying colonial extraction; Macaulay's 1835 Minute on Indian Education.
- **Windrush scandal (2018)**: British state's wrongful deportation of Caribbean migrants invited under the 1948 British Nationality Act — Gilroyan reminder that the Black Atlantic produces postcolonial Britain.
- **Black Lives Matter (2013-)**: movement after Trayvon Martin, George Floyd, Eric Garner, Breonna Taylor — counter-storytelling against police killings.
- **Black maternal mortality**: in the UK, Black women are roughly 3-4 times more likely to die in pregnancy than white women (MBRRACE-UK reports), in the USA approximately 3 times more likely (CDC) — racial realism in healthcare.
- **UK Stop & Search**: Black people are 7-9 times more likely to be stopped than white people (Home Office); Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry found the Metropolitan Police "institutionally racist."
- **Affirmative action**: *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke* (1978) upheld limited race-conscious admissions; *SFFA v. Harvard* (2023) struck them down — interest convergence reversing.
- **Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972)**: US Public Health Service withholding treatment from Black men — scientific racism's afterlife in research ethics.
- **Minstrelsy and Black cultural appropriation**: nineteenth-century blackface performance; Gilroy's "pas de deux between … Michael Jordan and Bugs Bunny" (*Race Ends Here*, p. 252) as global commodification of Black culture.
- **Trump on "Mexican-Americans"** (slide 25): Morrison's argument that Americanness is colour-coded directly applies to twenty-first century border rhetoric.
- **Modern slavery**: UK Modern Slavery Act 2015 acknowledges roughly 100,000 victims; carceral capitalism and US prison labour (Thirteenth Amendment exception clause) — CRT's "school-to-prison pipeline."

## 8. Past-Q Essay Answers

### Past Q 2024 — How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to two theorists.

In this essay I argue that European modernity was not merely accompanied by unequal race relations but constituted by them, and I make this case through W.E.B. Du Bois and Paul Gilroy. I treat the question's word "benefit" seriously: the wealth, philosophy and self-image of European modernity were materially and conceptually subsidised by the racial subordination of African peoples.

I begin with the economic and legal foundation that Du Bois sets out. In "The Study of the Negro Problems" (1898), Du Bois observes that "in the latter part of the seventeenth and early in the eighteenth centuries, the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, *Annals of the American Academy*, 1898, pp. 3-4). The supply problem was solved by importing Africans and creating a racial category through statute, not biology — "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898, p. 3). I take this as a foundational point: race emerged as a legal-economic device because slavery was more profitable than indenture, and only afterwards congealed into the supposed natural fact that scientific racism would later "discover." European modernity benefited twice over: from the unpaid labour itself and from the ideological asset of being able to think of itself as the racially enlightened standard. As Du Bois insists in *Dusk of Dawn*, "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, *Dusk of Dawn*, p. 136). Modernity profited from a fiction it knew to be flimsy.

Gilroy radicalises this insight. In *The Black Atlantic* he argues that we must abandon the comforting story that slavery was a regrettable side-business of Europe's rise. "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 54) — it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" and "has retained a central place in the historical memories of the black Atlantic" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 55). I read Gilroy as showing that the Atlantic triangle of Liverpool, the Gold Coast and the Caribbean was the laboratory of modern bureaucratic management, modern industrial finance and modern consumption — sugar, cotton, tobacco. Crucially, his polemic against Weberian rationalism cuts to the heart of the benefit question: "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 56). Modernity did not benefit despite slavery; rationality and slavery rose together.

I then trace the cultural and epistemic dividend. The lecture's account of scientific racism — from Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) to Blumenbach's five-race skulls — shows that European science gained authority and funding precisely by classifying non-European peoples as inferior. This is Gilroy's point: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252). Knowledge production was an arm of empire. Concrete examples make the dividend visible. The Lancashire cotton mills depended on the slave-grown cotton of Mississippi and Alabama; the wealth of Bristol, Liverpool and Glasgow was built on the Atlantic trade; the British Museum's collections were filled by colonial extraction. Even the Enlightenment's universalist humanism — the bourgeois humanism Gilroy critiques — relied on excluding the people Du Bois calls a "tertium quid" (cited Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 55), an intermediate category between human and animal.

A counter-argument is that modernity was a generally progressive movement and that slavery was a pre-modern residue, abolished by modernity's own reforming impulse. I reject this. The legal innovations that produced racial slavery — chattel status, hereditary servitude, the "one-drop rule" — were not pre-modern relics but modern inventions. Emancipation, when it came, was incomplete: Du Bois observes that "the Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (*Souls*, p. 5). The Windrush scandal, the persistent racial wealth gap, the disproportionate Black maternal mortality and the Macpherson Report's finding of institutional racism in the Metropolitan Police all show that the benefits of unequal race relations continue to accrue.

Finally, I press Gilroy's normative implication. If modernity benefited from slavery, then a sociology that simply "adds" Black voices to a fundamentally white narrative is inadequate. We must, with Gilroy, attempt "the primal history of modernity … reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 55). This is not merely a matter of reparative recognition but of analytical accuracy: the slaves' standpoint reveals what the masters' standpoint conceals.

I conclude that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in three reinforcing ways — economically through unpaid labour, culturally through the prestige of "scientific" racial classification, and politically through the self-image of a free Europe defined against unfree others. Du Bois and Gilroy together show that race and modernity are inextricably linked and that the dividend has never fully been repaid.

### Past Q 2025/2023 — What is meant by 'scientific racism'? How have sociologists tried to counter this concept?

In this essay I define scientific racism as the nineteenth-century project of grounding racial classification in supposedly biological fact, and I show how sociologists from Du Bois through Morrison to Gilroy have countered it by treating race as a social, historical and political construction.

I begin with definition. The lecture is explicit that the word "race" had no biological referent before the nineteenth century. Its etymology is benign — the French "racine" (root) or Arabic "ras" (head, beginning). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology and seemed to refer to a particular biological group or type or category" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Scientific racism is the doctrine that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3). I trace its origin through two figures. Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) divided Homo sapiens into Europaeus, Afer, Asiaticus and Americanus, claiming not only morphological but also temperamental and psychological differences. Blumenbach (1752-1840) refined this into a five-race typology — Caucasian, Mongolian, Malayan, Negroid, American — supposedly evidenced by skull shape. This is the lineage of nineteenth-century craniometry, of Galton's eugenics, of the Nazi racial measurements photographed in the 1933 image on the lecture slide, and of the Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972).

I then identify what makes the project specifically "scientific." Following Foucault, the lecture frames scientific racism as power-knowledge: "Science claimed to 'observe' racial differences. The classifications they produced appeared to give them knowledge … This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 8). Gilroy makes the same point: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252), and the "truths of race were produced 'performatively' from the hat that biological science provided, like so many startled rabbits in front of a noisy, eager, imperial crowd" (p. 253). Scientific racism is not the application of neutral science to racial questions; it is the manufacture of racial reality by classificatory institutions tied to colonial and slaveholding power.

The first sociological counter-move is Du Bois's. In *Dusk of Dawn* he concedes that biology cannot ground race — "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (p. 136) — yet refuses the easy conclusion that race is therefore unreal. Instead he relocates its reality: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact … the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 153). I read this as the first sociological anti-essentialism about race: the reality of race lies in legal and social practices of unequal treatment, not in skulls or genes. This is the move that Critical Race Theory later names "racial realism" — race is real as social fact, fictive as biology.

The second counter-move is Toni Morrison's. In *The Origin of Others* she presses the question: "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter?" (Morrison, *Origin of Others*, p. 15). Her answer is twofold. First, citing Bruce Baum, "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, p. 25). Second, race functions through Othering — "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, p. 29). I take this to identify the affective and identarian motor of scientific racism: the dominant group needs racial others to stabilise its own normality. Whiteness, on this account, is not the absence of race but the unmarked form of racial power.

The third counter-move is Gilroy's. In *Race Ends Here* (1998) he argues that scientific racism rested on a specific perceptual regime — the "Euclidean anatomy" of comparative racial measurement — that has been outflanked by molecular biology and digital imaging. "Scientific and biological, historical and cultural, rational and irrational, skin, bone and even blood are no longer primary referents of racial discourse" (Gilroy, p. 254). Where racial difference vanishes at the resolution of MRI or genome sequencing, "'race' might best be approached as an after-image" (p. 253). I treat Gilroy's polemic as the sharpest scientific counter: the science that produced scientific racism has now superseded its own object.

There are caveats. Counter-arguing scientific racism in the lab does not abolish racism in the street. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all stress that racism persists as institutional and cultural fact even after biology repudiates it. The lecture conclusion makes this point sharply: "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do … forms of racism … are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 32). Concrete examples confirm this: the UK Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry found "institutional racism" in policing; UK Stop & Search ratios remain wildly disproportionate; Black maternal mortality in the UK and US is several times the white rate; the Windrush scandal weaponised state bureaucracy against legitimate Black British citizens.

I conclude that scientific racism is the modern project of dressing racial hierarchy in biological clothing, and that sociologists have countered it by relocating race's reality to social structure (Du Bois), to the affective work of othering (Morrison) and to the changing technologies of vision (Gilroy). The counter is unfinished because the legacy of scientific racism is sedimented in institutions and habits of perception that survive the discrediting of its biological premises.

### Past Q 2022 — Drawing from at least two of W.E.B Du Bois, Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy — discuss the implications of "race" for contemporary society.

In this essay I draw on Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy to argue that "race," though biologically empty, has profound implications for contemporary society in three registers: the constitution of the self, the production of national belonging, and the structure of global modernity.

I begin with the implication for selfhood, drawing on Du Bois. In *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) Du Bois introduces double consciousness as the experiential consequence of living within the colour line: "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 5). The self is constitutively split — "One ever feels his twoness, — an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (p. 5). For contemporary society, the implication is that the unitary actor presupposed by mainstream sociology (Weber's rational actor, Goffman's strategic self, Garfinkel's competent member) is unavailable to racialised subjects. Consider the everyday racialised question of "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) — it imposes a doubling that the unmarked white subject does not experience. The Windrush scandal of 2018 dramatises this: Black British citizens who arrived between 1948 and 1971, whose Britishness should have been unquestionable, were re-coded as foreign by a hostile-environment bureaucracy. Du Bois's veil is a UK Home Office form.

I then turn to Morrison for the implication for national belonging. In *The Origin of Others* she observes that "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, *Origin of Others*, p. 17). National identity is not just a passport but a racialised performance. I take this to illuminate Trump-era and post-Trump US politics — the rhetoric around "Mexican-Americans," DACA, family separations at the southern border — but it cuts equally in the UK, where the Hostile Environment, Prevent and the Rwanda asylum policy all depend on racialised assumptions about who belongs. Morrison adds that race operates through othering: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (p. 29). Contemporary right-wing populism uses Black, Muslim and migrant bodies precisely to stabilise an anxious white-majority self.

I take the third implication from Gilroy: the structural implication for modernity itself. In *The Black Atlantic* and *Race Ends Here*, Gilroy refuses to treat slavery and colonialism as side-issues. "The order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252). The cotton mills of Manchester, the sugar of Bristol, the Royal African Company — all built modernity through the Atlantic slave system. The implication for contemporary society is that the racial wealth gap, the geography of redlined US cities, the persistence of Black maternal mortality and the structure of global supply chains all reproduce the asymmetries that the Black Atlantic established. Gilroy's normative claim follows: we cannot fix this by adding Black voices to a fundamentally white modernity — we must reconstruct modernity from the slaves' point of view (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 55).

I consider examples across all three thinkers. Black Lives Matter, after Trayvon Martin (2012), Eric Garner (2014) and George Floyd (2020), exemplifies Du Boisian double consciousness politicised — the demand that the world look at Black death with the eyes Black people have always seen it with. Counter-storytelling, in the CRT vocabulary, is double consciousness turned outward. The Macpherson Report (1999) institutionalised the diagnosis of "institutional racism" in UK policing after the Stephen Lawrence inquiry; UK Stop & Search disparities (Black people stopped 7-9 times more often than white) make Morrison's "effect of power" empirically measurable. The Black Atlantic continues to circulate culturally — grime, drill, dancehall, Afrobeats — but is asymmetrically commodified, as Gilroy notes with his Michael Jordan-Bugs Bunny example (*Race Ends Here*, p. 252).

I anticipate the objection that race is a declining force in a globalising, multicultural world. I think this is wrong on the evidence. The 2008 financial crisis disproportionately destroyed Black household wealth in the US through subprime targeting; the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic killed Black, Asian and minority ethnic Britons at significantly higher rates than white Britons; the 2023 SFFA v Harvard ruling reversed affirmative action just as racial inequalities widened. As Du Bois warned, equality in law is not enough — "Work, culture, liberty, — all these we need, not singly but together" (*Souls*, p. 7).

I conclude that race has three deep implications for contemporary society. Through Du Bois, it shapes the very structure of racialised selfhood, producing a doubled, second-sighted experience that mainstream sociology must learn from. Through Morrison, it shapes national belonging by making whiteness the unmarked currency of full citizenship. Through Gilroy, it shapes global modernity by reproducing the asymmetries laid down by the Atlantic slave system. Race, in short, is biologically empty and sociologically full.

### Past Q 2021 — Du Bois asked: "How does it feel to be a problem?" Outline and assess his response to this question.

In this essay I outline Du Bois's response to his own question and then assess it on three criteria: descriptive accuracy, conceptual originality, and political adequacy.

Du Bois opens *The Souls of Black Folk* with the question and immediately defers it: "To the real question, How does it feel to be a problem? I answer seldom a word" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 2). The question is not really a question put to him; it is a polite circumlocution for the white interlocutor's racialised gaze. Du Bois's response unfolds across the rest of the chapter in three moves: an autobiographical scene that names the experience, a conceptual formulation of double consciousness, and a programmatic call for the integration of work, culture and liberty.

The autobiographical scene is the visiting cards incident. As a New England schoolboy, Du Bois recalls a tall newcomer who "refused my card, — refused it peremptorily, with a glance. Then it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (*Souls*, p. 4). I take this to be Du Bois's empirical answer: "being a problem" feels like the sudden, unwilled recognition that one is on the wrong side of an invisible barrier. The metaphor of the veil names this barrier; the metaphor of "second-sight," the compensatory perceptual gift it bestows: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world, — a world which yields him no true self-consciousness, but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world" (*Souls*, p. 4).

The conceptual formulation is double consciousness: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (*Souls*, p. 5). Crucially, Du Bois insists that this doubling is not a deficit to be repaired by assimilation but a structural feature of African-American life. "One ever feels his twoness, — an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (*Souls*, p. 5). The history of African America is the history of this strife — "this longing to attain self-conscious manhood, to merge his double self into a better and truer self" (*Souls*, p. 5). The aim is not to abandon either pole: "He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (p. 5).

The programmatic move is the demand for substantive rather than formal equality. Despite the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the Fifteenth Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (*Souls*, p. 5). Du Bois ends with the famous demand: "Work, culture, liberty, — all these we need, not singly but together, not successively but together" (p. 7). The implication is that being a problem feels like being formally free but substantively unfree, formally a citizen but substantively excluded.

I now assess. On descriptive accuracy, Du Bois's response is remarkable. The visiting cards scene captures the everyday, often petty, mechanism by which racial difference is communicated — not by lynching, not by Jim Crow law, but by a glance and a refused card. The persistence of microaggressions in contemporary qualitative research on Black students in PWIs (predominantly white institutions) confirms this. The "Where are you really from?" question (lecture slide 4) is the contemporary equivalent of the refused card.

On conceptual originality, double consciousness is one of the most important contributions of American social theory. The lecture is explicit that it differs from "the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel." Du Bois anticipates Mead's social self, Lacan's mirror stage, Fanon's epidermalisation, and Goffman's interaction order — but unlike them he ties the doubling to race and power rather than treating it as a universal feature of selfhood. This specificity is the originality.

On political adequacy, I find Du Bois's response more ambivalent. The closing demand for the merger of African and American selves can sound assimilationist — he insists he "would not Africanize America" (*Souls*, p. 5). Later thinkers, especially in Black Power and Afrocentric traditions, would press much harder against the American pole. Gilroy raises a different concern: the demand to merge the two selves may understate the irreducibility of the rupture that slavery represents. Gilroy's "primal history of modernity … reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 55) implies that some doublings cannot and should not be merged.

There are limitations to acknowledge. Du Bois's frame is gendered — "self-conscious manhood," "two warring ideals in one dark body" — which obscures the specifically gendered racial experience that later thinkers (bell hooks, Crenshaw) would name. His class politics (the talented tenth) has been criticised as elitist. His American focus underplays the transnational frame that Gilroy's Black Atlantic recovers. Yet the analytical core — that racial subordination produces a doubled, partial, mediated selfhood — survives these critiques.

I conclude that Du Bois's response to "How does it feel to be a problem?" is descriptively acute (the veil and the refused card), conceptually transformative (double consciousness and second sight), and politically incomplete but generative (the demand for substantive freedom). It feels, he tells us, like always seeing yourself through someone else's eyes; the response is to fight for a world in which that doubling becomes optional rather than imposed.

## 9. Mock-Q Essay Answers

### Mock 1 — "Double consciousness is no longer the right concept for the twenty-first century." Discuss.

I argue against this claim. Double consciousness remains the right concept for the twenty-first century, though it must be extended to cover globalised, digital and intersectional contexts that Du Bois did not anticipate. I make the case in three steps: by recovering Du Bois's original formulation, by considering objections, and by showing the concept's continuing analytical power.

Du Bois introduces double consciousness in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) as "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 5). Its two key features are the externality of the measuring gaze (the world's tape, not the self's) and the unreconciled twoness — "an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (p. 5). It is, importantly, not a transitional condition to be resolved by assimilation but a structural feature of life under the colour line, separated from the white world by a "vast veil" (p. 4).

I consider three objections to its continued relevance. First, that the legal framework has changed: Jim Crow is gone, civil rights are codified. Second, that identity politics has fragmented the unitary "Negro/American" pole into hyphenated and intersectional identities that double consciousness does not capture. Third, that Gilroy himself has suggested race is now "an after-image" (*Race Ends Here*, p. 253) — vestigial in the age of molecular biology and digital culture.

I rebut each. The legal change is real but partial. Du Bois already saw this: "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (*Souls*, p. 5). The Windrush scandal of 2018, the Macpherson Report's 1999 finding of "institutional racism" in the Metropolitan Police, the persistence of UK Stop & Search disparities, the disproportionate Black COVID-19 mortality, and the structural racial wealth gap in the US all show that formal equality leaves the veil intact. As Du Bois insists in *Dusk of Dawn*, "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (p. 153) — race is real as social practice. Whatever the law says, racialised subjects continue to measure themselves by a world that watches in contempt or pity.

On the fragmentation objection, I agree that "Negro/American" is too tidy. But double consciousness was never meant to be a fixed binary; it was a description of the doubling produced by being on the wrong side of a colour line. Intersectionality (Crenshaw) and standpoint epistemology (Patricia Hill Collins) extend rather than replace the concept. The Black woman in the Met or the British Asian Muslim in Prevent-monitored Birmingham experiences not two but three or four mirrors. Double consciousness scales.

On the after-image objection, I read Gilroy more carefully. He argues that the perceptual regime of nineteenth-century scientific racism — measuring skulls, dissecting bodies — is genuinely outflanked by molecular and digital techniques. But he does not abandon the experiential reality of racialised perception; he relocates it to "epidermalization," the colonial process by which bodies are made meaningful through "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 254). Epidermalisation is Fanon's update of double consciousness, and Gilroy's whole point is that we need to find its "contemporary analogs" — "deep within" the body, in genomic profiling and predictive policing. Double consciousness is not retired; it is digitised.

I support this with concrete examples. The Black Lives Matter movement (2013-) is double consciousness politicised: George Floyd's death was filmed because Black observers know that the white world will not see what they see without evidence. Social media puts the white gaze in every Black pocket. The phenomenon of "code switching" in workplaces, documented in countless qualitative studies, is double consciousness in real time. The 2023 SFFA v Harvard decision struck down affirmative action just as the racial wealth gap widened — interest convergence (Derrick Bell) collapsing precisely because the white interest is no longer aligned. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate in the UK and US) confirms that healthcare itself is a tape by which Black bodies are measured.

I also extend the concept beyond African-American experience. British Muslims under Prevent live a doubling between the security state's surveillance and their own communities. Indigenous Australian students in the Closing the Gap framework live a doubling between settler-colonial benchmarks and Aboriginal life. Du Bois's framework is more transportable, not less, than it was in 1903.

A residual worry is that double consciousness is too negative — that it pathologises racialised selfhood. Du Bois himself anticipates this by pairing double consciousness with "second-sight" (p. 4): the gift of seeing what the dominant world cannot see. This is the methodological asset that standpoint epistemology and CRT's counter-storytelling formalise. Far from pathologising, double consciousness names a critical perceptual advantage.

I conclude that double consciousness remains the right concept for the twenty-first century. It captures the doubling produced by the colour line, extends naturally to intersectional and transnational contexts, and is empirically supported by ongoing racial disparities in policing, healthcare and wealth. The concept needs updating, not retiring.

### Mock 2 — To what extent does Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* successfully displace Eurocentric accounts of modernity?

I argue that Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* substantially displaces Eurocentric accounts of modernity, but only when read as a methodological intervention rather than as a finished alternative narrative. I assess his success in three areas: theoretical reframing, empirical reach, and political-ethical pay-off.

Gilroy's theoretical reframing rests on the claim that Africa was not on the periphery of modernity but central to it. As the lecture summarises, "Africa was not on the side-lines of the West and the development of modernity. The transnational (as evidenced in the slave trade) was always a real part of Western Modernity" (slide 29). In *The Black Atlantic* itself, Gilroy treats plantation slavery as more than labour and racial domination; it "provided the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 54). His most cited line is the polemical claim that "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (p. 56). I take this as a direct displacement of the Weberian story in which modernity is the unfolding of rationalisation, bureaucracy and capitalism. For Gilroy, modernity's rationalisation was racially structured from the start.

The empirical reach is impressive. Gilroy traces transnational circuits of Black music (gospel, blues, jazz, reggae, hip-hop), Black political thought (Frederick Douglass's Scottish lectures, Du Bois's German training, Marcus Garvey's pan-Africanism), and Black religious and aesthetic traditions across Africa, the Americas, the Caribbean and Britain. The "Black Atlantic" names the cultural circuit these movements form. Crucially, he refuses the cultural-nationalist alternative — the Black Atlantic is not Afrocentrism but a genuinely transnational frame that messes up neat national containers. He explicitly criticises the "lazy essentialisms" of post-modern identity politics (*Race Ends Here*, p. 252).

The political-ethical pay-off is Gilroy's most ambitious move. He demands that "the primal history of modernity be reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 55). This is not just inclusion; the lecture insists that "We need to rethink history and modernity and not just 'include' the marginal positions that have been ignored. But to rewrite history and modernity from these positions" (slide 29). The slaves' standpoint, Gilroy claims, yields "an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood and individuation that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (p. 56). This is a normative project: modernity has been narrated wrong and must be re-narrated.

I now weigh his success. On reframing, Gilroy succeeds. After *The Black Atlantic*, it is harder to write Atlantic history without slavery, harder to do British cultural studies without postcolonial migration, harder to do US history without the Middle Passage. The Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (Birmingham), the Stuart Hall lineage and the rise of Black British sociology all confirm the institutional uptake. I take Frank B. Wilderson's Afro-pessimism and Saidiya Hartman's archival work on slavery as continuations.

On empirical reach, Gilroy's success is more uneven. *The Black Atlantic* is brilliant on music and on a canon of Black intellectuals (Douglass, Du Bois, Wright, Baldwin) but lighter on the political-economic infrastructure of Atlantic slavery — sugar, cotton, finance — that historians like Eric Williams, Walter Rodney and more recently Sven Beckert have documented. Critics like Laura Chrisman have argued that his focus on diasporic intellectual culture can underplay African specificity. A Black Atlantic that centres London and New York can re-marginalise Lagos and Accra.

On the political-ethical pay-off, Gilroy succeeds less cleanly. His call to "rewrite modernity" is more programmatic than executed. *The Black Atlantic* gives us a powerful methodological frame and a set of exemplary readings, but the comprehensive re-narration of modernity from the slaves' standpoint remains a project rather than a completed achievement. This is not a failure — single books rarely complete such projects — but it limits the displacement claim.

I consider two counter-arguments. First, the Habermasian defence: modernity has internal resources (Enlightenment universalism, human rights) to correct its racial sins, so we do not need a wholesale rewrite. I reject this on Gilroy's own grounds — he argues that bourgeois humanism is implicated in the rise of scientific racism (*Black Atlantic*, p. 55) and cannot be repaired simply by adding figures Du Bois called "tertium quid" (p. 55). Second, the Wallersteinian objection that world-systems analysis already centred slavery without needing the "Black Atlantic" frame. I think Gilroy's reply is that world-systems analysis still narrated the centre's story, just with the periphery included; the Black Atlantic flips the perspective.

Concrete contemporary examples support Gilroy. The 2020 toppling of Edward Colston's statue in Bristol, the British Museum's continuing battles over the Benin Bronzes, the Black Lives Matter protests in London after George Floyd's death — all show that the Atlantic circuit Gilroy described remains live. The Windrush generation embodies the Black Atlantic in postwar British history. Caribbean Carnival in Notting Hill, grime in Tottenham, and Stormzy at Glastonbury are the cultural continuations.

I conclude that Gilroy successfully displaces Eurocentric accounts of modernity at the methodological and theoretical level, partially at the empirical level, and aspirationally at the normative level. The displacement is not total — modernity is still mostly taught as a Eurocentric story — but the conceptual ground has shifted. After Gilroy, Eurocentric modernity is a position one has to defend, not a default.

### Mock 3 — How does Morrison's account of "othering" extend Du Bois's analysis of race?

I argue that Morrison's account of othering extends Du Bois's analysis of race in three ways: by shifting the analytical focus from the racialised subject to the racialising subject, by foregrounding the affective and identarian mechanics of racial classification, and by integrating media and migration into the picture. The extension is genuine but rests on Du Bois's foundations.

Du Bois's framework, set out in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) and refined in *Dusk of Dawn* (1940), focuses on the experience of being raced. The veil separates Black from white worlds; double consciousness names the experience of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" (*Souls*, p. 5). Du Bois is interested in what it feels like to be a problem — the racialised subject is the centre of his analysis. The white world is present in his account but as the object whose gaze does the doubling, not as the subject whose racial identity is itself an effect.

Morrison's shift is to ask what the white world's racialising does for it. In *The Origin of Others* (2017) she presses the analytical question: "Then what is this other thing — the hostility, the social racism, the Othering" (Morrison, p. 15). Her answer is that racial othering is a stabilising operation for the dominant group: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, p. 29). I take this as Morrison's central extension. Where Du Bois examines the wound, Morrison examines the weapon and its wielder. Whiteness is not the absence of racial position but the active production of a normal self through the construction of abnormal others.

The second extension is the affective dimension. Du Bois names "contempt and pity" (*Souls*, p. 5) as the affects of the racialising gaze but does not theorise them in depth. Morrison, drawing on Bruce Baum, formulates the famous compact statement that "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, p. 25) — and the power in question is identarian and emotional. Othering meets a need; it has a payoff. The hostility "demand[s]/encourage[s]" specific behaviours (p. 15). I read Morrison as building a moral psychology of racism that Du Bois gestures toward but does not develop. Her novels — *Beloved*, *Paradise*, *The Bluest Eye* — explore exactly this affective economy. The Bluest Eye's Pecola Breedlove longs for blue eyes because the racial gaze has taught her to hate her own face — Du Boisian double consciousness rendered as the affective destruction of the Black child.

The third extension is media and migration. Morrison observes that "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like (or ought to look like) and what in fact we are like" (Morrison, p. 37). She also tracks racial othering through immigration: "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, p. 17). I take these as updates Du Bois could not have written. His turn-of-the-century USA was less mediatised and operated with a different immigration regime. Morrison's twentieth and twenty-first century USA — film, television, news, social media, hyphenated immigrant identities — needs a more capacious account, and she supplies it.

The extension is genuine but partial. Three caveats. First, Du Bois's veil already contains the seeds of othering: the white world is unable to see Black life as fully human and must keep it behind the veil to preserve its own self-image. Morrison's contribution is to make this implicit move explicit. Second, Morrison's framing depends on what I take to be Du Boisian premises — that race is not biology, that racial classification serves power, that the racialised subject sees what the racialising subject cannot. Her epigraph could come from *Dusk of Dawn*. Third, Morrison is more pessimistic than Du Bois at his most hopeful. Du Bois's *Souls* ends on the possibility of merging two world-races; Morrison's *Origin of Others* is closer to Gilroy's diagnosis that racial othering is constitutive of modernity.

I trace contemporary applications. The Trump-era rhetoric of "Mexican-Americans," the British "small boats" panic and the Rwanda asylum policy all instantiate Morrison's racial-immigrant othering. Media coverage of refugees in 2022 routinely contrasted "blue-eyed, blonde" Ukrainians with darker-skinned earlier migrants — Morrison's media point in stark relief. The 2020 BLM coverage was unevenly framed in white-majority media, illustrating Morrison's "routine media presentations" thesis. The 2018 Windrush scandal weaponised media and bureaucracy together to render Black British citizens as outsiders.

I also consider how Morrison extends Du Bois's view of whiteness. Du Bois treats whiteness mostly as the position from which the veil is drawn; he does not develop whiteness as an object of study in itself. Morrison, especially in her literary criticism (*Playing in the Dark*, 1992), opens whiteness studies as a sociology of the dominant. This anticipates the work of Ruth Frankenberg, David Roediger and Robin DiAngelo, all of whom treat whiteness as a constructed, racialised position with its own affective and political economy.

I conclude that Morrison extends Du Bois's account in three connected ways: by analysing the racialising as much as the racialised, by elaborating the affective economy of othering, and by integrating media and migration. The extension is built on Du Bois's foundations and rests on Du Boisian premises, but it adds analytical resources that twentieth and twenty-first century racial politics require.

### Mock 4 — "Race is real because racism is real." Discuss with reference to Du Bois.

I argue that this proposition captures Du Bois's mature position on race with remarkable accuracy. Race is not biologically real, Du Bois insists; but it is socially real because the structures of racism — Jim Crow, the colour line, the veil — make it so. I develop this through Du Bois's progression from *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) to *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) and consider its implications.

Early Du Bois, in *Souls*, retains some essentialist vocabulary. He writes of "the ideal of fostering and developing the traits and talents of the Negro" and of "two world-races" (*Souls*, p. 7). But even here the real driver of his analysis is structural rather than essentialist. The reason "being a problem is a strange experience" (*Souls*, p. 2) is not that Black people have a problematic essence — it is that the white world treats them as a problem. The veil that separates Du Bois from his New England schoolmates is drawn by the white girl who refuses his card, not by anything in Du Bois himself (*Souls*, p. 4). Double consciousness is "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (*Souls*, p. 5) — note the "tape of a world": the measuring instrument is external and racist.

Mature Du Bois, in *Dusk of Dawn*, is explicit. "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 136). The phrasing is precisely "race is real because racism is real": colour is trivial, but its determining force is total. He then offers his pragmatic, social definition: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact … the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 153). Race is defined by social practices of differential treatment.

I think this is the most defensible position on race available to social theory. It avoids two errors. The first is racial essentialism — the claim that races are real biological kinds — which scientific racism took as its premise and which the lecture decisively rejects: "It was only in the 19th century that it [race] was incorporated into biology" (slide 3), and even there the categories shifted between Linnaeus's four races and Blumenbach's five and beyond. The second error is what I call the eliminativist illusion — the claim that, because race is not biologically real, we can simply stop talking about it. Du Bois exposes this as wishful thinking: Jim Crow does not disappear because biologists agree that there are no races; redlining does not unwind because the Human Genome Project finds more genetic variation within "races" than between them.

I provide evidence. The Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972) used the social fact of Black race to recruit subjects for unethical research; their long-term effect on Black Americans' medical trust is a social fact created by a fictive biology. The Black-white wealth gap in the US — about ten to one in household wealth — is reproduced by the social facts of historical redlining, GI Bill exclusions, and labour market discrimination, regardless of biology. UK Stop & Search disparities (Black people stopped 7-9 times more often than white) work on social, not biological, race. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate in the UK and US) tracks differential treatment in healthcare, not differential physiology. As Du Bois warned, "Equality in law is not enough" (lecture slide 19); the social reality of race operates beneath and around the law.

I anticipate Gilroy's challenge from *Race Ends Here*: that we should aspire to "silence raciology forever" (Gilroy, p. 255) and that even talking about race may reproduce it. Du Bois's position has resources for this. He never treats racial categories as worth preserving in themselves — they are forced upon the racialised by the racialising. The ideal end-state, as he puts it in *Souls*, is "to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (p. 5) without that combination being a problem. Race is a category we live in because racism puts us there; the goal is a world in which we no longer have to.

I consider Morrison's elaboration. Her formula "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, *Origin of Others*, p. 25) is the same thesis: race exists as the residue of power exerted by othering. Critical Race Theory, especially Derrick Bell, formalises this as "racial realism": the realistic recognition that racism is a permanent feature of US life, which means race is a permanent fact for the racialised.

A possible objection: if race is real because racism is real, then we cannot have a theory of race independent of a theory of racism. I think this is exactly right and is a virtue, not a vice. Race-talk that pretends to be neutral biological description always ends up dressing racial hierarchy as fact. Race-talk that openly tracks racism is more honest and more empirically accurate.

I conclude that "Race is real because racism is real" captures Du Bois's position precisely. Race is socially real, biologically trivial, and reproduced by the structures of racism. The implication is that ending racism is the only way to end race — and that, until that day, sociology must take race seriously as the social fact it has been made into.

### Mock 5 — Discuss the relationship between modernity and slavery, drawing on at least two theorists.

I argue, drawing on Du Bois and Gilroy, that modernity and slavery are not separate phenomena that briefly overlapped; they are co-constituted, with slavery serving as the economic engine, the legal laboratory and the philosophical foil of European modernity. I develop the argument in three connected steps.

I begin with the economic argument, where Du Bois is decisive. In "The Study of the Negro Problems" (1898) he shows that American slavery emerged not from racial ideology but from a labour shortage solved racially: "the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth … In the colonies of the mainland it was answered by the importation of Negroes and indented servants" (Du Bois, 1898, pp. 3-4). Crucially, "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (p. 3) — the racial category followed the economic need. The system that emerged because "of the economic superiority of the slave system" (p. 4) became the engine of plantation capitalism: sugar in the Caribbean, tobacco in Virginia, cotton in the Deep South. The British and American industrial revolutions ran on slave-grown cotton; the financial innovations of the Royal African Company and the South Sea Company processed slave-trade capital; the insurance industry (Lloyd's) underwrote slave ships. Modernity's accumulation phase was a slave phase.

I then move to the legal-political argument. Slavery did not just produce wealth; it produced legal categories. Du Bois notes the "dozens of enactments, from Massachusetts to Georgia" (1898, p. 3) that codified the racial-slavery system. The legal innovations were modern: chattel status, hereditary servitude, race-based statutory restrictions, the Three-Fifths Compromise of the US Constitution (1787). These were not feudal hangovers but modern political technologies. The lecture stresses that the racial state was constructed alongside, not before, modern political institutions.

I then turn to Gilroy for the philosophical argument. *The Black Atlantic* contends that "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" — it "provided the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 54). Crucially, slavery is "essentially precapitalist" in some Marxian accounts but Gilroy refuses this evasion. "Racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (p. 56). This is the most uncomfortable claim in the modernity-slavery debate: that the Enlightenment, scientific reason, bureaucratic administration and humanism developed not in spite of but in conjunction with the Atlantic slave system.

I make this concrete. John Locke, foundational philosopher of liberal modernity, drafted constitutional provisions for the Carolina colony that protected slavery; he held shares in the Royal African Company. Kant's *Physische Geographie* opined about the qualities of Negro skin "and the practical problems it presented when pain had to be inflicted with a split bamboo cane" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 254). Hegel's master-slave dialectic was developed even as he denied Africans' capacity for historical action. Modern liberalism, modern reason and modern racism share an archive.

Gilroy's normative move follows: "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 55). This is not just inclusion. Adding "the figures of black folks who had previously been confined to the intermediate category between animal and human that Du Bois identifies as a 'tertium quid'" (p. 55) does not fix bourgeois humanism. The fixing requires "an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood and individuation that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (p. 56).

I consider contemporary echoes. The persistent racial wealth gap in the US, the disproportionate Black incarceration rate ("school-to-prison pipeline" in CRT vocabulary), modern slavery in supply chains documented by the UK Modern Slavery Act (2015), and the Black maternal mortality crisis all show that the slavery-modernity bond continues to generate disparities. The Windrush scandal of 2018, in which Black British citizens invited under the 1948 British Nationality Act were re-coded as illegal migrants, is a domestic British echo of the Atlantic system that brought their parents here. Reparations debates — University College London's reparations programme for its links to slave-trade money, Caribbean states' formal claims against European powers — make the link operative in twenty-first century policy.

A counter-argument: modernity also produced abolition. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in the empire in 1833; the US abolished slavery in 1865. Surely this is modernity correcting itself? I follow Du Bois in being unimpressed. "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (*Souls*, p. 5). The 1833 Slavery Abolition Act compensated slave-owners, not slaves — Britain paid £20 million (about 5% of GDP, a debt only finished being paid in 2015). Emancipation produced sharecropping, Jim Crow and convict leasing in the US; in the Caribbean it produced indentured Indian labour. Abolition was a transition between forms of racial domination, not a clean break.

A second counter-argument: not all of modernity was implicated. There were abolitionist enlighteners — Wilberforce, Equiano, Mansfield. I accept this but treat it as confirming rather than undermining the thesis. Equiano's *Interesting Narrative* (1789) is itself a Black Atlantic text; Mansfield's 1772 Somerset judgment is a modern legal innovation. The abolitionists worked within modernity using the same tools that other moderns used to defend slavery.

I conclude that modernity and slavery were co-constituted. Du Bois shows the economic-legal foundation; Gilroy shows the philosophical-cultural complicity. The implication is that critiques of modernity that ignore slavery (Habermas, Beck in different ways) are not just incomplete; they reproduce the erasure that Du Bois and Gilroy diagnose. Honest modernity-talk has to be Atlantic talk.

### Mock 6 — "Whiteness is a form of power, not a colour." Discuss.

I argue, with Morrison and Du Bois, that whiteness is best understood as a form of power that operates partly through colour but is not reducible to colour. I make three claims: whiteness is constructed historically, it is sustained by unmarked-ness, and it generates concrete material privileges. I draw on Morrison primarily and Du Bois for the framing.

First, whiteness is constructed historically. The lecture's history of scientific racism shows that the categories of race — including white — have been remarkably unstable. Linnaeus (1735) had Europaeus; Blumenbach (1775) coined "Caucasian" for the white race based on the supposed beauty of Caucasus skulls. The boundaries of whiteness have shifted in living memory. Irish, Italian, Jewish and Eastern European immigrants to the US were historically not fully white before becoming so in the mid-twentieth century. Morrison notes this in *The Origin of Others*: "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, p. 17). Whiteness is not a stable phenotypic fact but a historical and political achievement.

Second, whiteness is sustained by unmarked-ness. Where Black, Asian, Latino and other non-white identities are racialised — perceived as racially specific — whiteness operates as the unmarked norm against which other identities are deviations. This is Morrison's analytical core. Racial othering is "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (*Origin of Others*, p. 29). The unmarked-ness is what makes whiteness powerful: it is the default, the human-as-such, the position from which judgements appear to be made impersonally. Du Bois named this dynamic when he said the Black self sees itself "through the revelation of the other world" (*Souls*, p. 5) — the other world being the white world whose racial position remains hidden to itself.

Third, whiteness generates concrete material privileges. Morrison cites Bruce Baum's compact formula: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, p. 25). I take this to mean that racial categories are the residue of power exerted asymmetrically. White households in the US hold about ten times the wealth of Black households (Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances). White Britons are far less likely to be stopped and searched, far less likely to die of COVID-19 (especially in 2020 waves), and far more likely to be admitted to elite universities. The Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry diagnosed the UK Metropolitan Police as "institutionally racist" — a finding that names whiteness as the operating standard of the institution. The 2018 Windrush scandal showed white British bureaucracy treating Black British citizens as foreign. None of this is a matter of pigment; it is a matter of how social institutions sort people by racial assignment.

I anticipate two objections. First, that not all white people have power — many are poor, marginalised or oppressed. I accept this but think it does not refute the thesis. Whiteness studies (Roediger, Frankenberg, DiAngelo) distinguish whiteness as a structural position from white individuals' material outcomes. A poor white American is materially worse off than a wealthy Black American but is still less likely to be killed by police, less likely to receive a harsher sentence for the same offence, and more likely to receive medical pain relief on the same complaint. Whiteness functions as a relative privilege even where absolute deprivation is severe. Second, that "whiteness" as analytical category essentialises white people and is itself a racialising move. I think the response is that whiteness studies aim precisely to denaturalise whiteness — to mark what has been unmarked — and that this is a deconstructive rather than essentialising operation.

I consider whether Du Bois has whiteness in his sights. He does, even if he does not develop it as fully as Morrison. In *Souls* he speaks of "the pale world about them" (p. 3) — pallor as a marker of dominance, not just a colour. In *Dusk of Dawn* he tracks how legal whiteness produces Jim Crow. His later work *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) introduces the famous concept of the "psychological wage" of whiteness — the compensation poor white workers receive for accepting racial division over class solidarity. This is whiteness as power in its sharpest formulation: poor whites receive less in wages and more in racial status.

I track concrete contemporary examples. The British "small boats" rhetoric in 2022-2024, which treats Channel-crossing migrants as a threat while welcoming Ukrainian refugees, deploys whiteness as the unspoken standard of acceptable migration. The 2023 SFFA v Harvard ruling against affirmative action restored a whiteness-friendly admissions environment without naming whiteness. The cultural production of "Karen" as a meme names a recurring practice — white women weaponising police against Black people — that operationalises whiteness daily. The Royal Family's reception of Meghan Markle and the post-2020 Sussex revelations about palace conversations regarding "how dark" their unborn child might be exemplify whiteness as institutional anxiety.

I also draw on Gilroy. In *Race Ends Here* he argues that "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, p. 252) and that scientific racism's classifications produced races performatively. Whiteness is the performative effect that bestows on its bearers the status of un-classed — they have race, but get to forget it.

I conclude that whiteness is indeed a form of power rather than a colour. Historically constructed, structurally unmarked and materially advantageous, whiteness operates as the silent norm against which other racial positions are made visible. Naming it — as Morrison, Du Bois and Gilroy do — is the first step in disrupting the power that depends on remaining nameless.

### Mock 7 — Single-author question: Outline and evaluate Du Bois's contribution to social theory.

I argue that Du Bois made four major contributions to social theory that justify his recovery as a foundational, not merely supplementary, classical sociologist. These are: an empirical, multi-method sociology of race; the conceptual apparatus of veil, double consciousness and second sight; a social-fact account of race; and a methodological standpoint that anticipates standpoint epistemology and CRT. I evaluate each.

Du Bois (1868-1963) was, as the lecture notes, born five years after the Emancipation Proclamation, the first Black PhD from Harvard, briefly trained in Berlin, and a polymath — sociologist, historian, novelist, activist (lecture slide 12). His *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) is one of the earliest large-scale urban sociological studies, combining ethnography, statistics and historical analysis decades before the Chicago School institutionalised these methods. The fact that mainstream sociology took until the 2010s to fully reincorporate him into the canon (Aldon Morris's *The Scholar Denied*, 2015) reflects the discipline's racial gatekeeping more than any limitation in Du Bois's work.

His first contribution is methodological. *The Philadelphia Negro*'s combination of door-to-door survey, ethnographic observation and historical contextualisation prefigured what we now call mixed-methods sociology. Crucially, his standpoint was reflexive — Du Bois acknowledged his own position as a Black researcher studying Black community, and treated this not as a methodological problem but as a methodological resource. This is the methodological side of double consciousness: the doubly aware investigator sees what the singly aware one cannot.

His second and most famous contribution is the conceptual triad of the veil, double consciousness and second sight, set out in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903). "It dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others … shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 4). The veil names the social barrier; second sight names the perceptual gift of those born on its other side ("the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world," p. 4); and double consciousness names the experience of self-perception through the dominant world's eyes — "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (p. 5). The crucial sentence is the formulation of twoness: "One ever feels his twoness, — an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (p. 5). I evaluate this as among the most influential conceptual contributions in twentieth-century social theory, taken up by Fanon (epidermalisation), Mead (the looking-glass self), Bhabha (mimicry), Crenshaw (intersectionality) and the entire tradition of Black studies.

His third contribution is his social-fact account of race in *Dusk of Dawn* (1940). Anticipating contemporary critiques of biological essentialism, Du Bois argued that "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 136). His operational definition — "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (p. 153) — is the founding statement of what CRT later named racial realism. Race is real not as biology but as the social practice of differential treatment.

His fourth contribution is the connection of slavery to modernity. In "The Study of the Negro Problems" (1898) Du Bois traces how American slavery emerged from a labour shortage: "the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth … by the importation of Negroes" (Du Bois, 1898, pp. 3-4). The racial categorisation followed the economic need. This anticipates Eric Williams's *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), Gilroy's *Black Atlantic*, and the broader project of treating modernity as a racially structured phenomenon.

I evaluate the contributions. On methodology, Du Bois's mixed-methods approach is now standard, and recognising him as a pioneer is overdue. On the conceptual triad, double consciousness has had enormous reach but has also been criticised as gendered ("self-conscious manhood," p. 5) and as too binary for intersectional contexts. I think the concept survives these critiques as a framework that subsequent thinkers have rightly extended. On racial realism, Du Bois's position remains the most defensible in social theory: race is biologically trivial and socially decisive. On the modernity-slavery connection, his analysis is correct and continues to anticipate twenty-first century revisionist economic history (Sven Beckert, Caitlin Rosenthal).

I consider limitations. Du Bois's political programme has been criticised. The "talented tenth" — the educated Black elite leading racial uplift — has been called elitist; later Black radicals (Garvey, the Black Panthers, contemporary Afro-pessimists) found it insufficient. His later turn toward communism and his emigration to Ghana (where he died in 1963) reflect a deeper pessimism about American reform than *Souls* suggests. His gendered framing of double consciousness has been productively extended by Anna Julia Cooper, Patricia Hill Collins and Crenshaw, who showed that Black women experience triple or quadruple consciousness. None of these criticisms undermine the foundational contribution; they extend it.

I weigh Du Bois against Weber, Durkheim and Marx, the canonical trio. He is closer to Weber in his attention to subjective experience and to Durkheim in his treatment of race as social fact, but he diverges from both by refusing the unitary actor and by centring racial domination as a constitutive social fact. He is closer to Marx in tracing the material foundations of racial categories in slavery and labour, but more attentive to culture, religion and aesthetics. Aldon Morris has argued persuasively that Du Bois should be ranked alongside the classical trio rather than as a footnote, and I agree.

Contemporary echoes are everywhere. Black Lives Matter is double consciousness politicised. CRT's interest convergence and counter-storytelling extend Du Bois's standpoint. The 1619 Project (2019) put slavery at the centre of US history — a Du Boisian move. The Windrush scandal in the UK illustrates the veil in the postcolonial metropole.

I conclude that Du Bois made four foundational contributions to social theory — mixed-methods sociology, the conceptual triad of veil/double consciousness/second sight, the social-fact account of race, and the modernity-slavery connection — that justify recognising him as a classical sociologist alongside Weber, Marx and Durkheim. His exclusion from the canon was a function of the colour line he diagnosed, not of any limitation in his work.

### Mock 8 — Comparison: Compare Du Bois's "double consciousness" with Gilroy's "Black Atlantic" as approaches to the experience of race.

I argue that Du Bois's double consciousness and Gilroy's Black Atlantic are complementary rather than competing approaches: Du Bois works at the level of the individual psyche under racial domination, Gilroy at the level of the transnational cultural-political circuit that produces that domination. Together they offer a stereoscopic account of racial experience.

Du Bois's double consciousness, as set out in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), is a phenomenological concept describing the experience of being Black in a white-dominant world: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 5). It is paired with the veil ("shut out from their world by a vast veil," p. 4) and second sight ("born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world," p. 4). The unit of analysis is the racialised self; the temporal frame is the moment of perception ("it dawned upon me," p. 4); the spatial frame is mostly national — the African-American condition in the post-Emancipation United States.

Gilroy's Black Atlantic, set out in his 1993 book of that name, is a structural-cultural concept describing the transnational circuit of slavery, migration, music and political thought that connects Africa, the Americas, the Caribbean and Europe. The unit of analysis is the cultural and political circuit; the temporal frame is the long modernity from the Middle Passage to the present; the spatial frame is the Atlantic ocean as a connective rather than a separating space. Plantation slavery, Gilroy insists, "provided the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 54) and remains "central … in the historical memories of the black Atlantic" (p. 55).

The complementarity becomes clear when we see what each cannot do alone. Double consciousness without the Black Atlantic risks becoming a purely psychological concept abstracted from its material conditions. The Black Atlantic without double consciousness risks becoming a structural framework that loses the lived experience that drives it. Gilroy explicitly draws Du Bois into his project — his book's subtitle is *Modernity and Double Consciousness* — and he reads Du Bois's German sojourn, his exposure to Frederick Douglass's Scottish lectures, and his trans-Atlantic intellectual formation as Black Atlantic moves. Conversely, Du Bois's later work, especially *Black Reconstruction* (1935), is implicitly Black Atlantic — he reads US Reconstruction in the context of global labour and racial movements.

I trace the differences in three registers. First, the scale: Du Bois moves from the schoolyard incident to the national problem, Gilroy from the Atlantic circuit to particular cultural moments. Second, the affective register: Du Bois is more interior, tracking the wound of the racialising gaze; Gilroy is more political, tracking the resistance and creativity of the racialised. Third, the normative ambition: Du Bois's *Souls* ends on the modest hope of merging the two selves into "a better and truer self" (p. 5); Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* demands "the primal history of modernity be reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (p. 55) — a more radical rewriting.

I consider how each handles experience. For Du Bois, experience is the raw material of racial understanding — "one's sense of race and racial terms comes only from experience," as the Gibson commentary puts it (lecture slide 15). For Gilroy, experience is suspect: in *Black Atlantic* he criticises "the clarion calls to epistemological narcissism and the absolute sovereignty of unmediated experience" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 56). He wants the slaves' "unstable standpoint" but not as raw experience — rather as a critical inventory of self and modernity. I think Gilroy is responding to a problem Du Bois did not face: in the post-1980s identity politics that Gilroy critiques, "Black experience" was being invoked as an epistemic trump card. Gilroy wants to keep the experiential ground without sliding into essentialism.

I trace concrete applications. The Windrush scandal of 2018 illustrates both concepts. Double consciousness names what the wrongly deported Caribbean Britons experienced — being seen as foreign by the country they had built their lives in. The Black Atlantic names the structural fact that they were there because of the post-1948 migration that the slave-trade-built British state had set in motion. Black Lives Matter likewise: double consciousness names the experience that animates the protests; the Black Atlantic names the diasporic circuit that allowed George Floyd's death in Minneapolis to mobilise crowds in Bristol, London, Berlin and Cape Town.

I consider how each handles modernity. Du Bois's *Souls* and *Dusk of Dawn* connect modernity to slavery via the labour question but stop short of Gilroy's full reconstruction. Gilroy's most provocative line — "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 56) — has no exact equivalent in Du Bois, though Du Bois's recognition that "the Nation has not yet found peace from its sins" (*Souls*, p. 5) gestures in the same direction. I think Gilroy gets to take the further step because he stands on Du Bois's shoulders.

I evaluate. Both concepts have travelled. Double consciousness is now used in psychology (W. E. Cross, William Cross's nigrescence model), in literary theory (Henry Louis Gates, Hortense Spillers), in political philosophy (Charles Mills's racial contract). The Black Atlantic has reshaped diaspora studies, postcolonial theory and Black British sociology. The risk for both is over-application: any difficult identity gets labelled "double consciousness," any transnational cultural exchange gets labelled "Black Atlantic." Both concepts work best when handled with the historical specificity their authors gave them.

I conclude that Du Bois's double consciousness and Gilroy's Black Atlantic are not rivals but levels of analysis. Double consciousness gives us the phenomenology of racialised experience; the Black Atlantic gives us the historical-structural condition in which that experience arises. A complete sociology of race needs both, and Gilroy's titular debt to Du Bois acknowledges as much.

### Mock 9 — Discuss with examples: "Racism is no longer about individuals; it is about institutions and ways of seeing." Discuss with examples.

I agree with this claim and develop it through the lecture's conclusion and the three theorists, drawing on concrete examples from contemporary Britain and the United States.

The lecture is explicit on this point. Its concluding slide states: "This is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist. It is about how the modern social world is constituted on forms of racism which we might not be able to recognise immediately but are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (lecture slide 32). I treat this as the operative thesis. The shift from individual to institutional racism is one of the most important developments in twentieth-century social theory of race, and Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy each contribute to it.

Du Bois's contribution is to show that the post-Emancipation state, even after formal equality, reproduced racial domination through institutional means. "Despite the Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 and the 'right to vote' of the 15th amendement to the Constitution — 'The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found freedom in his promised land'" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 7, cited lecture slide 19). Jim Crow segregation, voter suppression, the convict-leasing system that re-enslaved Black men under the Thirteenth Amendment's labour exception, sharecropping debt peonage — none of these required individual racists. They required institutions. Du Bois's later *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) develops this institutional analysis at book length.

Morrison's contribution is to show that institutional racism is sustained by ways of seeing — by media, by cultural narratives, by the visual economy of normality and otherness. "Routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like (or ought to look like) and what in fact we are like" (Morrison, *Origin of Others*, p. 37). Racial othering "demand[s]/encourage[s]" behaviours; it shapes how we read each other. I take this as the cultural complement to the institutional analysis. Institutions are staffed by people whose perception has already been racially configured.

Gilroy's contribution is to extend the institutional and perceptual analysis to the global level. "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252) — knowledge production itself, including science, is among the institutions that racialise. The "chemical gaze" (citing Foucault) and the "Euclidean anatomy" of scientific racism produced racial reality. Today, the algorithmic gaze (Big Data, facial recognition, predictive policing) does similar work. Joy Buolamwini's "Gender Shades" research showed that commercial facial recognition systems are significantly less accurate for darker-skinned women — algorithmic othering as Morrison's media argument writ in code.

I provide examples. The Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry diagnosed the Metropolitan Police as "institutionally racist" — defining institutional racism as the "collective failure of an organisation to provide an appropriate and professional service to people because of their colour, culture, or ethnic origin." This is now the standard definition. The Lawrence case (Stephen Lawrence murdered in Eltham in 1993; killers not convicted until 2012) showed how an institution can fail Black victims without any individual police officer holding overt racist beliefs.

UK Stop & Search disparities continue: Home Office data show Black people are 7-9 times more likely to be stopped than white people. This is not the work of a few racist officers; it is the institutional product of training, performance metrics, deployment patterns and discretionary stops. The Windrush scandal (2018) showed the Home Office wrongly deporting Black British citizens because its institutional culture treated dark-skinned long-term residents as suspect by default. The Hostile Environment policy (2012) explicitly engineered institutional racism into immigration enforcement.

In healthcare, the MBRRACE-UK reports consistently show Black women in the UK 3-4 times more likely to die in pregnancy than white women. This is not individual midwives' racism but the cumulative institutional effect of differential pain assessment, delayed referrals, and unsafe assumptions about Black anatomy that descend from scientific racism (the lecture slide showing the 1868 craniometry image and the 1933 Nazi measurement is uncomfortably close to current research findings on doctors' beliefs about Black skin thickness and pain tolerance).

In education, the UK attainment gap between Black Caribbean boys and their white peers persists despite decades of equality legislation; permanent school exclusion rates for Black Caribbean students are roughly three times higher than for white students. The CRT concept of the "school-to-prison pipeline" names the institutional sequence by which racialised school discipline feeds the criminal justice system.

In the arts, museums and galleries remain disproportionately white in staffing, audiences and collections, and the post-2020 returns debates (the Benin Bronzes, the Parthenon Marbles, the Maqdala collection) make the institutional racial geography of cultural inheritance visible. The 2020 toppling of Edward Colston's statue in Bristol forced institutional reflection on how British cities have memorialised slavers.

I anticipate the counter-argument that focusing on institutions lets individuals off the hook. I think the response is that individual racism remains real but is best understood as a downstream effect of institutional and perceptual conditioning. Robin DiAngelo's *White Fragility* (2018) argues that white individuals are made to react defensively to racial criticism by their institutional formation. Individual hostility is real but largely tracks the institutional conditions Morrison and Gilroy describe.

Two further examples. First, algorithmic policing: the Metropolitan Police's "Gangs Matrix" (now reformed after legal challenge) disproportionately listed young Black men with little gang involvement; the institutional logic of risk assessment racialised even where individual officers did not. Second, COVID-19 outcomes: in 2020, Black Britons died at significantly higher rates than white Britons, in part because of occupational exposure (frontline work), housing density, and pre-existing health inequalities — all institutional facts.

I conclude that the lecture is right: racism is no longer (and perhaps never was primarily) about individuals. It is about institutions and ways of seeing. Du Bois shows the institutional reproduction of Jim Crow under formal equality; Morrison shows the perceptual economy of othering; Gilroy shows the global-historical institutionalisation of race in modern knowledge and power. The implication is that anti-racist politics must be institutional and perceptual, not merely psychological. Diversity training without institutional change is insufficient; representation in media without structural change is cosmetic.

### Mock 10 — To what extent has sociology absorbed Du Bois into its canon? Discuss.

I argue that sociology has absorbed Du Bois unevenly and belatedly; the recovery is ongoing, has accelerated since the early 2010s, but still falls short of the central canonical status his contributions deserve. I make the case in four steps: outline the exclusion, document the recovery, identify the residual gap, and assess what full absorption would require.

The exclusion is the puzzle the recovery responds to. Du Bois (1868-1963) produced *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), one of the first large-scale urban sociological studies; *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), a foundational work of social theory; *The Study of the Negro Problems* (1898) and dozens of other empirical and theoretical contributions; *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935), a major revisionist history of US Reconstruction; and shaped American sociology for sixty years. Yet for most of the twentieth century he was largely absent from the sociological canon. Talcott Parsons's *The Structure of Social Action* (1937) did not include him. Robert Merton, Anthony Giddens, Jürgen Habermas — none of them treat Du Bois as a classical sociologist. The standard "founding fathers" trinity — Marx, Weber, Durkheim — held the canonical position. Aldon Morris's *The Scholar Denied: W.E.B. Du Bois and the Birth of Modern Sociology* (2015) names this exclusion and traces it to two causes: the institutional racism of US sociology (Du Bois could not get a tenure-track position at a major white university) and the parallel rise of the Chicago School (Park, Burgess) which appropriated Du Boisian methods without crediting him.

The recovery has accelerated since the early 2010s. Aldon Morris's book is the watershed. The American Sociological Association (ASA) named Du Bois its President in absentia and has named annual awards after him. Sociology curricula at major universities have added Du Bois to classical theory syllabi (the SC301 lecture itself is part of this trend). His *Souls of Black Folk* is now widely taught alongside Weber's *Protestant Ethic* and Durkheim's *Suicide*. The 2019 1619 Project at the *New York Times* drew explicitly on Du Bois's historical sociology of slavery. The post-2020 Black Lives Matter wave produced renewed interest in his concepts of double consciousness, the veil and racial realism. Specialist journals (*Du Bois Review*, founded 2004) have institutionalised the field.

The residual gap is the gap between syllabus inclusion and conceptual centrality. I think Du Bois has been added to the canon but not centred in it. Marx, Weber and Durkheim still structure most introductory theory courses; Du Bois is added as a fourth, often paired with race-specific topics, rather than treated as a thinker whose concepts illuminate the full range of sociological questions. This pattern reproduces the very gesture Gilroy criticises: it adds "the figures of black folks who had previously been confined to the intermediate category between animal and human" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 55) without rewriting the canon's basic architecture. Real absorption would mean teaching Weber on the racial constitution of rational-legal authority, Durkheim on race as social fact, Marx on slavery as constitutive of capitalism — all using Du Bois as a primary theoretical resource.

I consider what full absorption would require. Three moves. First, methodological: recognise *The Philadelphia Negro* as a foundational mixed-methods study and teach it alongside Durkheim's *Suicide* and Weber's *Economy and Society* as a model of empirical sociology. Second, conceptual: treat double consciousness, the veil, second sight and racial realism as general sociological concepts, not race-specific add-ons. Double consciousness applies wherever a subordinated group sees itself through the dominant group's eyes — gender (Smith's bifurcated consciousness), class (Bourdieu's symbolic violence), sexuality (Sedgwick's epistemology of the closet). Third, historical: recognise that the sociology of modernity has to be a sociology of slavery and colonialism as much as of industrialisation and bureaucratisation. The 1898 Du Bois article shows exactly how to do this.

I assess the institutional incentives. Sociology departments have improved on diversifying syllabi but still hire and promote unevenly. UK sociology has done relatively better than US sociology in incorporating Black British thought (Hall, Gilroy, Hesse), partly because the postcolonial frame is harder to ignore in a former imperial power. The CRT debates in the US have politicised Du Boisian recovery — paradoxically generating both more interest and more institutional pushback (Florida's 2023 ban on CRT teaching, Trump-era attacks on the 1619 Project).

I consider counter-arguments. First, that Du Bois is now genuinely centred — the very fact that an SC301 lecture exists shows the canon has expanded. I think this overstates the change. Lectures on race and social theory exist; Du Bois is named where race is the topic. The deeper centrality would mean naming him on lectures on bureaucracy, on the family, on religion, on capitalism — areas where his work also contributes. Second, that we should not over-promote Du Bois at the expense of other Black thinkers (Cooper, Frazier, Cox, Hurston, Gilroy himself). I accept this: the canon should be plural, not just expanded by one. Anna Julia Cooper's *A Voice from the South* (1892), Oliver Cromwell Cox's *Caste, Class, and Race* (1948), and Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) all deserve canonical status.

I provide a small but telling example. The Wikipedia article on "Classical Sociology" historically listed Marx, Weber, Durkheim, Simmel, Comte. Du Bois was added relatively recently. Citation analyses (e.g., Aldon Morris's data) show Du Bois's citation rate has surged since 2010 but remains below the classical trio. The post-2020 BLM moment produced an editorial flurry in major sociology journals (*American Sociological Review*, *British Journal of Sociology*) on Du Bois; whether this translates into permanent canonical centrality remains to be seen.

I conclude that sociology has absorbed Du Bois partially but not fully. The recovery is real and accelerating, especially since 2015 in the US and somewhat earlier in UK sociology. The residual gap is between syllabus inclusion and conceptual centrality — Du Bois is taught but not yet structuring. Full absorption would require treating his concepts as general sociological tools and rewriting the canonical narrative of modernity to centre slavery and racial classification. The work is in progress; SC301's inclusion of Lecture 8 is part of it.

### Mock 11 — To what extent has Critical Race Theory inherited Du Bois? Discuss.

I argue that Critical Race Theory (CRT) is one of Du Bois's most direct intellectual heirs, inheriting his core thesis that race is socially real even where biologically empty, his standpoint epistemology, and his attention to legal-institutional reproduction of racial domination. CRT extends Du Bois in several productive directions, especially through Derrick Bell's "interest convergence" and Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality, while occasionally outrunning the moderate liberal hope that Du Bois retained.

I begin with the inheritance. CRT emerged in US legal scholarship in the late 1970s and 1980s (Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, Cheryl Harris, Richard Delgado, Patricia Williams) before spreading to social theory. Its founding claims map onto Du Boisian positions almost point for point. First, race is socially constructed but materially real — Du Bois's "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 136) is the proto-CRT formulation. Second, racism is institutional and persistent, not merely an attitudinal error — Du Bois's analysis of post-Emancipation institutional racism (Jim Crow, sharecropping, convict leasing) prefigures CRT's analysis of post-Civil Rights institutional racism. Third, the racialised subject's perspective is epistemically privileged — Du Bois's second sight (Souls, p. 4) prefigures CRT's "counter-storytelling" methodology. Fourth, racial progress is illusory or contingent — Du Bois's observation that "the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (*Souls*, p. 5) prefigures Bell's racial realism.

Bell's interest convergence thesis (developed in his 1980 *Harvard Law Review* article on Brown v. Board of Education) holds that racial progress in the US occurs only when it converges with white interests. He used Brown as the example: desegregation served US Cold War interests (rebutting Soviet propaganda about American racism) and Northern industrial interests (a more mobile Black labour force), not just Black moral claims. I read this as a sharp extension of Du Bois's pessimism in *Souls* about formal-legal change. Where Du Bois held that "Equality in law is not enough" (lecture slide 19), Bell adds that even legal equality requires white interest alignment.

Crenshaw's intersectionality (her 1989 University of Chicago Legal Forum article) responds to a limitation in Du Bois — his framing was gendered ("self-conscious manhood," *Souls*, p. 5) and did not adequately theorise the specific position of Black women. Crenshaw shows that single-axis analyses (race or gender) fail Black women, whose experience is irreducible to either. I treat this as an inheritance because Crenshaw uses the same standpoint logic Du Bois used: those most subordinated by overlapping systems see what those less subordinated cannot. Intersectionality is multi-dimensional double consciousness.

CRT's other key concepts each have Du Boisian roots. Counter-storytelling — the use of narrative to disrupt majoritarian assumptions — extends Du Bois's literary-sociological method in *Souls* (note the chapter epigraphs from Black spirituals, the "Sorrow Songs" closing chapter). Whiteness as property (Cheryl Harris's 1993 article) extends Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction* concept of the "psychological wage" of whiteness. The "school-to-prison pipeline" extends Du Bois's institutional analysis of post-Emancipation racial control.

I consider where CRT outruns Du Bois. Three areas. First, on temporal pessimism: Bell's racial realism is more pessimistic than Du Bois's *Souls*. Du Bois's hope of merging the two selves into "a better and truer self" (p. 5) is gone in Bell, replaced by the realistic recognition that racism is permanent. (Du Bois himself moves toward this view late in life — his emigration to Ghana in 1961 and his rejection of liberal integration.) Second, on universalism: CRT is generally more skeptical of universalist humanism than Du Bois's early work, agreeing with Gilroy that "bourgeois humanism" is implicated in scientific racism. Third, on legal optimism: Du Bois retained some hope in the Fifteenth Amendment's promise; CRT, born of legal scholars who watched the Warren Court's victories rolled back, is more skeptical of law as an instrument of racial change.

I consider concrete CRT applications. *Plyler v. Doe* (1982, education rights for undocumented children) and *Regents v. Bakke* (1978, affirmative action) are CRT's foundational legal cases; *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013, gutting the Voting Rights Act) and *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023, ending race-conscious admissions) are CRT's vindication cases — they show how legal victories can be reversed when white interest convergence collapses. Bell predicted this; Du Bois would not have been surprised.

The CRT critique of "interest convergence" cuts particularly hard at the rhetoric of racial progress. The election of Barack Obama in 2008 was hailed as a post-racial moment; the rise of Trump and the post-2016 racial backlash, the police killings (Floyd, Garner, Taylor) and the post-2020 retrenchment showed that progress was contingent on white forbearance. CRT explains the backlash; the liberal-progressive narrative cannot.

I consider critics of the CRT-Du Bois lineage. Some scholars (Adolph Reed, John McWhorter) argue that CRT is too pessimistic and too culturalist, distracting from class-based politics that Du Bois increasingly embraced in his later years. I think this critique reads Du Bois selectively — *Black Reconstruction* is profoundly attentive to class, but it also insists that racial domination cuts across class in ways class-only politics cannot capture. Du Bois's late-life turn toward communism integrated rather than abandoned his race analysis.

The 2020-2024 anti-CRT panic in US politics (state bans on teaching CRT, Florida's Stop WOKE Act) is itself a Du Boisian moment. It confirms the institutional embeddedness of whiteness — the racial backlash treats analytical concepts that name whiteness as threats to white identity. Morrison's account of othering as confirming one's self as normal (*Origin of Others*, p. 29) explains the affective logic of the panic.

In the UK, CRT's influence is more diffuse. The 2021 Sewell Report on race and ethnic disparities was widely read as an institutional rejection of CRT framings (it denied that Britain is "institutionally racist"). The post-2020 BLM wave brought CRT into mainstream UK debate, with predictable partisan reactions. The Race Disparity Audit (commissioned 2017) and the disparities documented under successive governments confirm the structural facts that CRT analyses, even where the political vocabulary remains contested.

I conclude that CRT is one of Du Bois's most direct heirs. It inherits his social-fact realism about race, his standpoint epistemology, and his institutional analysis. It extends him through Bell's interest convergence, Crenshaw's intersectionality, Harris's whiteness as property and Delgado's counter-storytelling. It is more pessimistic than early Du Bois but consistent with late Du Bois. The political backlash against CRT confirms the analysis: whiteness mobilises institutional power to suppress the concepts that name it. Du Bois would have recognised the dynamic immediately.

### Mock 12 — Discuss with examples: What does it mean to say that race is "an effect of power" (Morrison)?

I argue that Morrison's claim that race is "an effect of power" (Morrison, *Origin of Others*, p. 25) is the most economical statement of the social-constructivist position on race in twentieth-century thought. To say race is an effect of power is to deny it three things — biological reality, natural inevitability and self-grounding meaning — and to affirm three others — historical contingency, institutional production and political functionality. I develop each in turn with examples.

Morrison's claim, citing Bruce Baum, occurs in a passage where she presses on the question "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter?" (Morrison, *Origin of Others*, p. 15). Her answer is that race is the residue of power exerted asymmetrically to confirm the dominant group's normality: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (p. 29). Race exists because power needs it, not because biology produces it.

The first denial is biological. The lecture is clear: scientific racism's project of grounding race in skull shape, brain size or melanin failed. "Races do not exist" (lecture slide 32). The Human Genome Project showed that genetic variation within so-called races is greater than between them. Du Bois saw this early: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 136). Gilroy presses harder: in *Race Ends Here* he argues that nanotechnology and molecular biology have rendered race "an after-image" at the level of bodily reality (Gilroy, p. 253). The biological denial is now well established in scientific consensus, even if popular and political discourse lags.

The second denial is naturalness. If race were a natural fact, it would have stable boundaries across time and place. It does not. The lecture's history of scientific racism — Linnaeus's four races (1735), Blumenbach's five (1775), later schemes proliferating into dozens — shows the categories shifting under classificatory pressure. The US Census has changed its racial categories repeatedly: "mulatto" appeared in 1850-1920; Hispanic became a "race" then an "ethnicity"; "two or more races" was added in 2000. In contemporary Brazil, racial categories are far more fluid (with dozens of self-identifications in popular use) than in the US one-drop rule. Irish, Italian and Jewish Americans were not fully "white" until the mid-twentieth century. Morrison notes: "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness" (Morrison, p. 17). Whiteness is acquired, not given.

The third denial is self-grounding meaning. Race does not mean what it seems to mean. The signs of race — skin colour, facial features, hair texture — do not carry their meanings inherently; meanings are imposed by the racialising system. "The signs and symbols of racial difference become apparent to our senses" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252) because "the human sensorium has had to be educated" (p. 252). Racial perception is a trained perception.

The three affirmations follow. First, historical contingency. The category emerged historically with the slave trade and colonialism. Du Bois shows how race was forged in seventeenth and eighteenth-century legal practice: "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898, p. 3) — race emerged as a solution to a labour problem. Cheryl Harris's "Whiteness as Property" (1993) extends this: whiteness was made legally heritable property in cases like *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896).

Second, institutional production. Race is produced and reproduced by institutions — the legal system, the education system, the policing system, the healthcare system, the media. The Macpherson Report (1999) named the Metropolitan Police "institutionally racist." The Windrush scandal (2018) showed Home Office bureaucracy treating Black British citizens as racially foreign by default. UK Stop & Search ratios (Black people stopped 7-9 times more often) operationalise racial categorisation daily. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate) institutionalises racial difference into birth and death.

Third, political functionality. Race serves identifiable political functions. Du Bois's "psychological wage" of whiteness in *Black Reconstruction* (1935) explains why poor whites accepted racial hierarchy over class solidarity: it gave them a relative status that material conditions did not. Trump-era political mobilisation showed the same dynamic — racial in-group appeals delivered electoral gains across class lines. The 2016 Brexit campaign mobilised racialised anxieties about migration. The 2022-2024 "small boats" panic in the UK showed racial categorisation doing direct policy work.

I consider concrete examples. The 2020 BLM movement after George Floyd's death made racial othering globally visible: the police officer's knee on Floyd's neck was a Morrisonian image of othering literalised. The Sewell Report (2021), which controversially denied that the UK is "institutionally racist," is an interesting case — it deployed the rhetoric of racial progress to deny the analysis that Morrison's "effect of power" would predict. Note the function: by denying institutional racism, the report worked to stabilise the dominant group's self-image as fair, exactly what Morrison's "othering as confirming one's self as normal" describes at the institutional level.

I anticipate two objections. First, that the "effect of power" formulation is too constructivist — it cannot explain why racial categorisation persists once we recognise it as power-effect. I think Morrison handles this: power is not just a mistake to be corrected by enlightenment; it is a self-reproducing system that institutions and ways of seeing keep in place. Second, that the formulation depoliticises by making race seem ephemeral. Again, the response is that "effect of power" does not mean illusory — power-effects can be the most stable and consequential facts about social life. Jim Crow was an effect of power; it lasted nearly a century.

I draw on Du Bois and Gilroy to triangulate. Du Bois's social-fact realism — "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (*Dusk of Dawn*, p. 153) — is consistent with Morrison: power's effect is what defines racial membership. Gilroy's "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (*Race Ends Here*, p. 252) is the same thesis at a longer temporal scale. The convergence across three theorists is striking.

I conclude that Morrison's "race is an effect of power" is the most efficient summary of contemporary critical race theory. It denies biology, naturalness and self-grounding meaning, and affirms historical contingency, institutional production and political functionality. It supplies the analytical framework within which Du Bois's veil and Gilroy's Black Atlantic operate. The concept does not deflate racial politics; it relocates it from biological dispute to the institutional and perceptual terrain where political action can actually intervene.

### Mock 13 — To what extent is "scientific racism" a thing of the past?

I argue that scientific racism is largely discredited as a scientific doctrine but is far from a thing of the past as a cultural and institutional residue. It survives in three forms: as a perceptual habit, as an institutional legacy, and as a recurring pseudo-scientific resurgence. I develop each.

I begin with what is past. The crude scientific racism of Linnaeus (1735) and Blumenbach (1775) — measuring skulls, ranking races by capacity for civilisation, predicting moral character from physiognomy — is comprehensively discredited in mainstream science. The Human Genome Project (completed 2003) confirmed that genetic variation within populations is greater than between them. UNESCO's 1950 and 1951 Statements on Race rejected the biological reality of race. Major scientific bodies (the American Anthropological Association, the American Society of Human Genetics) have issued repeated statements rejecting racial science. Twentieth-century atrocities — Nazi racial measurements (the 1933 image on lecture slide 9), Tuskegee, eugenic sterilisation — created moral consensus against racial science.

But the lecture is careful to say that "the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do" exist (slide 32). I think this is the key. Scientific racism's intellectual edifice has fallen, but its rubble shapes the ground we walk on.

First, perceptual residue. Gilroy's analysis in *Race Ends Here* is exactly that scientific racism's perceptual regime — the "Euclidean anatomy" of comparative measurement — has been outflanked by molecular biology but its "perspectival inclinations, perceptual habits and scalar assumptions" persist (Gilroy, p. 253). We continue to see race even after the science that authorised seeing it has collapsed. Frantz Fanon's "epidermalization" (cited Gilroy, p. 254) names this: race is sedimented in skin-deep perception. Joy Buolamwini's "Gender Shades" research showed that commercial facial recognition systems perform significantly worse on darker-skinned women — algorithmic perceptual racism inherited from training data that reflects scientific-racist visual habits.

Second, institutional legacy. Many institutions built during the scientific racism era still operate. The IQ testing industry, which originated partly in eugenic research (Goddard's translation of Binet, Yerkes's WWI Army Beta tests), continues to shape educational and employment selection despite its racially biased history. The US Census still uses "Caucasian" — a category Blumenbach invented in 1775 based on the supposed beauty of Caucasus skulls. The medical curriculum still includes "race-based medicine" — race-adjusted spirometry, eGFR formulas — even where the racial corrections derive from nineteenth-century pseudoscience about Black lung capacity or kidney function. UK medical training has only recently begun to address how Black patients' pain has been systematically underrated by clinicians who inherited beliefs about Black skin thickness from scientific racism. The Black maternal mortality rate (3-4 times the white rate in the UK and US) reflects this institutional legacy.

Third, recurring pseudo-scientific resurgence. Scientific racism has never fully gone away. Arthur Jensen's 1969 *Harvard Educational Review* article on race and IQ; Richard Herrnstein and Charles Murray's *The Bell Curve* (1994); the "human biodiversity" movement online; recent attempts to revive group-based IQ claims — each generation produces its own scientific racism. Nicholas Wade's *A Troublesome Inheritance* (2014) and various contemporary commentators on "race realism" repackage Blumenbach's classification with twenty-first century genetic vocabulary. The American Society of Human Genetics has repeatedly had to issue statements pushing back. These resurgences track political moments — the Jensen-Herrnstein wave coincided with backlash against civil rights gains; the contemporary wave tracks the post-2008 racial backlash.

The lecture flags Tuskegee (1932-1972) as scientific racism's twentieth-century shadow. The US Public Health Service withheld syphilis treatment from Black men to study disease progression, on the implicit premise that Black bodies were available for research in a way white bodies were not. The Henrietta Lacks case — HeLa cells taken without consent from a Black woman in 1951, now foundational to medical research — is another scientific-racist residue. The MOVE bombing in Philadelphia (1985), the radiation experiments on Black subjects, the disproportionate use of Black women in early contraceptive trials in Puerto Rico — each is institutionally specific but ideologically continuous.

In the UK, the legacy includes the eugenic origins of figures still honoured in academic life. Francis Galton, founder of eugenics, has had buildings named after him at UCL until recent renamings. The British eugenic society funded racial science into the mid-twentieth century. The 1981 Brixton riots and the Scarman Report, the 1999 Macpherson Report, the 2017 Lammy Review and the 2020 Windrush Lessons Learned Review each document the institutional half-life of racially encoded assumptions.

Gilroy's nuance is important. He argues in *Race Ends Here* that we should not simply repeat that race is biologically empty; we should attend to how new technologies (MRI, genome sequencing, predictive algorithms) are reshaping racial perception. The shift "downwards and inwards" (Gilroy, p. 253) — from skin to gene — can produce new pseudo-sciences of race even as it discredits the old ones. Direct-to-consumer ancestry testing (23andMe, AncestryDNA) and the associated "DNA racial identity" tourism have made racial categorisation seem newly scientific to mass audiences, even as the genetic findings actually undermine the social racial categories users want them to confirm.

I anticipate the counter-argument that mainstream science has decisively rejected scientific racism and that worrying about its persistence is fighting old battles. I think this confuses formal disavowal with practical effect. Scientific racism's formal authority is gone; its informal authority — in perceptual habits, institutional defaults, recurrent ideological resurgences and algorithmic systems — is alive. As the lecture concludes, racism is "present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 32). Ways of seeing outlast their explicit justifications.

I conclude that scientific racism is past as a scientific doctrine, present as cultural and institutional residue, and recurring as pseudo-scientific resurgence. Du Bois's, Morrison's and Gilroy's critique remains necessary. The biological case has been won; the institutional and perceptual cases remain to be won. The history of scientific racism is unfinished business.

### Mock 14 — Single-author question: Outline and evaluate Gilroy's argument that racial identities are not fixed.

I argue that Gilroy's view that "ethnic/racial identities are not fixed but are outcomes of wider political, economic, and cultural processes" (lecture slide 29) is correct, well-evidenced and politically productive. It anchors his broader project of refusing both biological essentialism and lazy cultural essentialism. I outline the argument, consider its evidence, and evaluate its implications.

Gilroy's argument unfolds across three of his major works. In *There Ain't No Black in the Union Jack* (1987) he showed how British national identity was racially coded against a Black British presence that was nevertheless constitutive of postwar British society. In *The Black Atlantic* (1993) he showed how Black diasporic identity is not a primordial Africanness but the product of the transatlantic circuit of slavery, migration and cultural exchange. In *Race Ends Here* (1998) and *Against Race* (2000; UK title *Between Camps*), he pressed against contemporary identity politics that treats racial categories as if they were natural.

The core claim is that racial identities are historically produced, not biologically given. "The specious ontologies of 'race' are anything but spontaneous and natural" (Gilroy, *Race Ends Here*, p. 252). They emerged through the slave trade, scientific classification, colonial administration and migration; they continue to be produced through media, policy and everyday practice. "Race was intrinsic to this change" — the shift from natural history to biology made race a "primary object of knowledge and power" (p. 252).

The Black Atlantic is Gilroy's central evidence. Black identity is not what survives of an African essence after enslavement; it is what is made in and through the Atlantic circuit. The mixing of West African, Caribbean, North American and European cultural materials produced distinct but related Black identities — African-American, Afro-Caribbean, Black British, Afro-Brazilian — that are recognisable to each other but not reducible to a single Africanness. Gilroy traces this through music (gospel-blues-jazz-reggae-hip-hop-grime), political thought (Douglass-Du Bois-Garvey-Padmore-James-Wright-Baldwin) and religion (the Black Church, Rastafari, Nation of Islam). Each is a Black Atlantic formation.

The argument has three implications. First, the political. If racial identities are produced rather than given, then political projects that treat racial purity as a starting point — both white supremacist and certain Black nationalist projects — are based on false premises. Gilroy's *Against Race* explicitly criticises Afrocentric essentialism along with white supremacism. He insists that the Black Atlantic frame is more analytically powerful than either cultural-nationalist alternative.

Second, the methodological. If identities are produced, then sociology should study the production processes — the slave trade, the migration circuits, the cultural industries, the state policies — not just the identities as finished products. Gilroy criticises identity politics that treats "Black experience" as a self-grounding epistemic ground; he wants the production processes back in view. "The clarion calls to epistemological narcissism and the absolute sovereignty of unmediated experience" (Gilroy, *Black Atlantic*, p. 56) are rejected as both philosophically naive and politically limited.

Third, the normative. If identities are produced, then they can also be remade. Gilroy in *Race Ends Here* expresses cautious optimism that contemporary technological and cultural shifts may weaken racial identity as a primary mode of self-categorisation. "Blackness can now signify vital prestige rather than abjection in a global info-tainment telesector" (Gilroy, p. 253) — global cultural commodification of Black culture (Michael Jordan, Beyoncé, hip-hop) has remade the affective valence of Blackness, even if the institutional disadvantages remain.

I evaluate. The argument is correct in its main lines and well-evidenced. Historical scholarship on race (Edmund Morgan, Theodore Allen, Eric Williams, Walter Rodney, Saidiya Hartman, Sven Beckert) confirms that racial categories were produced rather than given. Cultural studies (Stuart Hall, Hortense Spillers, bell hooks) confirm that racial identities are made and remade in cultural circuits. Sociology of migration confirms that diasporic identities change over generations, intermarry, hybridise.

There are objections. First, that the unfixity claim risks downplaying the persistence and weight of racial subordination. If identities are fluid, are we underestimating the brute facts of police killings, redlining, Stop & Search? I think Gilroy handles this. Unfixity does not mean ephemerality. The Black Atlantic was made over four centuries by violence; it cannot be unmade in a generation. Racial categories are historically contingent and politically functional, which means they are also stubborn — they last as long as the structures that produce them.

Second, that Gilroy's hope in *Race Ends Here* that race may end is premature. The 2010s-2020s have seen a global racial backlash (Trump, Brexit, the Sewell Report, anti-CRT legislation, the rise of European far-right parties) that suggests racial categories are far from declining. I think this is a fair pressure on Gilroy's optimism but does not refute the analytical claim that identities are produced. The backlash itself confirms the production thesis: someone is having to work hard to keep racial categories in place.

Third, that the Black Atlantic frame can decentre Africa within Black identity. By treating Black identity as a Black Atlantic formation, Gilroy can be read as making Africa peripheral to the diaspora — a reversal of older Afrocentric framings but not necessarily an improvement. Critics like Laura Chrisman and Kobena Mercer have pressed this. I think Gilroy can be defended: he wants to refuse both African essentialism and diasporic essentialism, and to keep the analytical focus on the production circuit.

I draw on contemporary examples. The "Black British" identity itself is a Black Atlantic product. Caribbean migrants of the Windrush generation did not arrive in Britain as "Black British"; they arrived as Jamaicans, Trinidadians, Bajans. The category "Black British" emerged in the 1970s-1980s in interaction with British racism, anti-racist politics, and the cultural innovations of figures like Stuart Hall, Linton Kwesi Johnson and the Race Today collective. Today it is a stable identity but historically produced. Mixed-race identification has grown substantially in UK census data, again reflecting the productive rather than fixed character of racial categorisation. The post-2020 acceptance of "Black" as a self-identifier (with capital B) is itself a recent shift in identity production.

I also draw on the Trump-era and post-2016 US data. "White" identity in the US has hardened politically over the past decade, with white evangelical political identity becoming a more distinct category than ever. This is identity production in real time — the "Make America Great Again" politics produced a more cohesive white identity by mobilising it against racialised others. Gilroy's frame predicts exactly this kind of identity work.

I conclude that Gilroy's argument that racial identities are not fixed is correct, productively evidenced through the Black Atlantic frame, and politically generative. It does not deflate the persistence of racial subordination but relocates it analytically — from given essence to made structure. It anchors a critical project that refuses both biological essentialism and lazy cultural essentialism. The hope that race may end — that we may, as Gilroy puts it, find "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (*Race Ends Here*, p. 255) — remains aspirational, but the analytical core stands.

### Mock 15 — Compare Du Bois and Foucault on race and power.

I argue that Du Bois and Foucault offer complementary but not interchangeable accounts of how racial power operates. Du Bois supplies a phenomenological and historical analysis of the racial subject under power; Foucault supplies an analytic of power-knowledge that explains how racial classification was produced. The lecture itself stages the comparison — Gilroy explicitly bridges them — so the question is well-posed.

Du Bois (1868-1963) writes from inside the colour line; Foucault (1926-1984) writes outside any specifically racial position, in the broader frame of European biopolitics. This positional difference shapes their approaches.

Du Bois's account of racial power proceeds from the experiential to the structural. The visiting cards scene in *Souls* — "Then it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others … shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, *Souls*, p. 4) — names racial power's operation at the level of perception. Double consciousness — "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" (p. 5) — names its internal effect. Jim Crow, the post-Emancipation institutional regime, names its structural form. *Dusk of Dawn*'s social-fact account of race ("the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia," *Dusk of Dawn*, p. 153) names its operational reality.

Foucault's account of power-knowledge proceeds from the structural to the productive. In *Discipline and Punish* (1975) and *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976), he develops the thesis that modern power is not primarily repressive but productive — it produces subjects, knowledge, categories. Power and knowledge are interlinked: "power-knowledge" names the way classificatory practices produce the very objects they claim to discover. The lecture explicitly applies this to race: "This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 8). Gilroy, in *Race Ends Here*, takes this further: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, p. 252) and "The truths of race were produced 'performatively' from the hat that biological science provided" (p. 253). Scientific racism — Linnaeus, Blumenbach, craniometry — is Foucault's argument in racial form.

The complementarity is clear. Du Bois shows what racial power feels like and how it operates institutionally; Foucault shows how it was produced through classificatory practice. Du Bois without Foucault risks underplaying how race was made; Foucault without Du Bois risks underplaying how race is lived. Together they give a stereoscopic picture.

There are tensions. Gilroy is explicit that Foucault did not fully attend to race. In *Race Ends Here* Gilroy writes that Foucault "seems to have been insufficiently attuned to the significance of protracted struggles over the unity of mankind that attended the emergence of biopolitics and appears to have been disinterested in the meaning of specifically raciological differences" (Gilroy, p. 254). Foucault treated the modern subject as racially unmarked, whereas, as Gilroy insists, the foundational debate of modernity was precisely whether Africans were members of the family of mankind. The lecture summarises Gilroy's view: "Gilroy was influenced by Foucault, though he does not completely agree with him" (slide 28).

The other major tension is on power's location. Du Bois locates racial power partly in the gaze — "the eyes of others" — and partly in institutions (Jim Crow, voting suppression, sharecropping). Foucault locates power primarily in capillary, productive practices — disciplinary techniques, classificatory practices, governmental rationality. Both are right but the emphases differ. Du Bois's racialised subject feels power directly; Foucault's subject is produced by power before they feel it.

I trace concrete examples where the two frames converge and diverge. On contemporary policing, both frames apply. Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power (the prison, the panopticon) illuminates how police surveillance shapes Black communities — Stop & Search ratios, predictive policing algorithms, the Met's Gangs Matrix all operate as disciplinary classification. Du Bois's analysis adds what the surveilled experience: double consciousness in the encounter with police, second sight that anticipates the danger. The two frames are mutually reinforcing.

On medical racism, similarly. Foucault's analysis of clinical knowledge (the "chemical gaze" Gilroy invokes) explains how medical classification produces racialised diagnostic categories — race-adjusted spirometry, eGFR formulas, the under-prescription of pain relief to Black patients. Du Bois's analysis adds what Black patients experience — the well-documented mistrust of medical institutions descending from Tuskegee, the doubled self-presentation Black women have described in maternity care.

On colonial knowledge, Foucault's analysis of the human sciences and Du Bois's account of the racial subject under colonial classification meet in postcolonial theory. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) drew explicitly on Foucault; Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) drew on phenomenology and prefigured Du Boisian themes. Said and Fanon together resemble the synthesis the lecture proposes.

I consider the limits of comparison. Foucault is generally suspicious of standpoint claims; he wants to analyse power without prescribing from any privileged position. Du Bois is committed to a Black standpoint — second sight is precisely the epistemic privilege of those below the colour line. Gilroy navigates between them, accepting standpoint without accepting "absolute sovereignty of unmediated experience" (*Black Atlantic*, p. 56). The synthesis is workable but requires care.

On political adequacy, the two frames diverge. Du Bois ends *Souls* with the demand for substantive freedom — "Work, culture, liberty, — all these we need, not singly but together" (p. 7). Foucault is more reluctant about positive programmes; his political stance is closer to ethos and resistance than to programme. Late Foucault's interest in the care of the self overlaps with Du Boisian concerns about self-making under racial domination, but the political traditions remain different.

I evaluate. I think the best contemporary work on race uses both frames. Critical Race Theory has Du Boisian roots; the analytics of biopolitical race (Achille Mbembe's necropolitics, Alexander Weheliye's habeas viscus, Sylvia Wynter's hermeneutics of man) draw on both Foucault and Du Bois. Gilroy's own work explicitly synthesises them. Charles Mills's *The Racial Contract* (1997) uses standpoint epistemology in the Du Boisian mode but analyses the contract through a Foucauldian lens of classificatory power.

A residual question: can Foucault be used in a way that does not reproduce the racial blindness Gilroy identifies? I think yes, but only by foregrounding what Foucault left in the background. Race must be analysed as a foundational rather than incidental case of biopolitical classification.

I conclude that Du Bois and Foucault offer complementary accounts of racial power: Du Bois the experiential and institutional, Foucault the productive and classificatory. Gilroy bridges them by insisting that race is "a primary object of knowledge and power" (*Race Ends Here*, p. 252) while preserving the Du Boisian attention to the racialised subject's standpoint. A complete sociology of race needs both, and the lecture's framing makes this explicit.
