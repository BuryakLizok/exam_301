# L8. A Question of Experience (2): Race and Social Theory

## 1. Topic Overview

This lecture moves the question of "experience" from gender (L7) to race, showing how sociology has had to confront its own complicity in racial classification while also drawing on Black theorists who treat race as a constitutive feature of modernity rather than a side issue. The argument unfolds in three parts. First, the lecturer traces scientific racism from Linnaeus (1735) and Blumenbach (1775) through nineteenth-century craniometry to twentieth-century eugenics, showing that race had no biological referent before this period. The word's etymology (French "racine," Arabic "ras") simply meant root or beginning. Only when classification was tied to power did "race" produce the pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy that justified slavery and colonialism. Following Foucault, the lecture frames this as power-knowledge: science "observed" what its own categories had already produced.

Second, W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963) is presented as the indispensable Black sociologist of modernity. Born five years before the Emancipation Proclamation's promise was anchored in the Fifteenth Amendment, the first Black PhD from Harvard, Du Bois asks in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) what it means that legal emancipation produced no substantive freedom. His answer reorganises the sociology of selfhood. The veil separates Black from white worlds; double consciousness names the "peculiar sensation" of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others"; twoness ("an American, a Negro") refuses the unitary actor presupposed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. In *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) Du Bois refines this by insisting that race is not biology but social fact: the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia." The revision document notes that this anticipates the "racial realism" of Critical Race Theory.

Third, the lecture turns to contemporary social theory through Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy. Morrison's *The Origin of Others* (2017) treats race as "an effect of power" and "Othering" as a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal. Americanness, she argues, "remains color for many people." Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) and 'Race Ends Here' (1998) refuse to bracket Africa as marginal to modernity: the slave trade is constitutive of Western modernity, racial terror is "cheerfully complicit" with occidental rationality, and we must rewrite history from the slaves' standpoint rather than merely include Black voices. The lecturer concludes: races do not exist, but the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of race do, and they saturate institutions, media, art and ways of seeing.

The pay-off for SC301 is methodological. The lecture argues that mainstream social theory (Weber, Durkheim, Goffman, Garfinkel) built its account of the modern self on a tacit white subject, treating racialised experience as a deviation or a special case. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy reverse this: the Black experience of modernity is the most revealing standpoint because modernity itself was forged through slavery, racial classification and colonial domination. The colour line is, in Du Bois's Forethought, "the problem of the Twentieth Century" (Du Bois, 1903: 1) and, given the persistence of the school-to-prison pipeline, of mass incarceration and the army as what the revision document calls "a modern form of Black slavery," equally a problem of the twenty-first.

## 2. Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Scientific racism**: The nineteenth-century project of tying "race" to biology, producing pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy. Originates with Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) and Blumenbach's five-race skull typology.
- **Power-knowledge** (Foucault, applied by Gilroy): Classification "produced" the very races it claimed to "observe." Race was "a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).
- **Eugenics**: Pseudo-science of "improving" racial stock; the lecture connects it to the 1933 photograph of Nazi racial measurements and to the "eugenic ravings of Francis Crick" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **The colour line**: Du Bois's diagnostic of the twentieth century: "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line" (Du Bois, 1903: 1).
- **The veil**: The social barrier separating Black from white worlds; Black people are "shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4).
- **Second sight**: The compensatory gift of those born "with a veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4), a critical perspective on the dominant world they are excluded from yet forced to inhabit.
- **Double consciousness**: "This sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **Twoness**: "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **The talented tenth**: Du Bois's later concept (mentioned in the revision document) for the educated Black elite whose role is to lead racial uplift.
- **Racial realism**: Du Bois's view (later taken up by CRT, as the revision document notes) that race is not biology but social fact, defined by unequal treatment, Jim Crow, segregation.
- **Othering** (Morrison): The construction of racial outsiders to stabilise the dominant group's self-image; "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29).
- **Whiteness** (Morrison): Analysis of whiteness as a form of unmarked power; Americanness "remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17).
- **The Black Atlantic**: Gilroy's name for the transnational cultural and political space formed by slavery's middle passage; Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Caribbean as one circuit.
- **Epidermalisation** (Fanon, via Gilroy): The colonial process of making bodies meaningful by endowing them with "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254).
- **Interest convergence** (CRT, via revision document): Racial progress occurs only when it aligns with white interests.
- **Counter-storytelling** (CRT, via revision document): Narrative methods that reveal Black epistemologies erased by dominant historiography.
- **Primal history of modernity** (Gilroy after Walter Benjamin): Modernity rewritten "from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).
- **Tertium quid**: Du Bois's term for the "intermediate category between animal and human" into which Black people were forced (cited in Gilroy, 1993: 55).
- **Chemical gaze** (Gilroy after Foucault): The perceptual regime within which the modern idea of race "leaked out" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).

## 3. Slide-by-Slide Walkthrough

**Slides 1-3: Defining race and racism.** The lecture opens by noting that the word "race" is similar to French "racine" (root) and possibly Arabic "ras" (head, beginning). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology and seemed to refer to a particular biological group or type or category" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Racism is then defined as the belief that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3).

**Slides 4-5: Provocations.** The lecturer asks: who believes there are different races? How many races are there? What criteria identify them? The everyday question "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) is flagged as the contemporary trace of racial classification.

**Slides 6-7: Linnaeus.** Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) divided Homo Sapiens into four sub-species: Homo Europaeus, Homo Afer (Sub-Saharan Africans), Homo Asiaticus and Homo Americanus (Native Americans). Classification rested on morphology but Linnaeus also claimed differences in "temperament and psychology" (slide 6). The lecturer notes finer subdivisions ("Northern Europeans"/"Southern Europeans").

**Slide 8: Blumenbach.** Johann Friedrich Blumenbach (1752-1840) proposed five races, supposedly evidenced by skull shape: Caucasian (white), Mongolian (yellow), Malayan (brown), Negroid (black) and American (red). Again, supposed psychological differences track morphology.

**Slide 9: Power-knowledge.** "Science claimed to 'observe' racial differences. The classifications they produced appeared to give them knowledge … This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 9). The lecturer ties this to Foucault (L7) and asks whether the question of "colour" runs through race talk because there "needs to be something to be 'observed' in order to classify." This is vision and power. The accompanying image juxtaposes the 1868 "Apollo Belvidere" / "Creole Negro" / "Young chimpanzee" comparison with the 1933 photograph of Nazi racial measurements.

**Slides 10-11: Du Bois on race as social fact.** "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). In a "fictional" dialogue Du Bois insists race is "a cultural, sometimes a historical fact" and concludes: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153).

**Slide 12: Du Bois biography.** Born Massachusetts 1868, died 1963. Studied in Berlin in the 1890s; first Black PhD from Harvard; polymath; first generation of African Americans after Emancipation (1863).

**Slide 13: Post-Emancipation.** The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared all slaves free; the post-Civil-War amendments made it unconstitutional to deny the vote on the basis of "race" or "color" or prior slave status. Yet "writing a generation after all this, in the 1890s, Du Bois asked why nothing had really changed for African Americans" (slide 13).

**Slide 14: "Being a problem".** Du Bois's opening: "being a problem is a strange experience" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). The visiting cards anecdote: a girl refuses his card and "it dawned on me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others … shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). Gibson's editorial gloss: "one's sense of race and racial terms comes only from experience" (Gibson, 1996: xxv).

**Slide 15: Double consciousness.** "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The veiled "Negro" gets "no true self-consciousness but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

**Slide 16: Twoness vs the unitary actor.** "For Du Bois, being double is the basis of the experience of the world. This differs from the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel" (slide 16). "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). "The history of the American Negro is the history of this strife … this longing to attain self-conscious manhood, to merge his double self into a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

**Slide 17: The waste of double aims.** "This waste of double aims, this seeking to satisfy two unreconciled ideals, has wrought sad havoc with the courage and faith and deeds of ten thousand thousand people" (Du Bois, 1903: 6).

**Slide 18: Substantive freedom.** Despite the Emancipation Proclamation and the 15th Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found freedom in his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 7). "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11).

**Slide 19: Neither Africanise nor bleach.** "he wishes neither of the older selves to be lost. He would not Africanize America, for America has much to teach the world … He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The lecturer underlines the link of modernity and slavery.

**Slide 20: Modernity built race as a labour fix.** Long Du Bois quotation: "In the latter part of the seventeenth and early in the eighteenth centuries, the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4). "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). Source: Du Bois, W.E.B. 1898. 'The Study of the Negro Problems', *Annals of the American Academy*, Jan 1898: 1-23.

**Slide 21: Morrison biography.** Toni Morrison: novelist, writer, thinker. The lecturer flags the 2017 Harvard lectures published as *The Origin of Others* (Harvard University Press).

**Slide 22: Morrison's central question.** "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter? … Race is the classification of a species, and we are the human race, period [full-stop]. Then what is this other thing, the hostility, the social racism, the Othering" (Morrison, 2017: 15). Citing Bruce Baum: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29).

**Slide 23: Americanness as colour.** "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). The lecturer adds: "[Think also about Trump and what it means nowadays to be a 'Mexican-American']."

**Slide 24: Media and Othering.** "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like (or ought to look like) and what in fact we are like" (Morrison, 2017: 37). Suggested novels: *Beloved*, *Paradise*, *The Bluest Eye*; also Octavia Butler's *Dawn* and Angela Carter's *The Magic Toyshop*.

**Slide 25: Gilroy biography.** Paul Gilroy, currently at UCL, previously LSE, Harvard, Goldsmiths and Essex. Most famous books: *There Ain't No Black in the Union Jack*, *The Black Atlantic: Modernity and Double Consciousness*, *Between Camps* (published in the US as *Against Race*).

**Slide 26: Race as primary object of knowledge and power.** Gilroy is influenced by Foucault but does not fully agree. "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).

**Slide 27: Africa as constitutive, not peripheral.** Africa "was not on the side-lines of the West and the development of modernity." The transnational, evidenced in the slave trade, was always part of Western modernity; hence the "Black Atlantic." Ethnic/"racial" identities are outcomes of political, economic and cultural processes, not fixed essences. "We need to rethink history and modernity and not just 'include' the marginal positions that have been ignored. But to rewrite history and modernity from these positions" (slide 27).

**Slide 28: Racial terror and reason.** "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Annotated "(Think of Dussel)" by the lecturer. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

**Slide 29: Standpoint, not unmediated experience.** "This unstable standpoint is to be understood in a different way from the clarion calls to … unmediated experience which sometimes appear in association with the term." "In terms of contemporary politics and social theory, the value of this project lies in its promise to uncover both an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood … that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Annotated "(Think of Du Bois)."

**Slide 30: Conclusion.** "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do. This is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist. It is about how the modern social world is constituted on forms of racism which we might not be able to recognise immediately but are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 30).

## 5. Debates & Tensions

- **Biological vs social conceptions of race**: Scientific racism insists race is biologically real; Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all reject this but disagree on what to put in its place. Du Bois retains "race" as a cultural and historical fact; Gilroy presses towards "race ends here" and the abolition of raciology; Morrison occupies a middle ground, naming race as power-effect.
- **Inclusion vs rewriting**: Should we "add" Black voices to existing modernity narratives or rewrite modernity from the standpoint of the enslaved? Gilroy explicitly demands the latter, citing Walter Benjamin's "primal history."
- **Unitary vs split self**: Du Bois's double consciousness explicitly contests the unified actor of Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. Can sociology accommodate a self that is constitutively riven?
- **Racism as individual prejudice vs structural condition**: Lecture conclusion stresses that this "is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist" but how the social world is constituted on unrecognised forms of racism in institutions, art, music and ways of seeing.
- **Pessimism vs progress**: The revision document notes that CRT treats racial progress as a "myth" reversed by "interest convergence." Du Bois oscillates: Emancipation has not delivered, yet *Souls* ends on the possibility that two world-races may give each to each.
- **Universalism vs particularism**: Gilroy critiques bourgeois humanism for repairing itself by adding the figures Du Bois called "tertium quid"; he wants conceptions of selfhood that are "forever disassociated from the psychological and epistemic correlates of racial subordination" (Gilroy, 1993: 56).
- **Visibility and the body**: Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' asks whether nanoscale biology and digital imaging make race "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Yet Fanon's epidermalisation still organises everyday life (Gilroy, 1998: 254-255).
- **Africa as periphery vs Africa as constitutive**: Gilroy explicitly contests the view that Africa was on the side-lines of Western modernity (slide 27).

## 6. Examples & Applications

- **Jim Crow segregation**: Du Bois's empirical anchor for racial realism; the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The revision document names Jim Crow as Du Bois's foremost example of race as social fact.
- **The visiting cards refusal**: Du Bois's autobiographical scene in the New England schoolhouse (Du Bois, 1903: 2-3) as the everyday mechanism by which the veil is communicated.
- **The Ku Klux Klan and carpet-baggers**: Du Bois's catalogue of the Reconstruction failure: "the holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry" left the freedman with no substantive freedom (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **The Massachusetts-to-Georgia slave statutes**: Du Bois's (1898: 3-4) demonstration that racial law was a labour-supply fix; "such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color."
- **Indented servants vs life-slaves**: Du Bois shows how legal categories diverged once "the economic superiority of the slave system" combined with religious and linguistic difference to harden a racial line (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4).
- **The 1868 "Apollo Belvidere / Creole Negro / Young Chimpanzee" plate**: Slide 9's example of scientific racism's visual rhetoric, placing the Greek ideal at the top of a hierarchy that descends through the Black body to the chimpanzee.
- **The 1933 Nazi racial measurements photograph**: Slide 9's example of scientific racism institutionalised in twentieth-century state machinery.
- **The "Where are you really from?" question** (slide 4): The everyday racialised question through which the colour line still operates.
- **Sartje Baartman and Michael Jordan / Bugs Bunny**: Gilroy's paired example of nineteenth-century display of the racialised body and contemporary "pas de deux" of "the super-human, godly Michael Jordan and Bugs Bunny" as "the reductio ad absurdum of hybridized African trixter tale-telling" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).
- **The Linnaeus frontispiece (orangutan and Negro girl)**: Gilroy reads it as showing the "suggested kinship between these sub- and anti-human species" and the entanglement of race with sex and gender (Gilroy, 1998: 254).
- **Robert Lincoln Watkins and the "micromotoscope"**: Gilroy's example of an early-twentieth-century shift from comparative anatomy to microscopy in producing racial knowledge (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **Francis Crick's "eugenic ravings"**: Gilroy's example of how the founding of molecular biology was implicated in the "redefinition of life in terms of information, messages and code" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **NMR/MRI and PET imaging**: Gilroy notes that these technologies have "remade the relationship between the seeable and the unseen … status of bio-racial differences that vanish at these levels of resolution" (Gilroy, 1998: 253).
- **Kant's *Physische Geographie* and the split bamboo cane**: Gilroy cites Eze and Neugebauer on Kant's writing about "the qualities of Negro skin and the practical problems it presented when pain had to be inflicted with a split bamboo cane" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). Anchor case for epidermalisation avant la lettre.
- **Hegel on "the African's capacity for historical action"**: Gilroy (1998: 254-255) cites Hegel's "limitations he placed upon the African's capacity for historical action" as exemplifying "epidermal thinking in its emergent form."
- **Toussaint to the devil**: Du Bois names Toussaint Louverture in a catalogue of how white prejudice extends "from Toussaint to the devil" (Du Bois, 1903: 6); the Haitian revolutionary as the disavowed object of white contempt.
- **The Sorrow Songs**: Du Bois's Forethought announces that each chapter begins with "some echo of haunting melody from the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past" (Du Bois, 1903: Forethought).
- **Ethiopia the Shadowy and Egypt the Sphinx**: Du Bois names the long African past whose "powers of single black men flash here and there like falling stars" (Du Bois, 1903: 4).
- **The school-to-prison pipeline**: The revision document names this as a CRT example of how the US prison and military system functions as "a modern form of Black slavery."
- **Prisons and the army as modern slavery**: The revision document explicitly cites these as the CRT example of how "racial progress is always pushed back, and only occurs through 'interest convergence'."
- **American identity as whiteness**: Morrison's example of immigrants "embrac[ing] their whiteness" to become "real Americans" (Morrison, 2017: 17); the revision document highlights that "American identity is tied to whiteness, reinforced by media and culture / law and order / religion."
- **Trump-era "Mexican-American"** (slide 23): Lecturer's contemporary annotation on Morrison's whiteness-as-Americanness thesis.

## 9. Past-Q Essay Answers

### Past Q 2025. What is meant by 'scientific racism'? How have sociologists tried to counter this concept?

In this essay I define scientific racism as the nineteenth-century project of grounding racial classification in supposedly biological fact, and I show how sociologists from Du Bois through Morrison to Gilroy have countered it by treating race as a social, historical and political construction.

I begin with definition. The lecture is explicit that the word "race" had no biological referent before the nineteenth century. Its etymology is benign: the French "racine" (root) or Arabic "ras" (head, beginning). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology and seemed to refer to a particular biological group or type or category" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Scientific racism is the doctrine that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3). I trace its origin through two figures. I conclude that scientific racism is the modern project of dressing racial hierarchy in biological clothing, and that sociologists have countered it by relocating race's reality to social structure (Du Bois), to the affective work of othering (Morrison) and to the changing technologies of vision (Gilroy). The counter is unfinished because the legacy of scientific racism is sedimented in institutions and habits of perception that survive the discrediting of its biological premises.

### Past Q 2024. How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to two theorists.

In this essay I argue that European modernity was not merely accompanied by unequal race relations but constituted by them, and I make this case through W.E.B. Du Bois and Paul Gilroy. I treat the question's word "benefit" seriously: the wealth, philosophy and self-image of European modernity were materially and conceptually subsidised by the racial subordination of African peoples.

I begin with the economic and legal foundation that Du Bois sets out. In 'The Study of the Negro Problems' (1898), Du Bois observes that "in the latter part of the seventeenth and early in the eighteenth centuries, the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4). The supply problem was solved by importing Africans and creating a racial category through statute, not biology: "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). Du Bois shows that "dozens of enactments, from Massachusetts to Georgia, were made 'for the proper regulation of slaves and servants'" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4), and that the racial line hardened when "the economic superiority of the slave system" combined with linguistic, religious and morphological difference. I take this as a foundational point: race emerged as a legal-economic device because slavery was more profitable than indenture, and only afterwards congealed into the supposed natural fact that scientific racism would later "discover." European modernity benefited twice over: from the unpaid labour itself and from the ideological asset of being able to think of itself as the racially enlightened standard. As Du Bois insists in *Dusk of Dawn*, "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). Modernity profited from a fiction it knew to be flimsy.

Gilroy radicalises this insight. In *The Black Atlantic* he argues that we must abandon the comforting story that slavery was a regrettable side-business of Europe's rise. "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" (Gilroy, 1993: 54); it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" and "has retained a central place in the historical memories of the black Atlantic" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). I read Gilroy as showing that the Atlantic triangle of European masters, Caribbean plantations and African slaves was the laboratory of modern bureaucratic management, modern industrial finance and modern consumption. Crucially, his polemic against Weberian rationalism cuts to the heart of the benefit question: "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Modernity did not benefit despite slavery; rationality and slavery rose together.

I then trace the cultural and epistemic dividend. The lecture's account of scientific racism, from Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) to Blumenbach's five-race skulls, shows that European science gained authority precisely by classifying non-European peoples as inferior. This is Gilroy's point: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). Knowledge production was an arm of empire. Gilroy makes this concrete with the orangutan-and-Negro frontispiece of Linnaeus's *A Genuine and Universal System of Natural History*, in which "the suggested kinship between these sub- and anti-human species" anchors the European self-image (Gilroy, 1998: 254). The lecture's slide 9 reinforces the visual rhetoric: the 1868 plate that places "Apollo Belvidere" above the "Creole Negro" and the "Young Chimpanzee" is exactly the cultural dividend of scientific classification. Even the Enlightenment's universalist humanism, the bourgeois humanism Gilroy critiques, relied on excluding the people Du Bois calls a "tertium quid," an "intermediate category between animal and human" (cited in Gilroy, 1993: 55).

A counter-argument is that modernity was a generally progressive movement and that slavery was a pre-modern residue, abolished by modernity's own reforming impulse. I reject this. The legal innovations that produced racial slavery were not pre-modern relics but modern inventions, as Du Bois (1898: 3-4) documents in the statutes that hardened the slave/servant boundary. Emancipation, when it came, was incomplete: Du Bois observes that "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). "The holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) left the freedman in formally free but substantively unfree conditions. The revision document specifies the continuity: the prison and the army operate as "a modern form of Black slavery," and CRT's "interest convergence" thesis shows that racial progress is reversed when it ceases to align with white interests.

Finally, I press Gilroy's normative implication. If modernity benefited from slavery, then a sociology that simply "adds" Black voices to a fundamentally white narrative is inadequate. We must, with Gilroy, attempt "the primal history of modernity â€¦ reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). Following Walter Benjamin, this is "the primal history of modernity" written from the underside (Gilroy, 1993: 55). This is not merely a matter of reparative recognition but of analytical accuracy: the slaves' standpoint reveals what the masters' standpoint conceals. "We need to rethink history and modernity and not just 'include' the marginal positions that have been ignored" (slide 27).

I conclude that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in three reinforcing ways: economically through unpaid labour (Du Bois, 1898), culturally through the prestige of "scientific" racial classification (Linnaeus, Blumenbach, slide 9), and politically through the self-image of a free Europe defined against unfree others. Du Bois and Gilroy together show that race and modernity are inextricably linked.

### Past Q 2022. Drawing from at least two of W.E.B Du Bois, Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy, discuss the implications of "race" for contemporary society.

In this essay I draw on Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy to argue that "race," though biologically empty, has profound implications for contemporary society in three registers: the constitution of the self, the production of national belonging, and the structure of global modernity.

I begin with the implication for selfhood, drawing on Du Bois. In *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) Du Bois introduces double consciousness as the experiential consequence of living within the colour line: "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The self is constitutively split: "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). For contemporary society, the implication is that the unitary actor presupposed by mainstream sociology (Weber's rational actor, Goffman's strategic self, Garfinkel's competent member) is unavailable to racialised subjects. The lecture is explicit: Du Bois's "being double" "differs from the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel" (slide 16). Consider the everyday racialised question "Where are you really from?" (slide 4); it imposes a doubling that the unmarked subject does not experience. Du Bois's veil is reproduced every time citizenship is bureaucratically questioned along racial lines.

I then turn to Morrison for the implication for national belonging. In *The Origin of Others* she observes that "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). National identity is not just a passport but a racialised performance. The lecturer adds the contemporary annotation: "[Think also about Trump and what it means nowadays to be a 'Mexican-American']" (slide 23). The revision document echoes the point: "American identity is tied to whiteness, reinforced by media and culture / law and order / religion." Morrison adds that race operates through othering: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). The dominant group uses racial categorisation to stabilise its own normality.

I take the third implication from Gilroy: the structural implication for modernity itself. In *The Black Atlantic* and 'Race Ends Here', Gilroy refuses to treat slavery and colonialism as side-issues. "The order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" (Gilroy, 1993: 54); it built modernity through the Atlantic slave system. The implication for contemporary society is structural: the modern social world is built on the racialised foundations Gilroy describes, and Morrison's "routine media presentations" (Morrison, 2017: 37) continue to "narrow our views of what humans look like." Gilroy's normative claim follows: we cannot fix this by adding Black voices to a fundamentally white modernity; we must reconstruct modernity from the slaves' point of view (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

I consider examples across all three thinkers. The revision document's catalogue of CRT examples (the school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as a "modern form of Black slavery"; the myth of racial progress reversed by interest convergence; counter-storytelling as method) gives contemporary content to all three implications. The Black presence in the global media commodification Gilroy names with his Sartje Baartman / Michael Jordan / Bugs Bunny pairing (Gilroy, 1998: 252) shows that the Black Atlantic continues to circulate culturally but on asymmetric terms; the "pas de deux between the super-human, godly Michael Jordan and Bugs Bunny" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) is contemporary spectacle in a long racialising tradition.

I anticipate the objection that race is a declining force in a globalising, multicultural world. I think this is wrong on Du Bois's evidence. As Du Bois warned, equality in law is not enough: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). And on Morrison's evidence: "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like (or ought to look like) and what in fact we are like" (Morrison, 2017: 37); racial narrowing of perception is part of how contemporary media operates. And on Gilroy's evidence: the chemical gaze (Gilroy, 1998: 253) and Fanon's epidermalisation (Gilroy, 1998: 254-255) continue to shape the way bodies are read.

I conclude that race has three deep implications for contemporary society. Through Du Bois, it shapes the very structure of racialised selfhood, producing a doubled, second-sighted experience that mainstream sociology must learn from. Through Morrison, it shapes national belonging by making whiteness the unmarked currency of full citizenship. Through Gilroy, it shapes global modernity by reproducing the asymmetries laid down by the Atlantic slave system. Race, in short, is biologically empty and sociologically full.

### Past Q 2021. Du Bois asked: "How does it feel to be a problem?" Outline and assess his response to this question.

In this essay I outline Du Bois's response to his own question and then assess it on three criteria: descriptive accuracy, conceptual originality, and political adequacy.

Du Bois opens *The Souls of Black Folk* with the question and immediately defers it: "To the real question, How does it feel to be a problem? I answer seldom a word" (Du Bois, 1903: 2). The question is not really a question put to him; it is a polite circumlocution for the white interlocutor's racialised gaze. As Du Bois reports, white people approach him "in a half-hesitant sort of way" and offer evasions: "I know an excellent colored man in my town; or, I fought at Mechanicsville; or, Do not these Southern outrages make your blood boil?" (Du Bois, 1903: 2). Du Bois's response unfolds across the rest of the chapter in three moves: an autobiographical scene that names the experience, a conceptual formulation of double consciousness, and a programmatic call for the integration of work, culture and liberty.

The autobiographical scene is the visiting cards incident. As a New England schoolboy "away up in the hills of New England, where the dark Housatonic winds between Hoosac and Taghkanic to the sea" (Du Bois, 1903: 2-3), Du Bois recalls a tall newcomer who "refused my card, refused it peremptorily, with a glance. Then it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). I take this to be Du Bois's empirical answer: "being a problem" feels like the sudden, unwilled recognition that one is on the wrong side of an invisible barrier. The metaphor of the veil names this barrier; the metaphor of "second-sight" names the compensatory perceptual gift it bestows: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world, a world which yields him no true self-consciousness, but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 3).

The conceptual formulation is double consciousness: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Crucially, Du Bois insists that this doubling is not a deficit to be repaired by assimilation but a structural feature of African-American life. "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The history of African America is the history of this strife: "this longing to attain self-conscious manhood, to merge his double self into a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The aim is not to abandon either pole: "He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

The programmatic move is the demand for substantive rather than formal equality. Despite the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the Fifteenth Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Du Bois catalogues the post-Emancipation failures: "the holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry, and the contradictory advice of friends and foes, left the bewildered serf with no new watch-word beyond the old cry for freedom" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Du Bois ends with the famous demand: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together, not successively but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). The implication is that being a problem feels like being formally free but substantively unfree, formally a citizen but substantively excluded.

I now assess. On descriptive accuracy, Du Bois's response is remarkable. The visiting cards scene captures the everyday, often petty, mechanism by which racial difference is communicated, not by lynching, not by Jim Crow law, but by a glance and a refused card. The contemporary "Where are you really from?" question (slide 4) is the visiting-card refusal at the front door of citizenship. The revision document's CRT example of how "racial progress is always pushed back" gives institutional content to Du Bois's diagnostic: being a problem feels like a recurring reversal of formally granted equality.

On conceptual originality, double consciousness is one of the most important contributions of American social theory. The lecture is explicit that it differs from "the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel." Gilroy reads Du Bois's twoness as anticipating "the new conceptions of selfhood and individuation that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Unlike the mainstream sociology of the self, Du Bois ties the doubling to race and power rather than treating it as a universal feature of selfhood. This specificity is the originality.

On political adequacy, I find Du Bois's response more ambivalent. The closing demand for the merger of African and American selves can sound assimilationist; he insists he "would not Africanize America, for America has too much to teach the world" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Gilroy raises a different concern: the demand to merge the two selves may understate the irreducibility of the rupture that slavery represents. Gilroy's "primal history of modernity â€¦ reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55) implies that some doublings cannot and should not be merged. The revision document's note that CRT treats racial progress as a "myth" continually pushed back gives Du Bois's optimism a sober contemporary check.

There are limitations to acknowledge. Du Bois's frame is gendered ("self-conscious manhood," "two warring ideals in one dark body"), which obscures the specifically gendered racial experience that later thinkers (Hill Collins, Crenshaw, hooks; see revision document Â§5) would name. His American focus underplays the transnational frame that Gilroy's Black Atlantic recovers. Yet the analytical core, that racial subordination produces a doubled, partial, mediated selfhood, survives these critiques.

I conclude that Du Bois's response to "How does it feel to be a problem?" is descriptively acute (the veil and the refused card), conceptually transformative (double consciousness and second sight), and politically incomplete but generative (the demand for substantive freedom). It feels, he tells us, like always seeing yourself through someone else's eyes; the response is to fight for a world in which that doubling becomes optional rather than imposed.

In this essay I answer the two parts of the question in sequence. First, I reconstruct what Du Bois means by the veil. Second, I show why he treats it as constitutive, not contingent or remediable by individual effort, of African American experience.

I begin with definition. The veil enters *The Souls of Black Folk* in the autobiographical scene of the refused visiting card. Du Bois recalls the moment as a New England schoolboy when "it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). The veil is, in the first instance, a metaphor for social exclusion that operates without overt segregation: no wall, no fence, no law required, just the refused card, the averted glance, the failure of recognition. The veil is invisible from the white side and palpable from the Black.

I then add three properties Du Bois gives the veil. First, it is perceptual: it organises how the two sides see each other. Black people see themselves "through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 3); white people see Black people through the categories the veil makes available (curiosity, contempt, pity). Second, the veil bestows second sight: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world" (Du Bois, 1903: 3). To live behind the veil is to know one's own world and to perceive, through partial transparency, the world that excludes one, an asymmetric epistemic position. Third, the veil generates double consciousness, the specific psychological consequence: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

I now turn to the second part of the question: why constitutive. I read three reasons in Du Bois.

The first reason is historical-structural. The veil is the lingering presence of slavery and post-Emancipation failure. Du Bois insists that "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Formal Emancipation (1863), the Fifteenth Amendment, even the post-Civil-War legal reforms, did not dismantle the veil. The veil is the social architecture left behind when the legal structure of slavery is removed but the racial categories it produced are not. Du Bois names the catalogue of post-Emancipation failures: "the holocaust of war, the terrors of the Ku-Klux Klan, the lies of carpet-baggers, the disorganization of industry, and the contradictory advice of friends and foes, left the bewildered serf with no new watch-word beyond the old cry for freedom" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Later, in *Dusk of Dawn*, Du Bois makes the social-fact argument explicit: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact â€¦ the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The veil is what makes Jim Crow durable.

The second reason is sociological-relational. The veil is constitutive because the racialised self is constituted in relation to the white world's perception. There is no Black selfhood "behind" the veil that is independent of it; the very experience of being on the wrong side of the veil is what produces the doubled selfhood Du Bois names. This is why the question "How does it feel to be a problem?" (Du Bois, 1903: 2) is constitutive, not occasional. To be a problem is to be defined by the white world's framing of one's presence as a question to be answered. The lecture is explicit: the Du Boisian self "differs from the sense of unity, of being a single actor" presumed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel (slide 16).

The third reason is phenomenological. The veil names a perceptual condition that organises everyday life: every encounter, every glance, every conversation. Du Bois's literary method in *Souls* (autobiographical scene, sociological generalisation, historical reflection, lyric meditation) communicates the texture of veiled experience. The Forethought announces that "before each chapter, as now printed, stands a bar of the Sorrow Songs, some echo of haunting melody from the only American music which welled up from black souls in the dark past"; veiled experience speaks through Black musical tradition. The veil is not an occasional irritation; it is the medium of perception.

I then make Du Bois's case against the alternatives that would treat the veil as non-constitutive. The first alternative is individualist: "any Black person can transcend the veil by talent, education or virtue." Du Bois personally exemplifies how much this fails; the first Black PhD from Harvard, the polymath sociologist studying in Berlin, the international intellectual, still describes the visiting cards moment as definitive. The second alternative is assimilationist: "the veil will dissolve as Black Americans become more like white Americans." Du Bois's refusal, "He would not bleach his Negro blood in a flood of white Americanism" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), names this as both undesirable and impossible while the veil persists. The third alternative is separatist: "Black Americans can step out from behind the veil by leaving white America." Du Bois rejects this too: "He would not Africanize America, for America has too much to teach the world and Africa" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The veil is constitutive precisely because it cannot be exited by individual, assimilationist or separatist means.

I bring the argument into the present through the lecture's framing. The everyday "Where are you really from?" question (slide 4) is the contemporary refused card. The revision document's CRT note that "racial progress is always pushed back, and only occurs through 'interest convergence'" generalises the veil's persistence: every formally granted equality is liable to bureaucratic and institutional erosion. Morrison's adjacent observation that "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like" (Morrison, 2017: 37) shows the veil operating in contemporary media perception.

I anticipate Gilroy's worry that the veil's constitutive character might encase Black experience in fixity. Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' argues that we should be wary of "the absolute sovereignty of unmediated experience" (Gilroy, 1993: 56) and seek "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). Du Bois has a Gilroyan resource: second sight is not a permanent metaphysical condition but a critical capacity that the veil generates and that political action can deploy.

I conclude that the veil, for Du Bois, names the invisible-from-one-side social barrier that produces double consciousness, second sight and the doubled selfhood of the racialised subject under formal equality. He treats it as constitutive because it is historical-structural (the residue of slavery and Reconstruction failure), sociological-relational (Black selfhood is made in its presence) and phenomenological (it organises everyday perception). The veil is what the colour line feels like from the wrong side; African American experience is shaped by it because it has nowhere else to be.

### Mock 4. Compare Du Bois's double consciousness with Fanon's epidermalisation as discussed by Gilroy.

I begin with Du Bois. Double consciousness, set out in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), names "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The accompanying triad is the veil ("shut out from their world by a vast veil," Du Bois, 1903: 3), second sight ("born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world," Du Bois, 1903: 3) and twoness ("an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings," Du Bois, 1903: 5). The frame is the post-Emancipation United States; the social architecture is the post-Reconstruction colour line.

I then turn to Fanon via Gilroy. In 'Race Ends Here' Gilroy describes Fanon's concept of "epidermalization" as the colonial process of making the Black body meaningful by depositing "qualities of 'colour'" on its surface. "Let me propose that the dismal order of power and differentiation, defined by its intention to make the mute body disclose the truth of its racial identities, can be apprehended through the critical notion of 'epidermalization' bequeathed to us by Frantz Fanon" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). Fanon's idea, Gilroy explains, "was born from a philosopher-psychologist's phenomenological ambitions and their distinctive way of seeing, as well as understanding, the importance of sight" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). Epidermalisation refers to "a historically specific system for making bodies meaningful by endowing in them qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254).

The structural diagnosis is shared. Both writers say: the racialised subject is not free to inhabit a unitary self because the dominant world's perception constitutes the subject as racial. Du Bois's "the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) is the precursor of Fanon's colonial gaze; Du Bois's veil is the precursor of Fanon's epidermal screen; Du Bois's second sight is the precursor of Fanon's critical phenomenology of being looked at. Gilroy explicitly draws the link by treating epidermalisation as a Black Atlantic concept continuous with Du Bois's analytic.

I then trace three substantive differences.

First, the frame. Du Bois writes from the post-slavery United States; Fanon from the late colonial Francophone world. The "American, Negro" twoness is national-internal; both poles are claims on the same polity. Fanon's epidermalisation, on Gilroy's reading, names "an era where colonial power had made epidermalizing into a dominant principle of political power" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). Du Bois's question is what citizenship means under formal equality; Fanon's is what subjectivity means under colonial subjection. The two frames map closely onto Gilroy's Black Atlantic, which integrates the US and the Caribbean-Francophone strands into one circuit.

Second, the bodily emphasis. Du Bois names "two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5); the body is the site of the strain but not the explicit object of analysis. Fanon foregrounds the body. As Gilroy summarises, "Epidermalized power violated the human body in its symmetrical, intersubjective, social humanity, in its species being, in its fragile relationship to other fragile bodies and in its connection to the redemptive potential inherent in its own wholesome or perhaps its suffering corporeality, our being towards death" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). Epidermalisation is precisely the colonial process by which "the human sensorium has had to be educated" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) to read race off the body. Gilroy invokes Kant's *Physische Geographie* and its remarks on Negro skin and "the practical problems it presented when pain had to be inflicted with a split bamboo cane" (Gilroy, 1998: 254) as a prefiguration of epidermal thinking; Hegel's restrictions on "the African's capacity for historical action" (Gilroy, 1998: 254-255) are a parallel example.

Third, the political consequence. Du Bois ends *Souls* with a programmatic hope of merging the two souls into "a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) while demanding substantive equality: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). Gilroy's reading of Fanon (Gilroy, 1998: 255) is more pessimistic about the colonial-epidermal regime and more focused on its overturning. Gilroy concludes with the hope that close attention to epidermalisation's "contemporary analogs not where he looked, on the surface of the body, but deep within it" might yield "the anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255).

I consider how Gilroy synthesises the two. *The Black Atlantic* draws on both, treating diasporic Black thought as an Atlantic conversation in which Du Bois's "twoness" and Fanon's "epidermalisation" name two stages of one experiential analytic, modulated by different historical conditions. Gilroy uses Du Bois's standpoint epistemology and Fanon's epidermal-phenomenological vocabulary to argue for the slaves' standpoint as the privileged position from which to rewrite modernity (Gilroy, 1993: 55-56).

I provide examples that show the analytic still operates. The 1868 plate of "Apollo Belvidere / Creole Negro / Young Chimpanzee" (slide 9) is exactly the epidermal logic in visual form: the body is made to disclose racial truth at a glance. The 1933 photograph of Nazi racial measurements (slide 9) is the same logic instrumentalised. Gilroy's Sartje Baartman / Michael Jordan / Bugs Bunny example (Gilroy, 1998: 252) extends the analytic into contemporary spectacle: the racialised body remains an object of fascinated public gaze, even when scientific racism's biological supports have collapsed.

I anticipate the objection that the two concepts have been over-applied. The safeguard is historical specificity. Du Bois's concept tracks the particular doubling of post-Emancipation US racialised citizens; Fanon's tracks the particular phenomenology of the colonial Black subject. Used carefully, they remain analytically distinct: double consciousness is about the perceived doubling of the self; epidermalisation is about the imposed making-meaningful of the body's surface.

I evaluate the comparison politically. Both concepts ground anti-racist politics in the perceptual privilege of those subordinated. The revision document's catalogue of CRT methods (counter-storytelling, racial realism) operationalises Du Boisian double consciousness; the rejection of bourgeois humanism by Gilroy (1993: 55) extends Fanonian epidermal critique into a critique of modern philosophical anthropology.

I conclude that Du Bois's double consciousness and Fanon's epidermalisation, as Gilroy reads it, are siblings of one Black Atlantic analytic. They share the structural diagnosis of racialised constitution by the dominant gaze, differ in their national versus colonial frame, in their psychological versus phenomenological-bodily emphasis, and in their reformist versus revolutionary political horizon. Together they constitute the experiential foundation on which contemporary critical race theory rests. The revision document names the inheritance: "Gilroy in the Black Atlantic claims that Modernity / world is shaped by African people through resistance, slavery and cultural exchange."

The image is optical: an after-image is the visual trace left on the retina after the source of light is removed. It is real to the perceiver but has no current external referent. I unpack the analogy into three claims.

The after-image is what is left after biology has been refuted; it is not the whole of racial reality.

The metaphor gives us the perceptual diagnosis; Du Bois gives us the institutional diagnosis.

I conclude that Gilroy's after-image metaphor captures something important (race persists as a perceptual reality after its biological supports have collapsed) but is incomplete on its own. It can mean: should sociology stop using the category in its measurements and theorisations? It can mean: can sociology's use of the category be reformed without dropping it? It can mean: what is the timeline of category-loss? I take all three readings seriously.

I start with the abolitionist case. The strongest version comes from Paul Gilroy. In 'Race Ends Here' (1998) and *Against Race* (2000) he hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) and worries that sociological use of racial categories, even in anti-racist mode, can reinscribe the very thing it tries to dismantle. His worry is that the categorising practices of sociology are continuous with the classificatory practices that originally produced race. The abolitionist programme would have sociology stop using race at all, treating it as a category that should fade as institutional practice changes.

I find the abolitionist case ethically attractive and practically premature. Three reasons.

The first reason is that race remains real as social fact. The institutional sites Du Bois describes have changed but the disparities they produced persist. The revision document's CRT examples (the school-to-prison pipeline; prisons and the army as a "modern form of Black slavery") name contemporary institutional sites where racial categorisation continues to operate. To stop measuring race would be to make these disparities harder to see and harder to address. The abolitionist would have sociology measure something else; it is not obvious that "something else" would capture the racialised facts better.

Power continues to manufacture racial categorisation. Sociology has to track the production to analyse it. An abolitionist sociology would describe institutional racism without naming the categorising operation that institutional racism performs.

The third reason is that anti-racist politics requires racial measurement. The revision document records that CRT's diagnostic work depends on the racial category: interest convergence depends on data showing differential treatment by race; counter-storytelling depends on identifying the subjugated standpoint from which to tell stories. Removing the category does not remove the inequality but does remove the political tools for naming it.

I now consider what reform could look like, abolishing race in the long term without losing the analytical category in the short term.

First, treat the category as historical and constructed, not natural. The lecture is explicit: "Races do not exist" biologically (slide 30). Sociological use of race should always foreground this. Du Bois's "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact" (Du Bois, 1940: 153) is the right framing.

Second, distinguish use from reification. Sociology can use racial categories analytically without claiming that the categories track natural kinds. The same logic applies to "class" and "gender", categories that are socially real, analytically useful and biologically loose. Du Bois used race in his sociological studies without essentialising; the lecture's slide 12 notes he was "a polymath, a true intellectual" whose research method he developed at Berlin and Harvard. The discipline can do this.

Third, design categorisation to be self-undermining. Anti-racist measurement should aim at its own redundancy. The category exists because the disparity exists; the goal is to dismantle the disparity, after which the category becomes unnecessary. Gilroy's "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) is the right horizon, but the toxins do not finish their work in a year.

Fourth, integrate Morrison and Du Bois. Track the affective and identarian work of racial categorisation (Morrison) alongside its structural and institutional reproduction (Du Bois, CRT). Without Morrison, the structural account misses why people resist the de-categorisation. Without Du Bois, the affective account loses its institutional traction.

I anticipate three objections.

The first objection is from Gilroy: any use of race entrenches it. I think this is partly true but cannot win the practical argument. Choosing not to measure race does not dissolve the racial reality measured; it just makes the reality invisible to the measurement. The right response is more reflexive use, not abolition.

The second objection is from a colour-blind position: race-conscious sociology contributes to the very divisions it claims to study. The revision document's CRT reply is that colour-blindness in a racially structured society protects the structure. Refusing to see race does not unmake it.

The third objection is methodological: race in survey data conflates different things (self-identification, ascription, ancestry). The right response is methodological refinement, not abolition.

I bring the discussion back to Du Bois. Du Bois warned that "Equality in law is not enough"; formal abolition of racial categorisation in law does not abolish the social fact. The same logic applies to sociology: abolishing race in the discipline does not abolish race in the society. The category can be retired when the disparity has been dismantled; not before.

I close with Gilroy's normative horizon. Sociology should work towards a future in which race is no longer needed as an analytical category, not because we have ignored it but because the institutional and affective infrastructure that makes it analytically necessary has been dismantled. The work is generational. In the meantime, use the category honestly, reflexively and with the aim of dissolving the conditions that require it.

I conclude that sociology cannot abolish race in the present without losing the analytical traction needed to measure and contest racial disparities, but that the discipline can and should work towards a future in which the category becomes unnecessary. The intermediate path is reflexive use: race in sociology as historical and constructed, not natural; held analytically rather than reified. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy together support this position. The end of race is a horizon, not a starting move.
