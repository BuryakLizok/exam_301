# L8. A Question of Experience (2): Race and Social Theory

## 1. Topic Overview

This lecture moves the question of "experience" from gender (L7) to race, showing how sociology has had to confront its own complicity in racial classification while also drawing on Black theorists who treat race as a constitutive feature of modernity rather than a side issue. The argument unfolds in three parts. First, the lecturer traces scientific racism from Linnaeus (1735) and Blumenbach (1775) through nineteenth-century craniometry to twentieth-century eugenics, showing that race had no biological referent before this period. The word's etymology (French "racine," Arabic "ras") simply meant root or beginning. Only when classification was tied to power did "race" produce the pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy that justified slavery and colonialism. Following Foucault, the lecture frames this as power-knowledge: science "observed" what its own categories had already produced.

Second, W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963) is presented as the indispensable Black sociologist of modernity. Born five years after the Emancipation Proclamation, the first Black PhD from Harvard, Du Bois asks in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) what it means that legal emancipation produced no substantive freedom. His answer reorganises the sociology of selfhood. The veil separates Black from white worlds; double consciousness names the "peculiar sensation" of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others"; twoness ("an American, a Negro") refuses the unitary actor presupposed by Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. In *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) Du Bois refines this by insisting that race is not biology but social fact: the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia." This anticipates the racial realism of Critical Race Theory.

Third, the lecture turns to contemporary social theory through Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy. Morrison's *The Origin of Others* (2017) treats race as "an effect of power" and "Othering" as a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal. Americanness, she argues, "remains color for many people." Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) and 'Race Ends Here' (1998) refuse to bracket Africa as marginal to modernity: the slave trade is constitutive of Western modernity, racial terror is "cheerfully complicit" with occidental rationality, and we must rewrite history from the slaves' standpoint rather than merely include Black voices. The lecturer concludes: races do not exist, but the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of race do, and they saturate institutions, media, art and ways of seeing.

The pay-off for SC301 is methodological. The lecture argues that mainstream social theory (Weber, Durkheim, Goffman) built its account of the modern self on a tacit white subject, treating racialised experience as a deviation or a special case. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy reverse this: the Black experience of modernity is the most revealing standpoint because modernity itself was forged through slavery, racial classification and colonial domination. The colour line is, in Du Bois's famous Forethought, "the problem of the Twentieth Century" and, given Windrush, Black Lives Matter and persistent racial inequalities, of the twenty-first.

## 2. Key Concepts & Definitions

- **Scientific racism**: The nineteenth-century project of tying "race" to biology, producing pseudo-scientific differences in intelligence, character, morality and physiognomy. Originates with Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) and Blumenbach's five-race skull typology (1775).
- **Power-knowledge** (Foucault, applied by Gilroy): Classification "produced" the very races it claimed to "observe." Race was "a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).
- **Eugenics**: Early twentieth-century pseudo-science of "improving" racial stock, basis of Nazi racial policy and twentieth-century sterilisation programmes.
- **The colour line**: Du Bois's diagnostic of the twentieth century: "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line" (Du Bois, 1903: 1).
- **The veil**: The social barrier separating Black from white worlds; Black people are "shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4).
- **Second sight**: The compensatory gift of those born "with a veil", a critical perspective on the dominant world they are excluded from yet forced to inhabit.
- **Double consciousness**: "This sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **Twoness**: "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).
- **The talented tenth**: Du Bois's later concept, the educated Black elite whose role is to lead racial uplift (referenced in his broader programme).
- **Racial realism**: Du Bois's view (later taken up by CRT, especially Derrick Bell) that race is not biology but social fact, defined by unequal treatment, Jim Crow, segregation.
- **Othering** (Morrison): The construction of racial outsiders to stabilise the dominant group's self-image; "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29).
- **Whiteness studies**: Analysis of whiteness as a form of unmarked power; Americanness "remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17).
- **The Black Atlantic**: Gilroy's name for the transnational cultural and political space formed by slavery's middle passage; Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Caribbean as one circuit.
- **Epidermalization** (Fanon, via Gilroy): The colonial process of making bodies meaningful by endowing them with "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254).
- **Interest convergence** (Derrick Bell, CRT): Racial progress occurs only when it aligns with white interests.
- **Counter-storytelling** (CRT): Narrative methods that reveal subjugated Black epistemologies erased by dominant historiography.
- **Primal history of modernity** (Gilroy after Benjamin): Modernity rewritten "from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).
- **Tertium quid**: Du Bois's term for the "intermediate category between animal and human" into which Black people were forced (cited in Gilroy, 1993: 55).

## 3. Slide-by-Slide Walkthrough

**Slides 1-3: Defining race and racism.** The lecture opens by noting that the word "race" is similar to French "racine" (root) and possibly Arabic "ras" (head, beginning). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology and seemed to refer to a particular biological group or type or category" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Racism is then defined as the belief that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3).

**Slides 4-5: Provocations.** The lecturer asks: who believes there are different races? How many races are there? What criteria identify them? The everyday question "Where are you really from?" (slide 4) is flagged as the contemporary trace of racial classification.

**Slides 6-7: Linnaeus.** Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) divided Homo Sapiens into four sub-species: Homo Europaeus, Homo Afer (Sub-Saharan Africans), Homo Asiaticus and Homo Americanus (Native Americans). Classification rested on morphology but Linnaeus also claimed differences in "temperament and psychology" (slide 6). The lecturer notes finer subdivisions ("Northern Europeans"/"Southern Europeans").

**Slide 8: Blumenbach.** Johann Friedrich Blumenbach (1752-1840) proposed five races, supposedly evidenced by skull shape: Caucasian (white), Mongolian (yellow), Malayan (brown), Negroid (black) and American (red). Again, supposed psychological differences track morphology.

**Slide 9: Power-knowledge.** "Science claimed to 'observe' racial differences. The classifications they produced appeared to give them knowledge … This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 9). The lecturer ties this to Foucault (L7) and asks whether the question of "colour" runs through race talk because there "needs to be something to be 'observed' in order to classify." This is vision and power.

**Slides 10-11: Du Bois on race as social fact.** "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). In a "fictional" dialogue Du Bois insists race is "a cultural, sometimes a historical fact" and concludes: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153).

**Slide 12: Du Bois biography.** Born Massachusetts 1868, died 1963. Studied in Berlin in the 1890s; first Black PhD from Harvard; polymath; first generation of African Americans after Emancipation (1863).

**Slide 13: Post-Emancipation.** The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared all slaves free; the post-Civil-War amendments made it unconstitutional to deny the vote on the basis of "race" or "color" or prior slave status. Yet "writing a generation after all this, in the 1890s, Du Bois asked why nothing had really changed for African Americans" (slide 13).

**Slide 14: "Being a problem".** Du Bois's opening: "being a problem is a strange experience" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). The visiting cards anecdote: a girl refuses his card and "it dawned on me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others … shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). Gibson's editorial gloss: "one's sense of race and racial terms comes only from experience" (Gibson, 1996: xxv).

**Slide 15: Double consciousness.** "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The veiled "Negro" gets "no true self-consciousness but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

**Slide 16: Twoness vs the unitary actor.** "For Du Bois, being double is the basis of the experience of the world. This differs from the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel" (slide 16). "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). "The history of the American Negro is the history of this strife … this longing to attain self-conscious manhood, to merge his double self into a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

**Slide 17: The waste of double aims.** "This waste of double aims, this seeking to satisfy two unreconciled ideals, has wrought sad havoc with the courage and faith and deeds of ten thousand thousand people" (Du Bois, 1903: 6).

**Slide 18: Substantive freedom.** Despite the Emancipation Proclamation and the 15th Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found freedom in his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 7). "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11).

**Slide 19: Neither Africanise nor bleach.** "he wishes neither of the older selves to be lost. He would not Africanize America, for America has much to teach the world … He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The lecturer underlines the link of modernity and slavery.

**Slide 20: Modernity built race as a labour fix.** Long Du Bois quotation: "In the latter part of the seventeenth and early in the eighteenth centuries, the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4). "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). Source: Du Bois, W.E.B. 1898. 'The Study of the Negro Problems', *Annals of the American Academy*, Jan 1898: 1-23.

**Slide 21: Morrison biography.** Toni Morrison: novelist, writer, thinker. The lecturer flags the 2017 Harvard lectures published as *The Origin of Others* (Harvard University Press).

**Slide 22: Morrison's central question.** "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter? … Race is the classification of a species, and we are the human race, period [full-stop]. Then what is this other thing, the hostility, the social racism, the Othering" (Morrison, 2017: 15). Citing Bruce Baum: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29).

**Slide 23: Americanness as colour.** "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). The lecturer adds: "[Think also about Trump and what it means nowadays to be a 'Mexican-American']."

**Slide 24: Media and Othering.** "routine media presentations deploy images and language that narrow our views of what humans look like (or ought to look like) and what in fact we are like" (Morrison, 2017: 37). Suggested novels: *Beloved*, *Paradise*, *The Bluest Eye*; also Octavia Butler's *Dawn* and Angela Carter's *The Magic Toyshop*.

**Slide 25: Gilroy biography.** Paul Gilroy, currently at UCL, previously LSE, Harvard, Goldsmiths and Essex. Most famous books: *There Ain't No Black in the Union Jack*, *The Black Atlantic: Modernity and Double Consciousness*, *Between Camps* (published in the US as *Against Race*).

**Slide 26: Race as primary object of knowledge and power.** Gilroy is influenced by Foucault but does not fully agree. "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252).

**Slide 27: Africa as constitutive, not peripheral.** Africa "was not on the side-lines of the West and the development of modernity." The transnational, evidenced in the slave trade, was always part of Western modernity; hence the "Black Atlantic." Ethnic/"racial" identities are outcomes of political, economic and cultural processes, not fixed essences. "We need to rethink history and modernity and not just 'include' the marginal positions that have been ignored. But to rewrite history and modernity from these positions" (slide 27).

**Slide 28: Racial terror and reason.** "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Annotated "(Think of Dussel)" by the lecturer. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

**Slide 29: Standpoint, not unmediated experience.** "This unstable standpoint is to be understood in a different way from the clarion calls to … unmediated experience which sometimes appear in association with the term." "In terms of contemporary politics and social theory, the value of this project lies in its promise to uncover both an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood … that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Annotated "(Think of Du Bois)."

**Slide 30: Conclusion.** "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do. This is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist. It is about how the modern social world is constituted on forms of racism which we might not be able to recognise immediately but are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 30).

## 5. Debates & Tensions

- **Biological vs social conceptions of race**: Scientific racism insists race is biologically real; Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all reject this but disagree on what to put in its place. Du Bois retains "race" as a cultural and historical fact; Gilroy presses towards "race ends here" and the abolition of raciology; Morrison occupies a middle ground, naming race as power-effect.
- **Inclusion vs rewriting**: Should we "add" Black voices to existing modernity narratives or rewrite modernity from the standpoint of the enslaved? Gilroy explicitly demands the latter, citing Walter Benjamin's "primal history."
- **Unitary vs split self**: Du Bois's double consciousness explicitly contests the unified actor of Weber, Goffman and Garfinkel. Can sociology accommodate a self that is constitutively riven?
- **Racism as individual prejudice vs structural condition**: Lecture conclusion stresses that this "is not simply about 'racism' and individuals being racist" but how the social world is constituted on unrecognised forms of racism in institutions, art, music and ways of seeing.
- **Pessimism vs progress**: CRT (after Bell) treats the USA as still white-supremacist; the myth of racial progress masks "interest convergence." Du Bois oscillates: Emancipation has not delivered, yet *Souls* ends on the possibility that two world-races may give each to each.
- **Universalism vs particularism**: Gilroy critiques bourgeois humanism for repairing itself by adding the figures Du Bois called "tertium quid"; he wants conceptions of selfhood that are "forever disassociated from the psychological and epistemic correlates of racial subordination" (Gilroy, 1993: 56).
- **Visibility and the body**: Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' asks whether nano-biology and digital imaging make race "an after-image", vestigial. Yet the lecture insists epidermal experience (Fanon) still organises everyday life.
- **Africa as periphery vs Africa as constitutive**: Gilroy explicitly contests the view that Africa was on the side-lines of Western modernity.

## 6. Examples & Applications

- **Jim Crow segregation (1877-1965)** as Du Bois's empirical anchor for racial realism; the Black man is "a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153).
- **Redlining and US housing discrimination**: HOLC maps from the 1930s denying mortgage credit to Black neighbourhoods; structural wealth gap persists.
- **British Empire's "civilising mission"**: rhetoric of cultural uplift justifying colonial extraction; Macaulay's 1835 Minute on Indian Education.
- **Windrush scandal (2018)**: British state's wrongful deportation of Caribbean migrants invited under the 1948 British Nationality Act; Gilroyan reminder that the Black Atlantic produces postcolonial Britain.
- **Black Lives Matter (2013-)**: movement after Trayvon Martin, George Floyd, Eric Garner, Breonna Taylor; counter-storytelling against police killings.
- **Black maternal mortality**: in the UK, Black women are roughly 3-4 times more likely to die in pregnancy than white women (MBRRACE-UK reports), in the USA approximately 3 times more likely (CDC); racial realism in healthcare.
- **UK Stop & Search**: Black people are 7-9 times more likely to be stopped than white people (Home Office); Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry found the Metropolitan Police "institutionally racist."
- **Affirmative action**: *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke* (1978) upheld limited race-conscious admissions; *SFFA v. Harvard* (2023) struck them down; interest convergence reversing.
- **Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972)**: US Public Health Service withholding treatment from Black men; scientific racism's afterlife in research ethics.
- **Minstrelsy and Black cultural appropriation**: nineteenth-century blackface performance; Gilroy's "pas de deux between … Michael Jordan and Bugs Bunny" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) as global commodification of Black culture.
- **Trump on "Mexican-Americans"** (slide 23): Morrison's argument that Americanness is colour-coded directly applies to twenty-first century border rhetoric.
- **Modern slavery**: UK Modern Slavery Act 2015 acknowledges roughly 100,000 victims; carceral capitalism and US prison labour (Thirteenth Amendment exception clause); CRT's "school-to-prison pipeline."

## 9. Past-Q Essay Answers

### Past Q 2024. How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to two theorists.

In this essay I argue that European modernity was not merely accompanied by unequal race relations but constituted by them, and I make this case through W.E.B. Du Bois and Paul Gilroy. I treat the question's word "benefit" seriously: the wealth, philosophy and self-image of European modernity were materially and conceptually subsidised by the racial subordination of African peoples.

I begin with the economic and legal foundation that Du Bois sets out. In 'The Study of the Negro Problems' (1898), Du Bois observes that "in the latter part of the seventeenth and early in the eighteenth centuries, the central and all-absorbing economic need of America was the creation of a proper labor supply to develop American wealth" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4). The supply problem was solved by importing Africans and creating a racial category through statute, not biology: "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). I take this as a foundational point: race emerged as a legal-economic device because slavery was more profitable than indenture, and only afterwards congealed into the supposed natural fact that scientific racism would later "discover." European modernity benefited twice over: from the unpaid labour itself and from the ideological asset of being able to think of itself as the racially enlightened standard. As Du Bois insists in *Dusk of Dawn*, "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). Modernity profited from a fiction it knew to be flimsy.

Gilroy radicalises this insight. In *The Black Atlantic* he argues that we must abandon the comforting story that slavery was a regrettable side-business of Europe's rise. "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination" (Gilroy, 1993: 54); it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" and "has retained a central place in the historical memories of the black Atlantic" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). I read Gilroy as showing that the Atlantic triangle of Liverpool, the Gold Coast and the Caribbean was the laboratory of modern bureaucratic management, modern industrial finance and modern consumption: sugar, cotton, tobacco. Crucially, his polemic against Weberian rationalism cuts to the heart of the benefit question: "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). Modernity did not benefit despite slavery; rationality and slavery rose together.

I then trace the cultural and epistemic dividend. The lecture's account of scientific racism, from Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) to Blumenbach's five-race skulls (1775), shows that European science gained authority and funding precisely by classifying non-European peoples as inferior. This is Gilroy's point: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). Knowledge production was an arm of empire. Concrete examples make the dividend visible. The Lancashire cotton mills depended on the slave-grown cotton of Mississippi and Alabama; the wealth of Bristol, Liverpool and Glasgow was built on the Atlantic trade; the British Museum's collections were filled by colonial extraction. Even the Enlightenment's universalist humanism, the bourgeois humanism Gilroy critiques, relied on excluding the people Du Bois calls a "tertium quid" (cited in Gilroy, 1993: 55), an intermediate category between human and animal.

A counter-argument is that modernity was a generally progressive movement and that slavery was a pre-modern residue, abolished by modernity's own reforming impulse. I reject this. The legal innovations that produced racial slavery, chattel status, hereditary servitude, the "one-drop rule", were not pre-modern relics but modern inventions. Emancipation, when it came, was incomplete: Du Bois observes that "the Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The Windrush scandal, the persistent racial wealth gap, the disproportionate Black maternal mortality and the Macpherson Report's finding of institutional racism in the Metropolitan Police all show that the benefits of unequal race relations continue to accrue.

Finally, I press Gilroy's normative implication. If modernity benefited from slavery, then a sociology that simply "adds" Black voices to a fundamentally white narrative is inadequate. We must, with Gilroy, attempt "the primal history of modernity … reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). This is not merely a matter of reparative recognition but of analytical accuracy: the slaves' standpoint reveals what the masters' standpoint conceals.

I conclude that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in three reinforcing ways: economically through unpaid labour, culturally through the prestige of "scientific" racial classification, and politically through the self-image of a free Europe defined against unfree others. Du Bois and Gilroy together show that race and modernity are inextricably linked and that the dividend has never fully been repaid.

### Past Q 2025/2023. What is meant by 'scientific racism'? How have sociologists tried to counter this concept?

In this essay I define scientific racism as the nineteenth-century project of grounding racial classification in supposedly biological fact, and I show how sociologists from Du Bois through Morrison to Gilroy have countered it by treating race as a social, historical and political construction.

I begin with definition. The lecture is explicit that the word "race" had no biological referent before the nineteenth century. Its etymology is benign: the French "racine" (root) or Arabic "ras" (head, beginning). "It was only in the 19th century that it was incorporated into biology and seemed to refer to a particular biological group or type or category" (Lecture 8 slide 3). Scientific racism is the doctrine that there are "real, fixed, differences in terms of character, intelligence or morals between different 'types' of humans" (slide 3). I trace its origin through two figures. Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) divided Homo sapiens into Europaeus, Afer, Asiaticus and Americanus, claiming not only morphological but also temperamental and psychological differences. Blumenbach (1775) refined this into a five-race typology: Caucasian, Mongolian, Malayan, Negroid, American, supposedly evidenced by skull shape. This is the lineage of nineteenth-century craniometry, of Galton's eugenics, of the Nazi racial measurements photographed in the 1933 image on the lecture slide, and of the Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972).

I then identify what makes the project specifically "scientific." Following Foucault, the lecture frames scientific racism as power-knowledge: "Science claimed to 'observe' racial differences. The classifications they produced appeared to give them knowledge … This power-knowledge 'produced' ways of viewing people which still have effects today" (slide 9). Gilroy makes the same point: "'race' was a primary object of knowledge and power" (Gilroy, 1998: 252), and the "truths of race were produced 'performatively' from the hat that biological science provided, like so many startled rabbits in front of a noisy, eager, imperial crowd" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Scientific racism is not the application of neutral science to racial questions; it is the manufacture of racial reality by classificatory institutions tied to colonial and slaveholding power.

The first sociological counter-move is Du Bois's. In *Dusk of Dawn* he concedes that biology cannot ground race: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136); yet refuses the easy conclusion that race is therefore unreal. Instead he relocates its reality: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact … the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). I read this as the first sociological anti-essentialism about race: the reality of race lies in legal and social practices of unequal treatment, not in skulls or genes. This is the move that Critical Race Theory later names "racial realism": race is real as social fact, fictive as biology.

The second counter-move is Toni Morrison's. In *The Origin of Others* she presses the question: "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter?" (Morrison, 2017: 15). Her answer is twofold. First, citing Bruce Baum, "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). Second, race functions through Othering: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). I take this to identify the affective and identarian motor of scientific racism: the dominant group needs racial others to stabilise its own normality. Whiteness, on this account, is not the absence of race but the unmarked form of racial power.

The third counter-move is Gilroy's. In 'Race Ends Here' (1998) he argues that scientific racism rested on a specific perceptual regime: the "Euclidean anatomy" of comparative racial measurement, which has been outflanked by molecular biology and digital imaging. "Scientific and biological, historical and cultural, rational and irrational, skin, bone and even blood are no longer primary referents of racial discourse" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). Where racial difference vanishes at the resolution of MRI or genome sequencing, "'race' might best be approached as an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). I treat Gilroy's polemic as the sharpest scientific counter: the science that produced scientific racism has now superseded its own object.

There are caveats. Counter-arguing scientific racism in the lab does not abolish racism in the street. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy all stress that racism persists as institutional and cultural fact even after biology repudiates it. The lecture conclusion makes this point sharply: "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do … forms of racism … are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 30). Concrete examples confirm this: the UK Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry found "institutional racism" in policing; UK Stop & Search ratios remain wildly disproportionate; Black maternal mortality in the UK and US is several times the white rate; the Windrush scandal weaponised state bureaucracy against legitimate Black British citizens.

I conclude that scientific racism is the modern project of dressing racial hierarchy in biological clothing, and that sociologists have countered it by relocating race's reality to social structure (Du Bois), to the affective work of othering (Morrison) and to the changing technologies of vision (Gilroy). The counter is unfinished because the legacy of scientific racism is sedimented in institutions and habits of perception that survive the discrediting of its biological premises.

### Past Q 2022. Drawing from at least two of W.E.B Du Bois, Toni Morrison and Paul Gilroy, discuss the implications of "race" for contemporary society.

In this essay I draw on Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy to argue that "race," though biologically empty, has profound implications for contemporary society in three registers: the constitution of the self, the production of national belonging, and the structure of global modernity.

I begin with the implication for selfhood, drawing on Du Bois. In *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) Du Bois introduces double consciousness as the experiential consequence of living within the colour line: "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The self is constitutively split: "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). For contemporary society, the implication is that the unitary actor presupposed by mainstream sociology (Weber's rational actor, Goffman's strategic self, Garfinkel's competent member) is unavailable to racialised subjects. Consider the everyday racialised question of "Where are you really from?" (slide 4); it imposes a doubling that the unmarked white subject does not experience. The Windrush scandal of 2018 dramatises this: Black British citizens who arrived between 1948 and 1971, whose Britishness should have been unquestionable, were re-coded as foreign by a hostile-environment bureaucracy. Du Bois's veil is a UK Home Office form.

I then turn to Morrison for the implication for national belonging. In *The Origin of Others* she observes that "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). National identity is not just a passport but a racialised performance. I take this to illuminate Trump-era and post-Trump US politics, the rhetoric around "Mexican-Americans," DACA, family separations at the southern border, but it cuts equally in the UK, where the Hostile Environment, Prevent and the Rwanda asylum policy all depend on racialised assumptions about who belongs. Morrison adds that race operates through othering: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). Contemporary right-wing populism uses Black, Muslim and migrant bodies precisely to stabilise an anxious white-majority self.

I take the third implication from Gilroy: the structural implication for modernity itself. In *The Black Atlantic* and 'Race Ends Here', Gilroy refuses to treat slavery and colonialism as side-issues. "The order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). The cotton mills of Manchester, the sugar of Bristol, the Royal African Company all built modernity through the Atlantic slave system. The implication for contemporary society is that the racial wealth gap, the geography of redlined US cities, the persistence of Black maternal mortality and the structure of global supply chains all reproduce the asymmetries that the Black Atlantic established. Gilroy's normative claim follows: we cannot fix this by adding Black voices to a fundamentally white modernity; we must reconstruct modernity from the slaves' point of view (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

I consider examples across all three thinkers. Black Lives Matter, after Trayvon Martin (2012), Eric Garner (2014) and George Floyd (2020), exemplifies Du Boisian double consciousness politicised: the demand that the world look at Black death with the eyes Black people have always seen it with. Counter-storytelling, in the CRT vocabulary, is double consciousness turned outward. The Macpherson Report (1999) institutionalised the diagnosis of "institutional racism" in UK policing after the Stephen Lawrence inquiry; UK Stop & Search disparities (Black people stopped 7-9 times more often than white) make Morrison's "effect of power" empirically measurable. The Black Atlantic continues to circulate culturally (grime, drill, dancehall, Afrobeats) but is asymmetrically commodified, as Gilroy notes with his Michael Jordan-Bugs Bunny example (Gilroy, 1998: 252).

I anticipate the objection that race is a declining force in a globalising, multicultural world. I think this is wrong on the evidence. The 2008 financial crisis disproportionately destroyed Black household wealth in the US through subprime targeting; the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic killed Black, Asian and minority ethnic Britons at significantly higher rates than white Britons; the 2023 SFFA v Harvard ruling reversed affirmative action just as racial inequalities widened. As Du Bois warned, equality in law is not enough: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11).

I conclude that race has three deep implications for contemporary society. Through Du Bois, it shapes the very structure of racialised selfhood, producing a doubled, second-sighted experience that mainstream sociology must learn from. Through Morrison, it shapes national belonging by making whiteness the unmarked currency of full citizenship. Through Gilroy, it shapes global modernity by reproducing the asymmetries laid down by the Atlantic slave system. Race, in short, is biologically empty and sociologically full.

### Past Q 2021. Du Bois asked: "How does it feel to be a problem?" Outline and assess his response to this question.

In this essay I outline Du Bois's response to his own question and then assess it on three criteria: descriptive accuracy, conceptual originality, and political adequacy.

Du Bois opens *The Souls of Black Folk* with the question and immediately defers it: "To the real question, How does it feel to be a problem? I answer seldom a word" (Du Bois, 1903: 2). The question is not really a question put to him; it is a polite circumlocution for the white interlocutor's racialised gaze. Du Bois's response unfolds across the rest of the chapter in three moves: an autobiographical scene that names the experience, a conceptual formulation of double consciousness, and a programmatic call for the integration of work, culture and liberty.

The autobiographical scene is the visiting cards incident. As a New England schoolboy, Du Bois recalls a tall newcomer who "refused my card, refused it peremptorily, with a glance. Then it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). I take this to be Du Bois's empirical answer: "being a problem" feels like the sudden, unwilled recognition that one is on the wrong side of an invisible barrier. The metaphor of the veil names this barrier; the metaphor of "second-sight" names the compensatory perceptual gift it bestows: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world, a world which yields him no true self-consciousness, but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 4).

The conceptual formulation is double consciousness: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Crucially, Du Bois insists that this doubling is not a deficit to be repaired by assimilation but a structural feature of African-American life. "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The history of African America is the history of this strife: "this longing to attain self-conscious manhood, to merge his double self into a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The aim is not to abandon either pole: "He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

The programmatic move is the demand for substantive rather than formal equality. Despite the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the Fifteenth Amendment, "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Du Bois ends with the famous demand: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together, not successively but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). The implication is that being a problem feels like being formally free but substantively unfree, formally a citizen but substantively excluded.

I now assess. On descriptive accuracy, Du Bois's response is remarkable. The visiting cards scene captures the everyday, often petty, mechanism by which racial difference is communicated, not by lynching, not by Jim Crow law, but by a glance and a refused card. The persistence of microaggressions in contemporary qualitative research on Black students in PWIs (predominantly white institutions) confirms this. The "Where are you really from?" question (lecture slide 4) is the contemporary equivalent of the refused card.

On conceptual originality, double consciousness is one of the most important contributions of American social theory. The lecture is explicit that it differs from "the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel." Du Bois anticipates Mead's social self, Lacan's mirror stage, Fanon's epidermalisation, and Goffman's interaction order; but unlike them he ties the doubling to race and power rather than treating it as a universal feature of selfhood. This specificity is the originality.

On political adequacy, I find Du Bois's response more ambivalent. The closing demand for the merger of African and American selves can sound assimilationist; he insists he "would not Africanize America" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Later thinkers, especially in Black Power and Afrocentric traditions, would press much harder against the American pole. Gilroy raises a different concern: the demand to merge the two selves may understate the irreducibility of the rupture that slavery represents. Gilroy's "primal history of modernity … reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55) implies that some doublings cannot and should not be merged.

There are limitations to acknowledge. Du Bois's frame is gendered ("self-conscious manhood," "two warring ideals in one dark body"), which obscures the specifically gendered racial experience that later thinkers (bell hooks, Crenshaw, 1989) would name. His class politics (the talented tenth) has been criticised as elitist. His American focus underplays the transnational frame that Gilroy's Black Atlantic recovers. Yet the analytical core, that racial subordination produces a doubled, partial, mediated selfhood, survives these critiques.

I conclude that Du Bois's response to "How does it feel to be a problem?" is descriptively acute (the veil and the refused card), conceptually transformative (double consciousness and second sight), and politically incomplete but generative (the demand for substantive freedom). It feels, he tells us, like always seeing yourself through someone else's eyes; the response is to fight for a world in which that doubling becomes optional rather than imposed.

## 10. Mock-Q Essay Answers

### Mock 1. "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro." Discuss this passage from Du Bois.

In this essay I discuss Du Bois's famous twoness passage, "One ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), and argue that it should be read neither as a complaint about identity confusion nor as a programme for assimilation, but as a sociological diagnosis of what the colour line does to selfhood under conditions of formal equality.

I begin with the immediate context. The passage closes Du Bois's opening chapter 'Of Our Spiritual Strivings', which has already laid down the question "How does it feel to be a problem?" (Du Bois, 1903: 2), the autobiographical scene of the refused visiting card and the veil ("shut out from their world by a vast veil," Du Bois, 1903: 4), the gift of second sight ("born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world," Du Bois, 1903: 4), and the core formulation of double consciousness ("this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity," Du Bois, 1903: 5). The twoness sentence is the experiential apex of this chain. It tells us what double consciousness produces in the racialised self.

I read four claims in the passage. First, the doubling is involuntary ("One ever feels"); it is not a chosen identity politics but an imposed perceptual condition. Second, the two poles are non-negotiable: not "a Negro or an American" but "an American, a Negro." Each is constitutive. Third, the conflict is structural, not psychological: "two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals." Fourth, the body is the site of the strain: "in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder." Du Bois locates racial experience not in the soul abstracted from physiology but in the dark body that bears it.

I then situate the passage against the alternatives Du Bois rejects. The first alternative is racial separation, "Africanise America." Du Bois explicitly refuses it: "He would not Africanize America, for America has too much to teach the world and Africa" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The second is racial assimilation, "bleach his Negro soul." He refuses that too: "He would not bleach his Negro blood in a flood of white Americanism" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The third is to abandon the question, to "let the Negro nation go" as a separate project. The twoness passage is the only available alternative: hold the two strivings together, not by reconciling them but by refusing to surrender either.

I take this to be Du Bois's central contribution to social theory of the self. Mainstream classical sociology (Weber's rational actor, Durkheim's social fact, Mead's I-and-me) assumed a unitary, integrated subject. Du Bois insists that the racialised subject in a white-majority polity cannot be unitary because the polity itself is double: nominally inclusive, structurally exclusive. The lecture is explicit that this contrasts with "the sense of unity, of being a single actor, which is presumed by Weber, Goffman, Garfinkel." Twoness is the experiential consequence of the colour line as a social fact.

I consider the contemporary purchase of the passage. The Windrush scandal of 2018 dramatised twoness for Black British citizens whose British identity, formally guaranteed by the 1948 British Nationality Act, was bureaucratically suspended by the hostile-environment regime; they were British and not-British simultaneously. The everyday racialised question "Where are you really from?" performs the same operation more politely. Black Lives Matter mobilisations after the killings of Eric Garner (2014) and George Floyd (2020) make twoness visible globally: Black observers see what white observers must be shown on video, the differential perception that second sight already named.

I then take up objections. The first is that twoness over-essentialises a binary that fragments into many ("American" itself is plural; "Negro" is no longer the right noun; intersectional identities multiply the strivings beyond two). I think Crenshaw's intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) should be read as extending rather than refuting twoness; the Black woman experiences more than two strivings, but the structural logic Du Bois names (warring ideals produced by a society's racial settlement) still applies. The second objection is that twoness pathologises racialised subjectivity. Du Bois's coupling of double consciousness with second sight is the answer: the doubled subject sees what the unitary subject cannot. Standpoint epistemology and CRT's counter-storytelling formalise this perceptual advantage.

I press a Gilroyan extension. In *The Black Atlantic* Gilroy argues that the slaves' standpoint demands "an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood and individuation that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). The twoness passage is one such conception in embryo: it refuses the masters' unitary subject without conceding the slaves' isolation. Gilroy's diasporic frame globalises Du Bois's American twoness; the Black Atlantic is composed of doubled selves who are also tripled (Caribbean, British, African) and quadrupled (gender, class, religion).

I conclude that the twoness passage is Du Bois's most concentrated diagnosis of the racialised self under formal equality. It names an imposed doubling, refuses the false choices of assimilation and separation, and grounds Black critical perception in the body that endures the strain. It anticipates intersectionality, standpoint epistemology, racial realism and CRT. The strain it names persists wherever the colour line organises citizenship, which is to say, in the twenty-first century United States and United Kingdom alike.

### Mock 2. Critically assess Gilroy's argument in *The Black Atlantic* in light of contemporary debates about reparations.

In this essay I critically assess Gilroy's argument in *The Black Atlantic* (1993) in light of contemporary debates about reparations (the British 1833 compensation paid to slave-owners, the Caribbean CARICOM reparations claims of the 2010s-2020s, US H.R. 40, and the University College London and University of Glasgow reparative justice programmes) and argue that Gilroy's frame both grounds and complicates the reparations argument.

I begin with the Gilroy thesis. *The Black Atlantic* contends that "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination"; it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, 1993: 54). Its most polemical line is that "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). The normative pay-off is that "the primal history of modernity [must] be reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). Modernity was a Black Atlantic system, and the wealth of European cities (Bristol, Liverpool, Glasgow, Manchester) was made in and through it.

I then frame the reparations debate. Reparations claims rest on three premises: that slavery and the slave trade produced specific, traceable wealth transfers; that these transfers underwrite ongoing racialised disparities (wealth, health, education, incarceration); and that descendants of the enslaved and the formerly colonised have legitimate claims on institutions and states that benefited. Gilroy's argument supports all three. His insistence that the slave system was the laboratory of modern industrial finance and modern consumption (sugar, cotton, tobacco, insurance, banking) gives reparations the right scale. The wealth in question is not marginal but constitutive.

I assemble concrete cases. The Slavery Abolition Act 1833 compensated British slave-owners £20 million, about 5% of British GDP, a debt the Treasury only finished servicing in 2015. The 1833 compensation went to the masters, not to the freed. UCL's research on the database of British slave-owners (Catherine Hall et al., *Legacies of British Slave-ownership*) has shown how British capital and institutional wealth absorbed these transfers. The CARICOM reparations commission (founded 2013) presented a Ten Point Plan to European powers seeking institutional reparations. The University of Glasgow's £20 million reparative justice programme (2019) and UCL's reparations scholarship programme (2019-) acknowledge institutional inheritance. In the US, H.R. 40 (a bill to study reparations) has been reintroduced repeatedly without passing; Georgetown University and several private institutions have begun reparative programmes for descendants of enslaved people they once owned or sold.

Gilroy's frame helps the reparations argument in three ways. First, it refuses the "few bad apples" account of slavery. If modernity is Black Atlantic, reparations are not a marginal correction but a structural debt. Second, it refuses the "long ago and far away" temporal objection. The "central place in the historical memories of the black Atlantic" (Gilroy, 1993: 55) is also the central place in present-day institutional structures: UCL's Galton legacy, Lloyd's insurance underwriting of slave ships, the cotton-mill wealth of Manchester. Third, it refuses the inclusion-without-redistribution gesture. Adding Black voices to existing institutions without rewriting the institutions' economic settlement is, in Gilroy's terms, repairing bourgeois humanism without disturbing it. Reparations are the material form of "the primal history of modernity … reconstructed from the slaves' points of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55).

But Gilroy's frame also complicates reparations. Three tensions. First, the diasporic frame troubles the national container in which most reparations claims operate. To whom is the debt owed if the Black Atlantic is one circuit? CARICOM addresses the question by aggregating Caribbean states; H.R. 40 addresses it through US-descendant tracing; UCL and Glasgow address it through institutional rather than individual repair. Gilroy would press all three to think transnationally; Caribbean reparations should engage US wealth as well as UK wealth, since the slave-trade network spanned both.

Second, Gilroy is wary of identity politics that grounds claims in racial essentialism. He criticises "the absolute sovereignty of unmediated experience" (Gilroy, 1993: 56) and "lazy essentialisms" of identity politics in 'Race Ends Here' (Gilroy, 1998: 252). Reparations arguments that rely on biologically defined racial descent could trigger his critique. The reply, I think, is that descent here is not biology but historical inheritance; being a descendant of the enslaved is a position in a historical relation, not a phenotypic fact.

Third, Gilroy's hope in 'Race Ends Here' that we might "silence raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) sits in some tension with reparations programmes that necessarily classify by race. To compensate descendants of the enslaved is to use racial categories administratively. Gilroy would want this to be transitional, not permanent; a calculus that ends with the debt rather than entrenches racial sorting.

I consider counter-arguments to reparations. The first is that the wealth transfer is too diffuse to trace; the second that reparative payments cannot heal the original wrong; the third that contemporary white populations do not owe debts incurred by ancestors. Gilroy answers the first: the Black Atlantic is precisely the traceable network. He answers the second by acknowledging that material repair is necessary but insufficient; the cultural and epistemic rewriting of modernity is the longer project. The third he answers by relocating the debt from individual ancestors to institutional inheritance. UCL inherits Galton's wealth; the Treasury inherited the 1833 compensation; the City of London absorbed slave-trade insurance profits.

Contemporary examples reinforce the argument. The 2020 toppling of Edward Colston's statue in Bristol, the post-Windrush 2018 compensation scheme (deemed inadequate by the National Audit Office), the British Museum's Benin Bronzes debates, and the post-2020 BLM call for institutional accountability are all reparative moments in Gilroy's expanded sense. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate, MBRRACE-UK) and the racial wealth gap are the present-day measurements of an unpaid debt.

I conclude that Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* both grounds and complicates reparations. It grounds them by showing that modernity's wealth is the Black Atlantic's wealth and that the inheritance is institutional, not just individual. It complicates them by requiring transnational accounting, by warning against racial-essentialist framings, and by holding open the longer project of rewriting modernity itself. Reparations are necessary but not sufficient. The full primal history is, as Gilroy wrote, still waiting to be reconstructed.

### Mock 3. What does Du Bois mean by the "veil", and why does he treat it as constitutive of African American experience?

In this essay I answer the two parts of the question in sequence. First, I reconstruct what Du Bois means by the veil. Second, I show why he treats it as constitutive, not contingent or remediable by individual effort, of African American experience.

I begin with definition. The veil enters *The Souls of Black Folk* in the autobiographical scene of the refused visiting card. Du Bois recalls the moment as a New England schoolboy when "it dawned upon me with a certain suddenness that I was different from the others; or like, mayhap, in heart and life and longing, but shut out from their world by a vast veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). The veil is, in the first instance, a metaphor for social exclusion that operates without overt segregation: no wall, no fence, no law required, just the refused card, the averted glance, the failure of recognition. The veil is invisible from the white side and palpable from the Black.

I then add three properties Du Bois gives the veil. First, it is perceptual: it organises how the two sides see each other. Black people "see [themselves] only through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 4); white people see Black people through the categories the veil makes available (curiosity, contempt, pity). Second, the veil bestows second sight: "the Negro is a sort of seventh son, born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world" (Du Bois, 1903: 4). To live behind the veil is to know one's own world and to perceive, through partial transparency, the world that excludes one, an asymmetric epistemic position. Third, the veil generates double consciousness, the specific psychological consequence: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5).

I now turn to the second part of the question: why constitutive. I read three reasons in Du Bois.

The first reason is historical-structural. The veil is the lingering presence of slavery and Jim Crow in the post-Emancipation US. Du Bois insists that "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Formal Emancipation (1863), the Fifteenth Amendment, even the antilynching agitation, did not dismantle the veil. The veil is the social architecture left behind when the legal structure of slavery is removed but the racial categories it produced are not. Later, in *Dusk of Dawn*, Du Bois makes this explicit: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact … the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The veil is what makes Jim Crow durable.

The second reason is sociological-relational. The veil is constitutive because the racialised self is constituted in relation to the white world's perception. There is no Black selfhood "behind" the veil that is independent of it; the very experience of being on the wrong side of the veil is what produces the doubled selfhood Du Bois names. This is why the question "How does it feel to be a problem?" (Du Bois, 1903: 2) is constitutive, not occasional. To be a problem is to be defined by the white world's framing of one's presence as a question to be answered.

The third reason is phenomenological. The veil names a perceptual condition that organises everyday life: every encounter, every glance, every conversation. Du Bois's literary method in *Souls* (autobiographical scene, sociological generalisation, historical reflection, lyric meditation) communicates the texture of veiled experience. The Sorrow Songs chapter that closes *Souls* lets veiled experience speak through Black musical tradition. The veil is not an occasional irritation; it is the medium of perception.

I then make Du Bois's case against the alternatives that would treat the veil as non-constitutive. The first alternative is individualist: "any Black person can transcend the veil by talent, education or virtue." Du Bois personally exemplifies how much this fails; the first Black PhD from Harvard, the polymath sociologist, the international intellectual, still describes the visiting cards moment as definitive. The second alternative is assimilationist: "the veil will dissolve as Black Americans become more like white Americans." Du Bois's bitter refusal, "He would not bleach his Negro blood" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), names this as both undesirable and impossible while the veil persists. The third alternative is separatist: "Black Americans can step out from behind the veil by leaving white America." Du Bois rejects this too, "He would not Africanize America" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), and his commitment to the merger of the two souls implies that retreat is no answer. The veil is constitutive precisely because it cannot be exited by individual, assimilationist or separatist means.

I bring the argument into the present. The Windrush scandal of 2018 is the veil in late-modern Britain; Black British citizens, formally British by birth or by the 1948 British Nationality Act, were re-coded as foreign by Home Office bureaucracy. The "Where are you really from?" question is the contemporary refused card. Black Lives Matter mobilisations after Eric Garner, Michael Brown and George Floyd globalise the veil's perception: the world that watched the video saw something Black observers had always seen. UK Stop & Search ratios (Black people 7-9 times more likely to be stopped) measure the veil quantitatively. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate) measures it in healthcare.

I anticipate Gilroy's worry that the veil's constitutive character might encase Black experience in fixity. He treats it as constitutive because it is historical-structural (the residue of slavery and Jim Crow), sociological-relational (Black selfhood is made in its presence) and phenomenological (it organises everyday perception). ### Mock 4. Compare Du Bois's double consciousness with Fanon's "white masks".

In this essay I compare Du Bois's double consciousness (1903) with Fanon's "white masks" (1952) as the two foundational concepts of the experiential analytic of race. I argue they share a structural diagnosis (the racialised subject sees themselves through the dominant world's eyes) but differ in their colonial frame, their bodily emphasis and their political consequence.

I begin with Du Bois. Double consciousness, set out in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), names "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The accompanying triad is the veil ("shut out from their world by a vast veil," Du Bois, 1903: 4), second sight ("born with a veil, and gifted with second-sight in this American world," Du Bois, 1903: 4) and twoness ("an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings," Du Bois, 1903: 5). The frame is the post-Emancipation United States; the social architecture is Jim Crow.

I then turn to Fanon. *Black Skin, White Masks* (*Peau noire, masques blancs*, Fanon, 1952), written by a French-trained Martinican psychiatrist working in colonial Algeria, develops the concept of epidermalisation: the colonial process of making the Black body meaningful by depositing "qualities of 'colour'" on its surface. Gilroy summarises Fanon as showing "how this process operates: the imposition of these meanings has been mediated 'epidermally'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). The "white mask" is what the Black colonial subject puts on to be intelligible in the colonial order (French language, French education, white intellectual habits) over a body that the colonial gaze refuses to admit as fully human. Fanon's famous Paris train scene, "Look, a Negro!", is the colonial counterpart of Du Bois's refused visiting card. The frame is the Francophone colonial world; the social architecture is colonial racism and post-slavery French métropole life.

The structural diagnosis is shared. Both writers say: the racialised subject is not free to inhabit a unitary self because the dominant world's perception constitutes the subject as racial. Du Bois's "the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) is Fanon's colonial gaze; Du Bois's veil is Fanon's epidermal screen; Du Bois's second sight is Fanon's critical phenomenology of being looked at. Gilroy explicitly draws the link by treating Fanon's epidermalisation as the colonial update of Du Boisian double consciousness.

I then trace three substantive differences.

First, the frame. Du Bois writes from the post-slavery United States; Fanon from the late colonial Francophone world. The "American, Negro" twoness is national-internal; both poles are claims on the same polity. The Fanonian white mask is a colonial relation; the Black colonial subject is asked to be like the metropole while being kept at a distance from it. Du Bois's question is what citizenship means under formal equality; Fanon's is what subjectivity means under colonial subjection. The two frames map closely onto Gilroy's Black Atlantic, which integrates the US and the Caribbean-Francophone-French strands into one circuit.

Second, the bodily emphasis. Du Bois names "two warring ideals in one dark body, whose dogged strength alone keeps it from being torn asunder" (Du Bois, 1903: 5); the body is the site of the strain but not the explicit object of analysis. Fanon foregrounds the body. *Black Skin, White Masks* anatomises the colonial making of the Black body: the smell, the size, the sexual fantasy, the medical pathologisation. Epidermalisation is precisely the colonial process by which "the human sensorium has had to be educated" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) to read race off the body. The white mask is not just a posture but a phenomenological injury: a body forced into a meaning it did not choose.

Third, the political consequence. Du Bois ends *Souls* with a programmatic hope of merging the two souls into "a better and truer self" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) while demanding substantive equality: "Work, culture, liberty, all these we need, not singly but together" (Du Bois, 1903: 11). Fanon's political trajectory radicalises into anti-colonial revolution. *The Wretched of the Earth* (Fanon, 1961) advocates that decolonisation requires violence, not because violence is intrinsically good but because the colonial settlement was instituted by it. The two thinkers diverge on the politics that follows the diagnosis: Du Bois's later turn to communism and emigration to Ghana approaches Fanonian radicalism but the *Souls* programme remains within US reform.

I consider how Gilroy synthesises the two. *The Black Atlantic* draws on both, treating diasporic Black thought as an Atlantic conversation that includes Du Bois (Massachusetts to Berlin to Ghana) and Fanon (Martinique to Lyon to Algiers). Gilroy's "epidermalisation" terminology comes from Fanon; his standpoint epistemology from Du Bois. The two are stages of one experiential analytic, modulated by different historical conditions.

I provide examples that show the analytic still operates. The Windrush scandal of 2018 instantiates double consciousness: Black Britons formally British but bureaucratically suspended. UK Stop & Search ratios (Black people 7-9 times more likely to be stopped) instantiate epidermalisation: the body itself is the trigger. Joy Buolamwini's "Gender Shades" research on facial-recognition error rates for darker-skinned faces operationalises both concepts at once: the algorithmic system reads race off the body (Fanon) and Black users see themselves through a system that misrecognises them (Du Bois). The medical underestimation of Black patients' pain (descended from nineteenth-century scientific racism about Black "skin thickness") is epidermalisation in healthcare. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate, MBRRACE-UK) is both.

I anticipate the objection that the two concepts have been over-applied. Any difficult identity gets labelled "double consciousness"; any embodied minority experience gets labelled Fanonian. I think the safeguard is historical specificity. Du Bois's concept tracks the particular doubling of post-Emancipation US racialised citizens; Fanon's tracks the particular phenomenology of the colonial Black subject. Used carefully, they remain analytically distinct: double consciousness is about the perceived doubling of the self; white masks is about the imposed performance over a misrecognised body.

I evaluate the comparison politically. Both concepts ground anti-racist politics in the perceptual privilege of those subordinated. CRT's counter-storytelling, Fred Moten's fugitivity, Saidiya Hartman's "wayward lives," and contemporary BLM organising all rely on the analytic Du Bois and Fanon inaugurate. The political pay-off of both concepts is that they refuse the white liberal claim to neutral perception. The world is seen better from below.

I conclude that Du Bois's double consciousness and Fanon's white masks are siblings of one analytic. They share the structural diagnosis of racialised doubling, differ in their national versus colonial frame, in their psychological versus phenomenological-bodily emphasis, and in their reformist versus revolutionary political consequence. Together they constitute the Black Atlantic's contribution to the sociology of the self. Used with care, they remain among the most powerful concepts available for analysing racialised experience in the twenty-first century.

In this essay I argue that Critical Race Theory (CRT), especially Derrick Bell's interest convergence thesis, Cheryl Harris's whiteness as property, and the broader CRT account of racial realism, supplies the most adequate sociological framework for understanding the 2023 *Students for Fair Admissions v. With CRT, it is precisely what Bell predicted.

I begin with the case. In June 2023 the US Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in *SFFA v. Harvard* (and the companion case against the University of North Carolina) that race-conscious admissions violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The decision reversed the limited race-conscious admissions framework established in *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke* (1978) and reaffirmed in *Grutter v. Bollinger* (2003). The plaintiffs were a group founded by Edward Blum, a conservative legal activist who had previously brought *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), which gutted the Voting Rights Act. The court's majority opinion appealed to colour-blind constitutionalism: admissions should not consider race.

I then frame CRT's analytical resources. CRT emerged in US legal scholarship in the late 1970s and 1980s (Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, Cheryl Harris, Richard Delgado, Patricia Williams). Its founding theses are: race is socially constructed but materially real (racial realism); racism is institutional and ordinary, not aberrational; legal-liberal "colour-blindness" tends to entrench rather than dismantle racial hierarchy; racial progress occurs through interest convergence (Bell); whiteness has been historically configured as a form of property (Harris); and the subordinated have epistemic resources, counter-storytelling, that majoritarian narratives erase. CRT inherits Du Bois's racial realism: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136); and his diagnosis that "Equality in law is not enough."

I apply CRT to SFFA in four ways.

First, interest convergence (Bell, 1980). Bell argued that *Brown v. Board* (1954) succeeded not because of moral conviction alone but because desegregation served US Cold War interests and Northern industrial interests. Affirmative action survived from *Bakke* (1978) through *Grutter* (2003) for analogous reasons: corporate America had concluded that diverse workforces were strategically beneficial; the military had endorsed diversity in its officer corps. The interest convergence faded over the 2010s. Post-2008 economic anxiety, the racial backlash that elected Trump in 2016, the rise of conservative legal infrastructure (the Federalist Society's judicial pipeline), and the demographic shifts that made white majority status feel precarious to many voters dissolved the convergence. CRT predicts exactly the reversal SFFA delivered.

Second, whiteness as property (Cheryl Harris, *Harvard Law Review*, 1993). Harris argued that whiteness has historically functioned as property, a status with rights of use, enjoyment and disposition that the legal system recognises and protects. Affirmative action was, in Harris's terms, a partial and provisional reduction of whiteness's exclusive value in admissions. SFFA restores it. The colour-blind framing of the majority opinion preserves the property-value of whiteness by treating any race-conscious remedy as a violation of equal protection, a move that, as Harris predicted, makes the historical injury invisible and the remedy unconstitutional.

Third, racial realism (Bell). Bell's *Faces at the Bottom of the Well* (Bell, 1992) argues that racism is a permanent feature of American life and that integration is a temporary, contingent, externally-induced state. The SFFA reversal is exactly what racial realism expects: the gains of the civil rights era are not progressive accumulations but provisional concessions that the dominant group reverses when its interests change. The fact that the reversal occurred just as the racial wealth gap widened, post-2020 racial backlash strengthened, and conservative state legislatures passed anti-CRT laws (Florida's Stop WOKE Act, 2022) confirms the structural reading.

Fourth, counter-storytelling. CRT methodology privileges the narratives of those subordinated by majoritarian frameworks. SFFA's majority opinion told a story in which race-conscious admissions stigmatise the very groups they purport to help, in which Asian American applicants are disadvantaged by Harvard's preferences. CRT counter-storytelling notes that this narrative obscures the wealth-and-legacy advantages of white applicants (legacy admissions, athletic recruiting, donor preferences) and the documented effects of removing affirmative action in California after Proposition 209 (1996); Black and Latino enrolment at UC Berkeley and UCLA dropped sharply. The CRT counter-narrative is empirically grounded; the majority's story is doctrinally tidy.

I consider examples to extend the argument. The post-2020 racial backlash has been institutional: Florida's 2022 Stop WOKE Act banning teaching that creates "guilt" on the basis of race; multiple US states banning "CRT" in schools (often defining the term loosely to capture any frank racial-historical instruction); the 2023 reversal in *Allen v. Milligan* trends; the Sewell Report (2021) in the UK, which denied institutional racism. Each is a moment of interest divergence; the dominant group's perceived interest no longer aligns with redistribution. Morrison's analysis applies: each move is, in part, a "desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29) by denying the structural facts that name whiteness.

I anticipate objections. The first is that SFFA is a defensible colour-blind reading of equal protection. The CRT reply, citing Justice Sonia Sotomayor's dissent, is that colour-blindness in a racially structured society protects the racial structure. The second objection is that CRT over-predicts: the post-2008 reversal might have been a regulatory swing rather than structural. The CRT reply is that the swing tracks the documented dynamics of interest convergence and racial backlash; it is not coincidence. The third objection is that affirmative action was already constrained (no quotas, narrow tailoring, etc.) and SFFA merely tidied the doctrine. The CRT reply is that doctrinal tidying has been the historical instrument of whiteness as property; each constraint reduced the redistributive force of the remedy.

I close by drawing on Du Bois. Du Bois warned that "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The SFFA reversal confirms that warning. Formal equality is not substantive equality; legal victories are reversible; racial progress requires permanent vigilance, not assumed accumulation. CRT, as Du Bois's heir, gives us the conceptual tools to name what happened in June 2023: not an anomaly but a predictable instance of how racialised institutions reproduce themselves.

I conclude that CRT explains the SFFA reversal through interest convergence (the white interest in racial inclusion has frayed), whiteness as property (admissions value is restored), racial realism (gains are reversible) and counter-storytelling (the majority's narrative obscures distributive facts). Without CRT, the reversal looks like an isolated doctrinal correction; with CRT, it looks like the operation of the very racial order Du Bois first diagnosed.

### Mock 6. Is whiteness a racial identity or a structural position? Discuss.

In this essay I argue that whiteness is best understood as a structural position that, in racialised societies, also becomes available as a racial identity, and that the two registers are connected, with the structural position generating the identity rather than the other way round. I make the case through Morrison, Du Bois and Gilroy and consider the political implications.

I begin with the dichotomy the question poses. To call whiteness a racial identity is to treat it like any other racial identity: a self-ascription, a felt belonging, a culture, a community. To call whiteness a structural position is to treat it as the unmarked position in a racial hierarchy that confers material and symbolic advantages without requiring any subjective identification. The two are not mutually exclusive; most racial categories operate as both; but they generate different politics.

I think whiteness is primarily structural. Three reasons.

First, historically, whiteness has been produced by the operations of racial classification, not by a pre-existing community claiming the name. Morrison observes that "immigrants to the United States understood that if they wanted to become 'real' Americans they must sever or at least downplay their ties to their native country, in order to embrace their whiteness. The definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17). Irish, Italian, Jewish and Eastern European immigrants were not fully white when they arrived in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century; they became white through political and labour processes (Noel Ignatiev's *How the Irish Became White*, 1995; David Roediger's *The Wages of Whiteness*, 1991). Whiteness was achieved, not inherited.

Second, sociologically, whiteness operates as unmarked-ness, the default racial position against which other positions are read as racially specific. Du Bois named the dynamic when he described the racialised self as seeing itself "through the revelation of the other world" (Du Bois, 1903: 4), the "other world" being the white world whose racial position is itself not visible to itself. Morrison's account of othering, "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29), depends on whiteness functioning as the normal that the othering confirms. Gilroy's analysis of "the order of active differentiation that gets called 'race'" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) treats whiteness as the structural anchor of the differentiation.

Third, materially, the advantages whiteness confers operate regardless of whether a given white individual identifies with the category. Federal Reserve data show white US households hold roughly ten times the wealth of Black households. UK Stop & Search disparities operate by phenotype, not self-identification; Black people are 7-9 times more likely to be stopped (Home Office). Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate, MBRRACE-UK) tracks differential clinical treatment, not patient identification. Whiteness as structural position generates these disparities; whiteness as identity is downstream.

I now consider the identity register. Whiteness does function as identity in some contexts. The Trump-era "white grievance" politics, the rise of explicit white-nationalist movements (Charlottesville 2017, the British Defence League, Alternative für Deutschland), and Robin DiAngelo's documentation of white "fragility" in *White Fragility* (2018) all show whiteness mobilised as positive identity. Christopher Bail's data on the Tea Party movement, Arlie Hochschild's *Strangers in Their Own Land* (2016), and Ashley Jardina's *White Identity Politics* (2019) all document that a sizeable proportion of white Americans now identify consciously with their whiteness. Brexit campaigning, the "small boats" rhetoric in 2022-2024 UK politics, and the Sewell Report's defence of British institutions against "institutional racism" framings all mobilise an identity register too. Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction* (1935) anticipated this with the "psychological wage" of whiteness: the symbolic compensation that holds poor white workers to racial hierarchy over class solidarity.

I take the relation between the two registers to be this: the structural position is primary and produces the identity. When whiteness is unchallenged, it operates as the unmarked default and does not need to identify itself; when whiteness is challenged (by Black civil rights, by anti-racist scholarship, by demographic change), it becomes self-conscious and the identity register thickens. The post-2020 anti-CRT panic in the US is exactly this transition: institutional whiteness is named by CRT, and white identity organises to defend the structural position against the naming. Morrison's diagnosis fits: "rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). The defence of whiteness as identity is the affective ground of the defence of whiteness as structure.

I consider three test cases.

First, the Royal Family's 2021 conversation about Meghan Markle's unborn child's skin colour. The reported palace anxiety about "how dark" the child might be is whiteness as identity (the institution sees itself as white) protecting whiteness as structure (the inheritance of British monarchic capital).

Second, the 2018 Windrush scandal. The Home Office's bureaucratic categorisation of long-term Black British residents as "illegal" was whiteness as structure operating without explicit identity claims; officials did not need to feel racially white to enforce racially differential policy.

Third, the "Karen" meme. The widely circulated cultural figure of the white woman who weaponises police against Black people is whiteness as identity (the figure performs white entitlement) and whiteness as structure (the police she summons respond to her racial signal).

I consider whether whiteness as identity can be politically productive. Some anti-racist scholarship (Frankenberg, DiAngelo) treats white self-reflection as necessary to dismantling whiteness: "white people must do white work." Others (Gilroy in *Against Race*, 2000) worry that doubling down on racial identity, even in a deconstructive mode, may entrench the very categories anti-racism should dismantle. I think both have a point: white self-reflection is useful only insofar as it acts on the structural position; it becomes counter-productive when it stops at affective recognition.

I draw on Du Bois's normative horizon. *Souls* ends not with the celebration of racial identity but with the demand that "He simply wishes to make it possible for a man to be both a Negro and an American" (Du Bois, 1903: 5); for racial categories to lose their forced character. Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). The end-state both writers imagine is one where neither whiteness nor any other racial category is the structural position.

I conclude that whiteness is, in racialised societies, both a racial identity and a structural position, with the structural position primary. The disparities (wealth, health, education, incarceration, policing) operate at the structural level. The identity register thickens when the structural position is challenged. Anti-racist politics that targets only the identity register without redistributing the structural position will fail. Anti-racist politics that redistributes the structural position will, in time, dissolve the identity register because whiteness as identity is parasitic on whiteness as structure.

In this essay I take the Windrush scandal and the hostile-environment immigration regime as a case study in the persistence of racial categories, arguing through Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy that the affair shows how the British state continues to produce racial difference administratively long after biological scientific racism has been formally repudiated.

Black British citizens were forced to see themselves through the regime's racialised gaze, which treated their dark skin and Caribbean origin as evidence of suspect status, regardless of decades of British residence. Morrison's observation that "the definition of 'Americanness' (sadly) remains color for many people" (Morrison, 2017: 17) translates directly: the definition of Britishness, in the operating logic of the hostile environment, also remained colour. The hostile environment is the British state retroactively trying to disavow the Black Atlantic settlement.

Du Bois's veil names what Windrush-generation citizens experienced; Morrison's othering names what the regime was doing to confirm an implicit white British self; Gilroy's Black Atlantic names the historical context that made Windrush possible and necessary. ### Mock 8. Does the genomics revolution vindicate or refute scientific racism?

In this essay I argue that the genomics revolution refutes scientific racism as a doctrine while creating new conditions under which racial categories can be reified by molecular techniques. The genome's verdict, taken honestly, is that "race" has no biological reality; but the social and institutional uses of genomic data are increasingly performing the work scientific racism used to do. I make the case through Gilroy, the lecture's account of scientific racism, and concrete examples.

I begin with the genomic refutation. The Human Genome Project (completed 2003) and subsequent population-genomic research have produced near-consensus on three points. First, human genetic variation is mostly within rather than between populations; typically 85-90% of variation is found within populations conventionally labelled "races," with only 10-15% between them (Lewontin's 1972 paper, refined by Rosenberg et al. 2002 and subsequent work). Second, the boundaries of any cluster correspond poorly to lay racial categories: clusters identified by genomic clustering software map onto continental ancestry only at very coarse resolution and dissolve at finer ones. Third, the social racial categories used by US and UK institutions (white, Black, Asian, Hispanic) do not pick out genetically homogeneous groups; they cross multiple ancestry clusters and miss most of the population they purport to describe.

This is the empirical undoing of scientific racism. Linnaeus's four races, Blumenbach's five, the nineteenth-century craniometric typologies, eugenic claims about "Aryan" or "Nordic" superiority; none survive engagement with genomic data. The lecture is unambiguous: "Races do not exist" (slide 30). UNESCO statements of 1950 and 1951, the American Anthropological Association's 1998 statement, and the American Society of Human Genetics's 2018 statement on race and genetics all confirm this. As Gilroy summarises in 'Race Ends Here', "Scientific and biological, historical and cultural, rational and irrational, skin, bone and even blood are no longer primary referents of racial discourse" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). The crude scientific racism of the nineteenth century is biologically dead.

But the question hides a sharper second part: do genomic technologies refute scientific racism in practice as well as in doctrine? I argue not yet, and for two reasons.

First, race-based medicine. Several medical interventions continue to use race as a clinical proxy. BiDil (isosorbide dinitrate/hydralazine), a combination drug approved by the FDA in 2005, was the first drug marketed specifically for self-identified Black patients with heart failure. The clinical trials that supported BiDil's approval were conducted in self-identified African American patients only, and the drug's efficacy in other groups was not directly tested. Critics (Jonathan Kahn, Dorothy Roberts) argued that BiDil's race-specific approval was a regulatory and commercial artefact; it used race as a stand-in for genetic variation that the trials had not directly measured. Race-adjusted formulas in spirometry (lung function), eGFR (kidney function), bone density and several other clinical measures continued to use race-based correction factors descended from nineteenth-century pseudoscience well into the 2020s. Some have been retired (the eGFR race adjustment was removed by major US guidelines in 2021); others remain. Race-based medicine is genomic-era scientific racism in clinical clothing.

Second, ancestry-testing consumerism. Companies like 23andMe and AncestryDNA market genomic ancestry tests that report percentages of "African," "European," "Native American" and other ancestries. The results are commercially packaged in ways that re-naturalise racial categories: customers receive maps colour-coded by continental ancestry, with implicit racial assumptions baked into the visualisation. The technology can support nuanced ancestry claims; the marketing tends to support racial reification. Critics (Alondra Nelson, *The Social Life of DNA*, 2016) note that ancestry tests have become identity-claiming infrastructure, sometimes deployed in court cases over tribal enrolment, school admissions, and even immigration claims.

Third, polygenic scores. The current frontier of genomic research includes polygenic risk scores that predict disease susceptibility from genome-wide variants. Polygenic scores derived from European-ancestry training data have lower predictive accuracy in other-ancestry populations, raising the prospect that genomic medicine could become differentially effective by ancestry. Without explicit racial categorisation, the practical outcome could be that medicine works better for those of European ancestry; recreating racialised health disparities through technical rather than ideological means.

Fourth, behavioural genomics. Recurrent attempts to claim group-based differences in cognitive or behavioural traits (Arthur Jensen, 1969; Richard Herrnstein and Charles Murray's *The Bell Curve*, 1994; Nicholas Wade's *A Troublesome Inheritance*, 2014) repackage scientific racism in genomic vocabulary. Each wave is rebutted by mainstream geneticists, but the pattern recurs. As long as the social category of race persists, some research will try to find genomic correlates of it.

I draw on Gilroy's nuance. In 'Race Ends Here' Gilroy argues that scientific racism's "Euclidean anatomy", measuring skulls, classifying physiognomies, has been outflanked by molecular biology, but that "racial discourse is to be regarded as something that has become unstuck in its temporal location" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). The perceptual habits of scientific racism survive the techniques that produced them. Race becomes "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253), present in perception even when its biological supports have collapsed. Genomic technologies can either undo or reinforce the after-image, depending on how they are used.

I provide further examples. The Henrietta Lacks case (HeLa cells taken from a Black woman in 1951 and used without consent in foundational medical research) is the genomic-era's most cited scientific-racism legacy. Decades of medical research benefited from her cells while her family received nothing; this asymmetry follows the colonial pattern of extracting from racialised bodies what scientific knowledge requires. The Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972) are the same logic in earlier form. UK trials of asthma drugs, vaccine trials and pharmaceutical research have had to confront the underrepresentation of minority groups in clinical samples, and the question of how genomic medicine can be equitable across populations.

I anticipate two objections. The first is that the persistence of group genetic differences (variant frequencies, polygenic risk scores) shows that genomics partly vindicates the concept of race. I think this misreads the data: variant frequencies vary continuously across populations and do not produce discrete racial categories. The second objection is that race-based medicine, even if imperfect, helps target treatments. I think the better solution is to measure the underlying genetic variation directly rather than use race as a proxy, which BiDil's trials did not do.

I draw on Du Bois and Morrison for the closing frame. Du Bois's insistence that "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136) is empirically vindicated by the genomic data. Morrison's insistence that "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) explains why race nevertheless persists in genomic-era institutions: the power that produced racial categorisation has not dissolved, and is now operating through new techniques.

I conclude that the genomics revolution decisively refutes scientific racism as biological doctrine while creating new institutional conditions under which racial categories can be molecularly reified. The intellectual war is won; the practical war is not. Race-based medicine, ancestry consumerism, polygenic disparities and recurrent behavioural-genomic claims show that scientific racism keeps finding new infrastructures. The lesson is the one the lecture draws from Gilroy: ways of seeing outlast their explicit justifications, and undoing racialised perception is a longer project than refuting any one doctrine.

### Mock 9. How convincing do you find Morrison's claim that whiteness creates the Other?

In this essay I find Morrison's claim that whiteness creates the Other deeply convincing (more convincing than its main rivals) and defend it on three grounds: it captures the historical record, it explains the affective dynamics of contemporary racism, and it provides a productive analytical frame for institutional analysis. I qualify the claim only by noting that it works best when paired with Du Bois's structural account of racial categorisation.

I begin with Morrison's claim. In *The Origin of Others* (2017) Morrison presses on the question "What is race (other than genetic imagination) and why does it matter?" (Morrison, 2017: 15). Her answer is that race is the residue of power exerted to confirm the dominant group's normality: "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29). And she gives the compact thesis, quoting Bruce Baum: "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25). Othering, on this account, is not an unfortunate accident of racism but its productive mechanism: whiteness manufactures Black, Asian, Latino, indigenous and migrant Others because that manufacture is what makes whiteness possible as a stabilised dominant position.

I take this to invert the more familiar account. A naive view treats race as a property of the racialised (Black people are Black; the question is how white-majority societies treat them). Morrison reverses the priority: whiteness is the operation, Black is the residue. This puts the analytical focus on the racialising rather than the racialised, on the producer of othering rather than its object.

I evaluate the claim on three grounds.

First, the historical record. Morrison's account fits the documented history of racial categorisation. Du Bois had already shown that the slave statutes that produced racial categories in the seventeenth century were responses to a labour-supply problem, not articulations of pre-existing racial categories: "Such statutes sought to solve problems of labor and not of race or color" (Du Bois, 1898: 3). The legal infrastructure that gave racial categories their stable boundaries (chattel slavery, the one-drop rule, the partus sequitur ventrem doctrine that made slavery hereditary through the mother) were creations of white legal authority, not features of any pre-existing racial reality. The British Empire's "civilising mission" likewise produced colonial races through administrative and educational practice. Whiteness made Blackness, not the other way round.

Second, the affective dynamics of contemporary racism. Morrison's account explains why racial backlash is so often disproportionate to the threat. The 2008 election of Barack Obama produced an immediate backlash that elected Trump in 2016; the post-2020 BLM mobilisations produced a wave of state-level anti-CRT laws; the Sewell Report (2021) explicitly denied institutional racism in the UK at a moment when most empirical measures showed it. In each case the dominant group reacts to the visibility of its own position by reinforcing the categories that obscure that position. Morrison's "desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29) describes the affective register precisely. Without something like Morrison's account, these reactions look gratuitous; with her account, they are necessary maintenance.

Third, the productive analytical frame for institutional analysis. The Macpherson Report (1999) on the Stephen Lawrence inquiry, which named the Metropolitan Police "institutionally racist," can be read as Morrison-applied: the institution's failure to investigate the murder of a Black teenager as it would investigate the murder of a white teenager confirmed an implicit white-British self that the institution was protecting. The Windrush scandal (2018) operated likewise; bureaucratic decisions categorising Black British citizens as foreign confirmed the implicit whiteness of British identity. UK Stop & Search disparities (Black people 7-9 times more likely to be stopped, Home Office) are this dynamic operationalised in everyday policing.

I consider the rival accounts.

The first rival treats racism as individual prejudice. Morrison's account is more powerful because it explains why racism persists even where individual prejudice is unusual; by locating the work of racial production in institutions and practices, not just in individual hearts. The post-Civil-Rights US and the post-Race-Relations-Act UK both have institutional racial disparities that no plausible aggregation of individual prejudice can explain. Morrison handles this; pure-prejudice accounts do not.

The second rival treats racism as economic exploitation that incidentally takes racial form. This account, associated with class-first traditions, says that capitalism needed cheap labour and used race to organise its workforce. There is truth here; Du Bois shows the slave statutes as responses to labour supply problems; but the account underplays the affective and identarian dimensions Morrison foregrounds. Why has racism persisted across capitalist restructuring? Why does post-industrial right-wing populism mobilise racial categorisation as energetically as did plantation capitalism? Morrison's "effect of power" framing handles this: power produces race because power needs race to confirm itself.

The third rival treats racism as biological misperception that science can correct. This account assumes the path from biological refutation to social dissolution. Decades of biological refutation, including the Human Genome Project, have failed to dissolve race. Morrison explains why: race is not primarily a perceptual error but a power operation. Correcting the perception does not address the power.

I qualify Morrison's account. Whiteness creates the Other, but it does so by exploiting a historical accumulation that Du Bois has already named: the colour line, the veil, the structures of Jim Crow and segregation. Without Du Bois's structural picture, Morrison's account can sound voluntaristic, as if whiteness wills the othering each time. With Du Bois's picture, Morrison's account specifies the affective and identarian machinery that keeps an inherited structural settlement reproducing. The two are mutually reinforcing.

I add Gilroy. In *The Black Atlantic* Gilroy argues that modernity required "the figures of black folks who had previously been confined to the intermediate category between animal and human that Du Bois identifies as a 'tertium quid'" (Gilroy, 1993: 55). The othering Morrison describes was Atlantic in scale: European bourgeois humanism defined itself against the Africans it enslaved and the colonised peoples it administered. Morrison's claim makes more sense when scaled to the Black Atlantic.

I press contemporary examples. The 2022-2024 UK "small boats" rhetoric racialised Channel-crossing migrants while welcoming Ukrainian refugees; othering selecting its targets by racial cues. The 2020 BLM moment was met by a wave of "all lives matter" responses that performed the Morrison operation: rendering the Black-specific claim as deviation from a universal that, on inspection, was implicitly white. The post-Floyd policing reforms in some US cities and the anti-CRT backlash in others showed Morrison's dynamic playing out in real time: where the visibility of whiteness threatened, the dominant group worked to restore unmarked-ness.

I conclude that Morrison's claim that whiteness creates the Other is convincing because it fits the historical record, explains contemporary affective dynamics, and grounds productive institutional analysis. The claim is not a complete sociology of race; it works best paired with Du Bois's structural picture and Gilroy's Black Atlantic scale; but it identifies the affective and identarian core that other accounts miss. Whiteness, as Morrison sees it, is a project of self-confirmation that requires the manufacture of difference. The project succeeds because the manufactured difference is so often mistaken for the explanation rather than the symptom.

### Mock 10. Answer with reference to Gilroy's 'Race Ends Here': what does it mean to say that race has become an "after-image"?

In this essay I answer with reference to Gilroy's 'Race Ends Here' (1998) and explain what he means by saying race has become "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). I show how the metaphor combines a thesis about technological-perceptual change with a normative project of overcoming raciology, and I assess its purchase against contemporary evidence.

I begin with the passage. Gilroy writes that "perhaps 'race' might best be approached as an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253), reflecting on the question of how to write about race when its biological supports have collapsed but its social effects persist. The image is optical: an after-image is the visual trace left on the retina after the source of light is removed. It is real to the perceiver but has no current external referent. Race, on Gilroy's analogy, is the after-image of scientific racism: a perceptual reality whose biological cause is gone.

I unpack the analogy into three claims.

The first claim is about technological-perceptual change. The "Euclidean anatomy" of nineteenth-century scientific racism, measuring skulls, classifying physiognomies, comparing morphologies, produced race as an observable scientific object. Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735) and Blumenbach's five-race typology (1775) belong to this perceptual regime. But contemporary techniques (molecular biology, MRI imaging, genome sequencing) have outflanked it. At the molecular level, race vanishes: genomic variation is mostly within groups, not between them. Gilroy notes: "Scientific and biological, historical and cultural, rational and irrational, skin, bone and even blood are no longer primary referents of racial discourse" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). The technical apparatus that supported scientific racism has been replaced by an apparatus that does not see race.

The second claim is about the persistence of perception. Despite the dissolution of the biological referent, racial perception continues. We still see race when we look at people; institutions still sort by it; political mobilisations still organise around it. The after-image is real to the perceiver. Gilroy phrases this carefully: "racial discourse is to be regarded as something that has become unstuck in its temporal location" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). Race exists out of step with the scientific moment that produced it; it survives its own refutation.

The third claim is normative. The after-image is something to be dissolved. Gilroy explicitly hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255). The after-image's persistence is not a fact to be lived with but a residue to be cleared. 'Race Ends Here' is a programmatic title: not race ended here but race is to end here, and Gilroy treats his task as helping to end it.

I think the metaphor does important work but has limits.

The work it does. First, it captures something true about scientific racism's afterlife. Du Bois's insight that "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136) was both correct and powerless to dissolve race because race operates as perception, not biology. Gilroy's metaphor names the gap. Second, it locates the political problem in perceptual training, not just in scientific argument. Closing the gap requires retraining sight, not just rebutting craniometry. Third, it suggests that race may yet end, since after-images fade. Gilroy is more optimistic in 'Race Ends Here' than many of his contemporaries; the metaphor licences this optimism.

The limits. First, the after-image metaphor underplays the institutional reproduction of race. After-images fade because no new light reinforces them. Race does not fade because institutions continue to reproduce it. UK Stop & Search ratios (Black people 7-9 times more likely to be stopped, Home Office) reinforce racial perception daily. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate, MBRRACE-UK) is reproduced by clinical practice. The 2018 Windrush scandal produced fresh racialised effects from a putatively post-racial bureaucracy. After-images do not have these mechanisms; race does.

Second, the metaphor risks suggesting that scientific argument can dissolve race. Decades of scientific argument since the 1950 UNESCO Statement have not done so. The post-2020 anti-CRT panic in the US (Florida's Stop WOKE Act, 2022; state-level bans on race-historical education) shows that the political infrastructure of racialisation is mostly indifferent to scientific argument. Du Bois's racial realism (race is real because racism is real) explains the persistence more directly than the after-image metaphor.

Third, the metaphor underplays the agential dimension that Morrison emphasises. Race is not just a perceptual leftover; it is actively produced by power. Morrison's claim that "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) and "rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29) gives race a producer and a function in the present, not just in the past. The after-image metaphor risks freezing race in the past while it is still being made.

I bring evidence to bear. The 2023 SFFA v Harvard decision reversed affirmative action just as the racial wealth gap widened; interest convergence (Bell) collapsing, racial categorisation actively reproduced through legal doctrine. The Sewell Report (2021) in the UK denied institutional racism while empirical disparities persisted; racial categorisation maintained discursively even where the report claimed it had been dissolved. The 2020 COVID-19 disparities (BAME Britons dying at higher rates) made racial categorisation visible in healthcare. The 2018 Windrush scandal made it visible in immigration enforcement. These are not after-images; they are current operations.

But Gilroy has a Gilroyan reply. The metaphor is meant to displace the apparatus of biological scientific racism specifically, not to deny that race has institutional life. He acknowledges that "the order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252) and that race persists as institutional and perceptual fact even where biology no longer underwrites it. The after-image is what is left after biology has been refuted; it is not the whole of racial reality.

I extend the analysis through other Gilroy concepts. He cites Fanon's "epidermalization" as the colonial process by which bodies are made meaningful through "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254). Epidermalisation continues even where the biological theory that originally authorised it has been retired. The "chemical gaze" of contemporary medical and forensic technologies (DNA testing, ancestry profiling, algorithmic facial recognition) produces new perceptual regimes that can reinscribe race. Joy Buolamwini's "Gender Shades" research on facial recognition error rates shows the after-image being reproduced algorithmically. BiDil and race-based pharmacogenomics show the after-image being reproduced medically. Direct-to-consumer ancestry testing shows it being reproduced commercially.

I draw on Du Bois to test the metaphor. The lecture summarises Du Bois's racial realism: race is biologically trivial but socially decisive: "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact … the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). If Du Bois is right, the after-image metaphor needs an addition: race is the after-image of scientific racism that institutions continue to use as if it were a current reality. The metaphor gives us the perceptual diagnosis; Du Bois gives us the institutional diagnosis.

I conclude that Gilroy's after-image metaphor captures something important (race persists as a perceptual reality after its biological supports have collapsed) but is incomplete on its own. Race is reproduced as well as remembered: institutions, media and political mobilisations actively manufacture racial categorisation. The after-image is being recharged. Race ends here remains an aspiration, and Gilroy's 'Race Ends Here' is best read as a normative call rather than a descriptive claim. The fade is not automatic; it requires the political and institutional work the metaphor hopes to provoke.

### Mock 11. Using at least two authors, explain why "race ends here" is harder than it sounds.

In this essay I use Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy himself to explain why Gilroy's title-claim that race ends here (gestured at in his 1998 essay 'Race Ends Here' and developed in *Against Race*, 2000) is harder than it sounds. The slogan is right in its biological premise and admirable in its normative direction, but the social, institutional and affective infrastructure of race is far harder to dismantle than the doctrine of scientific racism that has now been refuted.

I begin with Gilroy's own claim. In 'Race Ends Here' he hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) and treats race as becoming "an after-image" (Gilroy, 1998: 253). The argument has scientific and political ambition. Scientifically: molecular biology and digital imaging have outflanked the "Euclidean anatomy" of nineteenth-century scientific racism, so race no longer has the biological supports it once claimed. Politically: contemporary cultural and technological shifts may enable the end of raciology. Gilroy is more optimistic in 'Race Ends Here' than many of his contemporaries.

But Gilroy himself names the difficulty. He writes that "the order of active differentiation that gets called 'race' may be modernity's most pernicious signature" (Gilroy, 1998: 252). Modernity's signature, on this account, is not a residue easily dissolved but a structuring principle deeply embedded. The same essay notes that "racial discourse is to be regarded as something that has become unstuck in its temporal location" (Gilroy, 1998: 253); race persists out of step with the science that produced it. Gilroy is at once optimistic about the scientific dissolution and realistic about the persistence.

I bring in Du Bois to specify the social difficulty. Du Bois's mature position in *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) anticipates Gilroy's worry by separating biology from social reality: "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial. And yet this fact of racial distinction based on color was the greatest thing in my life and absolutely determined it" (Du Bois, 1940: 136). The "and yet" is the difficulty. Even where biology fails to ground race, the social practice of racial categorisation remains binding: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). Du Bois's social-fact realism shows that ending race requires ending the practices that constitute it, not just the doctrine that justified them.

I bring in Morrison to specify the affective and identarian difficulty. Morrison's "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) names the producing mechanism: power needs racial categorisation to confirm itself. "Rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29) names the affective payoff. Until the affective economy that othering serves is itself dismantled, race will keep being recreated. The post-2020 anti-CRT panic in the US, the rise of explicit white-nationalist politics in Europe, the Brexit campaign's mobilisation of racialised migration anxieties, the 2022-2024 UK "small boats" rhetoric; each is the affective economy doing maintenance work on a category whose biology has been refuted for half a century.

I assemble five reasons why race ends here is harder than it sounds.

The first reason is institutional inertia. Major institutions (the US Census, medical clinical practice, the British Home Office, university admissions, school curricula) operate with racial categories. The US Census still uses "Caucasian" (a Blumenbach 1775 coinage). UK NHS data is collected by "ethnic group." BiDil (FDA-approved 2005) markets a drug to "self-identified Black patients with heart failure." UK Stop & Search records (Black people 7-9 times more likely to be stopped) presuppose racial categorisation. Each is sticky. Ending these institutional practices requires reformatting their data infrastructures, retraining their personnel, and changing their statutory frameworks.

The second reason is perceptual habit. Gilroy himself invokes Fanon's epidermalisation, bodies "imposed" with "qualities of 'colour'" (Gilroy, 1998: 254), to name the deep perceptual training that race involves. Closing the racial gaze requires retraining sight, not just rebutting craniometry. Algorithmic perception (Joy Buolamwini's "Gender Shades" research on commercial facial recognition error rates by skin tone) shows the inherited perceptual habits being reproduced in code. Direct-to-consumer ancestry tests (23andMe, AncestryDNA) reinscribe racial categorisation through colour-coded continental ancestry maps.

The third reason is the political utility of race. Morrison's account explains why political actors keep recreating the category: race confirms the dominant group's normality. Brexit, Trump-era US politics, the 2022-2024 UK small-boats discourse, the Sewell Report's denial of institutional racism, the post-2020 anti-CRT panic; each mobilises racial categorisation for political ends. Until the political economy of othering is reconfigured, race ends here will keep being undone by political work.

The fourth reason is the symmetric problem of anti-racist mobilisation. Anti-racist politics often necessarily uses racial categories (to measure disparities, to demand remedies, to organise solidarity). CRT, anti-racist statistics, reparations programmes, racial-impact assessments all categorise by race. Gilroy in *Against Race* (2000) acknowledges this tension; he wants a transitional anti-racism that uses racial categories to dismantle racialisation. But the transition is hard. Every census category, every disparity metric, every demographic dataset uses race. Ending race ends here would require dismantling much of the anti-racist measurement infrastructure too.

The fifth reason is the historical depth of the Black Atlantic. Gilroy in *The Black Atlantic* (1993) treats slavery as constitutive of modernity: "Plantation slavery … provided the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, 1993: 54). The wealth, institutions, cities and cultural canons of European modernity were forged through the Black Atlantic. Ending race ends here is incompatible with a modernity built on race; it would require either rewriting modernity itself or living with a contradiction.

I provide concrete examples that show the difficulty empirically. The Windrush scandal (2018) shows the British state administratively racialising long after the law had formally repudiated biological race. UK Stop & Search ratios persist despite decades of attempted reform. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate) persists despite explicit healthcare-policy attention. The 2023 SFFA v Harvard reversal of affirmative action shows that racial-redistributive remedies can be reversed when interest convergence (Bell) collapses. The 2020 COVID-19 racial disparities operated through occupational and housing segregation that pre-existed the pandemic. The 2020 toppling of Edward Colston's statue produced a public conversation about Britain's slave-trade past that immediately ran into the limits of how much past Britain was willing to acknowledge.

I anticipate a Gilroyan reply. The point of 'Race Ends Here' is not that race has ended but that we should work to end it. The aspirational reading saves the slogan from the empirical objections by treating it as a normative target. I accept this; 'Race Ends Here' is best read as a programmatic call. But the case I have built shows what the programme has to overcome: institutional inertia, perceptual habit, political utility, the symmetric problem of anti-racist measurement and the historical depth of the Black Atlantic. None of these is impossible to overcome; all are harder than the slogan suggests.

I close with the connection between the two authors I have used most. Du Bois shows that race is real because racism is real; biological refutation is necessary but not sufficient. Morrison shows that race is an effect of power; refuting the doctrine does not deprive power of the affective work race performs for it. Gilroy's race ends here is correct in principle and admirable in aspiration, but it requires institutional and affective work that the slogan alone cannot do.

I conclude that race ends here is harder than it sounds because race is reproduced by institutions, perceptual habits, political utility, anti-racist measurement and the historical depth of modernity itself. Ending race requires dismantling these infrastructures, not just refuting scientific racism. Gilroy is right about the direction; Du Bois and Morrison show why the journey is longer than the slogan suggests.

### Mock 12. Can sociology abolish race without losing the analytical category?

In this essay I argue that sociology cannot abolish race in the present without losing important analytical and political traction, but that it can and should work towards a future in which the category becomes unnecessary. The argument rests on the distinction between abolishing race as biological doctrine (already done in principle) and abolishing race as analytical category (premature, dangerous, but worth aspiring to). I make the case through Du Bois, Morrison, Gilroy and the contemporary record.

I begin with the question. "Can sociology abolish race without losing the analytical category?" can mean several things. It can mean: should sociology stop using the category in its measurements and theorisations? It can mean: can sociology's use of the category be reformed without dropping it? It can mean: what is the timeline of category-loss? I take all three readings seriously.

I start with the abolitionist case. The strongest version comes from Paul Gilroy. In 'Race Ends Here' (1998) and *Against Race* (2000) he hopes for "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) and worries that sociological use of racial categories, even in anti-racist mode, can reinscribe the very thing it tries to dismantle. His worry is that the categorising practices of sociology are continuous with the classificatory practices that originally produced race. Sociology measures racial disparities, names racial groups, runs racial-difference regressions. Each measurement reinforces the salience of the category. The abolitionist programme would have sociology stop using race at all, treating it as a category that should fade as institutional practice changes.

I find the abolitionist case ethically attractive and practically premature. Three reasons.

The first reason is that race remains real as social fact. Du Bois's mature position in *Dusk of Dawn* (1940) is that race is biologically trivial but socially decisive: "the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia" (Du Bois, 1940: 153). The institutions Du Bois described (Jim Crow, voting suppression, redlining, sharecropping) are gone; the disparities they produced persist. UK Stop & Search disparities (Black people 7-9 times more likely to be stopped, Home Office), Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate, MBRRACE-UK), the racial wealth gap (in the US, white households hold roughly ten times the wealth of Black households, Federal Reserve data), educational attainment gaps, COVID-19 mortality disparities; each is documented through racial categorisation. To stop measuring race would be to make these disparities harder to see and harder to address. The abolitionist would have sociology measure something else; it is not obvious that "something else" would capture the racialised facts better.

The second reason is that race operates as power-effect that continues to be reproduced. Morrison's "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) names the producing mechanism. Power continues to manufacture racial categorisation: the 2023 SFFA v Harvard reversal of affirmative action, the post-2020 anti-CRT panic, the Sewell Report's denial of institutional racism, the 2022-2024 UK small-boats discourse all show racial categorisation being actively produced. Sociology has to track the production to analyse it. An abolitionist sociology would describe institutional racism without naming the categorising operation that institutional racism performs.

The third reason is that anti-racist politics requires racial measurement. CRT's diagnostic work (interest convergence, racial realism, counter-storytelling), reparations programmes (CARICOM, UCL, University of Glasgow), affirmative action where it survives, racial-impact assessments and equality monitoring all require the category. Bell's interest convergence argument, for instance, depends on data showing differential treatment by race. Removing the category does not remove the inequality but does remove the political tools for naming it.

I now consider what reform could look like, abolishing race in the long term without losing the analytical category in the short term.

First, treat the category as historical and constructed, not natural. The lecture is explicit: "Races do not exist" biologically (slide 30). Sociological use of race should always foreground this; race in survey data is self-identified or institutionally assigned, not biological fact. Du Bois's "Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact" (Du Bois, 1940: 153) is the right framing. Census categories should be revised to track the social facts of racial classification rather than to imply biological groupings.

Second, distinguish racial categorisation from ancestry. Genomic ancestry (the distribution of allele frequencies across populations) is real but does not correspond to lay racial categories. Where ancestry is medically or statistically relevant, sociology should use ancestry, not race. BiDil's race-specific approval would have been better cast as ancestry-specific or, better still, mechanism-specific. The eGFR race-adjustment formulas have been retired in favour of mechanism-based formulas; this is a model.

Third, design categorisation to be self-undermining. Anti-racist measurement should aim at its own redundancy. The category exists because the disparity exists; the goal is to dismantle the disparity, after which the category becomes unnecessary. Gilroy's "anti-toxins capable of silencing raciology forever" (Gilroy, 1998: 255) is the right horizon, but the toxins do not finish their work in a year. Cécile Vergès's *A Decolonial Feminism* and contemporary abolitionist work treat this as a long-term programme.

Fourth, distinguish use from reification. Sociology can use racial categories analytically without claiming that the categories track natural kinds. The same logic applies to "class" and "gender", categories that are socially real, analytically useful and biologically loose. Du Bois used race in *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) and *Black Reconstruction* (1935) without essentialising. The discipline can do this.

I anticipate three objections.

The first objection is from Gilroy: any use of race entrenches it. I think this is partly true but cannot win the practical argument. Choosing not to measure race does not dissolve the racial reality measured; it just makes the reality invisible to the measurement. The right response is more reflexive use, not abolition.

The second objection is from the colour-blind right: race-conscious sociology contributes to the very divisions it claims to study. The SFFA opinion implied this. The CRT reply, citing Sotomayor's dissent, is that colour-blindness in a racially structured society protects the structure. Refusing to see race does not unmake it.

The third objection is methodological: race in survey data conflates different things (self-identification, ascription, ancestry). I accept this and think the right response is methodological refinement (multiple-categorisation, ancestry plus race, longitudinal tracking) not abolition.

I draw on contemporary examples. The 2018 Windrush scandal shows what happens when racialised institutional practice is invisible to data: the disparate impact was clear in retrospect but was not measured until after the harm. The 2020 COVID-19 disparities became visible only because UK NHS data tracked ethnicity. Black maternal mortality is now discussable because MBRRACE-UK reports by ethnicity. Where the category is in use, the disparities can be named and addressed; where it is absent, they cannot.

I bring the discussion back to Du Bois. Du Bois warned that "Equality in law is not enough"; formal abolition of racial categorisation in law does not abolish the social fact. The same logic applies to sociology: abolishing race in the discipline does not abolish race in the society. The category can be retired when the disparity has been dismantled; not before.

I close with Gilroy's normative horizon. Sociology should work towards a future in which race is no longer needed as an analytical category, not because we have ignored it but because the institutional and affective infrastructure that makes it analytically necessary has been dismantled. The work is generational. In the meantime, use the category honestly, reflexively and with the aim of dissolving the conditions that require it.

I conclude that sociology cannot abolish race in the present without losing the analytical traction needed to measure and contest racial disparities, but that the discipline can and should work towards a future in which the category becomes unnecessary. The intermediate path is reflexive use: race in sociology as historical and constructed, not natural; distinguished from ancestry; designed for self-undermining; and held analytically rather than reified. Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy together support this position. The end of race is a horizon, not a starting move.

### Mock 13. Tuskegee, Henrietta Lacks and the long shadow of medical racism.

In this essay I take the Tuskegee syphilis experiments (1932-1972) and the Henrietta Lacks case (1951-) as two emblematic cases of medical racism in the twentieth-century United States and argue, through Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy, that medical racism is the institutional afterlife of scientific racism and continues to shape racialised health outcomes in the twenty-first century.

I begin with the cases. The Tuskegee Study of Untreated Syphilis in the Negro Male was a US Public Health Service research project that enrolled 600 Black men in Macon County, Alabama, between 1932 and 1972; 399 with syphilis, 201 controls. The men were not told they had syphilis; they were told they were being treated for "bad blood." When penicillin became the standard cure in the mid-1940s, the men were not given it. The study continued for 40 years; it ended in 1972 only after a whistleblower leak to the *Washington Star* and the Associated Press. The 1973 Senate hearings, the 1974 National Research Act, and the eventual 1997 presidential apology by Bill Clinton came decades too late.

Henrietta Lacks was a Black woman from Baltimore who was treated for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins Hospital in 1951 and died of the disease aged 31. During her treatment, cells from her tumour were taken without her consent or her family's knowledge. They became the HeLa cell line, the first immortal human cell line, foundational to modern medical research. HeLa cells contributed to the polio vaccine, in vitro fertilisation, cancer drug development, and an enormous range of contemporary biomedicine. The Lacks family received no compensation for decades and only learned of HeLa in the 1970s. The case was popularised by Rebecca Skloot's *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* (2010); a partial settlement with Thermo Fisher Scientific was reached in 2023.

I take Tuskegee and Lacks as connected, not isolated. They share three features. First, both involved the extraction of medical value from racialised bodies without consent or fair compensation. Second, both reflected an institutional logic that treated Black bodies as available for research in ways white bodies would not have been. Third, both produced damage that long outlived the original injury: Tuskegee through generations of Black-American medical distrust; Lacks through the continuing commercial value of HeLa cells against the Lacks family's lack of recompense until very recently.

I now frame the cases through the lecture's theorists.

Du Bois first. The Tuskegee study began in 1932, while Du Bois was alive and still writing on race in America. It exemplifies what Du Bois called the colour line in healthcare: "The Nation has not yet found peace from its sins; the freedman has not yet found in freedom his promised land" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Tuskegee operated on the implicit principle that the Black man's body was an available resource for white medical knowledge. The veil ("shut out from their world by a vast veil," Du Bois, 1903: 4) separated the men from the researchers in a way that allowed the researchers to forget the men's humanity. Du Bois's social-fact realism ("the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia," Du Bois, 1940: 153) extends easily to medicine: the Black man is a person whose body must be available for research in the Tuskegee study.

Morrison next. The institutional logic that authorised Tuskegee and the Lacks extraction is Morrison's othering. The researchers' capacity to enrol men in a study without telling them they had a treatable disease, and the surgeons' capacity to take cells without consent, depended on the racialised body being available in a way that the unracialised white body was not. "The necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29); and the foreign-species logic survives slavery into medical research. Morrison's analysis of othering as media-and-institution helps explain why neither case provoked institutional reform for decades: the Othering had been so thoroughly internalised that it did not register as injury.

Gilroy third. The Tuskegee study and the HeLa case fit Gilroy's analysis of scientific racism's afterlife. Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' argues that scientific racism's perceptual regime, the "Euclidean anatomy" of comparative racial measurement, has been outflanked by molecular biology but its perceptual habits persist. Tuskegee took place inside the same epistemic frame as Blumenbach: bodies sorted by race for differential treatment. Lacks's cells were taken inside an institutional structure where Black women's bodies had been historically available for medical experimentation (the lecture flags this as part of scientific racism's "long history of medical experimentation on Black bodies"). The molecular era's clinic inherits the nineteenth-century laboratory's racial assumptions.

I now trace the long shadow. Three lines.

The first line is medical distrust. Decades of Tuskegee literature document the impact on Black-American attitudes towards medical research and public health. The COVID-19 vaccination rollout in 2020-2022 ran into pockets of Black-American distrust traceable, in qualitative research, to Tuskegee memory. This is rational distrust, given history; calling it a misperception misses the institutional cause.

The second line is race-based medicine. The lecture's mention of Tuskegee anchors a discussion of how scientific racism's clinical assumptions have persisted. BiDil (FDA-approved 2005), a heart-failure drug marketed for self-identified Black patients; race-adjusted spirometry formulas; eGFR race adjustments (retired in 2021); skin-thickness assumptions affecting pain prescribing; each is contemporary scientific-racist residue in clinical practice. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate in the UK and US) tracks differential clinical treatment, not differential physiology, and is shaped in part by inherited assumptions about Black women's pain tolerance and "strength."

The third line is consent and bioethics. Tuskegee and Lacks reshaped US bioethics: the National Research Act (1974), the Belmont Report (1979), institutional review boards (IRBs), informed consent procedures. But these reforms have not addressed the underlying institutional patterns. Racialised underrepresentation in clinical trials persists; drug efficacy and safety data are often based on samples dominated by European-ancestry participants, with implications for genomic-era pharmacology. The 2020 COVID-19 trials made some progress on enrolling diverse populations; the gap remains.

I bring in further examples. The 1985 MOVE bombing in Philadelphia (a Black liberation organisation; the police drop incendiary devices killing 11 including five children) is medical-racism's policing equivalent: the institutional logic that authorises lethal force against racialised bodies. The 1932-1972 timeframe of Tuskegee overlapped with US eugenic sterilisation programmes that disproportionately targeted Black and Latina women; North Carolina's eugenics programme alone sterilised 7,600 people between 1929 and 1974, the majority Black women and girls. The 1960s-1970s Puerto Rican contraceptive trials enrolled Puerto Rican women in early oral-contraceptive trials with limited consent and documented harm. Each is part of the same institutional logic.

In the UK, the medical-racism shadow is less spectacular but no less institutional. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate, MBRRACE-UK) is well documented; the 2020 COVID-19 disparities for BAME Britons reflect inherited occupational, housing and health-system inequalities; the Windrush scandal of 2018 included NHS-side effects: wrongly classified residents lost access to NHS treatment for cancer and other conditions. The Macpherson Report's definition of institutional racism ("the collective failure of an organisation to provide an appropriate and professional service to people because of their colour, culture, or ethnic origin") applies to UK healthcare as well as to policing.

I anticipate the objection that contemporary medicine has moved on from Tuskegee. I think this confuses formal disavowal with practical effect. The IRB system, informed consent and bioethics training have changed how research is conducted, but the institutional patterns of differential treatment have proved harder to dismantle. The persistence of Black maternal mortality despite policy attention is evidence.

I draw on the lecture's closing frame. Lecturer summary: "Races do not exist. But the experiences constituted by the belief, practices, history and economics of 'race' do … forms of racism … are present in, for example, institutions, art galleries, music, and ways of seeing" (slide 30). The medical institutions whose history includes Tuskegee and Lacks are exactly the institutions that the lecture asks us to examine. Race in medicine is not biological; it is institutional and perceptual.

I conclude that Tuskegee and Henrietta Lacks are not relics but cases that exemplify the institutional logic of medical racism. Du Bois names the veil that permitted the studies; Morrison names the othering that authorised them; Gilroy names the scientific-racism afterlife within which they operated. The long shadow extends through medical distrust, race-based medicine, racialised health disparities and the gaps in genomic-era inclusion. Ending medical racism, like ending race itself, is harder than retiring the doctrine; it requires retraining institutional perception and redistributing the resources that medicine generates from racialised bodies. The work continues.

### Mock 14. "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison). Discuss with reference to BiDil and racial pharmacogenomics.

In this essay I discuss Morrison's claim that "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) with reference to BiDil (the heart-failure drug approved by the FDA in 2005 for "self-identified Black patients") and the broader field of racial pharmacogenomics. I argue that BiDil instantiates Morrison's thesis with unusual clarity: a regulatory and commercial process produced "Black" as a clinical category despite the underlying genetic evidence not supporting the category. The case shows how race continues to be manufactured by power even in molecular medicine.

I begin with the BiDil case. BiDil is a fixed-dose combination of isosorbide dinitrate and hydralazine, two existing drugs used in heart failure. The combination had been studied in mixed-population trials in the 1980s with equivocal results. NitroMed, the company that acquired the rights, conducted the African-American Heart Failure Trial (A-HeFT, published *NEJM* 2004) exclusively in self-identified Black patients with heart failure, found efficacy, and the FDA approved the drug in 2005 for that group. BiDil became the first FDA drug labelled for a specific racial population.

I then frame the case through Morrison's claim. Three points.

First, the "Black" of BiDil is a regulatory and commercial product, not a biological discovery. Critics, most prominently Jonathan Kahn (*Race in a Bottle*, 2013) and Dorothy Roberts (*Fatal Invention*, 2011), argued that BiDil's race-specific approval was an artefact of trial design and patent strategy. NitroMed had a race-specific patent that could be enforced; a race-neutral patent had expired. The decision to run A-HeFT in self-identified Black patients was responsive to patent and regulatory incentives, not to genomic findings. The trial showed that the combination works in self-identified Black patients with heart failure; it did not show that it works only in them, because it did not compare with other populations. "Black" emerged as a clinical category through the operation of regulatory, commercial and statistical power.

Second, the underlying genomic evidence does not support race-specific efficacy. Heart failure responsiveness to nitric oxide donors and vasodilators tracks underlying physiological and genetic variation (endothelial function, renin-angiotensin system polymorphisms, NOS3 variants) that crosses racial categories. Self-identified race is an unreliable proxy for the relevant variants. The American Society of Human Genetics has issued repeated statements urging caution about race-specific drug labelling. The race-specific framing was statistical-administrative, not molecular.

Third, the regulatory framing produced effects. BiDil's approval established a regulatory precedent for race-specific drug labelling; race-specific dosing guidelines proliferated in cardiology, oncology and pharmacology. Race-adjusted formulas in spirometry (lung function), eGFR (kidney function), bone density, and pain management have continued well into the 2020s, some only recently retired. These formulas inherited their racial assumptions from nineteenth-century scientific racism (beliefs about Black "lung capacity" and "skin thickness") and were maintained by clinical inertia rather than fresh evidence.

I read this as a precise instantiation of Morrison's claim. Race in BiDil is an effect of regulatory, commercial and statistical power, not of biological fact. The power-effect is institutional: a regulatory agency, a pharmaceutical company, a statistical methodology and a clinical-trial design jointly produced "Black" as a category to which a drug is licensed. Morrison's "necessity of rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29) extends to medicine: the racial categorisation in BiDil renders "Black patients" as a distinct medical species whose pharmacology may differ, even where the underlying physiology does not support the distinction.

I extend the analysis through racial pharmacogenomics more broadly.

First, polygenic risk scores. Current genomic medicine increasingly relies on polygenic risk scores, composite indices of disease susceptibility derived from genome-wide variants. Most polygenic scores have been derived from European-ancestry training data; they perform less well in other-ancestry populations. The risk is that genomic medicine becomes differentially effective by ancestry, recreating racialised health disparities through technical rather than ideological means. This is power-effect at the molecular level: institutional choices about training samples produce differential medical outcomes.

Second, race in clinical trial recruitment. The COVID-19 vaccine trials of 2020 made progress on diversity of enrolment, but historical patterns of underrepresentation persist. The Tuskegee shadow, the Henrietta Lacks shadow, and ongoing structural barriers to participation in clinical research mean that drug efficacy and safety data are often less reliable for non-European-ancestry populations. The category "race" is used in trial reporting partly to track these gaps; the same category produces the gaps.

Third, ancestry-testing consumerism. Companies like 23andMe and AncestryDNA produce continental ancestry estimates that are commercially packaged in colour-coded maps with implicit racial assumptions. Alondra Nelson's *The Social Life of DNA* (2016) documents how ancestry tests have become identity-claiming infrastructure, sometimes deployed in court cases, tribal-enrolment claims and immigration disputes. The genomic data can support nuanced ancestry claims; the marketing tends to support racial reification. Power continues to produce racial categories through the commercial packaging of molecular data.

I bring in Du Bois and Gilroy.

Du Bois's racial realism ("Race is a cultural, sometimes a historical fact … the black man is a person who must ride 'Jim Crow' in Georgia," Du Bois, 1940: 153) applies to BiDil: the black patient is a person to whom a race-specific cardiac drug is licensed. Race is real because institutional practice makes it so, not because biology grounds it. Du Bois's earlier observation that "the difference in skin color was vastly overemphasized and intrinsically trivial" (Du Bois, 1940: 136) speaks directly to the genomic case: the molecular difference is trivial; the racial category is decisive.

Gilroy's analysis of scientific racism's perceptual regime is also relevant. Gilroy in 'Race Ends Here' argues that the "Euclidean anatomy" of nineteenth-century scientific racism has been outflanked by molecular biology, but that the perceptual habits persist (Gilroy, 1998: 253). BiDil shows the persistence: the molecular era's clinic uses race-specific labelling that the molecules themselves do not justify. Gilroy's worry, that contemporary technological-perceptual regimes can reinscribe race, is empirically supported by the racial pharmacogenomic record.

I anticipate objections. The first is that race-specific medicine helps target therapies where the alternative would be slower progress. I think the better solution is to measure underlying physiological and genetic variation directly. The A-HeFT trial could have measured the molecular markers it now correlates with the racial category; it did not. The race-specific framing was administratively cheaper than the molecular framing. Cheaper is not the same as scientifically right. The second objection is that critics like Kahn and Roberts overstate the case and that BiDil does help some Black patients. I accept that the drug may help patients; the critique is of the racial labelling, not the efficacy. The third objection is that abandoning racial categories in medicine might mask disparities. I think this is partly right (race-specific data is needed to track institutional disparities) but using race for tracking disparities is not the same as using it for drug labelling.

I add concrete contemporary examples. The post-2020 racial reckoning has produced some reforms: the eGFR race adjustment was retired by major US guidelines in 2021; spirometry guidance is under review; the FDA has issued statements urging caution about race-specific labelling. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate in UK and US) is institutionally addressed in part because the racial category is in use. Tuskegee and Henrietta Lacks remain reference points for what happens when institutional power treats racialised bodies as available without consent.

I conclude that Morrison's claim that "Race, in short, is an effect of power" (Morrison, 2017: 25) is exemplified with unusual clarity in the BiDil case and in the broader field of racial pharmacogenomics. Race is not discovered by molecular medicine; it is administratively, commercially and regulatorily produced even in molecular medicine. Du Bois's racial realism explains why this matters (race is real because power makes it so) and Gilroy's analysis of scientific racism's afterlife predicts the persistence. The reform agenda is the right one: measure mechanism, not race; track disparities, not types; retire race-based clinical adjustments inherited from scientific racism. The end-state Gilroy hopes for, race ends here, is not closer in genomic medicine than elsewhere; it may, given current incentives, be further.

### Mock 15. Compare the "civilising mission" of the British Empire with the "anti-racism" common sense of the 2020s.

In this essay I compare two ideological formations that present themselves as benign racial improvement projects: the nineteenth-century British "civilising mission" and the twenty-first-century "anti-racism" common sense that emerged in mainstream Western institutions after 2020. I argue, through Du Bois, Morrison and Gilroy, that the two are very different (the civilising mission rationalised colonial extraction; the anti-racism common sense is mostly genuine moral progress) but that they share a structural feature: both can operate as projects of the dominant group that manage rather than dismantle the underlying racial settlement.

I begin with the civilising mission. The British Empire's official self-image was that imperial rule was a form of moral and political uplift, conferred on colonised peoples through education, law and Christianity. Macaulay's 1835 Minute on Indian Education proposed creating "a class of persons Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect", colonised subjects refashioned in imperial form. Rudyard Kipling's 'The White Man's Burden' (1899) gave the doctrine literary form. The doctrine justified the extraction of Indian, African, Caribbean, Australian and Pacific wealth through the rhetoric of cultural improvement.

The lecture frames this as scientific racism's political shadow. The slide tracing scientific racism from Linnaeus (1735) through Blumenbach (1775) and on to nineteenth-century craniometry shows how the empire's civilising mission rested on a hierarchy of races whose intellectual and moral capacities were taken to differ. The "white man's burden" was the burden of governing inferior races for their own good. Du Bois exposed the racial-economic engine beneath the rhetoric: in 'The Study of the Negro Problems' he showed that American slavery emerged from a labour shortage that "the central and all-absorbing economic need" (Du Bois, 1898: 3-4) had to solve, the same pattern in the colonies. The civilising mission was the cultural ornamentation of colonial economic extraction.

Gilroy is sharpest here. In *The Black Atlantic* he argues that "Plantation slavery was more than just a system of labour and a distinct mode of racial domination"; it provided "the foundations for a distinctive network of economic, social, and political relations" (Gilroy, 1993: 54). The Royal African Company, the Atlantic insurance market, the sugar economy of the Caribbean, the cotton economy that fed Manchester; all were the structural reality of which the civilising mission was the public-relations cover. His most provocative claim is that "racial terror is not merely compatible with occidental rationality but cheerfully complicit with it" (Gilroy, 1993: 56). The civilising mission's reason and the slave trade's violence rose together.

I then turn to the 2020s anti-racism common sense. After the 2020 killing of George Floyd, a wave of institutional anti-racism swept Western corporations, universities, NGOs and governments. Diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) programmes proliferated; corporate boards pledged racial accounting; museums and universities began renaming buildings and reviewing collections; the UK BBC, the BFI, the Royal Shakespeare Company and many others announced anti-racism programmes. Books like Reni Eddo-Lodge's *Why I'm No Longer Talking to White People About Race* (2017), Robin DiAngelo's *White Fragility* (2018) and Ibram X. Kendi's *How to Be an Antiracist* (2019) became bestsellers. UK universities reviewed colonial legacies (UCL's Galton inquiry, Glasgow's slavery report); Oxford's Rhodes Must Fall debate intensified; the 2020 toppling of Edward Colston's statue forced public reckoning.

I think the 2020s anti-racism common sense is mostly genuine. The Black Lives Matter mobilisations, the workplace reckonings, the curricular reforms and the institutional pledges responded to real and documented racial harms. Many of the changes are net improvements: removing the eGFR race adjustment, reviewing race-based clinical formulas, paying reparative scholarships, decolonising curricula, renaming buildings whose namesakes funded slavery. To dismiss the moment as cynical would be unfair to the people whose work made it.

But I also think the moment has the structural feature the question asks me to examine. Three points.

First, much of the 2020s anti-racism was performed at the level of symbol rather than redistribution. Diversity statements, hiring pledges and public apologies are cheap; redistributing wealth, institutional power and educational access is expensive. The UCL reparations scholarship programme is genuine but modest relative to the wealth absorbed from slave-trade compensation; the BBC's anti-racism plan does not change media ownership; the corporate DEI bonanza did not change racial-wealth-gap data. Morrison's account of othering ("rendering the slave a foreign species appears to be a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal," Morrison, 2017: 29) has an inverted twin in 2020s anti-racism: the performance of anti-racism can itself confirm the institution's normal self-image as fair, just by another route.

Second, the 2020s anti-racism produced a sharp counter-reaction that pulled in the opposite direction. The US anti-CRT panic of 2021-2024 (Florida's 2022 Stop WOKE Act; multiple state bans on classroom discussion of structural racism), the 2023 SFFA v Harvard reversal of affirmative action, the UK Sewell Report (2021) explicitly denying institutional racism, the Conservative government's 2022 abandonment of the Race Disparity Unit, and the Suella Braverman-era rhetoric on "small boats"; each was a calibrated rollback. Bell's interest convergence thesis predicted exactly this: when the white-majority interest in anti-racism wavers, the gains contract. By 2024-2025 much of the 2020 corporate DEI commitment had been quietly retired.

Third, the structure-not-individuals issue. Macpherson's 1999 definition of institutional racism ("the collective failure of an organisation to provide an appropriate and professional service to people because of their colour, culture, or ethnic origin") addressed institutional structure. Much 2020s anti-racism focused on individual training (unconscious bias courses, sensitivity sessions) without changing institutional structure. The UK National Audit Office (2021) found unconscious-bias training largely ineffective; the Cabinet Office cancelled mandatory training in 2020. The civilising mission also focused on individuals (refashioning native subjects) without dismantling the structure of colonial extraction.

I now make the comparison explicit. The civilising mission and the 2020s anti-racism common sense are very different in intent and effect. The civilising mission rationalised colonial extraction; the 2020s anti-racism is, in large part, a sincere attempt to remedy racial harm. But they share three structural features: both are projects largely framed and led by the dominant group; both centre symbolic and individual remediation over structural redistribution; and both are vulnerable to reabsorption by the racial order they nominally challenge.

The differences matter as much as the similarities. The civilising mission had no Black self-direction; the 2020s anti-racism is shaped substantially by Black, Asian and minority-ethnic intellectuals, activists and writers. The civilising mission's beneficiaries were the imperial powers; the 2020s anti-racism's beneficiaries include the racialised. The civilising mission rested on explicit racial hierarchy; the 2020s anti-racism explicitly disavows hierarchy. Treating them as equivalent would be politically and ethically wrong.

But the structural overlap is worth naming because it predicts the rollback. The 2024-2025 retreat from DEI in US corporations, the post-2022 conservative-populist resurgence in UK and US politics, the legal reversals in affirmative action and voting rights, and the rhetoric that the 2020s "went too far" all suggest that the anti-racism common sense was permitted by an interest convergence (Bell) that has now waned. The civilising mission lasted as long as it served imperial interests; the 2020s anti-racism lasted as long as it served corporate and political interests. The gains will persist only where they have been institutionalised at a level deeper than the affective.

I bring in concrete examples. The Empire Windrush 75th anniversary (2023) produced major UK cultural acknowledgment of Caribbean migration; the 2018 Windrush scandal continues to under-compensate victims. The 2020 toppling of Colston's statue produced public reckoning; the Bristol replacement projects have been contentious. The 2020-2021 wave of US police-reform pledges saw most cities increase rather than reduce police budgets by 2024. UCL's reparations programme is real but modest. Black maternal mortality (3-4 times the white rate) persists in the UK and US despite policy attention; UK Stop & Search disparities (7-9 times more often) are unchanged. The structural disparities remain where the symbolic remediations advanced.

I conclude that the civilising mission and the 2020s anti-racism common sense are not equivalents but cousins, projects of the dominant racial order that nominally improve the position of the racialised while leaving the underlying structure mostly intact. The civilising mission was a cover for colonial extraction; the 2020s anti-racism is a genuine but partial moral reform that has run into the limits Bell, Du Bois and Morrison would predict. The pay-off lies in distinguishing structural redistribution from symbolic remediation: anti-racism that changes who owns what, who runs what and who decides what is harder than anti-racism that changes who speaks and what we name. Du Bois's warning that "Equality in law is not enough" remains the standard against which any anti-racism common sense should be measured.
