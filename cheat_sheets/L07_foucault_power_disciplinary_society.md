# L7 - Foucault on Power and Disciplinary Society

## 1. Lecture Overview

Lecture 7 marks a decisive pivot in SC301: from classical models of power as ideology, repression, or class domination toward Foucault's far more subtle and pervasive account of power as something that operates through everyday practices, knowledges, institutions, and the very formation of the subject. The lecturer opens with Sarah Kember's example of obstetric ultrasound, the way scanning "eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998), to dramatise the central problem: how can we understand what is happening here? An ideology critique (the doctor as agent of patriarchy) captures part of the story but, as Foucault puts it, "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

The lecture's spine is the contrast between **sovereign power** and **disciplinary power**. Sovereign power is the spectacular, corporal, top-down power of the pre-modern monarch, illustrated by the grotesque public execution of Damiens the regicide that opens *Discipline and Punish*. Modern power, since the nineteenth century, looks different: it is dispersed, capillary, productive rather than purely repressive, and works through surveillance, examination, and normalisation rather than through the visible application of force. Bentham's panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, becomes the architectural figure of this new regime: a "machine for dissociating the see/being seen dyad" that produces, in the inmate, "a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader).

Three aspects of discipline are unpacked: **hierarchical observation** (we are watched from positions we cannot see); **normalising judgement** (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal"); and **examination** (the combination of observation and judgement in documented, testable form, such as registers, progress reports, key stages, transcripts). The aim and product of this triad is the **docile body**: passive, self-regulating, useful to capital and to the state.

The body and subjectivity sit at the centre of the second half of the lecture. Susan Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" (1993) extends Foucault into feminist territory: norms of slenderness, tone, control, and self-mastery shape bodies, particularly women's, and produce subjects "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). Judith Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* pushes further: power is "what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Power forms the subject; we cannot stand outside it.

Behind all of this stands Foucault's polemic against traditional sociological theories of power: liberal (power as state institutions), psychoanalytic (power as rule/law), and Marxist (power as class domination via ideology). Foucault's complaint is consistent: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). His project is to look at **how** power operates, at its capillary, micro-physical level, rather than asking only **who** holds it. Biopower extends this analysis from individual bodies to the management of populations (birth rates, mortality, public health), preparing the ground for later lectures on sexuality, race, and governmentality.

## 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Sovereign power**: pre-modern, spectacular, top-down power that operates through visible force, corporal punishment, and the abuse of the body. Concentrated in a person (the king) and exercised through edict and execution.
- **Disciplinary power**: modern, dispersed, capillary power that works through training, observation, examination, and normalisation. Subtle, productive, everywhere.
- **The panopticon**: Jeremy Bentham's architectural design (central tower, back-lit ring of cells) that allows constant, asymmetric visibility. Foucault treats it as the diagram of disciplinary modernity.
- **Surveillance**: continuous observation that need not be actual to be effective; the possibility of being watched is enough to produce self-regulation.
- **Hierarchical observation**: monitoring conducted from positions of invisibility, with observers themselves unobserved.
- **Normalising judgement**: measurement of individuals against an invented standard of "the normal"; the nineteenth-century invention of "the normal" is "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (lecture slide).
- **Examination**: the ritual combination of observation and judgement that produces documented, comparable knowledge of individuals (transcripts, medical records, performance reviews).
- **Docile bodies**: passive, self-regulating, useful bodies produced by disciplinary techniques; bodies that fit the modern capitalist economy.
- **Power/knowledge**: power and knowledge are inseparable; the production of knowledge (psychiatry, criminology, demography) is itself an exercise of power, and power produces knowledge.
- **Productive power**: power is not only repressive; it produces subjects, bodies, types of people, institutions, and forms of conduct.
- **Biopower**: power exercised at the level of populations (birth rates, mortality, public health, demographics), managing life rather than threatening death.
- **Governmentality**: the rationalities and techniques through which populations are governed (cross-applied from related lectures).
- **The subject**: the modern individual as both subjected to power and constituted as a subject by power.
- **Regimes of truth**: historically specific configurations of what counts as true and how truth is produced.
- **The disciplinary society**: contemporary society understood as saturated by the techniques of discipline that emerged in the nineteenth century, including schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces.
- **Force relations**: Foucault's basic micro-unit of power; relations that push, urge, or compel, immanent in social interactions (Lynch, 2011).
- **Capillary power**: power that operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies and conduct.

## 6. Common Essay Pitfalls

- Treating Foucault as if he simply said "power is bad" or "everything is power"; both miss the productive, omnipresent-but-not-totalising character of his analysis.
- Conflating disciplinary power with sovereign power; failing to mark the historical shift Foucault traces from spectacular punishment to surveillance.
- Reducing the panopticon to "being watched" without explaining the **asymmetry** (visible/unverifiable) and the **internalisation** that produces self-regulation.
- Forgetting that power is **productive**: it creates subjects, knowledges, and types of people, not just constraints.
- Treating biopower and disciplinary power as synonyms. Disciplinary power targets the individual body; biopower targets the population. They overlap but are not identical.
- Ignoring resistance. Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance", so making him sound more deterministic than he is.
- Failing to engage critics: Marxists (Poulantzas) accuse him of dissolving class; feminists (Hartsock, McNay) ask whether his account leaves room for agency; Habermas accuses him of relativism.
- Using only one example (the prison) when the lecture and reading insist that disciplinary power runs through schools, hospitals, workplaces, families, and bodies.
- Quoting "panopticon" without naming Bentham, or quoting Bordo/Butler without anchoring them to Foucault.
- Reading "docile bodies" as universal slavery rather than as a tendency produced by specific institutions and techniques.
- Treating Foucault as a "postmodernist" who denies truth; he writes about regimes of truth, not the abolition of truth.
- Forgetting the lecture's opening: ideology is not wrong, but it is "much more and much less" than what Foucault wants to analyse.

## 7. Past-Year Exam Questions and Answers

### 2024 (Q7): How does Foucault conceptualise disciplinary power? Why is this conceptualisation important?

Foucault's notion of disciplinary power names the form of power that emerged in Western societies from roughly the late eighteenth century onwards and that, he argues, came to displace the older logic of sovereign power. Sovereign power, as he reconstructs it in the opening of *Discipline and Punish*, was concentrated in the person of the monarch, exercised through visible force, and dramatised in spectacular rituals of corporal and capital punishment; the slow public execution of Damiens for regicide is its emblem. Disciplinary power, by contrast, is "subtle", "everywhere", and works not by stopping people from doing things but by training them into doing certain things. As Foucault puts it, this is "a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets; it is a 'physics' or an 'anatomy' of power" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in the Calhoun reader).

I would isolate four features that together make up Foucault's conceptualisation. First, disciplinary power is **capillary**: it operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies, timetables, classrooms, examinations, queues, and records. The school register, the progress report, the medical chart, the HR review: these are its instruments. Second, it is **productive**: it does not merely repress but produces new kinds of subjects (the modern prisoner, the schoolchild, the patient, the worker) through new kinds of institutions. Third, it works through three linked techniques: **hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and the examination**. We are observed from positions we cannot see, measured against an invented standard of "the normal", and tested and documented in rituals that fuse observation and judgement. Fourth, it produces **docile bodies**: bodies that are useful, obedient, and crucially, **self-regulating**, because the subject internalises the gaze.

The architectural diagram of this regime is Bentham's panopticon. Foucault writes that its "major effect" is "to induce in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315). Because the inmate cannot verify when they are being watched, they behave as if they always are. Visibility, as Foucault summarises, is a trap. Crucially, the panopticon is not just a prison design but a model that "infiltrates" schools, hospitals, factories, and the police, what Foucault calls the disciplinary society.

Why is this conceptualisation important? I think it matters for at least four reasons. First, it reorients sociology away from the question "who holds power?" and toward the question "how does power operate?" That methodological shift is huge: it lets us examine an Amazon warehouse, a school inspection, or a Fitbit dashboard without first reducing them to the will of a ruling class or a sovereign. Second, it explains the otherwise puzzling fact that modern liberal societies, despite claiming to expand freedom, generate ever more intensive forms of conduct-shaping. Disciplinary power does not contradict liberal freedom; it is its underside. Third, it identifies the link between knowledge and power, what Foucault calls power/knowledge. Psychiatry, criminology, demography, and pedagogy are not innocent sciences but apparatuses that produce the very subjects they claim to describe. Fourth, it opens space for politics at the micro-level: the school examination, the CCTV camera, the credit score, the social media algorithm all become legitimate sites of political contestation.

The conceptualisation also has limitations I want to acknowledge. Critics have charged that Foucault dissolves class and gender into a generalised analytic of conduct, leaving us without the tools to identify durable structural inequalities. Nancy Fraser and others have asked whether resistance becomes intelligible if power is everywhere. Marxists like Nicos Poulantzas have argued that disciplinary power names a real phenomenon but cannot replace political economy. I find these criticisms partially convincing; Foucault's account works best as a supplement to, rather than a substitute for, structural analyses. But the supplement is essential. We cannot understand Foxconn worker surveillance, the medicalisation of ADHD, or China's Social Credit System without his vocabulary. The British school's regime of registers, key stages, and SATs is unintelligible if we have only "the ruling class" as our analytic.

Disciplinary power, then, names not a thing but a mode, a way power has worked in modern societies. Its importance lies in how it allows us to see, in the most mundane practices (clocking in at work, scanning a passport, posting on Instagram), the political form of our present. As Foucault writes, this is "a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212), and that double sense, subjected to and constituted as, is the productive heart of his concept.

### 2024 (Q8): "Contemporary societies are biopolitical." Critically analyse this statement.

The claim that contemporary societies are biopolitical asks us to extend Foucault's analysis of power from the disciplined individual body to the managed population. Foucault's account of biopower in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* and the lectures collected as *Society Must Be Defended* identifies a shift, dating from roughly the late eighteenth century, in which power is no longer principally exercised as the sovereign right to "take life or let live" but as a positive technology of "making live", fostering, optimising, and managing the vital processes of populations. Where disciplinary power targets the individual body through schools and prisons, biopower targets the species body through statistics, public health, demography, vaccination, and insurance.

I want to argue that the statement is largely defensible (contemporary societies are indeed extensively biopolitical) but only with three qualifications: biopolitics does not replace disciplinary or sovereign power but layers onto them; biopolitics is uneven across populations and geographies; and its operations are intensely contested.

First, the affirmative case. Public health regimes provide some of the clearest examples. The UK's vaccination programme treats the population as the unit of intervention; herd immunity is a biopolitical concept par excellence. The NHS Test and Trace system during COVID-19, the mass roll-out of vaccines by demographic priority groups, and the statistical apparatus of the Office for National Statistics during the pandemic are all instances of what Foucault would recognise as biopower. The same logic governs statins prescribed at population level: nobody decides this individual must take a statin because they will die otherwise; the population's cardiovascular risk is modelled and intervention targets are set. Fertility policy in countries from Hungary to Singapore, contraception campaigns, school meal regulation, sugar taxes, smoking bans, these too are biopolitical. Foucault's prescient observation that "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order...but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215) captures the long arc.

Second, biopower in the twenty-first century has acquired new technical capacities. Genomic medicine and biomarkers extend biopolitical management into molecular bodies. Algorithmic predictive systems (used in policing, social services, credit) sort populations by risk in ways that recall what Foucault called normalising judgement at population scale. China's Social Credit System makes biopolitical management of conduct explicit and computational. The Cambridge Analytica scandal showed that political campaigns themselves now operate biopolitically, segmenting populations into psychographic clusters.

Third, the biopolitical is gendered, raced, and classed. Biopower, Foucault writes, does not work on an undifferentiated population but produces and acts upon stratifications. The HIV/AIDS response of the 1980s, and the disastrous neglect of gay men, sex workers, drug users, and African populations, is a textbook case of biopower's selectivity. Achille Mbembe's concept of necropolitics extends Foucault to the production of populations who are "let die": Gaza, the Mediterranean migrant crossings, Black mortality in the US. Foetal ultrasound and reproductive medicine, as Sarah Kember argued, make women's bodies biopolitically visible while paradoxically "eliminat[ing] the mother's body from view" (Kember, 1998).

Now to qualifications. The statement risks overgeneralisation if read as "contemporary societies are *only* biopolitical." Foucault is clear that biopower coexists with disciplinary power and even with residual sovereign power. The US death penalty, drone strikes, and the violent policing of Black bodies show that sovereign right has not vanished. Inside a school, disciplinary techniques (registers, exams) operate alongside biopolitical ones (national obesity surveys). At Foxconn, individual disciplinary surveillance and population-level health metrics work together. So the statement is best read additively: contemporary societies are biopolitical *as well as* disciplinary and partially sovereign.

The second qualification concerns resistance and contestation. If biopower were total, the anti-vax movement, the body positivity movement, fat liberation, queer activism on HIV criminalisation, and Indigenous data sovereignty struggles would be unintelligible. Foucault himself insists that "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Biopolitics provokes biopolitical counter-movements, sometimes reactionary (anti-vaxxers), sometimes liberatory (HIV/AIDS activism by ACT UP).

The third qualification is methodological. Critics (Mitchell Dean, Thomas Lemke, the governmentality school) have argued that biopolitics names many different rationalities, not one. Neoliberal biopolitics (responsibilising individuals for their own risk through Fitbits and private insurance) differs from welfarist biopolitics (mid-century public health). Both are biopolitical, but they cannot be conflated.

I find the statement convincing on balance. Contemporary societies are saturated by biopolitical rationalities, from the COVID app on your phone to the actuarial tables underwriting your mortgage. But "saturated" does not mean "exhausted by". Biopolitics is one rationality among several, it is unevenly distributed, and it is constantly resisted. Foucault's concept is most useful when it sharpens our perception of these uneven, contested politics of life, rather than when it is wielded as a totalising diagnosis.

### 2025 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing do you find his arguments?

Foucault's challenge to traditional sociological approaches to power is set out polemically in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* and elaborated in *Discipline and Punish* and the interviews collected as *Power/Knowledge*. Traditional sociology, on Foucault's reading, has tended to think about power in three problematic ways: liberal accounts treat power as a possession of the state and its institutions; psychoanalytic and juridical accounts treat power as rule and prohibition; and Marxist accounts treat power as class domination operating through ideology. Foucault's complaint, as Richard Lynch summarises, is that each of these "mischaracterize an accidental feature of power in a particular context as an essential characteristic of power in general" (Lynch, 2011 [VERIFY PAGE?]).

The lecture frames the challenge sharply. Liberal/Enlightenment thinkers, including first-wave feminists, treat power as a thing, possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom, and therefore something to be seized, shared, or constrained. Marxists treat power as flowing from ownership of the means of production, concentrated in the bourgeoisie, and exercised over the proletariat through ideology. Foucault's response to the latter is biting: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). What is missing in all three traditions is an account of **how** power operates, its mechanics, its techniques, its capillary distribution.

Foucault's positive counter-proposal has at least six distinctive moves. First, **power is dispersed, not concentrated**. It does not sit at the top of a pyramid but circulates through micro-relations of force throughout the social body. Second, **power is productive, not (only) repressive**. It produces subjects, knowledges, types of people. Third, **power and knowledge are inseparable**: there is no neutral science of human beings, and the human sciences (psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy, sexology) are themselves apparatuses of power. Fourth, **power operates through everyday practices**: timetables, examinations, medical scans, performance reviews, not principally through the state and the law. Fifth, **modern power forms the subject** rather than merely constraining a pre-existing subject. As Foucault writes, it "makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212). Sixth, **resistance is internal to power**: "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95).

The pay-off is methodological. We should not ask "who has power?" but "how is power exercised?" We should look at the panopticon, the school timetable, the medical chart, the credit score, not only at parliament and capital.

How convincing do I find these arguments? Largely, yes, but with reservations.

On the convincing side: Foucault's account illuminates phenomena that traditional sociology struggles to explain. The Marxist asks who benefits from CCTV; Foucault asks how CCTV trains us to behave as if always watched. The liberal asks whether the state has overstepped; Foucault asks how Fitbit and Apple Watch enrol us in our own self-management. The school inspector regime, NHS digital records, Amazon warehouse algorithms, the medicalisation of ADHD: none of these are well captured by "the ruling class wants this." All are well captured by Foucault's vocabulary of normalising judgement, hierarchical observation, and examination. I find the productive thesis especially convincing: when psychiatry invents "the homosexual" as a kind of person, when criminology invents "the delinquent", when pedagogy invents "the gifted child", power is not just constraining behaviour, it is producing identities.

The challenge to ideology critique is also persuasive. The lecture's opening example, Kember on obstetric ultrasound, shows why. Calling the doctor "ideological" is not exactly wrong, but it misses how the technology itself, the scanning practice, the protocols, organise women's bodies and knowledge of foetuses in ways that exceed any individual doctor's intent. "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

The reservations. First, Foucault's analysis can be hard to operationalise against concentrated, sovereign power, such as Putin's Russia, the death penalty, drone warfare. The capillary model risks underplaying the brutal and very visible exercises of power that have not disappeared. Second, feminist critics including Nancy Hartsock and Lois McNay have argued that if power constitutes the subject, it becomes hard to ground feminist agency in anything other than power itself. Butler's response (in *The Psychic Life of Power*) accepts this but reframes agency as immanent rather than oppositional. Third, Marxist critics like Nicos Poulantzas argue that Foucault sees the techniques but loses the structure; he sees Amazon warehouse surveillance but loses sight of the capitalist logic that organises it. Fourth, Habermas accuses Foucault of relativism: if there are only regimes of truth, on what basis do we criticise? I find this critique less compelling because Foucault is not claiming truth does not exist, only that truth has a history and a politics.

On balance, then, I find Foucault's challenge highly convincing as a supplement to traditional approaches but less so as a replacement. The most fruitful contemporary sociology, Wendy Brown's work on neoliberalism, Achille Mbembe's necropolitics, Shoshana Zuboff's surveillance capitalism, combines Foucauldian analysis of mechanism with Marxist or feminist analysis of structure. That hybrid is precisely what Foucault's challenge made possible. To ask "how" without abandoning "who benefits" seems to me the best legacy of his intervention.

### 2023 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing?

Foucault's intervention into the sociology of power was, at root, a question about method. Traditional sociology had asked **who** holds power: the state, the ruling class, the patriarch, the king. Foucault wanted to ask **how** power operates. As he insisted, "we need to look at HOW power operates" (lecture slide paraphrasing Foucault, 1980). The shift sounds modest but reorganises the entire field of analysis.

I will set out Foucault's challenge under three headings (what he rejects, what he proposes, and what follows) and then assess how convincing it is.

What he rejects. Foucault's foils, identified clearly in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1*, are three. The **liberal/juridical** model treats power as a property of the state, exercised through law and prohibition, dangerous because it limits a pre-existing free individual. The **psychoanalytic** model treats power as rule, the form of the law within the subject. The **Marxist** model treats power as class domination, operating through ideology, with the bourgeoisie at the top of a pyramid. All three, Foucault argues, share what he calls a "juridico-discursive" representation: they understand power as negative, prohibitive, top-down, and uniform. All three mistake "terminal forms" of power (the state, the law, class domination) for its essence. None analyses the **mechanics** by which power is exercised. The Marxist version receives Foucault's sharpest rebuke: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116).

What he proposes. Foucault counters with what he calls an "analytics of power". Power is a "multiplicity of force relations immanent in the sphere in which they operate" (Foucault, 1978: 92). Several propositions follow. Power is exercised, not possessed. Power comes from below, from the micro-relations of everyday life, and produces, through aggregation, the larger structures we recognise as states and hegemonies. Power is intentional but non-subjective: tactics have aims, but no single agent controls the overall effect. Power is omnipresent ("always already there") and so resistance is internal to it. Disciplinary power and biopower are the specifically modern forms of this generalised mechanics. The panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, is the architectural diagram. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). Power is "much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

What follows. Methodologically, sociology should examine the school register, the medical chart, the CCTV camera, the Fitbit, the credit score, the performance review: the everyday techniques through which conduct is shaped. The human sciences themselves (psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy) should be treated not as innocent knowledge but as part of the apparatus of power/knowledge. The subject is not the starting point but the product of power.

How convincing? I find this challenge largely convincing, with three positive points and two reservations.

The first positive: the empirical reach is remarkable. The lecture lists schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces, the regulation of bodies, the rise of gym membership in the City of London, the slimming industry, and these can be extended to contemporary cases the lecturer did not need to mention. Amazon warehouse surveillance, the British university's REF and TEF, the NHS Performance Indicators, the rise of Fitbit and Apple Watch, the medicalisation of ADHD: none are well explained by a top-down model of power. The Foucauldian vocabulary fits them.

The second positive: the productive thesis is genuinely original. Power produces kinds of people, kinds of knowledge, kinds of bodies. The "homosexual", in the famous formulation, became a "personage" in the late nineteenth century not because there were no same-sex acts before but because medical and psychiatric discourses produced a new kind of subject. This was something traditional sociology genuinely missed.

The third positive: the linkage of knowledge and power answers a real problem. Why are some sciences trusted, others not? Why does the diagnosis of "borderline personality disorder" or "anti-social personality disorder" disproportionately fall on certain populations? Power/knowledge supplies a framework.

The reservations. First, the diffuse model can underplay concentrated power. Wars, mass incarceration, drone strikes, billionaire wealth: these are real, and a Foucauldian analysis must work harder to capture them than a Marxist one. Second, the question of agency. If "power forms the subject" (Butler, 1997: 1), what grounds resistance? Foucault answers that resistance is internal to power, but critics like Nancy Fraser argue this is unsatisfying; we want to know whose resistance counts as emancipatory and whose does not.

My overall verdict: Foucault's challenge is convincing as a transformation of what sociology can see, less convincing as a complete replacement for traditional accounts. The best contemporary work (Wendy Brown, Mbembe, Zuboff) combines Foucauldian micro-analysis with structural critique. Foucault expanded the toolkit; he did not (and probably did not intend to) close it.

### 2022 (Q10): Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as "disciplinary". What did he mean by this? How useful do you find this term?

When Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as disciplinary, he meant something quite specific: a historically new mode of power, emerging in Western societies in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, that operates through training rather than force, through surveillance rather than spectacle, through normalisation rather than prohibition, and that produces subjects who regulate themselves.

I want to set out what he meant under five headings, then assess the term's usefulness.

First, **disciplinary power is contrasted with sovereign power**. The opening of *Discipline and Punish* describes the gruesome public execution of Damiens the regicide in 1757, a spectacle of corporal punishment that displayed the king's power over the criminal's body. Within decades, this had largely vanished; in its place came the prison timetable, the school bell, the hospital ward round. The shift, Foucault argues, is not from cruelty to humanity but from one logic of power to another. Sovereign power was concentrated, visible, intermittent, and worked by abusing the body. Disciplinary power is dispersed, invisible, continuous, and works by training the body.

Second, **disciplinary power is capillary**. It operates not principally through the state but through everyday institutions (schools, hospitals, factories, asylums, families, barracks). As Foucault writes, "discipline may be identified neither with an institution nor with an apparatus; it is a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). Schools deploy registers, progress reports, school photos, key stages, exams; workplaces deploy clock-cards, KPIs, performance reviews; medicine deploys charts, scans, screenings.

Third, **disciplinary power works through three techniques** the lecture isolates: hierarchical observation (we are watched from positions of asymmetric invisibility), normalising judgement (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal", itself a nineteenth-century invention), and the examination (the documented combination of the two).

Fourth, **its architectural figure is the panopticon**. Bentham's prison design, with its central tower and back-lit cells, achieves "the major effect" of inducing "in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). Because the inmate cannot tell when they are being watched, they internalise the gaze.

Fifth, **its product is the docile body**: useful, obedient, self-regulating. Such bodies, Foucault notes in the lecture's account, fit well into factories and offices. They "question little and behave well." They do not need to be coerced because they have been trained.

How useful do I find the term? Very useful, for four reasons, but with three limits.

Useful, first, because it names something traditional sociology missed. Marxist theories of ideology and liberal theories of state power cannot adequately describe the school bell, the Ofsted inspection, the Fitbit, the airport security queue, or the LinkedIn endorsement. All these are disciplinary in Foucault's sense.

Useful, second, because it captures contemporary intensifications. Amazon warehouse algorithmic management, Foxconn worker surveillance, Uber driver ratings, university league tables, NHS waiting-time targets: these are recognisable, even paradigmatic, disciplinary phenomena. The TSA body scanner at airports, CCTV in UK cities, COVID-19 contact-tracing apps: these are panoptic in Bentham's exact sense, made unverifiable by their ubiquity.

Useful, third, because it illuminates how we participate in our own discipline. The Fitbit user, the calorie counter, the LinkedIn poster, the Strava cyclist: these are not coerced. They monitor themselves. As Bordo writes about body discipline, "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Useful, fourth, because it provides a vocabulary for resistance. If discipline runs through everyday techniques, then everyday techniques are sites of contestation: refusing the Fitbit, opting out of the optional standardised test, breaking the Amazon warehouse rhythm.

Limits. First, the term can homogenise. The disciplinary techniques of a Victorian workhouse are not the same as those of a contemporary Korean cram school; lumping them risks losing historical specificity. Second, disciplinary power does not exhaust contemporary power. As Foucault himself recognised, biopower (population-level management) and sovereign power (still operative in war, capital punishment, border violence) coexist with discipline. The student of Foxconn must mobilise all three. Third, the term has been popularised to the point of becoming a slogan; "disciplinary society" sometimes covers everything anyone disapproves of.

My overall view: the term remains one of the most productive in twentieth-century social theory, but it is most useful when wielded with historical precision and combined with other analytics. Contemporary society is partly disciplinary, partly biopolitical, partly sovereign, and Foucault's term captures one essential strand among these.

### 2022 (Q11): How useful is Foucault's notion of biopower for understanding contemporary society?

Foucault's notion of biopower, introduced in the final chapter of *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* and developed in the lecture series *Society Must Be Defended* and *Security, Territory, Population* (Foucault, 2007), names a form of power that operates not on individual bodies, as discipline does, but on populations as biological collectives. Where the sovereign exercised the right to "take life or let live", biopower exercises the right to "make live and let die": fostering, optimising, and managing the vital processes of populations through statistics, demography, public health, and the regulation of birth and death rates.

I will argue that the concept is highly useful for understanding contemporary society but with three caveats: it must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power; its applications vary historically and politically; and it requires updating for digital and algorithmic contexts.

On the affirmative side. Biopower illuminates a vast range of contemporary phenomena.

Public health and epidemiology are obvious cases. The COVID-19 pandemic was governed biopolitically from start to finish: R numbers, vaccination roll-outs by demographic priority, contact-tracing apps, school closures by infection rate, "vulnerable" populations identified by NHS data. The British state did not punish individuals; it modelled and managed populations. The same holds for routine vaccination programmes, antenatal screening, sugar taxes, the public health framing of obesity, and the prescription of statins to large segments of the over-fifties to manage cardiovascular risk at population level.

Reproductive politics is biopolitical par excellence. Foetal ultrasound, prenatal genetic screening, IVF regulation, fertility incentives in Hungary or Singapore, abortion law in post-Dobbs America, contraception campaigns in the Global South: all manage life at the level of the species. Sarah Kember's observation that "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view" (Kember, 1998) captures how biopolitical visibility can render some bodies hyper-visible (the foetus) while erasing others (the mother).

Insurance and risk management are biopolitical. Actuarial tables, credit scoring, predictive policing, the algorithmic sorting of populations into risk categories for mortgages, jobs, and parole are all biopolitical operations. Cambridge Analytica and the segmentation of voters into psychographic clusters are biopolitical operations on the political subject.

Border and migration regimes are biopolitical, sometimes sliding into what Achille Mbembe calls necropolitics. The Mediterranean migrant route, the US-Mexico border, the systematic abandonment of HIV-positive populations in 1980s and 90s America, Gaza: these are biopolitical decisions about whose lives are fostered and whose are let die.

The Foucauldian observation that "the police force was...invented...for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215) generalises: contemporary policing, urban planning, environmental regulation, even climate policy involve biopolitical rationalities.

Now the caveats.

First, biopower does not stand alone. The Amazon warehouse worker is governed disciplinarily (clock-in, scanner, rate metrics) **and** biopolitically (worker health insurance pooled and managed by algorithm) **and** sovereignly (when the legal system criminalises labour organising). Conflating these flattens the analysis. Mitchell Dean's *Governmentality* (1999) usefully distinguishes the different rationalities in play.

Second, biopolitics has changed over time. Mid-twentieth-century welfare biopolitics (the NHS, mass public housing, universal child allowance) operated according to a logic of solidaristic risk-pooling. Neoliberal biopolitics from the 1980s onwards responsibilises the individual for their own risk: you wear the Fitbit, you take out the private health insurance, you optimise your sleep with the Apple Watch. Both are biopolitical, but politically opposed.

Third, digital biopolitics requires new vocabulary. Shoshana Zuboff's *Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) describes a regime where private platforms (Google, Meta, Amazon) accumulate behavioural data at population scale and trade it as a commodity. This is biopolitical but routed through markets rather than states. Wendy Chun, Safiya Noble, and others have argued that algorithmic systems are biopolitical apparatuses with distinctive racialised and gendered effects (predictive policing falls disproportionately on Black communities; medical algorithms underestimate Black women's pain).

A final qualification: biopower is resisted. The HIV/AIDS activism of ACT UP, the body positivity movement, fat liberation, anti-vaccine movements (whatever one thinks of their politics), Indigenous data sovereignty struggles, queer organising against HIV criminalisation: these are biopolitical counter-movements. As Foucault writes, "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95).

My verdict: biopower is one of the most useful concepts contemporary social theory has. It names something we would otherwise miss, the politics of life itself. But its usefulness depends on careful articulation with disciplinary and sovereign forms, with attention to historical change, and with updates for digital and algorithmic contexts. Used carelessly, it explains too much; used carefully, it explains exactly what other vocabularies cannot.

### 2021 (Q10): Do we live in a "disciplinary society"? Use the work of Foucault to explain your response.

We do live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's specific sense, though we also live in a biopolitical and partially sovereign one. My answer is "yes, but not exclusively", and the qualification matters.

To answer the question I need first to clarify what Foucault means by a disciplinary society. The concept is articulated in *Discipline and Punish* and refers, as the Calhoun reader extract makes clear, to "the formation of a disciplinary society in this movement that stretches from the enclosed disciplines, a sort of social 'quarantine', to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316). The disciplinary society is one in which the techniques first developed within enclosed institutions (prisons, barracks, hospitals, schools) generalise across the social field, becoming the dominant mode through which conduct is shaped. The crucial features, set out in the lecture, are: power operates through everyday practices, not principally through state edict; it works by surveillance, normalisation, and examination rather than by physical coercion; it produces docile bodies that regulate themselves against invented norms; and it is productive (generating subjects, knowledges, and institutions), not merely repressive.

Now the affirmative case for the contemporary UK and similar societies.

Schools first. The British school, my own and probably the marker's, is a near-perfect Foucauldian institution. Registers, the school timetable, school photos, progress reports, key stages, SATs, GCSEs, A-levels, Ofsted inspections, parents' evenings, league tables: every one of these is an instrument of hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and examination. The pupil is observed continually, measured against age-appropriate norms, and documented in records that follow them through life. "This will go on your permanent record" is the disciplinary motto.

The workplace. HR performance reviews, 360-degree feedback, KPIs, time-sheets, productivity software (Hubstaff, Time Doctor), Slack message audits, Amazon warehouse handheld scanners that track pickers by the second: all disciplinary. Foxconn's worker surveillance, Uber's driver ratings, the gig economy's algorithmic management: all disciplinary.

Public space. CCTV in UK cities (Britain has among the highest CCTV density in the world), automatic number plate recognition, facial recognition trials by the Metropolitan Police, TSA-style body scanners at airports. These are panoptic in Bentham's exact sense: visible (we see the cameras) but unverifiable (we never know whether we are being watched on any given screen).

The body. Fitbit, Apple Watch, MyFitnessPal, Strava, sleep-tracking apps, period-tracking apps, mood-tracking apps. Susan Bordo's analysis of the slender body is now everywhere: "Self-management in consumer culture...becomes more elusive as it becomes more pressing...the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). The lecture notes the rise of gym membership in the City of London as a sign of professional self-mastery; that observation has only intensified.

Knowledge and expertise. The medicalisation of mental health, the rise of ADHD diagnoses, the DSM as a documenter of normal and pathological: these are power/knowledge in operation. Psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy, and social work are not innocent sciences; they produce the subjects they describe.

The credit score. A single normalised number, generated by hierarchical observation of every transaction, determines access to housing, employment, and loans. China's Social Credit System makes the logic explicit and universal.

So yes, by every test Foucault would apply, contemporary Britain is a disciplinary society. The lecture's claim that "contemporary society is a 'disciplinary society'" stands.

The qualifications. First, we also live in a biopolitical society. The COVID-19 response, the NHS, vaccination programmes, statins, sugar taxes, climate policy: these target populations, not individuals, and operate by a different (though related) logic. Disciplinary techniques work on bodies; biopolitical techniques work on populations. Both are present.

Second, sovereign power has not vanished. The British state still deports asylum seekers, imprisons, polices, sometimes lethally. The US executes prisoners. Drone strikes kill outside any disciplinary frame. The criminalisation of protest under the Public Order Act 2023 in the UK reasserts a sovereign capacity to forbid. Foucault was always clear that disciplinary power supplements, rather than abolishes, sovereign power.

Third, the disciplinary society is unevenly distributed. Working-class and racialised populations face more intensive surveillance: stop and search, predictive policing, school exclusion rates. The disciplinary society is itself a stratified one.

Fourth, resistance exists. Strikes against algorithmic management at Amazon and Uber, opt-out movements against school SATs, the Black Lives Matter response to predictive policing, anti-surveillance campaigns by groups like Big Brother Watch: these are disciplinary counter-movements. Foucault's insistence that "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95) remains operative.

My answer, then: we live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's sense, but the disciplinary register is one among several. The CCTV camera, the Fitbit, the school register, the credit score, the LinkedIn endorsement, the Ofsted report: all confirm Foucault's thesis. But the deportation flight, the COVID modelling, the welfare sanction: these require biopolitical and sovereign analyses alongside it. The right answer to the exam question is yes, with the right qualifications attached.

### 2021 (Q11): Outline and assess Foucault's discussions of power in terms of the governance of a population.

Foucault's discussions of the governance of a population centre on his concept of biopower, introduced in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (Foucault, 1978) and developed in the Collège de France lectures of the late 1970s, especially *Society Must Be Defended* and *Security, Territory, Population* (Foucault, 2007). Biopower names a form of power that takes the population, rather than the individual body, as its object. It complements the disciplinary power Foucault had analysed in *Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977), and together the two constitute what Foucault calls the "two poles" of modern power over life.

I will outline biopower in three steps, then assess it.

First, the historical claim. Foucault argues that from the late eighteenth century, a new form of power emerged alongside discipline. Where the sovereign had exercised the right to "take life or let live" (a negative, episodic, deductive power), modern states began to exercise the right to "make live and let die." Population becomes a problem of government: birth rates, mortality, fertility, longevity, public health, epidemics, hygiene, housing, food supply. The objects of this new power are statistical: not the individual body but the species body. The instruments are not the prison or the school but the census, the actuarial table, the epidemiological survey, the public health campaign, the urban plan.

Second, the conceptual structure. Biopower operates through what Foucault calls "regularising mechanisms": interventions that aim to optimise the vital processes of the population as a whole. Where disciplinary power individualises (each pupil has a transcript), biopower massifies (the cohort has a literacy rate). Where discipline aims at the docile and useful body, biopower aims at the secure and productive population. Knowledge here takes the form of statistics, demography, political economy, and epidemiology. Foucault's quip that "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order, nor for assisting governments in their struggle against their enemies, but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215) captures the genealogy: the modern "police" originally meant the management of urban life, of which crime control was only one piece.

Third, the link to governmentality. In his late lectures, Foucault generalises biopower into the broader concept of governmentality: the rationalities and techniques by which conduct is conducted. Modern liberal governmentality, Foucault argues, governs not by direct command but by structuring the field of choices so that individuals govern themselves. Neoliberal governmentality, in particular, responsibilises individuals for their own risks (private pensions, private insurance, Fitbit-style self-tracking) while treating populations as aggregates of human capital.

Now the assessment.

Strengths. Biopower captures something traditional theories miss. Mass public health, vaccination, sugar taxes, statins, contact tracing, NHS data analytics, the actuarial logic of insurance, predictive policing, credit scoring, COVID-era population-level interventions: none are well described by either "the ruling class wills it" or "the state forbids it." They are biopolitical: they take populations as objects and optimise them. The HIV/AIDS public health response of the 1980s, its targeting of risk groups, its failures around gay men, sex workers, and African populations, is unthinkable without biopolitical analysis. The reproductive politics of foetal ultrasound, IVF regulation, fertility incentives, and abortion law are biopolitical. So is climate policy, increasingly: carbon budgets, emissions per capita, the management of populations as ecological agents.

A second strength: biopower illuminates the dark side of modern humanitarianism. Mid-century welfare states cared for populations; eugenic movements managed them; the line between care and control is thin. The same statistical techniques that produce universal vaccination produce racial classification.

A third strength: biopower opens onto necropolitics. Achille Mbembe's extension of Foucault, biopower's "let die" pole, exercised at borders, in occupied territories, in slums, captures the violent underside of contemporary governance. Gaza, the Mediterranean, the abandonment of immunocompromised populations during late COVID: these are necropolitical operations.

Weaknesses. First, the concept can be over-extended. If everything that touches life is biopolitical, the term loses purchase. Some critics argue Foucault himself is partly responsible for this elasticity.

Second, biopower must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power. The Amazon warehouse worker faces all three. The COVID lockdown was biopolitical (modelling), disciplinary (police enforcement of household rules), and sovereign (criminalisation of breaches). To pick one is to miss the others.

Third, the concept is gendered and raced in ways Foucault did not fully develop. Feminist scholars like Penelope Deutscher and Jasbir Puar have shown how reproductive biopolitics operates differently across gendered and racialised populations. Black women in US obstetric care, working-class women in UK fertility services, migrant women in detention: biopower acts on them through specific mechanisms Foucault did not theorise.

Fourth, the question of resistance. If populations are constituted by biopower, what is the locus of biopolitical agency? Foucault's answer ("where there is power, there is resistance") is correct but thin. ACT UP, fat liberation, Indigenous data sovereignty, the right-to-die movement, abortion access campaigns: these are biopolitical counter-movements, but theorising them requires going beyond Foucault.

My assessment: Foucault's discussions of the governance of a population through biopower are essential to contemporary social theory but not sufficient. They illuminate what other vocabularies cannot, the politics of life itself, the statistical management of populations, the link between welfare and control. But they need supplementing with structural analyses of capital, gender, and race, with attention to historical change between welfarist and neoliberal biopolitics, and with updated frameworks for digital and algorithmic biopolitics (Zuboff, Noble, Chun). Used in this way, biopower remains one of the most powerful concepts in the toolkit.

## 8. Mock Exam Questions and Answers

### Mock Q1 (quote-driven): "Visibility is a trap." Discuss with reference to Foucault.

The line "visibility is a trap" appears in *Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader) at the moment Foucault is unpacking what Bentham's panopticon actually does. The trap is not that the inmate is seen (pre-modern sovereign power saw bodies too, in the spectacle of the scaffold) but that the inmate is rendered visible while the watcher is rendered invisible, so the inmate must assume continuous observation and behave accordingly even when no one is in the tower. My argument is that the line is a compressed theory of disciplinary power, and that taking it seriously means tracking how the trap operates in nineteenth-century institutions, in late-twentieth-century body discipline, and in the contemporary digital landscape, while also marking the points where the trap metaphor under-describes what is happening.

First, the original setting. Foucault uses the line to explain why Bentham's diagram induces "in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315). The trap has three components: asymmetric visibility (the inmate is seen but cannot see the watcher), the unverifiability of monitoring (one cannot check whether the tower is occupied), and the internalisation of the gaze (the inmate becomes her own watcher). The trap shuts when the inmate begins to self-regulate, at which point the central tower can in principle be empty. The school register, the exam invigilator, the ward round, and the factory foreman all share this logic. The trap is the automatic functioning of power.

Second, the body. Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" shows how the trap is gendered and extends beyond institutions into everyday self-relation. The norms of slenderness, tone, and control turn the bathroom mirror into a tower and the woman into her own watcher. "Bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) are the trap closed at the level of selfhood. The City of London banker on her treadmill before work, the teenage girl tracking calories on MyFitnessPal, the man checking his macro split on a Saturday: each has internalised the asymmetric visibility Foucault diagnoses.

Third, the digital landscape. CCTV across UK cities is the most literal panopticon: visible cameras, unverifiable monitoring, generalised self-regulation. Airport biometric scanners and automatic number plate recognition extend the trap into mobility. The Fitbit on my wrist is the trap miniaturised; I am inmate and tower simultaneously, and the device works regardless of whether I check the dashboard. Amazon warehouse handheld scanners and Foxconn floor surveillance use the trap industrially: pickers and assemblers self-regulate against rate metrics because they assume continuous observation even when no human supervisor is watching. The HR performance review and the university REF assessment do similar work for white-collar professionals. China's Social Credit System makes the trap explicit at population scale, but the British credit score (Experian, Equifax) achieves something similar in subtler form.

Fourth, the limits. The trap metaphor under-describes contemporary surveillance in four ways. (i) Much modern monitoring is data-driven rather than gaze-based; Shoshana Zuboff's surveillance capitalism in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) describes server farms, not towers. (ii) Algorithmic systems classify without anyone watching, as Safiya Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* (2018) shows for search ranking and Virginia Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* (2018) shows for welfare systems. (iii) Lateral exposure (TikTok, Instagram, doxxing, cancel campaigns) is synoptic rather than panoptic; the many watch the many, not the few watch the many. (iv) Biopolitical surveillance during COVID-19 contact-tracing aggregated bodies as a population, not as individuals to be normalised.

Fifth, the politics. The strength of the trap as a critical figure is that it captures the productive character of disciplinary power: the trap does not just constrain, it makes subjects. The school produces the pupil, the prison produces the delinquent, Strava produces the cyclist. The weakness is that the metaphor can flatten into a generalised cynicism. If every visibility is a trap, even reciprocal democratic visibility, then we lose the political distinction between accountability of power and surveillance of the powerless.

My argument is that "visibility is a trap" is correct as a description of the disciplinary mode, indispensable as a diagnosis of how self-regulation is engineered, and incomplete as a complete theory of contemporary observation. Read alongside biopower, surveillance capitalism, and Bordo's account of the gendered body, the trap remains one of the most useful images in social theory.

### Mock Q2 (named-work): Critically assess Foucault's argument in *Discipline and Punish* in light of contemporary platform workplaces.

*Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977) is built around a historical claim (that Western societies shifted from sovereign to disciplinary power over the long nineteenth century) and a diagnostic claim (that the techniques of the prison, articulated in the panopticon, became "an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader) that runs through schools, hospitals, factories, and armies). The question my essay tackles is whether the contemporary platform workplace (Amazon warehouses, Uber, Deliveroo, Foxconn, the call centre, the algorithmically managed white-collar Slack environment) vindicates, strains, or breaks Foucault's argument.

I argue that *Discipline and Punish* is largely vindicated at the level of technique, partially strained at the level of subjectivity, and clearly broken at the level of political economy, which Foucault deliberately bracketed.

Vindication at the level of technique. Amazon warehouses operationalise hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and the examination with industrial precision. Handheld scanners record every pick; algorithmic rate metrics produce a normal "units per hour" for each role; deviations trigger automated coaching messages, write-ups, and termination warnings. The pickers' bodies are partitioned in space (assigned aisles), timed in their activity (the bell of the scanner), and trained into postures (reach, scan, place). Foxconn dormitories, suicide nets, and shop-floor surveillance extend the logic. Uber's driver ratings, surge pricing, and quest bonuses examine and rank without any face-to-face supervisor. Deliveroo riders are normed by acceptance rates and delivery times. Even white-collar work in 2026 (Hubstaff screenshots, Microsoft Productivity Score, Slack response-time analytics) deploys exactly the techniques *Discipline and Punish* anatomises. The "physics of power" (Foucault, 1977: 316) Foucault described as an "anatomy" is recognisably the platform workplace's anatomy.

The productive nature of power is also vindicated. Platforms do not merely constrain the worker; they produce her as a particular kind of subject. The "Top Rated Plus" Upwork freelancer, the five-star Uber driver, the LinkedIn influencer, the high-performing Amazon picker: these are categories the platform invents, measures, and rewards, and which workers actively inhabit. Bordo's analysis of self-monitoring (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) maps directly onto the gig worker checking her dashboard before declining a ride.

Strain at the level of subjectivity. Two problems arise. First, *Discipline and Punish* assumes a relatively unified subject to be examined and normalised. The platform subject is fractured across credit scores, Uber ratings, LinkedIn endorsements, and health-insurance risk scores; unification is done by the platform, not the worker. Second, *Discipline and Punish* assumes an explicit norm. Algorithmic management often produces statistical patterns without an articulated norm; Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) describes this without-norm normativity. The worker is judged but cannot be told what is being judged.

Breaking at the level of political economy. Foucault's deliberate move in *Discipline and Punish* is to bracket the question "who benefits?" in order to ask "how does it work?" In the platform workplace, this brackets too much. Amazon's algorithmic management exists because Jeff Bezos accumulated capital under conditions of weak labour protection, a Marxist question Foucault cannot answer. Foxconn's regime serves Apple's quarterly margins. Uber's rating system extracts unpaid emotional labour and compresses wages. Zuboff's surveillance capitalism, Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism* (2017), and Veena Dubal's work on gig misclassification supply the political economy *Discipline and Punish* lacks.

Three contemporary critiques sharpen the assessment. First, the racial dimension. Simone Browne's *Dark Matters* (2015) argues that disciplinary surveillance has always been racialised; platforms inherit this. Facial recognition fails on darker faces, predictive policing targets Black neighbourhoods, gig algorithms penalise drivers in minority areas. Foucault under-theorised this. Second, the affective dimension. Sianne Ngai and Arlie Hochschild on emotional labour describe the affective demands platforms impose (be cheerful, be available, be brand-safe) that Foucault's disciplinary vocabulary does not fully capture. Third, the resistance dimension. *Discipline and Punish* offered little on collective resistance; the Amazon Labor Union, Foxconn strikes, RMT actions on Deliveroo, and the Uber drivers' Supreme Court win require organising frameworks Foucault did not supply.

My verdict: *Discipline and Punish* is essential reading for any course on the platform workplace because it names what is happening with unique precision. It is not sufficient. The full picture requires Zuboff on surveillance capitalism, Browne on racial surveillance, Hochschild on emotional labour, and good old-fashioned political economy on who profits. Foucault gives us the anatomy; we still need the autopsy.

### Mock Q3 (two-part): What does Foucault mean by power/knowledge, and why does he treat the two as inseparable?

This question has two parts and I will answer them in order, then connect them through examples.

Part one: what does Foucault mean by power/knowledge? The hyphen carries the argument. By power/knowledge Foucault means that the production of human-scientific knowledge (psychiatric, criminological, pedagogical, sexological, demographic, epidemiological) is itself a power operation, and that the exercise of power on bodies and populations cannot proceed without producing knowledge about them. The two are not externally related (knowledge serves power) but internally co-constitutive. In *Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977) the modern prison does not just punish a pre-existing criminal but produces criminology, the case file, and the figure of the delinquent. In *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (Foucault, 1978) the Victorian apparatus of confession, medicine, and law does not silence sex but multiplies it into a discourse, generating sexology and the homosexual as "a personage, a past, a case history". In "The Subject and Power" Foucault summarises: this power "imposes a law of truth on him which he must recognize and which others must recognize in him. It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212). Power/knowledge is the name for this fusion of subject-making with knowledge production.

Part two: why are they inseparable? Foucault gives three reasons. (i) Bodies cannot be disciplined without being known. The school cannot train pupils without registers, marks, transcripts, and key-stage records; the hospital cannot treat without the chart; Amazon cannot rate-manage without the scanner data. The technique of power requires the technique of measurement, which is knowledge. (ii) Knowledge in the human sciences cannot proceed without observed, classified, and normalised subjects. Criminology required prisons. Psychiatry required asylums. Sexology required clinics. Demography required the census. The "object" of human-scientific knowledge is itself produced by the practices that claim merely to describe it. (iii) The truths produced by power/knowledge are not false in the Marxist sense of ideology. Foucault explicitly rejects this: "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). Instead, truths circulate within historically specific regimes of truth, which determine what counts as a sayable, testable, or actionable truth about humans.

Why does this matter? Because it dissolves a picture social theory had inherited from the Enlightenment, the picture in which knowledge is the neutral mirror of nature, and power is what either obscures or applies that knowledge. Foucault is not saying that all truth is power or that science is just politics. He is saying that the human sciences in particular cannot be cleanly separated from the institutions that produced and use them.

Examples. The DSM-5-TR is a knowledge artefact (a diagnostic manual) and a power artefact simultaneously: insurance reimbursement, school accommodations, criminal responsibility, and self-understanding all route through it. The expansion of ADHD diagnoses in UK adults since 2020 illustrates power/knowledge. Pharmaceutical research, NHS commissioning, school assessment, and online self-diagnosis communities co-produce both a new kind of knowledge and a new kind of subject. The credit score is power/knowledge. Experian's probability-of-default model is a knowledge product that determines who can rent, borrow, and work. Predictive policing in US departments like LAPD's deployment of PredPol is power/knowledge in a feedback loop: the model predicts crime, police deploy on its predictions, deployment generates recorded crime, recorded crime trains the model. The Kember example from the lecture, obstetric ultrasound, is power/knowledge in clinical form: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY PAGE?]). The image is knowledge; what it does is power.

The inseparability is essential to Foucault's politics. If knowledge were neutral, critique could appeal to "the science". If power were merely repressive, resistance could appeal to "the truth". Power/knowledge denies both shortcuts. Critique has to engage with how knowledge is produced and what it does, simultaneously. This is why feminist theorists of medicine (Kember, Bordo), critical race theorists of policing (Browne, Eubanks), and critics of digital systems (Zuboff, Chun, Noble) find Foucault indispensable.

My conclusion: power/knowledge is inseparable not by definition but by historical analysis. The thesis applies most cleanly to the human sciences; whether it applies to physics is contested. Regimes of truth can still be better or worse; Foucault was not a strong relativist. Foucault shows the inseparability case by case, and the analysis remains alive every time a new diagnostic category, algorithm, or examination is introduced.

### Mock Q4 (specific-comparison): Compare Foucault's account of disciplinary power with Marx's account of ideology.

This essay compares two competing accounts of how modern societies hold their members in place: Marx's concept of ideology, especially as developed in *The German Ideology* (1846), *Capital* (1867), and the later Marxist tradition of Lukács, Gramsci, and Althusser; and Foucault's concept of disciplinary power, articulated in *Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977). I will set out each, locate the points of overlap, mark the decisive differences, and argue that the two operate at different registers and are best used together rather than against each other.

Marx's account of ideology. For Marx, ideology is the systematic distortion of consciousness produced by the conditions of capitalist production. The ruling class owns the means of production; "the ruling ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class". Workers come to believe that wage labour is free, that capital is productive, that the state is neutral, and that exploitation is just, beliefs that mystify the structural reality of surplus value extraction. Later Marxists refined this. Lukács in *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) added reification, the experience of social relations as thing-like. Gramsci added hegemony, consent secured through civil society, schools, media, churches. Althusser added ideological state apparatuses (schools, family, media, church) that "interpellate" subjects into class positions. Across all variants, ideology operates principally through cognition, beliefs, and consciousness.

Foucault's account of disciplinary power. For Foucault, the older repressive-hypothesis picture of power (power as constraint exercised by a ruling group on subordinates) misses the most distinctive feature of modern power. Disciplinary power operates not principally through cognition but through bodies: through the timetable, the partition of space, the examination, hierarchical observation, normalising judgement. It produces docile bodies: "useful, obedient, and self-regulating". The school child, the soldier, the patient, the worker, the prisoner are all docile bodies produced by their respective institutions. Disciplinary power does not need workers to believe in capitalism; it needs them to arrive on time, follow procedure, and submit to examination. As Foucault puts it polemically, power "is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

Both insist that what looks like free choice is heavily structured, both reject liberal autonomy, and both link the production of subjects to historically specific institutions. These overlaps are why theorists from Stuart Hall to Wendy Brown have combined them.

Four decisive differences. First, the register: ideology operates on consciousness, disciplinary power on bodies. The Amazon picker does not need to believe in capitalism; she needs to hit rate. Second, the location: ideology is concentrated in apparatuses controlled by the ruling class, disciplinary power is capillary, "always already there" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch, 2011: 19 [VERIFY PAGE?]). Foucault explicitly accuses Marxists: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). Third, the truth: ideology is false consciousness masking a class reality; disciplinary power produces regimes of truth and Foucault claims no standpoint outside them. Fourth, the agent: ideology presupposes a subject who could be undeceived; disciplinary power produces the subject, so there is no pre-disciplinary subject waiting to be liberated.

Worked examples. ADHD diagnosis in UK adults: ideology asks whose interests are served (pharmaceutical capital); discipline asks how the DSM, the CAMHS waitlist, and the medication regime examine, classify, and produce a particular subject. Amazon warehouse work: ideology explains why workers consent to a brutal wage relation; discipline explains how the rate is enforced through handheld scanners and automated coaching. Climate denial in Naomi Oreskes's *Merchants of Doubt* (2010) needs ideology; the carbon-budget biopolitics that replaces it needs Foucault.

My argument is that Foucault did not refute Marx; he changed the question. Marx asks who benefits and how consent is secured. Foucault asks how techniques work and how subjects are produced. The most fruitful contemporary social theory, Hall's *Policing the Crisis*, Brown's *Undoing the Demos*, Federici's *Caliban and the Witch*, Wacquant's prison ethnography, uses both. To replace ideology with disciplinary power is to lose tools; to dismiss disciplinary power as a Marxist heresy is to miss what makes modern power distinctive.

### Mock Q5 (contemporary-application): How does Foucault's notion of biopower help us think about pandemic public-health responses?

The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 to 2022 was, by common consensus, the most extensive biopolitical episode of the twenty-first century to date. I argue that Foucault's notion of biopower, introduced in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (Foucault, 1978) and developed in his Collège de France lectures, supplies the indispensable vocabulary for what happened, while also revealing its own limits when confronted with vaccine apartheid, algorithmic exception, and the racialised distribution of pandemic harm.

Foucault's biopower. Where disciplinary power targets the individual body and trains it to be docile and useful, biopower targets the population as a biological collective. Its objects are birth rates, mortality, morbidity, fertility, longevity, the demographic distribution of disease. Its techniques are statistics, modelling, public health, vaccination campaigns, screening programmes, and the regulation of urban hygiene. Its rationality is to "make live and let die", to manage the life of the population rather than to threaten its death. In "The Subject and Power" Foucault notes that even the police were originally instituted "for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). Biopower is what governmentality does when it discovers that populations have their own laws.

The pandemic as biopolitical episode. Five features of the COVID-19 response map directly onto biopower.

First, the R number and modelling. The British SAGE committee's daily reports, Imperial College's Ferguson model, the ONS Infection Survey: these turned the pandemic into a statistical object, and that object then governed lockdowns, school closures, and tier systems. Bodies were aggregated into a population, and the population's R was the lever.

Second, the contact-tracing apparatus. NHS Test and Trace, the COVID app, the Australian COVIDSafe app, South Korea's contact-tracing dashboards, Apple-Google exposure notification: these mapped social contacts onto bodies and bodies onto risk. This was disciplinary surveillance generalised into biopolitical surveillance.

Third, the vaccination programme. The UK's age-tiered roll-out, the JCVI priority groups, the booster cadence, the differential treatment of immunocompromised cohorts: these are textbook biopolitics. Vaccines were not distributed by individual desert but by population-level epidemiological logic. The "vaccinated" became a manageable demographic; the "unvaccinated" became a problem category. Vaccine passports for international travel and some domestic venues made the population/individual distinction concrete.

Fourth, the demographic stratification of harm. COVID disclosed and amplified inequality. Care home residents, ethnic minorities, the obese, the diabetic, the immunocompromised, and the elderly bore disproportionate mortality. Biopower's "make live and let die" had a darker underside that Achille Mbembe's *Necropolitics* (2003) names directly. Black and Asian British mortality rates, the abandonment of Indigenous Australians and Brazilians, and the deprioritisation of disabled people in some ICU triage protocols all show biopower shading into necropolitics.

Fifth, public health communication. "Hands, Face, Space", "Stay Home, Protect the NHS, Save Lives", "Eat Out to Help Out", and the daily Downing Street briefings turned the population into the addressee of moralised behavioural injunctions. Bordo's account of "bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) was scaled to the entire population.

Three limits to biopower as a complete framework. (i) Vaccine apartheid. The hoarding of vaccines by rich states and the deliberate non-waiving of TRIPS intellectual property protections during 2020 to 2022 are structural-economic phenomena that biopower alone does not explain. Marx is needed. (ii) The algorithmic. The pandemic accelerated algorithmic management (automated exam grading from the 2020 A-level fiasco, algorithmic furlough decisions, algorithmic content moderation of "misinformation"). Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) and Shoshana Zuboff's surveillance capitalism extend Foucault into the algorithmic register. (iii) Resistance. Anti-vaccine movements, lockdown protests, NHS workers' clap-back against austerity: these political phenomena had heterogeneous logics, some emancipatory and some reactionary. Foucault's vocabulary names the techniques but does not adjudicate the politics.

A worked synthesis. Statins prescribed at population level for cardiovascular risk are the routine biopolitical baseline; COVID was the acute episode that made the baseline visible. The HIV/AIDS response of the 1980s (risk groups, testing, ACT UP's resistance, the abandonment of African populations) was the earlier teacher. Climate change is the looming biopolitical horizon; carbon budgets, individual offsetting, and IPCC scenarios are biopower scaled to the species.

My verdict: biopower is essential but not sufficient for thinking about pandemic responses. It names the population-level rationality, the statistical apparatus, the demographic stratification, and the moralised self-government that constituted COVID-19 as a governable event. It needs supplementing with political economy (vaccine apartheid), critical race theory (racialised mortality), and algorithmic studies (automated decisions). Used with these supplements, biopower remains the most important single concept for thinking about public-health responses in the twenty-first century.

### Mock Q6 (disjunctive): Is the panopticon a metaphor or a model? Discuss.

The disjunction the question poses (metaphor or model) turns out to be the key to reading Foucault on the panopticon correctly. My argument is that the panopticon is best understood as a diagram, which is something between metaphor and model: a schema that names the logical structure of disciplinary surveillance without claiming that any actual institution implements it perfectly, and which therefore travels productively across institutional sites.

The case for "metaphor". A metaphor figures one thing in terms of another; it illuminates by analogy. Bentham's never-built prison stands metaphorically for modern surveillance: like the panopticon, contemporary surveillance makes us visible to an unverifiable watcher and induces self-regulation. Read as metaphor, the panopticon is rhetorically powerful but conceptually loose. Critics, David Lyon in some of his earlier writings, Mark Andrejevic, Roy Boyne, have argued that contemporary surveillance differs so much from Bentham's design (no central tower, no individual cells, no human watcher, no architectural enclosure) that the metaphor strains. If the panopticon is just a metaphor, perhaps better metaphors exist: the rhizome, the assemblage, the data shadow, the algorithmic black box.

The case for "model". A model is a structured representation that captures the essential mechanism of a phenomenon and can be tested against empirical instances. On this reading, the panopticon is Foucault's model of disciplinary power; particular institutions are evaluated for how closely they implement the model. The British prison estate, Victorian model schools, Auburn-style penitentiaries, Foxconn factories, Amazon warehouses, and CCTV networks would then be more or less panoptic. Read as model, the panopticon is conceptually rigorous but risks over-extension: if every visibility-asymmetry is panoptic, the model loses discriminating power.

The case for "diagram". Foucault himself uses the language of "diagram" in *Discipline and Punish*: the panopticon is "the diagram of a mechanism of power reduced to its ideal form" and "a figure of political technology" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader [VERIFY PAGE?]). A diagram is neither pure metaphor nor empirical model. It is a schema that captures the logical structure of a mechanism (asymmetric visibility, unverifiability, internalisation) without specifying particular implementations. Different institutions implement the diagram differently. This is why Foucault writes that "one can speak of the formation of a disciplinary society in this movement that stretches from the enclosed disciplines, a sort of social 'quarantine', to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316). The diagram generalises; specific instances vary.

Test cases.

CCTV across UK cities. Visible cameras, unverifiable monitoring, generalised self-regulation: the diagram holds. There is no single central tower; there are control rooms scattered across boroughs. The diagram does not require a single tower; it requires the logical structure of asymmetric visibility.

Amazon warehouses. Handheld scanners produce continuous bodily visibility to algorithmic supervisors. The model fits, with the qualification that the "tower" is software and the watching is automated. Amazon is panoptic by diagram, not by architecture.

Fitbit and Apple Watch. The user is inmate and tower simultaneously. The diagram bends but holds: the asymmetric visibility is between conscious self and unconscious body, and the "watcher" includes algorithms and dashboards.

TikTok and Instagram. The diagram strains. The watching is lateral (other users) and statistical (algorithms ranking content), not central. Synopticism (Mathiesen) or omnopticism is a better diagram.

Algorithmic credit scoring. The diagram strains differently. The "examination" is continuous and behind a corporate veil; the subject does not even know what is being measured. Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) describes this as without-norm normativity, which exceeds the panoptic diagram.

COVID contact-tracing apps. The diagram strains. The target is the population, not the individual; the rationality is biopolitical, not disciplinary. The panopticon is the wrong diagram; biopower is the right one.

My verdict: the panopticon is a diagram. As metaphor it under-claims; as model it over-claims. As diagram it does just what Foucault wanted it to do, name the disciplinary logic without specifying its empirical implementations, and leaves room for the supplementary diagrams (surveillance capitalism, biopower, omnopticism) that contemporary analysis requires. Where asymmetric visibility, unverifiability, and internalisation are in play, the panopticon is the right tool; where surveillance has shifted to lateral exposure, algorithmic classification, or biopolitical aggregation, we need to extend the toolbox.

### Mock Q7 (topic-as-title): Fitbits, Amazon warehouses and the algorithmic panopticon.

The three terms of my title (the Fitbit on the wrist, the Amazon warehouse on the industrial estate, the algorithmic panopticon as conceptual frame) together pose a question Foucault could not have posed in 1975 but which his vocabulary illuminates: what kind of power operates when the watcher is software, the watched is participatory, and the institution disappears into the device?

I will treat each term in order, then argue that the algorithmic panopticon is a genuine extension of Foucauldian discipline that requires three updates to the original framework.

The Fitbit. The wrist-worn fitness tracker is the canonical contemporary case of voluntary self-discipline. The wearer chooses the device, chooses to upload the data, and chooses to compare against the 10,000-step norm (a figure that, incidentally, originated in 1960s Japanese marketing rather than physiology). The Fitbit operationalises hierarchical observation (the device sees what the wearer cannot, such as heart rate variability, sleep stage architecture, VO2 max estimates), normalising judgement (against population norms calculated from millions of other wearers), and the examination (the dashboard reports). It produces a particular kind of subject (what some sociologists call the "quantified self") and a particular kind of body, "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). Crucially, no one forces participation. The trap is sprung at the moment of purchase.

The Amazon warehouse. The fulfilment centre at Coalville, Tilbury, or Rugeley is the canonical contemporary case of involuntary algorithmic discipline. Pickers carry handheld scanners that log every pick, time the route between racks, and trigger Time Off Task alerts when a worker pauses. Rate metrics produce normalised "units per hour" by role and shift; deviations trigger automated coaching, then write-ups, then termination. The cameras, the badge swipes, the bathroom-break tracking, the algorithmic rotation between roles: these are disciplinary techniques in Foucault's exact sense, mediated by software rather than by foremen. The pickers' bodies are partitioned (assigned zones), timed (the scanner clock), exercised (reach, scan, place), and observed (continuously). Foxconn workers and Deliveroo riders face similar regimes.

The algorithmic panopticon. The conceptual question is what unifies the Fitbit and the Amazon warehouse. Both involve continuous, asymmetric, software-mediated observation that produces self-regulating subjects. Both implement the panoptic diagram while diverging from Bentham's architectural specifications. Both link discipline to surveillance capitalism: the data they generate is valuable to platforms, employers, insurers, and advertisers. I call this configuration the algorithmic panopticon, following parallel uses in Andrejevic, Zuboff, and Cheney-Lippold.

Three updates the algorithmic panopticon requires to the original Foucauldian framework.

First, the observer is software, not a human. Bentham's tower required a real (or imaginable) guard. Amazon's algorithm needs no human in the loop; coaching messages can be generated automatically. The Fitbit's evaluation is purely statistical. This shifts the politics of resistance: there is no guard to bribe, no foreman to argue with, no tower window to break. Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) and Safiya Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* (2018) trace the implications.

Second, the norm is statistical, not articulated. Foucault's normalising judgement assumed an explicit norm (the 10,000 steps, the rate of pick, the BMI cutoff) against which deviation is measured. Many algorithmic systems produce statistical patterns without an articulated norm. The Amazon "rate" varies by warehouse, shift, season, and worker history; the Uber surge multiplier is opaque; the credit score's components are trade secrets. The worker is judged but cannot be told the criterion. This is what I called above without-norm normativity.

Third, the political economy is capital-driven. The Fitbit costs the wearer money; the data it produces enriches Google (which bought Fitbit in 2021). Amazon's algorithmic management exists because Bezos accumulated capital under conditions of weak labour protection. Foucault deliberately bracketed political economy in *Discipline and Punish*; the algorithmic panopticon makes that bracketing impossible. Zuboff's surveillance capitalism, Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism* (2017), and Veena Dubal on gig misclassification supply the missing analysis.

Worked illustrations. The Strava cyclist and the Amazon picker are subject to the same panoptic diagram; the difference is voluntariness, not architecture. The university lecturer subject to the REF, the Deliveroo rider subject to acceptance-rate metrics, the credit-scored renter, the algorithmically moderated TikTok creator: these are all algorithmic panopticons in different keys. China's Social Credit System makes the algorithmic panopticon a state project; Western platforms make it a market project. Resistance under the algorithmic panopticon is collective and infrastructural, not individual and psychological: the Fitbit user can unstrap the watch, but the Amazon worker cannot until the warehouse unionises.

### Mock Q8 (hypothetical): Does the rise of generative AI strengthen or weaken Foucault's account of governmentality?

Generative AI (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, the diffusion image and video models) has become, since 2022, a pervasive feature of education, work, governance, and intimate life. The question is whether Foucault's account of governmentality, developed in his Collège de France lectures of 1977 to 1979 and in essays like "Governmentality", is strengthened or weakened by this development. My argument is that generative AI strengthens governmentality in three respects and weakens it in two, with the net effect being that the framework needs extension rather than replacement.

Foucault's governmentality. The concept names the rationalities and techniques through which populations are governed, not as bodies in institutions (the disciplinary register) and not as biological collectives (the biopolitical register) but as conducting subjects whose conduct is the target of government. Liberal governmentality governs through freedom: it creates the conditions in which subjects can be relied upon to choose what is in their own and the state's interest. Neoliberal governmentality, on Foucault's late lectures, governs by extending market rationality across all domains (entrepreneurs of the self, human capital, performance metrics, choice architectures). The point is not that the state withdraws, but that government works through the cultivated self-government of free subjects.

Three ways generative AI strengthens governmentality.

First, the cultivation of self-governing subjects is dramatically accelerated. ChatGPT as personal coach, productivity assistant, writing tutor, therapy supplement, fitness planner, and life advisor extends the technologies of the self into algorithmic form. Foucault's late work on the care of the self (*The Hermeneutics of the Subject*, 2001) anticipates this: subjects work on themselves through dialogue, examination, and exercise. ChatGPT is parrhesia outsourced. The user is governed by a regime they helped train through prompt engineering, feedback ratings, and account preferences. This is governmentality intensified.

Second, regimes of truth proliferate and re-stabilise. Generative AI produces "truths" at industrial scale: summaries, explanations, classifications, predictions, recommendations. Whether or not these truths are accurate is, in Foucault's terms, less important than the regime of truth in which they are produced and circulated. Educational governance has been reshaped (Turnitin's AI detection, the redesign of essay assessment, the OECD's PISA discussions of AI literacy) within months of ChatGPT's release. Governments now govern AI usage (the EU AI Act, the UK's AI Safety Institute, China's algorithmic regulation), and that government is itself governmentality at the meta-level.

Third, the entrepreneurial self is digitised. Neoliberal governmentality positioned subjects as entrepreneurs of their own human capital. ChatGPT and its competitors offer the technology of that entrepreneurship: write the LinkedIn profile, draft the application letter, generate the side-hustle plan, optimise the CV. Self-improvement, formerly a labour-intensive practice, becomes a prompt. The "Foucauldian" reading of LinkedIn (the platform as governmentality) is reinforced rather than displaced by AI.

Two ways generative AI weakens governmentality.

First, the rationality is opacified. Foucault's governmentality presumed legible rationalities (liberalism, neoliberalism, welfarism) that subjects could in principle understand and contest. Generative AI models are not legible in this sense; they are statistical interpolations across training corpora, and their outputs do not flow from articulable rationality. This may not break governmentality but it makes the framework's appeal to "rationalities of government" harder to operationalise. Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) and the broader critical AI studies literature address this.

Second, the population/individual distinction blurs further. Governmentality always presupposed both an individual conduct of conduct and a population-level rationality. AI-driven recommender systems operate on individual data trails to produce individual outputs, but the training is population-wide and the optimisation target is platform-wide. The site of government is neither individual nor population in any classical sense; it is a vector in a high-dimensional embedding space. Foucault's framework can be extended here (Antoinette Rouvroy and Thomas Berns's "algorithmic governmentality", 2013, makes the move) but the extension stretches the concept.

Three illustrations. The disciplinary university now governs through AI literacy, AI detection, and "responsible use" pledges: REF-style management in the AI age. UK, Danish, and US welfare systems are increasingly algorithmic; Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* (2018) shows the punitive edge. Therapy apps and casual therapeutic ChatGPT use extend medical-psychological governance into the smartphone: technologies of the self now LLM-mediated.

My verdict: generative AI strengthens governmentality at the level of self-government, regimes of truth, and the entrepreneurial subject; it weakens it at the level of legible rationality and the population/individual articulation. Governmentality remains the most useful framework, provided it is extended through algorithmic governmentality, surveillance capitalism, and critical AI studies. The framework survives; it must adapt.

### Mock Q9 (single-author how-convincing): How convincing do you find Foucault's claim that "power is everywhere"?

Foucault writes that "power is everywhere; not because it embraces everything, but because it comes from everywhere" (Foucault, 1978: 93). This is one of his most-cited and most-contested lines. I find the claim convincing on a careful reading, unconvincing on a careless one, and politically productive only when paired with the specifications that prevent it from collapsing into a generalised cynicism.

The careless reading takes "power is everywhere" to mean that every social relation is equally an instance of power, that domination is universal and inescapable, and therefore that resistance is futile. This reading underwrites two opposed mistakes: a nihilistic acquiescence ("everything is power, so why bother?") and a paranoid maximalism ("everything is power, so everything is suspect"). Neither is what Foucault meant.

The careful reading distinguishes two parts of the sentence. (i) "Power is everywhere...because it comes from everywhere." Power is not a possession concentrated in a state, class, or institution and exercised from there outward; it is a multiplicity of force relations that arise from below and stabilise into larger configurations. As Richard Lynch summarises, "power...is the *name* given to a complex strategical situation" (Lynch, 2011 [VERIFY PAGE?]). (ii) "Not because it embraces everything." Power's omnipresence is not its totality. There are many force relations and many resistances; the social field is not unified under a single dominating principle. This second clause is what saves the claim from totalising determinism.

Three reasons I find the careful claim convincing.

First, the explanatory yield. The hypothesis that power "comes from everywhere" forces us to look at micro-techniques that other vocabularies miss. The school timetable, the hospital chart, the prison register, the office HR review, the Fitbit dashboard, the Uber rating, the Amazon scanner: all are sites of power that an "elite-domination" or "ideology" framework underdescribes. The yield is methodological: Foucault gives us tools to look at things classical sociology overlooked.

Second, the productive thesis. If power "comes from everywhere", it is not principally repressive but productive: it makes subjects, knowledges, types of people. The categories "homosexual", "ADHD adult", "credit-worthy renter", "high-rate picker", "underperforming pupil", "at-risk child" are produced by specific apparatuses. This explanation of category-proliferation is hard to obtain from any other vocabulary.

Third, the contemporary fit. Surveillance capitalism, algorithmic management, platform work, datafied self-government: all exhibit the distributed, capillary character Foucault diagnosed. Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) extends the analysis to platforms; Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) to algorithms; Simone Browne's *Dark Matters* (2015) to racial surveillance. These contemporary literatures are unthinkable without Foucault's omnipresence claim.

Three reasons I find the claim unconvincing if pushed.

First, the political economy lacuna. If power comes from everywhere, where does capital come from? Amazon's algorithmic management exists because Bezos accumulated capital under specific labour-law conditions; that is not "from everywhere" but from concentrated ownership. Marxist critics (Poulantzas, Pearce) push hard here. The fix is to read Foucault alongside structural analyses, not to abandon him.

Second, the normative reticence. If power is everywhere, the critic needs criteria for distinguishing better and worse configurations of power. Foucault is famously reluctant to supply such criteria. Habermas's *Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) makes the strongest version of this critique. Foucault's later work on parrhesia and the care of the self points toward an ethics, but the framework remains thinner than full normative political theory needs.

Third, the agency problem. If power produces subjects, the subject of resistance is itself produced by power; Hartsock's *Money, Sex, and Power* (1983) is the feminist version. Foucault's response that resistance is constitutive of power is correct but underdeveloped; Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* (Butler, 1997) extends it.

Worked examples. The British school is a site where power "comes from everywhere" (teachers, parents, peers, league tables, Ofsted, exam boards) and the omnipresence claim is convincing. The Foxconn dormitory is a site where power comes from somewhere quite specific (Foxconn management, Apple's procurement contract, the Chinese state) so concentration analysis is needed. The Fitbit looks tailor-made for the claim; the credit score needs Foucault plus Marx.

My verdict: "power is everywhere" is convincing as a description of the dispersed, productive, capillary character of modern power. It is not convincing as a complete theory that supersedes structural analysis of capital, gender, and race. Read as a methodological injunction (look everywhere) rather than as a metaphysical thesis, the claim is one of the most consequential in twentieth-century social theory.

### Mock Q10 (two-authors): Using the work of at least two authors, evaluate whether biopower has become bio-econ-data-power.

The neologism "bio-econ-data-power" names a contemporary configuration in which biological, economic, and data-driven rationalities of government have become mutually constitutive. The question is whether biopower, in Foucault's 1978 sense, has shaded into this triple compound. I will draw on Foucault, Shoshana Zuboff, Wendy Brown, and (briefly) Achille Mbembe to argue: yes, with three qualifications, and with one important historical continuity that the neologism risks obscuring.

Foucault's biopower. In *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (Foucault, 1978) and the Collège de France lectures, biopower names the form of power that takes "life itself" as its object (populations, birth rates, mortality, fertility, hygiene, public health). Its institutions are demographic statistics, vaccination campaigns, screening, sanitation, social insurance. Its rationality is to "make live and let die": the population is to be managed for its productive capacity, longevity, and aggregate wellbeing. Foucault's biopower presupposed the state and the welfare apparatus.

Zuboff's surveillance capitalism. *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) describes a regime in which firms (Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Palantir) accumulate "behavioural surplus" from human experience, render it as predictive data, and sell predictions in behavioural futures markets. The product is not the user; the product is prediction. The political economy is concentrated platform capital; the rationality is behavioural prediction; the techniques are pervasive datafication. Zuboff explicitly distinguishes surveillance capitalism from older capitalisms while drawing on Foucault and Polanyi.

Brown's neoliberal governmentality. *Undoing the Demos* (2015) and *In the Ruins of Neoliberalism* (2019) describe how neoliberal rationality has extended market logic across all domains: health, education, family, the self. The neoliberal subject is "human capital" maximising lifetime returns. Health is a portfolio; education is investment; even reproduction is rationalised through fertility apps and donor egg pricing. Brown's argument is that biopower and economic rationality have fused under neoliberalism.

The argument for "bio-econ-data-power". Combining these three authors, the contemporary management of life looks unmistakably triple. Health insurance combines biological (risk profile), economic (premium pricing), and data (continuous monitoring via Fitbit and Apple Watch) into a single technology. Vaccination governance combines biological (efficacy and side-effect statistics), economic (Pfizer's market cap, AstraZeneca's price tier), and data (centralised health records, vaccine passports, app-based proof) into one apparatus. Pregnancy now runs through ultrasound (biological), private clinics and IVF markets (economic), and ovulation/cycle apps (data); the Kember example from the lecture, "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY PAGE?]), extended into the smartphone era. Pandemic responses fused R-number epidemiology, vaccine market dynamics, and contact-tracing data infrastructures. Statins prescribed at population level combine cardiovascular risk (biological), pharmaceutical patent economics, and electronic health records (data). The credit-score-meets-health-data hybrid (life insurance using Fitbit data to set premiums, gym memberships using behavioural data to price discounts) is bio-econ-data-power in concentrated form.

Three qualifications.

First, the historical continuity. Biopower was always economic. Foucault himself notes that the police were instituted "for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). The Malthusian couple, the demographic transition, the actuarial life-insurance industry of the nineteenth century: these fused biological and economic from the start. The neologism risks suggesting a discontinuity where there is intensification. Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* (2004) makes the long view sharper: early-modern capitalism violently fused control over women's bodies, labour, and accumulation.

Second, the necropolitical underside. Mbembe's *Necropolitics* (2003) reminds us that biopower has always had a darker counterpart that distributes death along racial and colonial lines. Bio-econ-data-power preserves this: the Indigenous data sovereignty movement, the racialised distribution of pandemic mortality, the vaccine apartheid of 2020 to 2022, predictive policing's targeting of Black neighbourhoods. The neologism should not flatten this.

Third, the agency problem. Zuboff is sometimes accused of presenting users as passive data sources; Brown's neoliberal subjects can sound entirely captured. Foucault's omnipresence-with-resistance reading is the corrective. The fertility-app user who uses period-tracker data to challenge a post-Roe state, the Fitbit user who exports raw data for personal use, the union organising at Amazon: these are bio-econ-data resistances.

My verdict: biopower has not been replaced by bio-econ-data-power but intensified and reconfigured. NHS data sharing with Palantir, China's Social Credit System fusing health codes with consumer surveillance, and the 23andMe database all instantiate the triple form. Zuboff and Brown supply the supplements Foucault needed; Mbembe and Federici keep us honest about racial and gendered violence. The neologism is useful shorthand; the analysis must remain genealogical, attentive to continuity as well as novelty.

### Mock Q11 (can-we): Can Foucault's framework be used to think about resistance, or does it foreclose it?

This question repeats one of the oldest charges against Foucault (that his account of power leaves no room for resistance) but it does so productively, asking whether Foucault's framework can be used for thinking about resistance even granting the worries. I argue: yes, but only if we accept that Foucauldian resistance is local, tactical, and reversible rather than total, strategic, and final, and only if we read his late work on technologies of the self alongside the early disciplinary analyses.

The prosecution case. Critics from Hartsock and McNay (feminist) to Poulantzas (Marxist) to Habermas (universalist) have argued that Foucault's framework forecloses resistance in three ways. (i) Power is omnipresent: "always already there, one is never outside it" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch, 2011: 19 [VERIFY PAGE?]). If there is no outside, there is no Archimedean point from which to push back. (ii) Power produces the subject: "it is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212). If the subject of resistance is itself produced by power, resistance is just power moving against itself. (iii) Foucault refuses normative grounds for distinguishing better from worse configurations of power; without grounds, resistance is preference, not critique.

The defence case has five elements.

First, Foucault explicitly insists resistance is internal to power. *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1*: "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet, or rather consequently, this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Resistance is not outside power but constitutive of it; without resistance, what we have is domination, not power.

Second, the omnipresence claim is not a totality claim. "Power is everywhere; not because it embraces everything, but because it comes from everywhere" (Foucault, 1978: 93). The plurality of force relations is the condition for the plurality of resistances.

Third, Foucault's late work on technologies of the self (*The Use of Pleasure*, 1984; *The Care of the Self*, 1984; the *Hermeneutics of the Subject* lectures) addresses how subjects can work on themselves to become other than what power has made them. Parrhesia (truth-telling), askesis (practice), the care of the self: these are positive resources for an ethics of resistance.

Fourth, Foucault's political life included resistance. The Groupe d'Information sur les Prisons (GIP), which he co-founded in 1971, organised prisoners and their families against the French prison system. His support for the gay liberation movement, anti-psychiatric campaigns, and (controversially) the Iranian revolution shows that he believed in resistance, though he refused to write the master narrative of liberation.

Fifth, Foucauldian resistance is legible in contemporary politics. ACT UP's "die-ins" used the disciplinary visibility of the AIDS patient as a weapon. The body-positivity and fat-liberation movements refuse Bordo's slender-body norm. Queer politics denaturalises the heterosexual norm produced by sexological discourse. Disability activism's social model rejects medicalising classification. The neurodiversity movement reframes ADHD and autism. Predictive-policing opponents (Stop LAPD Spying Coalition, Big Brother Watch in the UK) contest the technique. Amazon Labor Union and gig-worker organising deploy collective Foucauldian resistance. These are not Foucault's resistance (local, tactical, reversible) disguised as something else; they are Foucault's resistance in action.

What Foucauldian resistance does not do: it does not promise final emancipation, seize the state, or produce a post-power society. Marxist critics are right that for projects of overthrowing capitalism or dismantling patriarchy, Foucault's vocabulary is inadequate; the fix is supplementation, not abandonment.

Three risks. First, recuperation: body positivity becomes a Dove campaign, Pride becomes a brand, mindfulness becomes corporate wellness; Brown's *Undoing the Demos* (2015) extends the analysis. Second, privatisation: technologies of the self can collapse into self-help if not paired with collective politics. Third, romanticisation of the local: pickets, strikes, and constitutional cases remain necessary, and the Amazon Labor Union deploys Foucauldian techniques inside a Marxist politics.

My verdict: Foucault's framework can be used to think about resistance, and does not foreclose it. What it forecloses is the master-narrative model (the proletariat, the universal subject, the final liberation). What it enables is granular analysis of how power and resistance entangle in specific institutions, bodies, and moments. For the politics of the credit score, the Fitbit, the rate metric, the school exam, the welfare algorithm, Foucauldian resistance is the right scale. For climate, capital, and structural inequality, Foucault must be supplemented. The framework opens space for resistance; the political work remains ours.

### Mock Q12 (specific-comparison): Compare Bordo's reading of disciplinary norms with Butler's account of performativity.

Susan Bordo and Judith Butler are the two most influential feminist readers of Foucault on the body and the subject. They share a Foucauldian commitment to the productive thesis (that power makes bodies and subjects rather than simply constraining them) but they develop the inheritance differently. Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" and *Unbearable Weight* (Bordo, 1993) work with disciplinary norms; Butler's *Gender Trouble* (Butler, 1990), *Bodies That Matter* (Butler, 1993), and *The Psychic Life of Power* (Butler, 1997) work with performativity. I will set out each, mark the convergences and divergences, and assess what each offers contemporary feminist analysis.

Bordo's disciplinary norms. Bordo extends Foucault's account of disciplinary power to the gendered production of bodies. The argument: late-twentieth-century norms of slenderness, tone, control, and self-mastery are not merely aesthetic preferences but disciplinary instruments. Women's bodies are continuously measured against these norms through the mirror, the magazine, the scale, the cosmetic counter, and increasingly the social-media feed. The key claim: "between the media images of self-containment and self-mastery and the reality of constant everyday stress...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). Eating disorders, cosmetic surgery, the gym, the wellness industry, the proliferation of "thinspiration" and "fitspo" content: all are read as gendered disciplinary phenomena. Bordo also reads the historical specificity of body norms: the bourgeois Victorian woman's pale skin and the contemporary woman's flat stomach are different normative targets produced by different historical configurations.

Butler's performativity. Butler takes the Foucauldian productive thesis to its strongest conclusion: gender is not the cultural expression of a natural sex but a "doing", performatively produced through repeated bodily acts that congeal into the appearance of natural sex. *Gender Trouble* (Butler, 1990) reads compulsory heterosexuality as a regulatory regime; *Bodies That Matter* (Butler, 1993) extends the analysis to sexed materiality; *The Psychic Life of Power* (Butler, 1997) deepens the question of subject formation. The central quotation: "we understand power as forming the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence...then power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Performativity opens space for resistance through disruptive repetition (drag, gender non-conformity, queer practice) that exposes the constructedness of the norm.

Convergences. Both authors are Foucauldian. Both reject voluntarist individualism and crude determinism. Both insist on the body as a site of social reproduction, treat the productive nature of power as central, and link the cultural production of gender to specific historical configurations of power/knowledge. Both share a method of close reading of cultural and clinical texts rather than survey or ethnography.

Divergences. First, the object: Bordo's primary object is the female body as disciplinarily normed; Butler's is the sexed/gendered subject as performatively produced. Second, the mechanism: Bordo's is disciplinary (observation, examination, internalised normalisation) while Butler's is performative, citational repetition that congeals into apparent essence. Third, the politics: Bordo's *Unbearable Weight* (1993) worries that performativity-talk can dematerialise the genuine suffering of women with eating disorders; Butler responds that performativity is not free choice but constraint. Fourth, the resistance: Bordo's is collective normative critique (feminist politics, fat-liberation, neurodiversity, pro-recovery work); Butler's is more aesthetic and disruptive (drag, queer practice, the "subversive bodily acts" of *Gender Trouble*).

Worked applications. The body-positivity movement on Instagram is both Bordoian (refusing the slender norm) and Butlerian (alternative performances of embodied womanhood). The cosmetic-surgery clinic is more Bordoian; the trans clinic's gatekeeping protocols are more Butlerian, though both frameworks illuminate. The Foxconn factory's gendered division of labour is more Bordoian; the legal recognition of non-binary identities more Butlerian. Both authors face a common critique (insufficient attention to race and class) which Patricia Hill Collins, Sara Ahmed, Federici, and Fraser address.

My verdict: Bordo and Butler are complementary, not competing. Bordo gives us a tool for analysing the disciplinary normalisation of women's bodies; Butler gives us a tool for analysing the performative production of gendered subjects. The disciplinary university, the social-media platform, the trans clinic, the Foxconn factory, the post-Roe US state: all require both lenses to be seen clearly. To force a choice between them is to lose half the feminist Foucauldian toolkit.
