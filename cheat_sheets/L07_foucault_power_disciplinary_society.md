# L7 - Foucault on Power and Disciplinary Society

## 1. Lecture Overview

Lecture 7 marks a decisive pivot in SC301: from classical models of power as ideology, repression, or class domination toward Foucault's far more subtle and pervasive account of power as something that operates through everyday practices, knowledges, institutions, and the very formation of the subject. The lecturer opens with Sarah Kember's example of obstetric ultrasound, the way scanning "eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998), to dramatise the central problem: how can we understand what is happening here? An ideology critique (the doctor as agent of patriarchy) captures part of the story but, as Foucault puts it, "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

The lecture's spine is the contrast between **sovereign power** and **disciplinary power**. Sovereign power is the spectacular, corporal, top-down power of the pre-modern monarch, illustrated by the grotesque public execution of Damiens the regicide that opens *Discipline and Punish*. Modern power, since the nineteenth century, looks different: it is dispersed, capillary, productive rather than purely repressive, and works through surveillance, examination, and normalisation rather than through the visible application of force. Bentham's panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, becomes the architectural figure of this new regime: a "machine for dissociating the see/being seen dyad" that produces, in the inmate, "a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader).

Three aspects of discipline are unpacked: **hierarchical observation** (we are watched from positions we cannot see); **normalising judgement** (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal"); and **examination** (the combination of observation and judgement in documented, testable form, such as registers, progress reports, key stages, transcripts). The aim and product of this triad is the **docile body**: passive, self-regulating, useful to capital and to the state.

The body and subjectivity sit at the centre of the second half of the lecture. Susan Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" extends Foucault into feminist territory: norms of slenderness, tone, control, and self-mastery shape bodies, particularly women's, and produce subjects "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). The lecture documents the shift from late-nineteenth-century norms (plump men signalling success, pale women signalling exemption from field work) to contemporary norms (the slender, well-toned body, advertised in titles like *Men's Health* and read off in the City-of-London gym). Judith Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* pushes further: power is "what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Power forms the subject; we cannot stand outside it.

Behind all of this stands Foucault's polemic against traditional sociological theories of power: liberal/Enlightenment (power as a thing possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom) and Marxist (power as class domination operating through ideology, concentrated in the owners of the means of production). Foucault's complaint is consistent: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). His project is to look at **how** power operates, at its capillary, micro-physical level, rather than asking only **who** holds it. The closing slide of the lecture returns to Kember's ultrasound image, now reread through the whole conceptual apparatus, disciplinary power, productive power, normalising judgement, and power/knowledge.

## 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Sovereign power**: pre-modern, spectacular, top-down power that operates through visible force, corporal punishment, and the abuse of the body. Concentrated in a person (the king) and exercised through edict and execution.
- **Disciplinary power**: modern, dispersed, capillary power that works through training, observation, examination, and normalisation. Subtle, productive, everywhere.
- **The panopticon**: Jeremy Bentham's architectural design (central tower, back-lit ring of cells) that allows constant, asymmetric visibility. Foucault treats it as the diagram of disciplinary modernity.
- **Surveillance**: continuous observation that need not be actual to be effective; the possibility of being watched is enough to produce self-regulation.
- **Hierarchical observation**: monitoring conducted from positions of invisibility, with observers themselves unobserved.
- **Normalising judgement**: measurement of individuals against an invented standard of "the normal"; the nineteenth-century invention of "the normal" is "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (lecture slide).
- **Examination**: the ritual combination of observation and judgement that produces documented, comparable knowledge of individuals (registers, progress reports, transcripts, medical records, school photos, key-stage testing).
- **Docile bodies**: passive, self-regulating, useful bodies produced by disciplinary techniques; bodies that fit the modern capitalist economy.
- **Power/knowledge**: power and knowledge are inseparable; the production of knowledge (psychiatry, criminology, demography) is itself an exercise of power, and power produces knowledge.
- **Productive power**: power is not only repressive; it produces subjects, bodies, types of people, institutions, and forms of conduct.
- **Biopower**: power exercised at the level of populations (birth rates, mortality, public health, demographics), managing life rather than threatening death.
- **Governmentality**: the rationalities and techniques through which populations are governed (cross-applied from related lectures).
- **The subject**: the modern individual as both subjected to power and constituted as a subject by power.
- **Regimes of truth**: historically specific configurations of what counts as true and how truth is produced.
- **The disciplinary society**: contemporary society understood as saturated by the techniques of discipline that emerged in the nineteenth century, including schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces.
- **Force relations**: Foucault's basic micro-unit of power; relations that push, urge, or compel, immanent in social interactions (Lynch, 2011).
- **Capillary power**: power that operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies and conduct.

## 6. Common Essay Pitfalls

- Treating Foucault as if he simply said "power is bad" or "everything is power"; both miss the productive, omnipresent-but-not-totalising character of his analysis.
- Conflating disciplinary power with sovereign power; failing to mark the historical shift Foucault traces from spectacular punishment to surveillance.
- Reducing the panopticon to "being watched" without explaining the **asymmetry** (visible/unverifiable) and the **internalisation** that produces self-regulation.
- Forgetting that power is **productive**: it creates subjects, knowledges, and types of people, not just constraints.
- Treating biopower and disciplinary power as synonyms. Disciplinary power targets the individual body; biopower targets the population. They overlap but are not identical.
- Ignoring resistance. Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance", so making him sound more deterministic than he is.
- Failing to engage the lecture's explicit foils: liberal/Enlightenment views (Slide 34) treat power as a thing to be seized or shared; Marxist views (Slide 35) reduce power to ownership of the means of production and ideology. Foucault's reply, that "the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116; Slide 36), names the methodological gap.
- Using only one example (the prison) when the lecture and reading insist that disciplinary power runs through schools, hospitals, workplaces, families, and bodies.
- Quoting "panopticon" without naming Bentham, or quoting Bordo/Butler without anchoring them to Foucault.
- Reading "docile bodies" as universal slavery rather than as a tendency produced by specific institutions and techniques.
- Treating Foucault as a "postmodernist" who denies truth; he writes about regimes of truth, not the abolition of truth.
- Forgetting the lecture's opening: ideology is not wrong, but it is "much more and much less" than what Foucault wants to analyse.

## 7. Past-Year Exam Questions and Answers

### 2025 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing do you find his arguments?

Foucault's challenge to traditional sociological approaches to power is set out polemically in *Discipline and Punish* (1977) and the interviews collected as *Power/Knowledge* (1980). Traditional sociology, on Foucault's reading, has tended to think about power in two problematic ways that the lecture isolates as its principal foils. Liberal and Enlightenment accounts treat power as a thing, possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom (Slide 34). Marxist accounts treat power as flowing from ownership of the means of production, concentrated in the bourgeoisie, and exercised over the proletariat through ideology (Slide 35). Foucault's complaint, as Lynch summarises, is that each of these "mischaracterize an accidental feature of power in a particular context as an essential characteristic of power in general" (Lynch, 2011: 19).

Foucault's response to the Marxist version is biting: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). What is missing in both traditions is an account of **how** power operates, its mechanics, its techniques, its capillary distribution.

Foucault's positive counter-proposal has at least six distinctive moves. First, **power is dispersed, not concentrated**. It does not sit at the top of a pyramid but circulates through micro-relations of force throughout the social body; as Lynch puts it, power "comes from below" rather than from a sovereign at the apex (Lynch, 2011: 25-26). Second, **power is productive, not (only) repressive**. It produces subjects, knowledges, types of people, "new kinds of subjects" through new institutions such as schools, prisons, and psychiatric hospitals (Slide 13). Third, **power and knowledge are inseparable**: the human sciences are themselves apparatuses of power, and the Kember example shows how the medical scan constitutes both knowledge of the foetus and a redistribution of visibility around the maternal body (Kember, 1998). Fourth, **power operates through everyday practices**: registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, key-stage tests (Slide 10), not principally through state edict. Fifth, **modern power forms the subject** rather than merely constraining a pre-existing one. As Foucault writes, it "makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212), and Butler extends this: "power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Sixth, **resistance is internal to power**: "Where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95).

The pay-off is methodological. We should not ask "who has power?" but "how is power exercised?" We should look at the panopticon, the school register, the medical scan, the body in the gym, not only at parliament and capital.

How convincing do I find these arguments? Largely, yes, but with reservations.

On the convincing side: Foucault's account illuminates phenomena that traditional sociology struggles to explain. The lecture's opening example, Kember on obstetric ultrasound, shows why. Calling the doctor "ideological" or "patriarchal" is not exactly wrong, but it misses how the technology itself, the scanning practice, the protocols, organise women's bodies and knowledge of foetuses in ways that exceed any individual doctor's intent. "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). The same applies to the school: the Marxist asks whose interests are served by mass schooling, but Foucault asks how the school's registers, progress reports, photos, and key-stage testing produce the pupil as a particular kind of subject (Slide 10).

I find the productive thesis especially convincing. Foucault writes that in the nineteenth century "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects) were created or produced through the creation [of] new institutions such as schools, prisons, psychiatric hospitals" (Slide 13). The modern prisoner is not a pre-existing criminal whom the prison contains; the prison produces "the modern prisoner" through its cellular, panoptic architecture. Bordo's diachronic analysis of body norms makes the productive thesis vivid for gender: the late-nineteenth-century plump-and-prosperous male body and the contemporary slender-and-toned body are not two responses to the same underlying nature but two different productions of the gendered subject by different disciplinary configurations (Bordo, 1993; Slides 25, 27).

The reservations. First, Foucault's analysis can be hard to operationalise against concentrated, spectacularly sovereign power. The lecture itself notes that disciplinary power displaces but does not abolish sovereign power; the brutal public execution of Damiens (Foucault, 1977) is exhibit A for a form of power that has not entirely vanished. The capillary model risks underplaying very visible, top-down exercises of power. Second, feminist critics within the Foucauldian tradition (Bordo herself, and Butler) have noted that if power constitutes the subject, it becomes harder to ground emancipatory agency in anything other than power itself. Butler's response is that "power is not simply what we oppose" but also "what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2): agency is immanent rather than oppositional. Third, Marxist critics argue that Foucault sees the techniques but loses the structure: he sees the disciplinary apparatus of the modern factory but loses sight of the capitalist logic that organises it, including the relation between docile bodies and "the modern capitalist economy" that the lecture's own Slide 23 invokes when it concludes "The bourgeoisie has won!"

On balance, I find Foucault's challenge highly convincing as a supplement to traditional approaches but less so as a replacement. Lynch's reading is helpful here: Foucault's theory is best understood as supplying the "analytics" that the older traditions lacked (Lynch, 2011: 17), not as abolishing the question of who benefits. To ask "how" without abandoning "who benefits" is the best legacy of his intervention.

### 2024 (Q7): How does Foucault conceptualise disciplinary power? Why is this conceptualisation important?

Foucault's notion of disciplinary power names the form of power that emerged in Western societies from roughly the late eighteenth century onwards and that, he argues, came to displace the older logic of sovereign power. Sovereign power, as he reconstructs it in the opening of *Discipline and Punish*, was concentrated in the person of the monarch, exercised through visible force, and dramatised in spectacular rituals of corporal and capital punishment; the slow public execution of Damiens for regicide is its emblem (Foucault, 1977; Slide 8). Disciplinary power, by contrast, is "subtle", "everywhere", and works not by stopping people from doing things but by training them into doing certain things (Slide 10). As Foucault puts it, this is "a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets; it is a 'physics' or an 'anatomy' of power" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in the Calhoun reader).

I would isolate four features that together make up Foucault's conceptualisation. First, disciplinary power is **capillary**: it operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies, timetables, classrooms, examinations, and records. The school's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10) are paradigmatic. Lynch's worked example of the academic transcript captures the same logic: "this will go on your permanent record" enrols each individual student in disciplinary observation (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). Second, it is **productive**: it does not merely repress but produces new kinds of subjects (the modern prisoner, the schoolchild, the patient, the worker) through new institutions (Slide 13). Third, it works through three linked techniques: **hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and the examination**. We are observed from positions of asymmetric invisibility (Slide 19), measured against an invented standard of "the normal" whose nineteenth-century invention the lecturer calls "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (Slide 22), and tested and documented in rituals that fuse observation and judgement. Fourth, it produces **docile bodies**: bodies that are useful, obedient, and crucially, **self-regulating**, because the subject internalises the gaze (Slide 23). The lecture is explicit: "We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!" (Slide 17).

The architectural diagram of this regime is Bentham's panopticon. As Foucault describes it via Calhoun, the design places "a supervisor in a central tower" and shuts "in each cell a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). Its "major effect" is "to induce in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). Because the inmate cannot verify when they are being watched, they behave as if they always are. Visibility, as Foucault summarises, is a trap. Crucially, the panopticon is not just a prison design but a model that "infiltrates" schools, hospitals, factories, and the police, what Foucault calls the disciplinary society (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader).

Why is this conceptualisation important? I think it matters for at least four reasons. First, it reorients sociology away from the question "who holds power?" and toward the question "how does power operate?" (Slide 6). That methodological shift is huge: it lets us examine the school's regime of registers and progress reports, the doctor's scan of the foetus, or the prison's cellular architecture without first reducing them to the will of a ruling class or a sovereign. Second, it explains the otherwise puzzling fact that modern liberal societies, despite claiming to expand freedom, generate ever more intensive forms of conduct-shaping. Disciplinary power does not contradict liberal freedom; it is its underside. Third, it identifies the link between knowledge and power, what Foucault calls power/knowledge. The human sciences (psychiatry, pedagogy, the medical clinic) are not innocent but apparatuses that produce the very subjects they claim to describe. The Kember example is the lecture's clinical illustration: the ultrasound is a medical knowledge artefact whose effect is to make the foetus visible and the mother's body disappear (Kember, 1998). Fourth, it opens space for politics at the micro-level: the examination, the medical scan, the body in the gym all become legitimate sites of political contestation.

The conceptualisation also has limitations I want to acknowledge. Critics within the Foucauldian and feminist traditions have charged that Foucault dissolves class and gender into a generalised analytic of conduct. Bordo's contribution is partly a response: her account of how women in particular are "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) gives the disciplinary register a gendered specificity that Foucault did not fully develop. Marxist critics have argued that disciplinary power names a real phenomenon but cannot replace political economy: the lecture's own concluding flourish on Slide 23, that docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy" and that "the bourgeoisie has won!", implicitly concedes that disciplinary analysis needs to be paired with a critique of capital. I find these criticisms partially convincing; Foucault's account works best as a supplement to, rather than a substitute for, structural analyses. But the supplement is essential. The British school's regime of registers, progress reports, key stages, and testing is unintelligible if we have only "the ruling class" as our analytic.

Disciplinary power, then, names not a thing but a mode, a way power has worked in modern societies. Its importance lies in how it allows us to see, in the most mundane practices (the school register, the medical scan, the bathroom-mirror appraisal of the body), the political form of our present. As Foucault writes, this is "a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212), and that double sense, subjected to and constituted as, is the productive heart of his concept.

### 2024 (Q8): "Contemporary societies are biopolitical." Critically analyse this statement.

The claim that contemporary societies are biopolitical asks us to extend Foucault's analysis of power from the disciplined individual body to the managed population. Lynch summarises the move: where disciplinary power targets individuals (the academic transcript, the medical case file), biopower "does not directly address particular individuals, but rather groups of people and populations as a whole" (Lynch, 2011: 16). His own illustration is epidemiological: "observing which groups in the population are most likely to contract a disease (such as lung cancer) can lead to a discovery of its causes (cigarette smoking, or asbestos exposure)" (Lynch, 2011: 16). The rationality is "to save lives" by intervening on the population, not by punishing the individual. Disciplinary and biopolitical power are the "two poles" of modern power over life: the one acting on the body, the other on the species (Lynch, 2011: 26).

I want to argue that the statement is largely defensible (contemporary societies are indeed extensively biopolitical) but only with three qualifications: biopolitics does not replace disciplinary or sovereign power but layers onto them; biopolitics operates through everyday moral injunctions as much as through state apparatus; and its operations remain contested.

First, the affirmative case. The lecture itself supplies the strongest examples. Slide 24 is built around the proposition "It is our duty to keep ourselves healthy" and unpacks the biopolitical injunctions that support it: "it is the duty of government to make us reduce our salt level"; "we feel bad if we do not eat 5 portions of fruit/veg per day"; "the government must stop obese children." None of these target a particular individual; all of them act on the population through dietary norms, public-health campaigns, and the moralisation of self-management. Slide 37 supplies the historical genealogy via the Foucault quotation: "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order, nor for assuring governments in their struggle against their enemies, but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). The modern "police", in Foucault's broader sense, is biopolitical from inception.

Second, biopower works through productive moral norms rather than coercion. Bordo's analysis of body norms makes this concrete at the population level. The slender, well-toned body of contemporary culture is not coerced by any sovereign; it is invited and incited by media, advertising, and the practices of self-management that Slide 28 traces through "the rise in gym membership" and the City-of-London expectation that one will "work-out" to "show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life." Bordo's clinching formulation is biopolitical-disciplinary in equal measure: "between the media images of self-containment and self-mastery and the reality of constant everyday stress...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Third, biopower in Foucault's lecture sits alongside the productive thesis of disciplinary power. Slide 13 names the productive yield: "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects) were created or produced through the creation [of] new institutions such as schools, prisons, psychiatric hospitals." Schools and hospitals act on bodies one by one but also on populations: their statistics, comparisons, and aggregate norms (key stages, school photos, health screenings) constitute the population as a knowable object. The Kember example, finally, sits at the intersection. The ultrasound scan is a clinical encounter with one mother but also part of a population-level apparatus of antenatal screening: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998). Both visibility on the individual body and the management of the antenatal population are at work.

Now to qualifications. The statement risks overgeneralisation if read as "contemporary societies are *only* biopolitical." Foucault is clear that biopower coexists with disciplinary power and even with residual sovereign power. Sovereign power has not vanished; the spectacular punishment Foucault describes at the opening of *Discipline and Punish*, the public torture of Damiens, has receded but persists in attenuated forms wherever the visible exercise of force on bodies is intended to display political authority. Inside the school, disciplinary techniques (registers, examinations) operate alongside biopolitical ones (public-health interventions like the 5-a-day campaign reaching children through school meals). The statement is best read additively: contemporary societies are biopolitical *as well as* disciplinary and partially sovereign.

The second qualification concerns resistance. If biopower were total, the impulse to question public-health framings, the contestation of who counts as "normal", and the politics of body acceptance that complicate Bordo's diagnosis would be unintelligible. Foucault himself insists that "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Lynch echoes this: power relations "can always be altered" through resistance (Lynch, 2011: 28). Biopolitical injunctions provoke biopolitical contestation.

The third qualification is conceptual. Lynch warns against using "theory" in a way that "answer[s] everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13). Biopolitics names a real and important phenomenon but should not be inflated into a master concept. It captures population-level rationalities of health, fertility, and longevity; it does not replace the disciplinary analysis of individual bodies or the structural analysis of capital.

I find the statement convincing on balance. Contemporary societies are saturated by biopolitical rationalities, from the salt-reduction targets on the side of a soup tin to the 5-a-day banner in a school canteen. But "saturated" does not mean "exhausted by". Biopolitics is one rationality among several; it is unevenly distributed; and it is constantly contested. Foucault's concept is most useful when it sharpens our perception of these uneven, contested politics of life, rather than when it is wielded as a totalising diagnosis.

### 2023 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing?

Foucault's intervention into the sociology of power was, at root, a question about method. Traditional sociology had asked **who** holds power: the state, the ruling class, the patriarch, the king. Foucault wanted to ask **how** power operates. As Slide 6 of the lecture puts it, paraphrasing Foucault, "if we want to know what power is, then we need to understand HOW power relations operate in society." The shift sounds modest but reorganises the entire field of analysis.

I will set out Foucault's challenge under three headings (what he rejects, what he proposes, and what follows) and then assess how convincing it is.

What he rejects. The lecture's two named foils are clearly identified. The **liberal/Enlightenment** model treats power as a thing, possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom, and so something to be seized, shared, or constrained (Slide 34). First-wave feminism, the lecture notes, shares this structure: power should be redistributed between men and women. The **Marxist** model treats power as flowing from ownership of the means of production, concentrated in a wealthy minority, and exercised over the working class principally through ideology (Slide 35). Both, Foucault argues, mistake "terminal forms" of power for its essence. Both fail to analyse the **mechanics** by which power is exercised. The Marxist version receives Foucault's sharpest rebuke: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116).

What he proposes. Foucault counters with what Lynch calls an "analytics of power". Power is a "multiplicity of force relations immanent in the sphere in which they operate" (Foucault, 1978: 92, cited in Lynch, 2011: 21). Several propositions follow. Power is exercised, not possessed. Power comes from below, from the micro-relations of everyday life, and produces, through aggregation, the larger structures we recognise as states and hegemonies (Lynch, 2011: 25-26). Power is intentional but non-subjective: tactics have aims, but no single agent controls the overall effect (Lynch, 2011: 26-27). Power is omnipresent, "always already there" (Lynch, 2011: 19), and so resistance is internal to it. Disciplinary power and biopower are the specifically modern forms of this generalised mechanics. The panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, is the architectural diagram (Foucault, 1977: 314-316 in Calhoun reader). Power is "much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

What follows. Methodologically, sociology should examine the school register, the progress report, the medical scan, the body in the mirror, the questionnaire ("Am I different from the rest?", Slide 21): the everyday techniques through which conduct is shaped. The human sciences themselves (psychiatry, pedagogy, the medical clinic) should be treated not as innocent knowledge but as part of the apparatus of power/knowledge. The subject is not the starting point but the product of power.

How convincing? I find this challenge largely convincing, with three positive points and two reservations.

The first positive: the empirical reach is remarkable. The lecture itself lists schools (registers, progress reports, school photos, key stages), prisons (the panopticon), the body (Bordo's slender norm, gym membership in the City of London), the medical clinic (Kember on ultrasound), and the modern police (urban supplies, hygiene, health, Slide 37). None of these phenomena is well explained by a top-down model of power. The Foucauldian vocabulary fits them. Lynch's own examples reinforce the reach: the academic transcript "begins from observation at the bottom of the pyramid, not from an edict from the top" (Lynch, 2011: 15), and even the everyday question of what to wear is shaped by capillary, extra-legal power relations (Lynch, 2011: 23).

The second positive: the productive thesis is genuinely original. Power produces kinds of people, kinds of knowledge, kinds of bodies. As Slide 13 puts it, "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects) were created or produced through the creation [of] new institutions such as schools, prisons, psychiatric hospitals." The modern prisoner is not a pre-existing criminal whom the prison contains; the prison produces him as a particular kind of subject. Bordo extends this to gender: the well-toned female body of contemporary culture is itself produced by specific disciplinary configurations of media, gym, and self-talk (Bordo, 1993).

The third positive: the linkage of knowledge and power answers a real problem. The Kember example crystallises it. The ultrasound is at once a knowledge artefact (an image of the foetus) and a power artefact (the maternal body's displacement from view). Calling this "ideology" misses the point; "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

The reservations. First, the diffuse model can underplay concentrated, spectacular power. The lecture itself notes that disciplinary power displaces but does not abolish sovereign power; the public torture of Damiens (Foucault, 1977; Slide 8) is exhibit A for a form of power that has not entirely vanished. Foucauldian analysis must work harder to capture spectacular and concentrated exercises of force than a structural model does. Second, the question of agency. If "power forms the subject" (Butler, 1997: 1-2), what grounds resistance? Foucault answers that resistance is internal to power, and Lynch reinforces the point: power relations "can always be altered" through resistance (Lynch, 2011: 28). But the answer remains thinner than a fully developed normative theory.

My overall verdict: Foucault's challenge is convincing as a transformation of what sociology can see, less convincing as a complete replacement for traditional accounts. The lecture's own closing flourish, that docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy" and that "the bourgeoisie has won!" (Slide 23), suggests that Foucault's disciplinary analysis benefits from being held in tension with a Marxist analysis of capital rather than replacing it. Foucault expanded the toolkit; he did not (and probably did not intend to) close it.

### 2022 (Q10): Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as "disciplinary". What did he mean by this? How useful do you find this term?

When Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as disciplinary, he meant something quite specific: a historically new mode of power, emerging in Western societies in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, that operates through training rather than force, through surveillance rather than spectacle, through normalisation rather than prohibition, and that produces subjects who regulate themselves.

I want to set out what he meant under five headings, then assess the term's usefulness.

First, **disciplinary power is contrasted with sovereign power**. The opening of *Discipline and Punish* describes the gruesome public execution of Damiens the regicide, a spectacle of corporal punishment that displayed the king's power over the criminal's body (Foucault, 1977; Slide 8). Within decades, this had largely vanished; in its place came the prison timetable, the school register, and the hospital ward round. The shift, Foucault argues, is not from cruelty to humanity but from one logic of power to another. Sovereign power was concentrated, visible, intermittent, and worked by abusing the body. Disciplinary power is dispersed, subtle, continuous, and works by training the body (Slide 10).

Second, **disciplinary power is capillary**. It operates not principally through the state but through everyday institutions (schools, hospitals, factories, asylums, families, barracks). As Foucault writes, "discipline may be identified neither with an institution nor with an apparatus; it is a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The lecture's canonical illustration is the school's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10); Lynch's worked example is the academic transcript, whose famous warning ("this will go on your permanent record") captures the disciplinary mode (Lynch, 2011: 15-16).

Third, **disciplinary power works through three techniques** the lecture isolates: hierarchical observation (we are watched from positions of asymmetric invisibility, Slide 19), normalising judgement (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal", itself "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity", Slide 22), and the examination (the documented combination of the two).

Fourth, **its architectural figure is the panopticon**. Bentham's design places "a supervisor in a central tower" and shuts "in each cell a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader); the design achieves "the major effect" of inducing "in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). Because the inmate cannot tell when they are being watched, they internalise the gaze.

Fifth, **its product is the docile body**: useful, obedient, self-regulating. Such bodies, Foucault notes in the lecture's account, "fit well into the modern capitalist economy and work well in factories and offices. They question little and behave well. And don't resist. Let alone revolt" (Slide 23). They do not need to be coerced because they have been trained.

How useful do I find the term? Very useful, for four reasons, but with three limits.

Useful, first, because it names something traditional sociology missed. Marxist theories of ideology and liberal theories of state power cannot adequately describe the school register, the progress report, the medical scan, the bathroom-mirror appraisal of the body. All these are disciplinary in Foucault's sense.

Useful, second, because it captures contemporary intensifications. The lecture itself names the gym, the diet industry, and the body imagery of consumer culture as paradigmatic. Slide 28 reports that in the City of London "it is important to 'work-out' to go to the gym, to show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life", and Bordo's analysis makes the more general point: norms of "trim", "well-toned", and "well-defined" bodies now reach "into the very insides of our bodies" (Bordo, 1993; Slide 27). These are recognisable, even paradigmatic, disciplinary phenomena.

Useful, third, because it illuminates how we participate in our own discipline. The dieter, the gym-goer, the person taking a self-evaluative questionnaire ("Am I different from the rest?", Slide 21) is not coerced. They monitor themselves. As Bordo writes, "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) is the condition of contemporary embodiment.

Useful, fourth, because it provides a vocabulary for resistance. If discipline runs through everyday techniques, then everyday techniques are sites of contestation. Bordo's own analysis is implicitly such a contestation: by historicising the slender body (Slide 25 documents how plumpness once signalled male success, muscle signalled working-class status, and pale skin for women signalled exemption from field labour), she denaturalises the present norm and so opens it to challenge.

Limits. First, the term can homogenise. The disciplinary techniques of a nineteenth-century prison are not the same as those of a twenty-first-century gym; lumping them risks losing historical specificity. Second, disciplinary power does not exhaust contemporary power. The lecture's biopolitical material (the Slide 24 injunctions about salt, 5-a-day, and obesity policy) targets the population as much as the individual, and sovereign power has not vanished, the Damiens scene at the opening of *Discipline and Punish* haunts every contemporary spectacular display of state force. Third, the term has been popularised to the point of becoming a slogan; "disciplinary society" sometimes covers everything anyone disapproves of.

My overall view: the term remains one of the most productive in twentieth-century social theory, but it is most useful when wielded with historical precision and combined with other analytics. Contemporary society is partly disciplinary, partly biopolitical, partly residually sovereign, and Foucault's term captures one essential strand among these.

### 2022 (Q11): How useful is Foucault's notion of biopower for understanding contemporary society?

Foucault's notion of biopower, the term Lynch identifies alongside disciplinary power as one of the two "closely related modes" of modern power (Lynch, 2011: 13), names a form of power that operates not on individual bodies, as discipline does, but on populations as biological collectives. Lynch's clearest illustration is epidemiological: "observing which groups in the population are most likely to contract a disease (such as lung cancer) can lead to a discovery of its causes (cigarette smoking, or asbestos exposure)" (Lynch, 2011: 16). The rationality is "to save lives" by acting on aggregate populations rather than by punishing the individual; biopower "does not directly address particular individuals, but rather groups of people and populations as a whole" (Lynch, 2011: 16).

I will argue that the concept is highly useful for understanding contemporary society but with three caveats: it must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power; it operates through productive moral norms as much as through state institutions; and it requires careful attention to the everyday register where it shows up.

On the affirmative side. Biopower illuminates phenomena the lecture itself foregrounds.

Public-health injunctions are the clearest case. Slide 24 is built around the proposition "It is our duty to keep ourselves healthy" and unpacks the biopolitical mechanisms that support it: "it is the duty of government to make us reduce our salt level"; "we feel bad if we do not eat 5 portions of fruit/veg per day"; "the government must stop obese children." None of these target a particular individual; all of them act on the population through dietary norms, school food policy, public-health campaigns, and the moralisation of self-management. Slide 37 supplies the historical genealogy via the Foucault quotation that the modern police were instituted not only for law and order but "for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). The contemporary biopolitical state inherits this longer logic.

Reproductive medicine, in the Kember example, is biopolitical par excellence. The ultrasound scan, the antenatal screening programme, and the apparatus of foetal visibility act on the population's reproductive future as much as on any one woman. Kember's observation that "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998) captures how biopolitical visibility can render some bodies hyper-visible (the foetus) while erasing others (the mother). This is the lecture's opening example, returned to at the close (Slide 38) once the biopolitical apparatus has been named.

The disciplinary/biopolitical body norms Bordo analyses also operate at the population level. Slide 28 makes the move explicit: "more demands are made on women than men in terms of them producing themselves as 'normal'", the rise of gym membership turns body work into a population-wide expectation, and consumer culture (Slide 32) "runs on us drinking coffee, eating cakes, take-away food etc." while demanding that "we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves." Public-health framings of obesity and the population-level injunctions of Slide 24 sit on top of this everyday biopolitical regime.

Now the caveats.

First, biopower does not stand alone. The lecture is clear that disciplinary and biopolitical registers coexist: the school applies disciplinary techniques to each pupil (registers, progress reports, school photos) and biopolitical interventions to the cohort (height-and-weight screening, 5-a-day campaigning). Lynch summarises the pairing as "a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26). Conflating these flattens the analysis.

Second, sovereign power persists. The brutal public torture of Damiens at the opening of *Discipline and Punish* (Slide 8) describes a power that visibly seizes the body of the criminal. Foucault's claim is that disciplinary and biopolitical regimes have layered over this, not that they have abolished it. Wherever the state still exercises violence visibly on bodies (corporal punishment, capital punishment, military force), sovereign power remains operative.

Third, biopower in the lecture works through productive moral norms as much as through state apparatus. Bordo's Slide 29 quotation, "Self-management in consumer culture...becomes more elusive as it becomes more pressing...the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3), names how the population is governed through aspirational images and felt failures rather than through coercion. Butler's reading of subject formation (Butler, 1997: 1-2) extends the point: power is what we "depend on for our existence", so its biopolitical operations are felt as our own projects.

A final qualification: biopower is resisted. Foucault insists that "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95), and Lynch reinforces the point: power relations "can always be altered" because resistance is internal to them (Lynch, 2011: 28). Biopolitical norms (about food, weight, fertility, health) provoke biopolitical contestation.

My verdict: biopower is one of the most useful concepts contemporary social theory has. It names something we would otherwise miss, the politics of life itself, the management of populations through diet, hygiene, antenatal care, and the moralisation of bodily self-management. But its usefulness depends on careful articulation with disciplinary and sovereign forms. Used carelessly, it explains too much; used carefully, it explains exactly what other vocabularies cannot.

### 2021 (Q10): Do we live in a "disciplinary society"? Use the work of Foucault to explain your response.

We do live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's specific sense, though we also live in a biopolitical and partially sovereign one. My answer is "yes, but not exclusively", and the qualification matters.

To answer the question I need first to clarify what Foucault means by a disciplinary society. The concept is articulated in *Discipline and Punish* and refers, as the Calhoun reader extract makes clear, to "the formation of a disciplinary society in this movement that stretches from the enclosed disciplines, a sort of social 'quarantine', to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316). The disciplinary society is one in which the techniques first developed within enclosed institutions (prisons, barracks, hospitals, schools) generalise across the social field, becoming the dominant mode through which conduct is shaped. The crucial features, set out in the lecture, are: power operates through everyday practices, not principally through state edict; it works by surveillance, normalisation, and examination rather than by physical coercion; it produces docile bodies that regulate themselves against invented norms; and it is productive (generating subjects, knowledges, and institutions), not merely repressive (Slides 10, 13, 19-23).

Now the affirmative case.

Schools first. The school is the lecture's paradigmatic disciplinary institution. Slide 10 names its instruments: "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages." Each is an instrument of hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and examination. The pupil is observed continually, measured against age-appropriate norms, and documented in records that follow them through life. Lynch's worked example crystallises the logic: the academic transcript is the canonical disciplinary instrument because "the fact that each one is observed (and not that the school has a principal), is what influences students' behaviour" (Lynch, 2011: 16).

The body. Bordo's analysis, extended through Slides 25-32, makes the body the second canonical site of disciplinary society. Body norms have shifted historically, in the late nineteenth century a plump male body signalled success and pale female skin signalled exemption from field labour (Slide 25); thirty years ago the imperative was to diet and "fight the flab"; today it is to be "slender", "in trim", "well-toned", "well-defined" (Slide 27). Across these shifts the disciplinary form persists: the body is held against a population norm, the subject self-monitors, and failure registers as moral failing. The lecture's example of the City of London gym (Slide 28) makes the disciplinary logic concrete: "it is important to 'work-out' to go to the gym, to show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life."

The medical clinic. The lecture's opening example, Kember on obstetric ultrasound, is a contemporary disciplinary scene: the foetus is rendered visible and the maternal body disappears, an arrangement that "raises the question of power and its relation to knowledge" (Slide 3). The scan is not coercive; it is normalised, expected, and clinically beneficial, which is exactly what makes it disciplinary in Foucault's sense.

Knowledge and expertise. The lecture's productive thesis applies in full. New institutions of the nineteenth century, schools, prisons, psychiatric hospitals, produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)" (Slide 13). The modern prisoner, the schoolchild, the psychiatric patient are not pre-existing kinds that the institution catches but products of disciplinary apparatuses. Butler extends this: "we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Contemporary society remains saturated by such subject-forming institutions.

So yes, by every test Foucault would apply, contemporary society in the lecture's sense is a disciplinary society. The lecture's claim that "contemporary society is a 'disciplinary society'" (Slide 10) stands.

The qualifications. First, we also live in a biopolitical society. Slide 24's catalogue of injunctions, salt reduction, 5-a-day, anti-obesity policy aimed at children, targets the population, not the individual. Slide 37's police-genealogy quote ("for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health", Foucault, 1982: 215) shows the longer biopolitical arc. Disciplinary techniques work on bodies; biopolitical techniques work on populations. Both are present.

Second, sovereign power has not vanished. The lecture is explicit that disciplinary power "displaces" sovereign power but does not abolish it (Slide 8). Wherever the state still seizes bodies visibly (corporal punishment, capital punishment, military force), sovereign power persists alongside discipline.

Third, the disciplinary society is unevenly distributed. Bordo's analysis already shows that the slender-body norm bears more heavily on women than on men (Slide 28: "more demands are made on women than men in terms of them producing themselves as 'normal'"). Race, class, and gender stratify who is most intensively disciplined.

Fourth, resistance exists. Foucault's insistence that "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95) is reinforced by Lynch: power relations "can always be altered" because resistance is constitutive of them (Lynch, 2011: 28). Bordo's own analysis is implicitly a disciplinary counter-movement: by historicising the slender norm she denaturalises it and so opens it to challenge.

My answer, then: we live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's sense, but the disciplinary register is one among several. The school register, the medical scan, the body in the gym, the questionnaire that asks whether we are "different from the rest" (Slide 21): all confirm Foucault's thesis. But the spectacular execution that opens *Discipline and Punish* and the population-level injunctions of Slide 24 require sovereign and biopolitical analyses alongside it. The right answer to the exam question is yes, with the right qualifications attached.

### 2021 (Q11): Outline and assess Foucault's discussions of power in terms of the governance of a population.

Foucault's discussions of the governance of a population centre on his concept of biopower, identified by Lynch as one of the two "closely related modes" of modern power that emerged alongside the older "sovereign power" (Lynch, 2011: 13). Biopower names a form of power that takes the population, rather than the individual body, as its object. It complements the disciplinary power Foucault analyses in *Discipline and Punish*, and together the two constitute what Lynch calls the two poles of modern power, "a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26).

I will outline biopower in three steps, then assess it.

First, the historical claim. Foucault argues that from the late eighteenth century a new form of power emerged alongside discipline. Where the sovereign had exercised the right to abuse or kill the body of the criminal (as in the spectacular execution of Damiens that opens *Discipline and Punish*, Slide 8), modern states began to manage the vital processes of populations. The lecture's biopolitical material is concentrated in Slide 24's catalogue of duties: "it is the duty of government to make us reduce our salt level"; "we feel bad if we do not eat 5 portions of fruit/veg per day"; "the government must stop obese children." These are population-level interventions whose object is the species body, not the individual.

Second, the conceptual structure. Lynch's illustration captures the basic shape: "observing which groups in the population are most likely to contract a disease (such as lung cancer) can lead to a discovery of its causes (cigarette smoking, or asbestos exposure)" (Lynch, 2011: 16). The knowledge is statistical, the intervention is aggregate, and the rationality is "to save lives" by acting on the population. Where disciplinary power individualises (each pupil has a transcript, each prisoner has a cell), biopower massifies (the cohort has a literacy rate, the population has its smoking-related mortality figures). Foucault's quip that "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order, nor for assisting governments in their struggle against their enemies, but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215; Slide 37) captures the genealogy: the modern "police" originally meant the management of urban life, of which crime control was only one piece.

Third, the link to subject formation. Biopower does not bypass the subject; it forms her differently. Bordo's analysis of contemporary body norms (Slides 25-32) shows how biopolitical health imperatives are taken up at the level of the embodied self. The City-of-London worker who goes to the gym to "show you 'mean business'" (Slide 28) is enacting a biopolitical injunction as personal aspiration. Consumer culture, which "runs on us drinking coffee, eating cakes, take-away food etc." while demanding "we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves" (Slide 32), is biopolitical in form even when it presents as private choice.

Now the assessment.

Strengths. Biopower captures something traditional theories miss. The public-health regime of salt-reduction targets, 5-a-day campaigns, and anti-obesity policy (Slide 24) is not well described by either "the ruling class wills it" or "the state forbids it." It is biopolitical: it takes the population as object and aims to optimise it. The reproductive scene Kember describes, "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998), is biopolitical in its rendering of the antenatal population as the unit of governance. The contemporary body-discipline regime Bordo analyses is biopolitical in the way it organises population-wide aspirations toward slenderness, tone, and control (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

A second strength: biopower illuminates the productive side of modern power. Slide 37's police quote captures it: the police "not only" repress but also "assure urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). Power produces healthy, useful populations, not merely punished individuals.

A third strength: biopower clarifies the productive-thesis Slide 13 articulates. The "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)" produced by nineteenth-century institutions include not only individuals (the prisoner, the schoolchild) but population categories (the delinquent, the abnormal, the at-risk). Both registers are productive.

Weaknesses. First, the concept can be over-extended. Lynch warns explicitly against using theory to "answer everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13). Biopower names a real phenomenon but should not be inflated into a master concept.

Second, biopower must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power. Inside a school, disciplinary techniques (registers, examinations) operate alongside biopolitical ones (5-a-day campaigns reaching children through school meals). The spectacular punishment of Damiens (Slide 8) is the trace of a sovereign register that persists. To pick one is to miss the others.

Third, the question of agency. If populations are constituted by biopower, what is the locus of biopolitical agency? Foucault's answer ("where there is power, there is resistance", 1978: 95) is correct and Lynch reinforces it: "in the relations of power, there is necessarily the possibility of resistance" (Lynch, 2011: 28-29). Bordo's diachronic historicisation of body norms is itself a biopolitical resistance: by showing that the slender body of today is no more natural than the plump body of 1880 (Slide 25), she denaturalises the present norm and opens it to contestation.

My assessment: Foucault's discussions of the governance of a population through biopower are essential to contemporary social theory but not sufficient. They illuminate what other vocabularies cannot, the politics of life itself, the everyday management of populations through diet, hygiene, antenatal care, and the moralisation of bodily self-management. But they need supplementing with disciplinary analysis of the individual body and with attention to the residual sovereign register. Used in this way, biopower remains one of the most powerful concepts in the toolkit.

## 8. Mock Exam Questions and Answers

### Mock Q1 (quote-driven): "Visibility is a trap." Discuss with reference to Foucault.

The line "visibility is a trap" appears in *Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader) at the moment Foucault is unpacking what Bentham's panopticon actually does. The trap is not that the inmate is seen (pre-modern sovereign power saw bodies too, in the spectacle of the scaffold) but that the inmate is rendered visible while the watcher is rendered invisible, so the inmate must assume continuous observation and behave accordingly even when no one is in the tower. My argument is that the line is a compressed theory of disciplinary power, and that taking it seriously means tracking how the trap operates in the architecture of the panopticon, in nineteenth-century institutions, in Bordo's account of the contemporary gendered body, and in the clinical scene of the ultrasound that opens the lecture.

First, the original setting. Foucault uses the line to explain why Bentham's diagram induces "in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). The trap has three components: asymmetric visibility ("in the peripheric ring, one is totally seen, without ever seeing; in the central tower, one sees everything without ever being seen", Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader), the unverifiability of monitoring ("the inmate must never know whether he is being looked at at any one moment; but he must be sure that he may always be so", Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader), and the internalisation of the gaze (the inmate becomes her own watcher). The trap shuts when the inmate begins to self-regulate, at which point the central tower can in principle be empty.

Second, the institutional generalisation. The lecture insists, with the Calhoun reader, that the panoptic logic is "an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The school register, the progress report, the school photo, the key-stage test (Slide 10) all share the panoptic structure: the pupil is rendered continually visible to monitors whose own monitoring is not symmetrically visible to the pupil. Lynch's worked example captures the same logic in compressed form: "this will go on your permanent record" turns the academic transcript into a panoptic trap (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). Foucault's own listing makes the generalisation explicit: the cell can hold "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader), the same diagram across institutions.

Third, the body. Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" shows how the trap is gendered and extends beyond institutions into everyday self-relation. The norms of slenderness, tone, and control turn the bathroom mirror into a tower and the subject into her own watcher. "Bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) are the trap closed at the level of selfhood. The lecture's example of City-of-London gym membership (Slide 28), where one works out "to show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life", is the trap in its contemporary form: no foreman is watching, but the subject behaves as if always under inspection. The diachronic move in Bordo's analysis sharpens the diagnosis: the trap operated differently in the late nineteenth century, when plumpness signalled male success and pale skin signalled women's exemption from field labour (Slide 25), but the trap-structure was the same.

Fourth, the clinical scene. Kember's ultrasound is the lecture's contemporary panoptic illustration. "The scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998). The clinical gaze is asymmetric (the doctor sees what the mother cannot), unverifiable in its routine application (the protocols are opaque to the patient), and productive of self-monitoring conduct on the part of the antenatal subject. Read through Slide 5's question, "can we use such notions of ideology to fully understand how power operates in modernity?", the trap is exactly what ideology critique misses: "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

Fifth, the limits. The trap diagram does not exhaust all power. Sovereign power, exemplified by the spectacular execution of Damiens at the opening of *Discipline and Punish* (Slide 8), still operates wherever the state visibly seizes bodies; biopower, exemplified by Slide 24's injunctions about salt, fruit and veg, and obesity, acts on populations rather than on individual bodies in cells. Where the field is collective and statistical rather than individual and observational, the panoptic trap is the wrong diagram. Lynch makes the general warning: theory should not "answer everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13), and the trap is best treated as the diagram of one mode of power, not of all power.

Sixth, the politics. The strength of the trap as a critical figure is that it captures the productive character of disciplinary power: the trap does not just constrain, it makes subjects. The school produces the pupil, the prison produces the modern prisoner (Slide 13), the gym produces the well-toned body. Butler's reading sharpens the productive thesis: power is "what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). The weakness is that the metaphor can flatten into a generalised cynicism. If every visibility is a trap, even reciprocal visibility, then we lose the political distinction between accountability of power and surveillance of the powerless.

My argument is that "visibility is a trap" is correct as a description of the disciplinary mode, indispensable as a diagnosis of how self-regulation is engineered, and incomplete as a complete theory of contemporary observation. Read alongside Bordo's account of the gendered body and Kember's account of clinical visibility, the trap remains one of the most useful images in social theory.

### Mock Q2 (named-work): Critically assess Foucault's argument in *Discipline and Punish*.

*Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977) is built around a historical claim (that Western societies shifted from sovereign to disciplinary power over the long nineteenth century) and a diagnostic claim (that the techniques of the prison, articulated in the panopticon, became "an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader) that runs through schools, hospitals, factories, and the army). The question this essay tackles is how convincing the argument is, both on its own terms and in light of the lecture's contemporary illustrations.

I argue that *Discipline and Punish* is convincing at the level of technique, partially vindicated at the level of subjectivity, and deliberately incomplete at the level of political economy, which Foucault brackets in favour of analytic precision.

Vindication at the level of technique. The lecture's account of the school operationalises the three techniques of discipline with paradigmatic clarity. Registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, and key stages (Slide 10) instantiate hierarchical observation (pupils are watched from positions of asymmetric invisibility), normalising judgement (deviations from age-appropriate "normal" performance are flagged), and the examination (the documented combination of the two). Foucault's claim in the Calhoun extract is even more general: the same cellular arrangement can hold "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). The panopticon is a diagram, not a building, and the diagram operates wherever continuous, asymmetric, individualising visibility produces self-regulation.

Lynch's worked illustration captures the same logic in compressed form. The academic transcript "begins from observation at the bottom of the pyramid, not from an edict from the top" (Lynch, 2011: 15); the disciplinary power of the warning "this will go on your permanent record" comes not from any authority's command but from the routine fact that "each one is observed" (Lynch, 2011: 16). Power, on this account, has its principle "not so much in a person as in a certain concerted distribution of bodies, surfaces, lights, gazes" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The "physics of power" Foucault calls an "anatomy" is recognisable wherever observation, normalisation, and examination combine.

Vindication of the productive thesis. Slide 13 puts it sharply: in the nineteenth century, "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects) were created or produced through the creation [of] new institutions such as schools, prisons, psychiatric hospitals." The modern prisoner, the schoolchild, and the psychiatric patient are not pre-existing kinds that the institution catches; they are produced by it. Butler extends the productive thesis to the formation of the subject as such: "we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Bordo extends it to gender: the slender, well-toned body of contemporary culture is produced by specific disciplinary configurations of mirror, magazine, gym, and self-talk (Bordo, 1993; Slide 28).

Strain at the level of subjectivity. *Discipline and Punish* assumes a relatively unified subject who is then examined and normalised. Butler's *Psychic Life of Power* pushes Foucault's account into a stronger position: power not only acts on the subject but constitutes it as a subject, so resistance cannot stand on a pre-power self. The lecture cites Butler precisely for this purpose (Slide 12). The strain is productive: it forces Foucault's reader to think about subject formation more carefully than the early framework demands.

Brackets at the level of political economy. Foucault's deliberate move in *Discipline and Punish* is to bracket the question "who benefits?" in order to ask "how does it work?" The lecture's own closing flourish on Slide 23 hints at the cost of this bracketing: "Such docile bodies fit well into the modern capitalist economy and work well in factories and offices. They question little and behave well. And don't resist. Let alone revolt. The bourgeoisie has won!" The disciplinary apparatus serves capital; the analysis of *how* it works needs to be paired with a Marxist analysis of *for whom* it works. Foucault's response to Marxism is on Slide 36: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). The right reading is that disciplinary analysis and Marxist analysis are complementary, not rivals.

Three further considerations sharpen the assessment. First, the productive register: Foucault's police-genealogy quote on Slide 37 reminds us that disciplinary power also "assures urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215); it produces useful populations as well as docile individuals, and so opens onto biopolitics. Second, the resistance register: Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95), and Lynch reinforces the point by stressing that resistance is internal to power and that "power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Third, the gendered register: Bordo shows that disciplinary norms bear more heavily on women's bodies than on men's (Slide 28), a specification *Discipline and Punish* itself did not develop.

My verdict: *Discipline and Punish* names what is happening with unique precision in the school, the prison, the hospital, the army, the clinic, and the body. It is convincing at the level of technique and productive subject formation; it is incomplete at the level of political economy by deliberate choice; and it benefits from being read alongside Bordo on the gendered body and Butler on the formation of the subject. Foucault gives us the anatomy; the structural critique remains the necessary supplement.

### Mock Q3 (two-part): What does Foucault mean by power/knowledge, and why does he treat the two as inseparable?

This question has two parts and I will answer them in order, then connect them through the examples the lecture provides.

Part one: what does Foucault mean by power/knowledge? The hyphen carries the argument. By power/knowledge Foucault means that the production of human-scientific knowledge (psychiatric, pedagogical, clinical, demographic) is itself a power operation, and that the exercise of power on bodies and populations cannot proceed without producing knowledge about them. The lecture cues the concept on Slide 3, where the answer to the question "what has this got to do with Foucault?" is "It raises the question of power and its relation to knowledge." Slide 15 then names the inseparability explicitly: in the panopticon "power is linked to visibility and vision and to knowledge." In "The Subject and Power" Foucault summarises: this power "imposes a law of truth on him which he must recognize and which others must recognize in him. It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212). Power/knowledge is the name for this fusion of subject-making with knowledge production.

Part two: why are they inseparable? Foucault gives three reasons that the lecture and Lynch's reading make clear.

(i) Bodies cannot be disciplined without being known. The school cannot train pupils without "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10); each of these is a knowledge instrument and a disciplinary instrument simultaneously. Lynch's worked example, the academic transcript, makes the same point: it is "the record of a student's courses and performance" and an "instrument of power" in the same artefact (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). The hospital cannot treat without the chart; the modern prison cannot reform without the case file; the panopticon "opens up an interiority into which both the prison guard and the inmate can 'look'" (Slide 15).

(ii) Knowledge in the human sciences cannot proceed without observed, classified, and normalised subjects. Slide 13's productive thesis spells this out: nineteenth-century institutions (schools, prisons, psychiatric hospitals) produced both new knowledges and "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)". The two productions were inseparable. Criminology required prisons; medical knowledge required clinics; pedagogy required schools. The object of human-scientific knowledge is itself produced by the practices that claim merely to describe it.

(iii) The truths produced by power/knowledge are not false in the Marxist sense of ideology. Foucault explicitly rejects the equation. Slide 5 closes with the key citation: "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). Slide 10 reinforces it: power "points out what is normal and invites us to behave in a similar way, to judge ourselves in relation to this 'norm'." Truth, on this account, has a history and a politics, not because it is fake but because it is produced.

Why does this matter? Because it dissolves a picture social theory had inherited from the Enlightenment, the picture in which knowledge is the neutral mirror of nature, and power is what either obscures or applies that knowledge. Foucault is not saying that all truth is power or that science is just politics. He is saying that the human sciences in particular cannot be cleanly separated from the institutions that produced and use them.

Examples from the lecture. The Kember scene is the canonical illustration. The obstetric ultrasound is a knowledge artefact (an image of the foetus) and a power artefact (the maternal body's displacement) in the same clinical encounter: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998). The image is knowledge; what it does is power. Slide 4 immediately presses the productive ambiguity: is it good that doctors can visualise the foetus? Could this make the woman seem unimportant? Both answers, the slide says, "could be 'yes'".

The body norms Bordo analyses are power/knowledge in another register. The slender body, the well-toned body, the body that "means business" in the City of London gym (Slide 28) are products of magazines, advertising, dietary science, and self-help discourse, all of which are knowledge regimes whose effect is to make particular bodies and to invalidate others. Slide 22's question, "Is the concept of the 'normal' one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity?", names the power-effect of an apparently neutral statistical category.

Slide 18 supplies the Foucault quote that gathers the argument: power "categorizes the individual, marks him by his own individuality, attaches him to his identity, imposes a law of truth on him which he must recognize and which others must recognize in him. It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212). Knowledge of the individual and power over the individual are produced together.

The inseparability is essential to Foucault's politics. If knowledge were neutral, critique could appeal to "the science". If power were merely repressive, resistance could appeal to "the truth". Power/knowledge denies both shortcuts. Critique has to engage with how knowledge is produced and what it does, simultaneously.

My conclusion: power/knowledge is inseparable not by definition but by historical analysis. The thesis applies most cleanly to the human sciences; whether it applies to physics is contested. Regimes of truth can still be better or worse; Foucault was not a strong relativist. The Kember example, the school's testing regime, and the Bordo body norms each show the inseparability in action, and the analysis remains alive every time a new diagnostic category, an examination, or a body ideal is introduced.

### Mock Q4 (specific-comparison): Compare Foucault's account of disciplinary power with Marx's account of ideology.

This essay compares two competing accounts of how modern societies hold their members in place: Marx's concept of ideology, which the lecture sets out on Slide 35 as the Marxist counterpart to Foucault's analysis; and Foucault's concept of disciplinary power, articulated in *Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977). I will set out each, locate the points of overlap, mark the decisive differences, and argue that the two operate at different registers and are best used together rather than against each other.

Marx's account of ideology. The lecture's Slide 35 summarises the traditional Marxist view: "Power is solely related to the ownership of the means of production"; "Power is in the hands of a small, wealthy minority"; "Ideology is one main tool by which power is exercised over the working-class in capitalist societies"; and "The proletariat need to 'discover' their own power and overthrow the current power arrangements of the bourgeoisie." Ideology is the form of class domination in the realm of ideas: workers come to share the worldview of the ruling class, the structural realities of exploitation are obscured, and the political work of socialism is to dispel that obscuring through revolutionary consciousness.

Foucault's account of disciplinary power. For Foucault, the older repressive picture of power (power as constraint exercised by a ruling group on subordinates) misses the most distinctive feature of modern power. Disciplinary power operates not principally through cognition but through bodies: through the timetable, the partition of space, the examination, hierarchical observation, and normalising judgement (Slide 19, 21). It produces docile bodies: useful, obedient, and self-regulating (Slide 23). The school child, the soldier, the patient, the worker, the prisoner are all docile bodies produced by their respective institutions; the panopticon's cell can hold "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). Disciplinary power does not need workers to believe in capitalism; it needs them to arrive on time, follow procedure, and submit to examination. As Foucault puts it polemically, power "is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

Convergences. Both insist that what looks like free choice is heavily structured, both reject liberal autonomy, and both link the production of subjects to historically specific institutions. The lecture's closing flourish on Slide 23 holds both in view: docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy and work well in factories and offices. They question little and behave well. And don't resist. Let alone revolt. The bourgeoisie has won!" The disciplinary apparatus serves capital, and the convergence is real.

Four decisive differences. First, the register: ideology operates on consciousness, disciplinary power on bodies. The schoolchild does not need to believe in the moral worth of the key-stage test; she needs to sit it. Second, the location: ideology is concentrated in apparatuses controlled by the ruling class, disciplinary power is capillary and "always already there" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch, 2011: 19). Foucault explicitly accuses Marxists: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116; Slide 36). Third, the truth: ideology is false consciousness masking a class reality; disciplinary power produces regimes of truth and Foucault claims no standpoint outside them. Fourth, the agent: ideology presupposes a subject who could be undeceived; disciplinary power produces the subject, and Butler extends the point: power "is not simply what we oppose, but also...what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). There is no pre-disciplinary subject waiting to be liberated.

Worked examples from the lecture. The school's regime of registers, progress reports, and key stages (Slide 10): ideology asks whose interests are served by mass schooling (capital's, in producing a literate and compliant workforce); discipline asks how the school's instruments examine each pupil, normalise her against age-appropriate standards, and produce her as a particular kind of subject. The Kember ultrasound scene: ideology asks whether obstetric medicine is patriarchal (a question Slide 5 acknowledges is "not exactly untrue"); discipline asks how the scan reorganises visibility and produces the foetus and the mother as different kinds of subject (Slide 5: "it is both much more and much less than ideology"). Bordo on the slender body (Slides 25-32): ideology asks whose interests are served by women's body discipline (capital, the slimming industry, patriarchy); discipline asks how the mirror, the magazine, and the gym produce the self-monitoring subject "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

My argument is that Foucault did not refute Marx; he changed the question. Marx asks who benefits and how consent is secured. Foucault asks how techniques work and how subjects are produced. The lecture itself holds both registers in view: Slide 23 names the capitalist-economic benefit of docile bodies, and Slide 35-36 frame Foucault's intervention as a methodological supplement to, not a dismissal of, the Marxist tradition. To replace ideology with disciplinary power is to lose tools; to dismiss disciplinary power as a Marxist heresy is to miss what makes modern power distinctive.

### Mock Q5 (contemporary-application): How does Foucault's notion of biopower help us think about contemporary public-health management?

Public-health management is one of the clearest contemporary expressions of biopower, the form of power Lynch describes as acting "not directly [on] particular individuals, but rather [on] groups of people and populations as a whole" (Lynch, 2011: 16). I argue that Foucault's notion of biopower supplies the indispensable vocabulary for what happens in the everyday public-health apparatus the lecture itself foregrounds, while also revealing the productive ambiguity at the heart of health governance: the same statistical care that saves lives also redistributes attention and disappears some bodies from view.

Foucault's biopower. Where disciplinary power targets the individual body and trains it to be docile and useful, biopower targets the population as a biological collective. Lynch's illustration, drawn from Foucault's own texts, is epidemiological: "observing which groups in the population are most likely to contract a disease (such as lung cancer) can lead to a discovery of its causes (cigarette smoking, or asbestos exposure)" (Lynch, 2011: 16). The rationality is to "save lives" by acting on aggregate populations rather than on individuals one by one. Disciplinary and biopolitical power are "two poles" of modern power over life, "a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26).

The lecture as biopolitical case-study. The lecture's own examples make the framework's purchase concrete.

First, the population-level duty of health. Slide 24 is built around the proposition "It is our duty to keep ourselves healthy" and unpacks the biopolitical mechanisms that support it. "It is the duty of government to make us reduce our salt level": the population's salt intake is an object of governmental intervention, and reformulation policy on processed foods acts on the species body. "We feel bad if we do not eat 5 portions of fruit/veg per day": the 5-a-day campaign is not a coercive law but a moralised population-level norm, and the felt failure registers Foucault's productive thesis at work. "The government must stop obese children": childhood obesity is a population-level problem; school meals, sugar policy, and PE provision are biopolitical interventions on the cohort. None of these target a particular individual; all act on the population.

Second, the police-genealogy of public health. Slide 37 supplies the longer historical arc with the Foucault quotation: "We should not forget that...the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order, nor for assisting governments in their struggle against their enemies, but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). The modern police, in Foucault's broader sense, is biopolitical from inception. Today's local-authority environmental-health officer, water-quality inspector, and public-health director inherit that genealogy. Power produces healthy populations, not merely punished individuals.

Third, the clinical apparatus. The Kember scene returns at the lecture's close (Slide 38) reread through the whole conceptual apparatus. The obstetric ultrasound is at once a clinical encounter with one mother and part of a population-level apparatus of antenatal screening. Kember's observation that "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998) captures how biopolitical visibility can render some bodies hyper-visible (the foetus, the population statistic) while erasing others (the maternal body). The same logic Slide 5 worries about ("Could this lead to the woman being viewed as unimportant?") generalises to the biopolitical scale: who is rendered visible and who is rendered invisible by a public-health apparatus?

Fourth, the disciplinary-biopolitical body. Bordo's analysis of body norms (Slides 25-32) shows how biopolitical health imperatives are taken up at the level of the embodied self. The City of London gym (Slide 28), where workers attend "to show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life", is the biopolitical injunction "keep yourself healthy" enacted as personal aspiration. Bordo's clinching formulation is at once disciplinary and biopolitical: "between the media images of self-containment and self-mastery and the reality of constant everyday stress...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Three productive ambiguities to flag.

(i) Biopower saves lives and redistributes attention. Slide 4's question, "Is it a good thing that doctors can 'visualize' a foetus?...Could this lead to the woman being viewed as unimportant?", makes the duality explicit. The lecturer's answer is that both answers "could be 'yes'". The biopolitical care that produces healthier populations is the same care that disappears some bodies from view.

(ii) Biopower must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power. The school applies disciplinary techniques to each pupil and biopolitical ones to the cohort (5-a-day reaching them through school meals); the spectacular punishment of Damiens (Slide 8) is the trace of a sovereign register that has not been wholly abolished. Lynch's two-pole summary (Lynch, 2011: 26) keeps both in view; the rest of modern power has not vanished beneath either pole.

(iii) Biopower is resisted. Foucault's "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95) is reinforced by Lynch: "in the relations of power, there is necessarily the possibility of resistance" (Lynch, 2011: 28-29). Bordo's diachronic historicisation of body norms is itself a biopolitical resistance: by showing that the slender body of today is no more natural than the plump body of 1880 (Slide 25), she denaturalises the present norm and opens it to contestation.

My verdict: biopower is essential for thinking about contemporary public-health management. It names the population-level rationality, the moralised self-government, and the productive but ambiguous visibility that the lecture's own examples disclose. It needs to be held in tension with disciplinary analysis of individual bodies and with attention to the residual sovereign register. Used in this way, biopower remains the most important single concept for thinking about how modern societies govern life itself.

### Mock Q6 (disjunctive): Is the panopticon a metaphor or a model? Discuss.

The disjunction the question poses (metaphor or model) turns out to be the key to reading Foucault on the panopticon correctly. My argument is that the panopticon is best understood as a diagram, which is something between metaphor and model: a schema that names the logical structure of disciplinary surveillance without claiming that any actual institution implements it perfectly, and which therefore travels productively across institutional sites.

The case for "metaphor". A metaphor figures one thing in terms of another; it illuminates by analogy. Bentham's prison stands metaphorically for modern surveillance: like the panopticon, contemporary disciplinary arrangements make us visible to an unverifiable watcher and induce self-regulation. Read as metaphor, the panopticon is rhetorically powerful but conceptually loose. The risk is that the metaphor breaks down whenever an institution lacks the central tower, the individual cell, or the human watcher.

The case for "model". A model is a structured representation that captures the essential mechanism of a phenomenon and can be tested against empirical instances. On this reading, the panopticon is Foucault's model of disciplinary power; particular institutions are evaluated for how closely they implement the model. The nineteenth-century reformative prison, the model school, the asylum, the factory, the army (all listed in the Calhoun reader as sites where discipline functions) would then be more or less panoptic. Read as model, the panopticon is conceptually rigorous but risks over-extension: if every visibility-asymmetry is panoptic, the model loses discriminating power.

The case for "diagram". The Calhoun extract supplies the right framing. The panopticon, Foucault writes, "automatizes and disindividualizes power. Power has its principle not so much in a person as in a certain concerted distribution of bodies, surfaces, lights, gazes; in an arrangement whose internal mechanisms produce the relation in which individuals are caught up" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). Discipline "may be identified neither with an institution nor with an apparatus; it is a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets; it is a 'physics' or an 'anatomy' of power, a technology" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). This is the language of diagram rather than model: a logical structure that crosses institutional sites. As Foucault continues, the disciplinary diagram can be taken over by "specialized" institutions (the penitentiary), by institutions using discipline as a means to another end (schools, hospitals), by pre-existing authorities reorganising themselves around it (the family, the administrative apparatus), or by the state itself (the police). "One can speak of the formation of a disciplinary society in this movement that stretches from the enclosed disciplines, a sort of social 'quarantine', to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The diagram generalises; specific instances vary.

Test cases from the lecture and reader.

The cell as universal container. Foucault's own list is decisive: in the panoptic cell one can place "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). The diagram does not specify what kind of subject is being formed; it specifies the conditions (asymmetric visibility, individual confinement, supervisor in a central tower) under which any subject is rendered observable, classifiable, and self-regulating.

The school. Slide 10's catalogue, "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages", instantiates the panoptic diagram without an architectural tower. There is no single guard's room from which all pupils are watched; there is a distributed apparatus of records that produces the same effect. Lynch's analysis of the academic transcript ("this will go on your permanent record", Lynch, 2011: 15-16) shows how the diagram operates capillary-fashion through paperwork.

The body. Bordo's account (Slides 25-32) shows the diagram operating without a watcher at all: the bathroom mirror becomes the tower, the woman becomes both watcher and watched, and the norms of slenderness and tone supply the unverifiable standard. "Bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) are the diagram closed at the level of the embodied self.

The clinical scene. Kember's ultrasound (Slide 2) is panoptic in the diagram's terms: asymmetric visibility (the doctor sees what the mother cannot), unverifiability of clinical evaluation, and the production of antenatal subjectivity. "The scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998).

Where the diagram strains. The biopolitical injunctions of Slide 24 ("reduce our salt level", "5 portions of fruit/veg per day", "stop obese children") target the population rather than the individual in the cell. The panoptic diagram of the individualised cell is the wrong figure here; biopower is the right one. Lynch's general warning, that theory should not "answer everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13), applies. The panoptic diagram is the diagram of one mode of power, not of all power.

My verdict: the panopticon is a diagram. As metaphor it under-claims; as model it over-claims. As diagram it does just what Foucault wanted it to do, name the disciplinary logic without specifying its empirical implementations, and leaves room for the biopolitical and sovereign diagrams that the lecture itself supplements it with. Where asymmetric visibility, unverifiability, and internalisation are in play (the school, the body, the clinic), the panopticon is the right tool; where the population rather than the individual is the target, we need the biopolitical diagram alongside it.

### Mock Q7 (topic-as-title): Bordo and the disciplinary body: from the slender norm to self-regulation.

The three terms of my title (Bordo's reading of body norms, the disciplinary form of the body, and the production of self-regulation) together name what Slide 28 calls "the intersections of power, the body, discipline and self-regulation. They are linked to what is regarded as normal. And we are all subject to normalizing judgements." This essay argues that Bordo's analysis is the lecture's clearest contemporary illustration of Foucauldian disciplinary power, that her diachronic move (the historical specificity of body norms) is decisive for the productive thesis, and that her account exposes the gendered character of disciplinary society that Foucault did not fully theorise.

The diachronic move. Slide 25 opens Bordo's analysis with a striking historical contrast. In the late nineteenth century, certain body images were "good": for men, "being 'plump' showed you were successful", whereas "having muscles was seen as working-class ('bad')"; for women, "pale skin was seen as good, as it meant that you did not have to work in the fields." Slide 27 then tracks the inversion: "Bordo says 30 years ago we were concerned with weight, how heavy we are. We were encouraged to diet, 'fight the flab'. Nowadays, we have to be 'slender'. Adverts talk of being in trim, well-toned, well-defined." Body parts are framed as "in revolt", thighs, stomach, upper arms, calves. The form of the norm persists; its content changes.

This diachronic move does decisive theoretical work. It instantiates the productive thesis (Slide 13) at the level of the body: different historical configurations produce different bodies, but each body is produced by its disciplinary configuration rather than being the natural ground on which discipline acts. It also denaturalises the present, opening it to contestation: if 1880's plump masculine body and 2020's slender feminine body are both products of historically specific norms, neither can claim priority as the body's "natural" form.

The disciplinary mechanism. Bordo's analysis fits the lecture's three aspects of discipline (Slide 19, 21) precisely. Hierarchical observation: the woman is watched by the magazine, the mirror, the advertising image, the peer group, and (Slide 28's example) the City-of-London colleague who notices whether one "means business" enough to be at the gym. Normalising judgement: the slender, well-toned norm is the standard against which deviation registers as moral failing ("being fat is equated with being lazy. Of not caring. Of being a failure", Slide 28). Examination: the bathroom scale, the calorie count, the gym-mirror self-assessment, the dressing-room verdict are the documented (in the subject's own memory if nowhere else) rituals of disciplinary self-evaluation.

The self-regulatory closure. Slide 17 puts the lecture's core thesis in compressed form: "If power 'shouted at us' all the time we would notice it. Rather, it invites and incites us to behave in certain ways. We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!" Bordo's clinching formulation completes the diagnosis: "Self-management in consumer culture...becomes more elusive as it becomes more pressing...Between the media images of self-containment and self-mastery and the reality of constant everyday stress...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3, Slide 29). Disciplinary power, in Bordo's reading, has closed at the level of the embodied self: no one needs to coerce the dieter; she coerces herself.

The gendered specification. Foucault's own analysis was relatively gender-neutral; Bordo's reading supplies the specification that the lecture itself endorses. Slide 28: "More demands are made on women than men in terms of them producing themselves as 'normal'." Slide 32: "Power operates more subtly but more closely in the regulation of women's bodies with regard to normalizing judgements. Foucault's work on power can help us understand this situation better/more accurately." The gendered specification is not a departure from Foucault; it is an application of his framework to a domain he did not develop.

The intersection with consumer culture. Slide 32 names a productive tension. "We live in a consumer society, one which runs on us drinking coffee, eating cakes, take-away food etc. But we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves, and this means being in control of our bodies!" The subject is simultaneously hailed as consumer (eat, drink, spend) and as self-disciplinary subject (be slender, well-toned, controlled). The chasm Bordo names is precisely the gap between these two imperatives, and it is the gap that produces "bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

The conclusion. Bordo's analysis is the lecture's most fully developed contemporary illustration of Foucault's account of disciplinary power and the production of docile bodies. The slender body, the gym membership in the City of London (Slide 28), the felt failure of missing a 5-a-day target (Slide 24), the consumer-culture paradox of food and self-mastery (Slide 32): all instantiate the panoptic diagram at the level of embodied selfhood. Butler's contribution (Slide 12) extends the analysis to the subject as such: power forms the subject rather than constraining a pre-existing one. The disciplinary body and the disciplined subject are produced together, and the productive thesis of Slide 13 reaches its sharpest form here.

### Mock Q8 (hypothetical): How does Lynch's exposition of Foucault help us read the lecture's account of power?

Richard Lynch's chapter "Foucault's theory of power" supplies the conceptual scaffolding that the lecture's slides rely on without always making explicit. The question is whether the lecture's account of power is strengthened by being read alongside Lynch. My argument is that Lynch's exposition supplies four indispensable supplements: a clearer account of what Foucault is rejecting, a clearer account of the micro/macro articulation, a sharper formulation of the "intentional but non-subjective" character of power, and a precise reading of resistance as internal to power.

Lynch on what Foucault rejects. The lecture's Slide 34 and Slide 35 set out the liberal and Marxist foils, but Lynch's account is more fine-grained. Lynch identifies three "mistaken, inadequate or misleading conceptions of power" against which Foucault writes: the liberal model (power as institutions and mechanisms of subservience), the psychoanalytic model (power as rule and law), and the Marxist model (power as class domination) (Lynch, 2011: 17). All three, Lynch argues, share what Foucault calls a "juridico-discursive" representation: they understand power as negative, prohibitive, top-down, and uniform (Lynch, 2011: 17-19). All three mistake the "terminal forms" of power for its essence. Lynch's framing makes the lecture's polemical moves on Slides 34-36 intelligible as a single, coherent rejection.

Lynch on micro/macro. The lecture's productive thesis (Slide 13) and the panoptic-society generalisation (Slide 10's claim that "contemporary society is a 'disciplinary society'") sit alongside biopolitical material (Slide 24) without the lecture making the structural relation between them fully explicit. Lynch supplies it: "we thus have a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26). The two registers are not rival theories but the two poles of Foucault's account, with the micro-level constituting the macro through aggregation. Lynch's pyramid analogy (Lynch, 2011: 15) is decisive: power can be built "from the bottom of a pyramid" rather than imposed from the top, and the academic transcript is the worked example.

Lynch on "intentional but non-subjective". This is one of Foucault's most counter-intuitive claims, and Lynch's account of it is essential. Power relations are intentional ("imbued, through and through, with calculation: there is no power that is exercised without a series of aims and objectives", Lynch, 2011: 26-27) but non-subjective ("this does not mean that it results from the choice or decision of an individual subject", Lynch, 2011: 27). The lecture's account of how the school's disciplinary apparatus produces docile bodies (Slide 23) gestures at this duality without naming it: nobody has decided that pupils should be docile, yet the school's tactics aim at producing docile pupils. Lynch's reading of this paradox, that "the effects of the exercise of power reach beyond any individual's (or group's) intentions or control", and that power is "best understood at the system level, rather than individual-level" (Lynch, 2011: 27), is what makes Slide 23's claim defensible without sliding into conspiracy theory.

Lynch on resistance. Slide 17's compressed claim, "We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!", risks sounding deterministic. Lynch's reading restores the resistance that Foucault insists is internal to power. "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet, or rather consequently, this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power" (Foucault, 1978: 95, cited in Lynch, 2011: 28). Lynch elaborates: "Without resistance, without two bodies (or minds) pushing or pulling against each other, there is no power relation. And through resistance, power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). This is essential for reading Bordo's analysis: the slender-body norm produces self-monitoring, but the very fact that the norm is contested (by alternative aesthetics, by body-acceptance discourse, by historical denaturalisation) confirms that disciplinary power is not totalising.

Three illustrations of the difference Lynch makes.

First, the Kember example. The lecture introduces the obstetric ultrasound on Slide 2 and returns to it on Slide 38, asking whether the doctor is "part of a patriarchal system" (Slide 5). The ideology-critique reading is "not exactly untrue", but Foucault's verdict is that "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). Lynch's micro/macro framing clarifies what the surplus is: the scan is a micro-encounter that aggregates into a macro-apparatus of antenatal screening, and the productive effects (foetus visible, mother displaced) cannot be reduced to any agent's intent.

Second, the school's regime. Slide 10's list (registers, progress reports, school photos, etc.) is rendered theoretically intelligible by Lynch's reading of the transcript as a bottom-up disciplinary instrument (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). The lecture's claim that contemporary society is a disciplinary society gains specificity from Lynch's claim that "power develops in the first instance in specific, local, individual choices, behaviours and interactions" (Lynch, 2011: 26) and aggregates into macro-forms.

Third, the body. Bordo's account on Slides 25-32 is most fully Foucauldian when read through Lynch's resistance clause: the slender norm produces self-monitoring, but the historicisation of body norms (plump in 1880, slender in 2020) is itself a resistive denaturalisation. Lynch's insistence that "power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28) is what keeps Bordo's analysis from sliding into pessimism.

My verdict: Lynch's exposition is not an alternative to Foucault but the reader's scaffold for the lecture. It clarifies what is being rejected, articulates the micro/macro structure, makes the "intentional but non-subjective" claim defensible, and restores the resistance Slide 17's compressed claim risks losing. The lecture and the Lynch chapter are designed to be read together; Lynch is the lecture's theoretical apparatus made explicit.

### Mock Q9 (single-author how-convincing): How convincing do you find Foucault's claim that "power is everywhere"?

Foucault writes that "power is everywhere; not because it embraces everything, but because it comes from everywhere" (Foucault, 1978: 93, cited in Lynch, 2011: 25). This is one of his most-cited and most-contested lines. I find the claim convincing on a careful reading, unconvincing on a careless one, and politically productive only when paired with the specifications that prevent it from collapsing into a generalised cynicism.

The careless reading takes "power is everywhere" to mean that every social relation is equally an instance of power, that domination is universal and inescapable, and therefore that resistance is futile. This reading underwrites two opposed mistakes: a nihilistic acquiescence ("everything is power, so why bother?") and a paranoid maximalism ("everything is power, so everything is suspect"). Neither is what Foucault meant.

The careful reading distinguishes two parts of the sentence. (i) "Power is everywhere...because it comes from everywhere." Power is not a possession concentrated in a state, class, or institution and exercised from there outward; it is a multiplicity of force relations that arise from below and stabilise into larger configurations. As Lynch summarises, "power develops in the first instance in specific, local, individual choices, behaviours and interactions" and aggregates into macro-forms (Lynch, 2011: 26). (ii) "Not because it embraces everything." Power's omnipresence is not its totality. Lynch is explicit: "power does not 'consolidate everything' or 'embrace everything' or 'answer everything'; power alone may not be adequate to explain all, or every aspect of, social relations" (Lynch, 2011: 14). This second clause is what saves the claim from totalising determinism.

Three reasons I find the careful claim convincing.

First, the explanatory yield. The hypothesis that power "comes from everywhere" forces us to look at micro-techniques that other vocabularies miss. The school's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10), Lynch's worked example of the academic transcript, the questionnaire that asks whether one is "different from the rest" (Slide 21), the bathroom-mirror appraisal of the body (Bordo, 1993), and the Kember ultrasound (Slide 2) are all sites of power that an "elite-domination" or "ideology" framework underdescribes. The yield is methodological: Foucault gives us tools to look at things classical sociology overlooked.

Second, the productive thesis. If power "comes from everywhere", it is not principally repressive but productive: it makes subjects, knowledges, types of people. Slide 13 puts the productive thesis directly: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)". The modern prisoner, the schoolchild, and the psychiatric patient are not pre-existing kinds; they are products of disciplinary apparatuses. This explanation of category-proliferation is hard to obtain from any other vocabulary.

Third, the resistance internal to power. Lynch's reading of Slide 17 ("We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!") restores what the compressed slogan can miss. Power, on Foucault's account, is constitutively accompanied by resistance: "Without resistance, without two bodies (or minds) pushing or pulling against each other, there is no power relation. And through resistance, power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). The omnipresence claim, properly read, is compatible with continuous contestation.

Three reasons I find the claim unconvincing if pushed.

First, the political economy lacuna. The lecture's own Slide 23 hints at the problem when it concludes that docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy" and that "the bourgeoisie has won!". The disciplinary apparatus serves capital; "comes from everywhere" cannot, on its own, explain why it serves the bourgeoisie rather than (say) the proletariat. The fix is to read Foucault alongside structural analyses, not to abandon him. Slide 36 acknowledges the issue: Foucault's complaint against Marxists is that they "never analysed" the mechanics of power (Foucault, 1980: 116), but this does not abolish the question of who benefits.

Second, the normative reticence. If power is everywhere, the critic needs criteria for distinguishing better and worse configurations of power. Lynch acknowledges that this is a real worry: "those who understand the claim that individuals are constituted by power relations to constitute a denial of freedom find his vision to be bleak" (Lynch, 2011: 28). His response is that Foucauldian analysis can "increase our self-awareness, and hence our own freedom" (Lynch, 2011: 28), but the framework remains thinner than a full normative political theory.

Third, the agency problem. If power produces subjects, the subject of resistance is itself produced by power. Lynch notes the apparent paradox and resolves it: "to understand subjectivity as constituted (in part) through power relations is not to deny that subjects can act intentionally" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Butler extends the same line: power "is not simply what we oppose" but "what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2; Slide 12).

Worked examples. The school is a site where power "comes from everywhere" (registers, progress reports, school photos, peer comparison, internalised norms) and the omnipresence claim is convincing. Bordo's slender-body norm is a site where power "comes from everywhere" (mirror, magazine, gym culture, peer judgement) and the omnipresence claim is again convincing. The Kember ultrasound, by contrast, is a site where a specific clinical apparatus organises the scene, and the omnipresence claim has to be paired with attention to the medical institution. The lecture's biopolitical material on Slide 24 (salt, 5-a-day, obesity policy) shows power coming from "the government", which is a specific source rather than "everywhere"; here the omnipresence claim must be balanced by attention to the state.

My verdict: "power is everywhere" is convincing as a description of the dispersed, productive, capillary character of modern power. It is not convincing as a complete theory that supersedes structural analysis of capital, gender, and the state. Read as a methodological injunction (look everywhere) rather than as a metaphysical thesis, the claim is one of the most consequential in twentieth-century social theory, and Lynch's reading is the indispensable scaffold for getting it right.

### Mock Q10 (two-authors): Using the work of at least two authors, evaluate Foucault's account of disciplinary power through the contributions of Bordo and Butler.

Bordo and Butler are the two named feminist readers Foucault's account of power finds in the lecture, and each contributes a different specification of the productive thesis. The question is whether Foucault's account is strengthened, qualified, or transformed by their interventions. I argue: strengthened at the level of bodily discipline (Bordo), transformed at the level of subject formation (Butler), and qualified in both cases by the gendered specification that Foucault did not fully develop.

Foucault's disciplinary power. The lecture's articulation comes through three slides. Slide 10 names contemporary society as a "disciplinary society" and lists the school's instruments (registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages). Slide 13 names the productive thesis: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)". Slide 19 names the three aspects of discipline: hierarchical observation, examination, and (Slide 21) normalising judgement. The product is the docile body (Slide 23), "passive and which regulate themselves according to the normalizing judgements of society". Power, on this account, "applies itself to immediate everyday life which categorizes the individual, marks him by his own individuality, attaches him to his identity, imposes a law of truth on him which he must recognize and which others must recognize in him. It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212; Slide 18).

Bordo on disciplinary norms. Bordo extends Foucault to the gendered production of bodies. The lecture's Slides 25-32 trace her argument through three moves. First, the diachronic move (Slide 25): in the late nineteenth century, "being 'plump' showed you were successful" for men, "having muscles was seen as working-class ('bad')", and "pale skin was seen as good" for women. The norms have inverted (Slide 27), but the form persists: there is always a norm, the body is always measured against it, and deviation always registers as moral failing. Second, the disciplinary mechanism (Slides 27-28): contemporary culture demands "slender", "in trim", "well-toned", "well-defined" bodies, and "more demands are made on women than men in terms of them producing themselves as 'normal'". The City of London gym membership is the lecture's worked example of the disciplinary body at work. Third, the self-regulatory closure (Slide 29): "between the media images of self-containment and self-mastery and the reality of constant everyday stress...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Bordo's contribution. She supplies what Foucault's account needed: the gendered specification. The lecture explicitly endorses the point. Slide 32: "Power operates more subtly but more closely in the regulation of women's bodies with regard to normalizing judgements. Foucault's work on power can help us understand this situation better/more accurately." Bordo does not so much critique Foucault as apply him to a domain he did not develop. The disciplinary register, in her hands, becomes the analytic for the slimming industry, the magazine, the mirror, and the gym.

Butler on power forming the subject. Slide 12 cites Butler's *Psychic Life of Power*: "We are used to thinking of power as what presses on the subject from the outside [...] But if [...] we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence [...] then power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2).

Butler's contribution. She pushes the productive thesis to its strongest form. Foucault's account already insists that power produces subjects (Slide 13, Slide 18); Butler insists that power constitutes the very condition of subjectivity. There is no pre-power self that power then constrains. This is at once a radicalisation and a complication. Radicalisation: the productive thesis cuts deeper than Slide 13 lets on. Complication: if power forms the subject, the subject of resistance is itself a product of power.

The complication is what Lynch addresses in his reading of "intentional but non-subjective" (Lynch, 2011: 26-28) and in his resistance clause: "to understand subjectivity as constituted (in part) through power relations is not to deny that subjects can act intentionally" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Resistance is not a return to a pre-power authenticity but the alteration of power relations from within. Lynch is explicit that this is hope rather than bleakness: "those of us...who find in his analyses the tools with which to increase our self-awareness, and hence our own freedom, hear wellsprings of hope" (Lynch, 2011: 28).

Convergences. Bordo and Butler agree with each other and with Foucault on the productive thesis. Both reject voluntarist individualism and crude determinism. Both insist on the embodied subject as a site of social reproduction. Both link the cultural production of gendered subjects to specific historical configurations of power.

Divergences. Bordo works mostly at the level of disciplinary norms acting on the body; Butler works mostly at the level of subject formation as such. Bordo's tools are the magazine, the mirror, and the gym; Butler's are the citational repetition of gendered acts and the psychic interiority that power forms. The lecture frames them as complementary: Bordo on Slides 25-32 supplies the disciplinary specification, Butler on Slide 12 supplies the formation-of-subject claim.

Worked illustrations. The Kember scene from Slide 2 is the clearest meeting point. The ultrasound is a disciplinary scene (in Bordo's terms: the maternal body is rendered visible and held against a clinical norm) and a subject-forming scene (in Butler's terms: the antenatal subject is constituted through the apparatus of clinical visibility). Both registers are at work in the same encounter. The City of London gym membership (Slide 28) is a Bordoian scene of self-regulating bodily discipline; the lecture's Slide 32 claim that we "have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves" is a Butlerian scene of subject formation through the consumer-cultural apparatus.

My verdict: Foucault's account of disciplinary power is strengthened by Bordo's gendered specification of bodily norms and transformed by Butler's account of power's formation of the subject. Both readers extend Foucault to domains he did not fully develop, and both expose the productive thesis as more radical than Slide 13's compressed version makes it appear. Reading the lecture without Bordo and Butler is reading Foucault without his most generative contemporary heirs.

### Mock Q11 (can-we): Can Foucault's framework be used to think about resistance, or does it foreclose it?

This question repeats one of the oldest charges against Foucault (that his account of power leaves no room for resistance) but it does so productively, asking whether Foucault's framework can be used for thinking about resistance even granting the worries. I argue: yes, and Lynch's reading of Foucault makes the case persuasively.

The prosecution case. Critics have argued that Foucault's framework forecloses resistance in three ways. (i) Power is omnipresent: "always already there, one is never outside it" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch, 2011: 19). If there is no outside, there is no Archimedean point from which to push back. (ii) Power produces the subject: "It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212; Slide 18). If the subject of resistance is itself produced by power, resistance is just power moving against itself. (iii) The lecture's Slide 17 ("We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!") risks sounding like a verdict of inescapability.

The defence case has five elements.

First, Foucault explicitly insists resistance is internal to power. Lynch quotes the canonical formulation: "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet, or rather consequently, this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power" (Foucault, 1990a: 95, cited in Lynch, 2011: 28). Resistance is not outside power but constitutive of it; without resistance, what we have is domination, not power.

Second, the omnipresence claim is not a totality claim. Lynch is explicit: "power does not 'consolidate everything' or 'embrace everything' or 'answer everything'; power alone may not be adequate to explain all, or every aspect of, social relations" (Lynch, 2011: 14). The plurality of force relations is the condition for the plurality of resistances.

Third, resistance is internal to power because power and resistance constitute each other. Lynch quotes Foucault's 1984 interview to clinch the point: "In the relations of power, there is necessarily the possibility of resistance, for if there were no possibility of resistance, of violent resistance, of escape, of ruse, of strategies that reverse the situation, there would be no relations of power" (Lynch, 2011: 28-29). He adds: "There cannot be relations of power unless the subjects are free...If there are relations of power throughout every social field it is because there is freedom everywhere" (Lynch, 2011: 29).

Fourth, the productive thesis is itself a resource for resistance. If power produces subjects, the historicisation of how it produces them denaturalises the present and opens it to contestation. Bordo's diachronic move (Slide 25) is the lecture's clearest example: by showing that the slender body of today is no more natural than the plump body of 1880, Bordo denaturalises the present norm and opens it to challenge. The same logic applies to the modern prisoner (Slide 14), the patient, the schoolchild: each is a historical product, not an essence, and so each is reformable.

Fifth, Lynch's reading is hopeful rather than bleak. "Those who understand the claim that individuals are constituted by power relations to constitute a denial of freedom find his vision to be bleak. Those of us, on the other hand, who find in his analyses the tools with which to increase our self-awareness, and hence our own freedom, hear wellsprings of hope in his discussion of the continuous transformations of power through history" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Resistance does not require an outside; it requires the kind of self-understanding Foucault's analyses make possible.

What Foucauldian resistance does not do. It does not promise final emancipation or produce a post-power society. The lecture's polemical Slide 23 ("the bourgeoisie has won!") gestures at the limit: disciplinary analysis without structural critique cannot mount a revolution. Marxist critics are right that for projects of overthrowing capitalism or dismantling patriarchy, Foucault's vocabulary is inadequate on its own; the fix is supplementation, not abandonment.

Worked illustrations from the lecture. Bordo's historicisation of body norms is a disciplinary resistance: by showing that the slender-body norm is a contingent product of contemporary culture, she opens it to political contestation. Butler's insistence that power "is not simply what we oppose" but also "what we depend on" (Butler, 1997: 1-2; Slide 12) is a more radical resistance: it denies the comforting picture of an authentic self prior to power but opens the field of immanent contestation. The lecture's Slide 5 question ("Are doctors part of a patriarchal system?") is itself a resistive move: by raising the question, the lecturer denaturalises the clinical apparatus that the Kember scan exemplifies. And Slide 22's framing of "the normal" as "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" is a resistive denaturalisation of the normalising judgement that Slide 21 describes.

My verdict: Foucault's framework can be used to think about resistance, and does not foreclose it. What it forecloses is the master-narrative model (the universal subject, the final liberation). What it enables is granular analysis of how power and resistance entangle in specific institutions, bodies, and moments. The school's regime of normalising judgement can be contested; Bordo's body norms can be denaturalised; the Kember scene can be reread to restore the maternal body to view. For these tasks, Foucauldian resistance is the right scale. For structural inequality at scale, Foucault must be supplemented. The framework opens space for resistance; the political work remains ours.

### Mock Q12 (specific-comparison): Compare Bordo's reading of disciplinary norms with Butler's account of the subject formed by power.

Susan Bordo and Judith Butler are the two named feminist readers of Foucault in the lecture, and each contributes a different specification of his account of power. They share a Foucauldian commitment to the productive thesis (that power makes bodies and subjects rather than simply constraining them) but they develop the inheritance differently. Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" (Bordo, 1993) works with disciplinary norms; Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* (Butler, 1997) works with the formation of the subject by power. I will set out each, mark the convergences and divergences, and assess what each offers a Foucauldian reading.

Bordo's disciplinary norms. Bordo extends Foucault's account of disciplinary power to the gendered production of bodies, and the lecture rehearses her argument across Slides 25-32. First, the diachronic move (Slide 25): in the late nineteenth century, plumpness signalled male success, muscle signalled working-class status, and pale skin signalled women's exemption from field labour. Body norms are historically specific products of disciplinary configurations, not natural facts. Second, the contemporary inversion (Slide 27): "Bordo says 30 years ago we were concerned with weight, how heavy we are. We were encouraged to diet, 'fight the flab'. Nowadays, we have to be 'slender'. Adverts talk of being in trim, well-toned, well-defined. We have to control our bodies. Our body parts are in revolt, thighs, stomach, upper arms, calves." Third, the gendered specification (Slide 28): "More demands are made on women than men in terms of them producing themselves as 'normal'." Fourth, the self-regulatory closure (Slide 29): "between the media images of self-containment and self-mastery and the reality of constant everyday stress...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). The body, in Bordo's analysis, is the site at which the three aspects of discipline (hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, examination) close on the subject as self-regulation.

Butler on power forming the subject. Slide 12 cites Butler at length: "We are used to thinking of power as what presses on the subject from the outside [...] But if [...] we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence [...] then power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Butler's contribution is to take the Foucauldian productive thesis to its strongest form: power does not act on a pre-existing subject; it constitutes the subject as such. The lecture cites Butler precisely for this purpose, between Slide 10's claim that contemporary society is a disciplinary society and Slide 13's productive-institutions argument.

Convergences. Both authors are Foucauldian. Both reject voluntarist individualism and crude determinism. Both insist on the embodied subject as a site of social reproduction, treat the productive nature of power as central, and link the cultural production of gendered subjects to specific historical configurations of power. The lecture treats them as complementary rather than competing.

Divergences. First, the object: Bordo's primary object is the gendered body as disciplinarily normed; Butler's is the subject as formed by power. Second, the mechanism: Bordo's is disciplinary (observation, examination, internalised normalisation) in the lecture's three-aspects sense (Slides 19, 21); Butler's is more fundamental, the formation of the very condition of subjectivity. Third, the temporality: Bordo's analysis is diachronic, showing how body norms have changed across historical periods (Slide 25); Butler's is structural, showing how power constitutes the subject as such. Fourth, the politics of resistance: Bordo's diachronic move opens resistance by denaturalising the present norm; Butler's account requires that resistance be immanent rather than oppositional, since there is no pre-power self to which one could return.

Worked applications from the lecture. The Kember ultrasound scene (Slide 2) is the clearest meeting point of the two readers. In Bordo's terms, the scan is a disciplinary instrument that produces the antenatal body against a clinical norm of normality (Slide 4: "could this lead to the woman being viewed as unimportant?"). In Butler's terms, the scan forms the maternal subject as such, conferring identity through the clinical apparatus that makes the foetus visible. The City of London gym membership (Slide 28) is more Bordoian: a paradigmatic disciplinary scene of normalised body-work. Slide 32's claim that we "have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves" is more Butlerian: it names the formation of the consumer-cultural subject through the very practices that constitute her.

My verdict: Bordo and Butler are complementary, not competing. Bordo gives us a tool for analysing the disciplinary normalisation of gendered bodies; Butler gives us a tool for analysing the formation of the subject by power. The lecture endorses both: Bordo on Slides 25-32 as the disciplinary specification of bodily norms, Butler on Slide 12 as the productive-formation claim. To force a choice between them is to lose half the feminist Foucauldian toolkit; to read them together is to get the full picture of the disciplinary subject the lecture describes.
