# L7 — Foucault on Power and Disciplinary Society

## 1. Lecture Overview

Lecture 7 marks a decisive pivot in SC301: from classical models of power as ideology, repression, or class domination toward Foucault's far more subtle and pervasive account of power as something that operates through everyday practices, knowledges, institutions, and the very formation of the subject. The lecturer opens with Sarah Kember's example of obstetric ultrasound — the way scanning "eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, *Virtual Anxiety*, 1998) — to dramatise the central problem: how can we understand what is happening here? An ideology critique (the doctor as agent of patriarchy) captures part of the story but, as Foucault puts it, "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 102).

The lecture's spine is the contrast between **sovereign power** and **disciplinary power**. Sovereign power is the spectacular, corporal, top-down power of the pre-modern monarch — illustrated by the grotesque public execution of Damiens the regicide that opens *Discipline and Punish*. Modern power, since the nineteenth century, looks different: it is dispersed, capillary, productive rather than purely repressive, and works through surveillance, examination, and normalisation rather than through the visible application of force. Bentham's panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, becomes the architectural figure of this new regime — a "machine for dissociating the see/being seen dyad" that produces, in the inmate, "a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 315 in Calhoun reader).

Three aspects of discipline are unpacked: **hierarchical observation** (we are watched from positions we cannot see); **normalising judgement** (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal"); and **examination** (the combination of observation and judgement in documented, testable form — registers, progress reports, key stages, transcripts). The aim and product of this triad is the **docile body** — passive, self-regulating, useful to capital and to the state.

The body and subjectivity sit at the centre of the second half of the lecture. Susan Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" (1992) extends Foucault into feminist territory: norms of slenderness, tone, control, and self-mastery shape bodies — particularly women's — and produce subjects "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1992, pp. 202–3). Judith Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997) pushes further: power is "what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997, pp. 1–2). Power forms the subject; we cannot stand outside it.

Behind all of this stands Foucault's polemic against traditional sociological theories of power — liberal (power as state institutions), psychoanalytic (power as rule/law), and Marxist (power as class domination via ideology). Foucault's complaint is consistent: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 116). His project is to look at **how** power operates — at its capillary, micro-physical level — rather than asking only **who** holds it. Biopower extends this analysis from individual bodies to the management of populations (birth rates, mortality, public health), preparing the ground for later lectures on sexuality, race, and governmentality.

## 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Sovereign power**: pre-modern, spectacular, top-down power that operates through visible force, corporal punishment, and the abuse of the body. Concentrated in a person (the king) and exercised through edict and execution.
- **Disciplinary power**: modern, dispersed, capillary power that works through training, observation, examination, and normalisation. Subtle, productive, everywhere.
- **The panopticon**: Jeremy Bentham's architectural design — central tower, back-lit ring of cells — that allows constant, asymmetric visibility. Foucault treats it as the diagram of disciplinary modernity.
- **Surveillance**: continuous observation that need not be actual to be effective; the possibility of being watched is enough to produce self-regulation.
- **Hierarchical observation**: monitoring conducted from positions of invisibility, with observers themselves unobserved.
- **Normalising judgement**: measurement of individuals against an invented standard of "the normal"; the nineteenth-century invention of "the normal" is "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (lecture slide).
- **Examination**: the ritual combination of observation and judgement that produces documented, comparable knowledge of individuals (transcripts, medical records, performance reviews).
- **Docile bodies**: passive, self-regulating, useful bodies produced by disciplinary techniques — bodies that fit the modern capitalist economy.
- **Power/knowledge**: power and knowledge are inseparable; the production of knowledge (psychiatry, criminology, demography) is itself an exercise of power, and power produces knowledge.
- **Productive power**: power is not only repressive; it produces subjects, bodies, types of people, institutions, and forms of conduct.
- **Biopower**: power exercised at the level of populations — birth rates, mortality, public health, demographics — managing life rather than threatening death.
- **Governmentality**: the rationalities and techniques through which populations are governed (cross-applied from related lectures).
- **The subject**: the modern individual as both subjected to power and constituted as a subject by power.
- **Regimes of truth**: historically specific configurations of what counts as true and how truth is produced.
- **The disciplinary society**: contemporary society understood as saturated by the techniques of discipline that emerged in the nineteenth century — schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces.
- **Force relations**: Foucault's basic micro-unit of power; relations that push, urge, or compel, immanent in social interactions (Lynch, *Foucault: Key Concepts*).
- **Capillary power**: power that operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies and conduct.

## 6. Common Essay Pitfalls

- Treating Foucault as if he simply said "power is bad" or "everything is power" — both miss the productive, omnipresent-but-not-totalising character of his analysis.
- Conflating disciplinary power with sovereign power; failing to mark the historical shift Foucault traces from spectacular punishment to surveillance.
- Reducing the panopticon to "being watched" without explaining the **asymmetry** (visible/unverifiable) and the **internalisation** that produces self-regulation.
- Forgetting that power is **productive** — it creates subjects, knowledges, and types of people, not just constraints.
- Treating biopower and disciplinary power as synonyms. Disciplinary power targets the individual body; biopower targets the population. They overlap but are not identical.
- Ignoring resistance — Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance" — and so making him sound more deterministic than he is.
- Failing to engage critics: Marxists (Poulantzas) accuse him of dissolving class; feminists (Hartsock, McNay) ask whether his account leaves room for agency; Habermas accuses him of relativism.
- Using only one example (the prison) when the lecture and reading insist that disciplinary power runs through schools, hospitals, workplaces, families, and bodies.
- Quoting "panopticon" without naming Bentham, or quoting Bordo/Butler without anchoring them to Foucault.
- Reading "docile bodies" as universal slavery rather than as a tendency produced by specific institutions and techniques.
- Treating Foucault as a "postmodernist" who denies truth — he writes about regimes of truth, not the abolition of truth.
- Forgetting the lecture's opening: ideology is not wrong, but it is "much more and much less" than what Foucault wants to analyse.

## 7. Past-Year Exam Questions and Answers

### 2024 (Q7): How does Foucault conceptualise disciplinary power? Why is this conceptualisation important?

Foucault's notion of disciplinary power names the form of power that emerged in Western societies from roughly the late eighteenth century onwards and that, he argues, came to displace the older logic of sovereign power. Sovereign power, as he reconstructs it in the opening of *Discipline and Punish* (1975), was concentrated in the person of the monarch, exercised through visible force, and dramatised in spectacular rituals of corporal and capital punishment — the slow public execution of Damiens for regicide is its emblem. Disciplinary power, by contrast, is "subtle", "everywhere", and works not by stopping people from doing things but by training them into doing certain things. As Foucault puts it, this is "a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets; it is a 'physics' or an 'anatomy' of power" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 316 in the Calhoun reader).

I would isolate four features that together make up Foucault's conceptualisation. First, disciplinary power is **capillary**: it operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies, timetables, classrooms, examinations, queues, and records. The school register, the progress report, the medical chart, the HR review — these are its instruments. Second, it is **productive**: it does not merely repress but produces new kinds of subjects (the modern prisoner, the schoolchild, the patient, the worker) through new kinds of institutions. Third, it works through three linked techniques — **hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and the examination**. We are observed from positions we cannot see, measured against an invented standard of "the normal", and tested and documented in rituals that fuse observation and judgement. Fourth, it produces **docile bodies** — bodies that are useful, obedient, and crucially, **self-regulating**, because the subject internalises the gaze.

The architectural diagram of this regime is Bentham's panopticon. Foucault writes that its "major effect" is "to induce in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (*Discipline and Punish*, p. 315). Because the inmate cannot verify when they are being watched, they behave as if they always are. Visibility, as Foucault summarises, is a trap. Crucially, the panopticon is not just a prison design but a model that "infiltrates" schools, hospitals, factories, and the police — what Foucault calls the disciplinary society.

Why is this conceptualisation important? I think it matters for at least four reasons. First, it reorients sociology away from the question "who holds power?" and toward the question "how does power operate?" That methodological shift is huge: it lets us examine an Amazon warehouse, a school inspection, or a Fitbit dashboard without first reducing them to the will of a ruling class or a sovereign. Second, it explains the otherwise puzzling fact that modern liberal societies, despite claiming to expand freedom, generate ever more intensive forms of conduct-shaping. Disciplinary power does not contradict liberal freedom; it is its underside. Third, it identifies the link between knowledge and power — what Foucault calls power/knowledge. Psychiatry, criminology, demography, and pedagogy are not innocent sciences but apparatuses that produce the very subjects they claim to describe. Fourth, it opens space for politics at the micro-level: the school examination, the CCTV camera, the credit score, the social media algorithm all become legitimate sites of political contestation.

The conceptualisation also has limitations I want to acknowledge. Critics have charged that Foucault dissolves class and gender into a generalised analytic of conduct, leaving us without the tools to identify durable structural inequalities. Nancy Fraser and others have asked whether resistance becomes intelligible if power is everywhere. Marxists like Nicos Poulantzas have argued that disciplinary power names a real phenomenon but cannot replace political economy. I find these criticisms partially convincing — Foucault's account works best as a supplement to, rather than a substitute for, structural analyses. But the supplement is essential. We cannot understand Foxconn worker surveillance, the medicalisation of ADHD, or China's Social Credit System without his vocabulary. The British school's regime of registers, key stages, and SATs is unintelligible if we have only "the ruling class" as our analytic.

Disciplinary power, then, names not a thing but a mode — a way power has worked in modern societies. Its importance lies in how it allows us to see, in the most mundane practices (clocking in at work, scanning a passport, posting on Instagram), the political form of our present. As Foucault writes, this is "a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 212), and that double sense — subjected to, and constituted as — is the productive heart of his concept.

### 2024 (Q8): "Contemporary societies are biopolitical." Critically analyse this statement.

The claim that contemporary societies are biopolitical asks us to extend Foucault's analysis of power from the disciplined individual body to the managed population. Foucault's account of biopower in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) and the lectures collected as *Society Must Be Defended* identifies a shift, dating from roughly the late eighteenth century, in which power is no longer principally exercised as the sovereign right to "take life or let live" but as a positive technology of "making live" — fostering, optimising, and managing the vital processes of populations. Where disciplinary power targets the individual body through schools and prisons, biopower targets the species body through statistics, public health, demography, vaccination, and insurance.

I want to argue that the statement is largely defensible — contemporary societies are indeed extensively biopolitical — but only with three qualifications: biopolitics does not replace disciplinary or sovereign power but layers onto them; biopolitics is uneven across populations and geographies; and its operations are intensely contested.

First, the affirmative case. Public health regimes provide some of the clearest examples. The UK's vaccination programme treats the population as the unit of intervention; herd immunity is a biopolitical concept par excellence. The NHS Test and Trace system during COVID-19, the mass roll-out of vaccines by demographic priority groups, and the statistical apparatus of the Office for National Statistics during the pandemic are all instances of what Foucault would recognise as biopower. The same logic governs statins prescribed at population level: nobody decides this individual must take a statin because they will die otherwise; the population's cardiovascular risk is modelled and intervention targets are set. Fertility policy in countries from Hungary to Singapore, contraception campaigns, school meal regulation, sugar taxes, smoking bans — these too are biopolitical. Foucault's prescient observation that "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order…but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 215) captures the long arc.

Second, biopower in the twenty-first century has acquired new technical capacities. Genomic medicine and biomarkers extend biopolitical management into molecular bodies. Algorithmic predictive systems (used in policing, social services, credit) sort populations by risk in ways that recall what Foucault called normalising judgement at population scale. China's Social Credit System makes biopolitical management of conduct explicit and computational. The Cambridge Analytica scandal showed that political campaigns themselves now operate biopolitically, segmenting populations into psychographic clusters.

Third, the biopolitical is gendered, raced, and classed. Biopower, Foucault writes, does not work on an undifferentiated population but produces and acts upon stratifications. The HIV/AIDS response of the 1980s — and the disastrous neglect of gay men, sex workers, drug users, and African populations — is a textbook case of biopower's selectivity. Achille Mbembe's concept of necropolitics extends Foucault to the production of populations who are "let die": Gaza, the Mediterranean migrant crossings, Black mortality in the US. Foetal ultrasound and reproductive medicine, as Sarah Kember argued in *Virtual Anxiety* (1998), make women's bodies biopolitically visible while paradoxically "eliminat[ing] the mother's body from view" (Kember, 1998).

Now to qualifications. The statement risks overgeneralisation if read as "contemporary societies are *only* biopolitical." Foucault is clear that biopower coexists with disciplinary power and even with residual sovereign power. The US death penalty, drone strikes, and the violent policing of Black bodies show that sovereign right has not vanished. Inside a school, disciplinary techniques (registers, exams) operate alongside biopolitical ones (national obesity surveys). At Foxconn, individual disciplinary surveillance and population-level health metrics work together. So the statement is best read additively: contemporary societies are biopolitical *as well as* disciplinary and partially sovereign.

The second qualification concerns resistance and contestation. If biopower were total, the anti-vax movement, the body positivity movement, fat liberation, queer activism on HIV criminalisation, and Indigenous data sovereignty struggles would be unintelligible. Foucault himself insists that "where there is power, there is resistance" (*History of Sexuality Vol. 1*, p. 95). Biopolitics provokes biopolitical counter-movements, sometimes reactionary (anti-vaxxers), sometimes liberatory (HIV/AIDS activism by ACT UP).

The third qualification is methodological. Critics — Mitchell Dean, Thomas Lemke, the governmentality school — have argued that biopolitics names many different rationalities, not one. Neoliberal biopolitics (responsibilising individuals for their own risk through Fitbits and private insurance) differs from welfarist biopolitics (mid-century public health). Both are biopolitical, but they cannot be conflated.

I find the statement convincing on balance. Contemporary societies are saturated by biopolitical rationalities, from the COVID app on your phone to the actuarial tables underwriting your mortgage. But "saturated" does not mean "exhausted by". Biopolitics is one rationality among several, it is unevenly distributed, and it is constantly resisted. Foucault's concept is most useful when it sharpens our perception of these uneven, contested politics of life, rather than when it is wielded as a totalising diagnosis.

### 2025 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing do you find his arguments?

Foucault's challenge to traditional sociological approaches to power is set out polemically in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) and elaborated in *Discipline and Punish* (1975) and the interviews collected as *Power/Knowledge* (1980). Traditional sociology, on Foucault's reading, has tended to think about power in three problematic ways: liberal accounts treat power as a possession of the state and its institutions; psychoanalytic and juridical accounts treat power as rule and prohibition; and Marxist accounts treat power as class domination operating through ideology. Foucault's complaint, as Richard Lynch summarises, is that each of these "mischaracterize an accidental feature of power in a particular context as an essential characteristic of power in general" (Lynch, *Michel Foucault: Key Concepts*).

The lecture frames the challenge sharply. Liberal/Enlightenment thinkers, including first-wave feminists, treat power as a thing, possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom, and therefore something to be seized, shared, or constrained. Marxists treat power as flowing from ownership of the means of production, concentrated in the bourgeoisie, and exercised over the proletariat through ideology. Foucault's response to the latter is biting: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 116). What is missing in all three traditions is an account of **how** power operates — its mechanics, its techniques, its capillary distribution.

Foucault's positive counter-proposal has at least six distinctive moves. First, **power is dispersed, not concentrated**. It does not sit at the top of a pyramid but circulates through micro-relations of force throughout the social body. Second, **power is productive, not (only) repressive**. It produces subjects, knowledges, types of people. Third, **power and knowledge are inseparable** — there is no neutral science of human beings, and the human sciences (psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy, sexology) are themselves apparatuses of power. Fourth, **power operates through everyday practices** — timetables, examinations, medical scans, performance reviews — not principally through the state and the law. Fifth, **modern power forms the subject** rather than merely constraining a pre-existing subject. As Foucault writes, it "makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 212). Sixth, **resistance is internal to power**: "where there is power, there is resistance" (*History of Sexuality Vol. 1*, p. 95).

The pay-off is methodological. We should not ask "who has power?" but "how is power exercised?" We should look at the panopticon, the school timetable, the medical chart, the credit score — not only at parliament and capital.

How convincing do I find these arguments? Largely, yes — but with reservations.

On the convincing side: Foucault's account illuminates phenomena that traditional sociology struggles to explain. The Marxist asks who benefits from CCTV; Foucault asks how CCTV trains us to behave as if always watched. The liberal asks whether the state has overstepped; Foucault asks how Fitbit and Apple Watch enrol us in our own self-management. The school inspector regime, NHS digital records, Amazon warehouse algorithms, the medicalisation of ADHD — none of these are well captured by "the ruling class wants this." All are well captured by Foucault's vocabulary of normalising judgement, hierarchical observation, and examination. I find the productive thesis especially convincing: when psychiatry invents "the homosexual" as a kind of person, when criminology invents "the delinquent", when pedagogy invents "the gifted child", power is not just constraining behaviour — it is producing identities.

The challenge to ideology critique is also persuasive. The lecture's opening example — Kember on obstetric ultrasound — shows why. Calling the doctor "ideological" is not exactly wrong, but it misses how the technology itself, the scanning practice, the protocols, organise women's bodies and knowledge of foetuses in ways that exceed any individual doctor's intent. "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 102).

The reservations. First, Foucault's analysis can be hard to operationalise against concentrated, sovereign power — Putin's Russia, the death penalty, drone warfare. The capillary model risks underplaying the brutal and very visible exercises of power that have not disappeared. Second, feminist critics including Nancy Hartsock and Lois McNay have argued that if power constitutes the subject, it becomes hard to ground feminist agency in anything other than power itself. Butler's response (in *The Psychic Life of Power*, 1997) accepts this but reframes agency as immanent rather than oppositional. Third, Marxist critics like Nicos Poulantzas argue that Foucault sees the techniques but loses the structure — he sees Amazon warehouse surveillance but loses sight of the capitalist logic that organises it. Fourth, Habermas accuses Foucault of relativism: if there are only regimes of truth, on what basis do we criticise? I find this critique less compelling because Foucault is not claiming truth does not exist, only that truth has a history and a politics.

On balance, then, I find Foucault's challenge highly convincing as a supplement to traditional approaches but less so as a replacement. The most fruitful contemporary sociology — Wendy Brown's work on neoliberalism, Achille Mbembe's necropolitics, Shoshana Zuboff's surveillance capitalism — combines Foucauldian analysis of mechanism with Marxist or feminist analysis of structure. That hybrid is precisely what Foucault's challenge made possible. To ask "how" without abandoning "who benefits" seems to me the best legacy of his intervention.

### 2023 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing?

Foucault's intervention into the sociology of power was, at root, a question about method. Traditional sociology had asked **who** holds power — the state, the ruling class, the patriarch, the king. Foucault wanted to ask **how** power operates. As he insisted, "we need to look at HOW power operates" (lecture slide paraphrasing Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980). The shift sounds modest but reorganises the entire field of analysis.

I will set out Foucault's challenge under three headings — what he rejects, what he proposes, and what follows — and then assess how convincing it is.

What he rejects. Foucault's foils, identified clearly in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976), are three. The **liberal/juridical** model treats power as a property of the state, exercised through law and prohibition, dangerous because it limits a pre-existing free individual. The **psychoanalytic** model treats power as rule, the form of the law within the subject. The **Marxist** model treats power as class domination, operating through ideology, with the bourgeoisie at the top of a pyramid. All three, Foucault argues, share what he calls a "juridico-discursive" representation — they understand power as negative, prohibitive, top-down, and uniform. All three mistake "terminal forms" of power (the state, the law, class domination) for its essence. None analyses the **mechanics** by which power is exercised. The Marxist version receives Foucault's sharpest rebuke: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 116).

What he proposes. Foucault counters with what he calls an "analytics of power". Power is a "multiplicity of force relations immanent in the sphere in which they operate" (Foucault, *History of Sexuality Vol. 1*, 1990 ed., p. 92). Several propositions follow. Power is exercised, not possessed. Power comes from below — from the micro-relations of everyday life — and produces, through aggregation, the larger structures we recognise as states and hegemonies. Power is intentional but non-subjective: tactics have aims, but no single agent controls the overall effect. Power is omnipresent — "always already there" — and so resistance is internal to it. Disciplinary power and biopower are the specifically modern forms of this generalised mechanics. The panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, is the architectural diagram. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 315 in Calhoun reader). Power is "much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 102).

What follows. Methodologically, sociology should examine the school register, the medical chart, the CCTV camera, the Fitbit, the credit score, the performance review — the everyday techniques through which conduct is shaped. The human sciences themselves — psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy — should be treated not as innocent knowledge but as part of the apparatus of power/knowledge. The subject is not the starting point but the product of power.

How convincing? I find this challenge largely convincing, with three positive points and two reservations.

The first positive: the empirical reach is remarkable. The lecture lists schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces, the regulation of bodies, the rise of gym membership in the City of London, the slimming industry — and these can be extended to contemporary cases the lecturer did not need to mention. Amazon warehouse surveillance, the British university's REF and TEF, the NHS Performance Indicators, the rise of Fitbit and Apple Watch, the medicalisation of ADHD — none are well explained by a top-down model of power. The Foucauldian vocabulary fits them.

The second positive: the productive thesis is genuinely original. Power produces — kinds of people, kinds of knowledge, kinds of bodies. The "homosexual", in the famous formulation, became a "personage" in the late nineteenth century not because there were no same-sex acts before but because medical and psychiatric discourses produced a new kind of subject. This was something traditional sociology genuinely missed.

The third positive: the linkage of knowledge and power answers a real problem. Why are some sciences trusted, others not? Why does the diagnosis of "borderline personality disorder" or "anti-social personality disorder" disproportionately fall on certain populations? Power/knowledge supplies a framework.

The reservations. First, the diffuse model can underplay concentrated power. Wars, mass incarceration, drone strikes, billionaire wealth — these are real, and a Foucauldian analysis must work harder to capture them than a Marxist one. Second, the question of agency. If "power forms the subject" (Butler, *The Psychic Life of Power*, 1997, p. 1), what grounds resistance? Foucault answers that resistance is internal to power, but critics like Nancy Fraser argue this is unsatisfying — we want to know whose resistance counts as emancipatory and whose does not.

My overall verdict: Foucault's challenge is convincing as a transformation of what sociology can see, less convincing as a complete replacement for traditional accounts. The best contemporary work — Wendy Brown, Mbembe, Zuboff — combines Foucauldian micro-analysis with structural critique. Foucault expanded the toolkit; he did not (and probably did not intend to) close it.

### 2022 (Q10): Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as "disciplinary". What did he mean by this? How useful do you find this term?

When Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as disciplinary, he meant something quite specific: a historically new mode of power, emerging in Western societies in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, that operates through training rather than force, through surveillance rather than spectacle, through normalisation rather than prohibition, and that produces subjects who regulate themselves.

I want to set out what he meant under five headings, then assess the term's usefulness.

First, **disciplinary power is contrasted with sovereign power**. The opening of *Discipline and Punish* (1975) describes the gruesome public execution of Damiens the regicide in 1757 — a spectacle of corporal punishment that displayed the king's power over the criminal's body. Within decades, this had largely vanished; in its place came the prison timetable, the school bell, the hospital ward round. The shift, Foucault argues, is not from cruelty to humanity but from one logic of power to another. Sovereign power was concentrated, visible, intermittent, and worked by abusing the body. Disciplinary power is dispersed, invisible, continuous, and works by training the body.

Second, **disciplinary power is capillary**. It operates not principally through the state but through everyday institutions — schools, hospitals, factories, asylums, families, barracks. As Foucault writes, "discipline may be identified neither with an institution nor with an apparatus; it is a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 316 in Calhoun reader). Schools deploy registers, progress reports, school photos, key stages, exams; workplaces deploy clock-cards, KPIs, performance reviews; medicine deploys charts, scans, screenings.

Third, **disciplinary power works through three techniques** the lecture isolates: hierarchical observation (we are watched from positions of asymmetric invisibility), normalising judgement (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal" — itself a nineteenth-century invention), and the examination (the documented combination of the two).

Fourth, **its architectural figure is the panopticon**. Bentham's prison design, with its central tower and back-lit cells, achieves "the major effect" of inducing "in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 315 in Calhoun reader). Because the inmate cannot tell when they are being watched, they internalise the gaze.

Fifth, **its product is the docile body** — useful, obedient, self-regulating. Such bodies, Foucault notes in the lecture's account, fit well into factories and offices. They "question little and behave well." They do not need to be coerced because they have been trained.

How useful do I find the term? Very useful, for four reasons, but with three limits.

Useful, first, because it names something traditional sociology missed. Marxist theories of ideology and liberal theories of state power cannot adequately describe the school bell, the Ofsted inspection, the Fitbit, the airport security queue, or the LinkedIn endorsement. All these are disciplinary in Foucault's sense.

Useful, second, because it captures contemporary intensifications. Amazon warehouse algorithmic management, Foxconn worker surveillance, Uber driver ratings, university league tables, NHS waiting-time targets — these are recognisable, even paradigmatic, disciplinary phenomena. The TSA body scanner at airports, CCTV in UK cities, COVID-19 contact-tracing apps — these are panoptic in Bentham's exact sense, made unverifiable by their ubiquity.

Useful, third, because it illuminates how we participate in our own discipline. The Fitbit user, the calorie counter, the LinkedIn poster, the Strava cyclist — these are not coerced. They monitor themselves. As Bordo writes about body discipline, "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, "Reading the Slender Body", 1992, pp. 202–3).

Useful, fourth, because it provides a vocabulary for resistance. If discipline runs through everyday techniques, then everyday techniques are sites of contestation: refusing the Fitbit, opting out of the optional standardised test, breaking the Amazon warehouse rhythm.

Limits. First, the term can homogenise. The disciplinary techniques of a Victorian workhouse are not the same as those of a contemporary Korean cram school; lumping them risks losing historical specificity. Second, disciplinary power does not exhaust contemporary power. As Foucault himself recognised, biopower (population-level management) and sovereign power (still operative in war, capital punishment, border violence) coexist with discipline. The student of Foxconn must mobilise all three. Third, the term has been popularised to the point of becoming a slogan; "disciplinary society" sometimes covers everything anyone disapproves of.

My overall view: the term remains one of the most productive in twentieth-century social theory, but it is most useful when wielded with historical precision and combined with other analytics. Contemporary society is partly disciplinary, partly biopolitical, partly sovereign — and Foucault's term captures one essential strand among these.

### 2022 (Q11): How useful is Foucault's notion of biopower for understanding contemporary society?

Foucault's notion of biopower, introduced in the final chapter of *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) and developed in the lecture series *Society Must Be Defended* (1976) and *Security, Territory, Population* (1977–78), names a form of power that operates not on individual bodies, as discipline does, but on populations as biological collectives. Where the sovereign exercised the right to "take life or let live", biopower exercises the right to "make live and let die" — fostering, optimising, and managing the vital processes of populations through statistics, demography, public health, and the regulation of birth and death rates.

I will argue that the concept is highly useful for understanding contemporary society but with three caveats: it must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power; its applications vary historically and politically; and it requires updating for digital and algorithmic contexts.

On the affirmative side. Biopower illuminates a vast range of contemporary phenomena.

Public health and epidemiology are obvious cases. The COVID-19 pandemic was governed biopolitically from start to finish: R numbers, vaccination roll-outs by demographic priority, contact-tracing apps, school closures by infection rate, "vulnerable" populations identified by NHS data. The British state did not punish individuals; it modelled and managed populations. The same holds for routine vaccination programmes, antenatal screening, sugar taxes, the public health framing of obesity, and the prescription of statins to large segments of the over-fifties to manage cardiovascular risk at population level.

Reproductive politics is biopolitical par excellence. Foetal ultrasound, prenatal genetic screening, IVF regulation, fertility incentives in Hungary or Singapore, abortion law in post-Dobbs America, contraception campaigns in the Global South — all manage life at the level of the species. Sarah Kember's observation that "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view" (*Virtual Anxiety*, 1998) captures how biopolitical visibility can render some bodies hyper-visible (the foetus) while erasing others (the mother).

Insurance and risk management are biopolitical. Actuarial tables, credit scoring, predictive policing, the algorithmic sorting of populations into risk categories for mortgages, jobs, and parole are all biopolitical operations. Cambridge Analytica and the segmentation of voters into psychographic clusters are biopolitical operations on the political subject.

Border and migration regimes are biopolitical, sometimes sliding into what Achille Mbembe calls necropolitics. The Mediterranean migrant route, the US–Mexico border, the systematic abandonment of HIV-positive populations in 1980s–90s America, Gaza — these are biopolitical decisions about whose lives are fostered and whose are let die.

The Foucauldian observation that "the police force was…invented…for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 215) generalises: contemporary policing, urban planning, environmental regulation, even climate policy involve biopolitical rationalities.

Now the caveats.

First, biopower does not stand alone. The Amazon warehouse worker is governed disciplinarily (clock-in, scanner, rate metrics) **and** biopolitically (worker health insurance pooled and managed by algorithm) **and** sovereignly (when the legal system criminalises labour organising). Conflating these flattens the analysis. Mitchell Dean's *Governmentality* (1999) usefully distinguishes the different rationalities in play.

Second, biopolitics has changed over time. Mid-twentieth-century welfare biopolitics (the NHS, mass public housing, universal child allowance) operated according to a logic of solidaristic risk-pooling. Neoliberal biopolitics from the 1980s onwards responsibilises the individual for their own risk: you wear the Fitbit, you take out the private health insurance, you optimise your sleep with the Apple Watch. Both are biopolitical, but politically opposed.

Third, digital biopolitics requires new vocabulary. Shoshana Zuboff's *Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) describes a regime where private platforms (Google, Meta, Amazon) accumulate behavioural data at population scale and trade it as a commodity. This is biopolitical but routed through markets rather than states. Wendy Chun, Safiya Noble, and others have argued that algorithmic systems are biopolitical apparatuses with distinctive racialised and gendered effects (predictive policing falls disproportionately on Black communities; medical algorithms underestimate Black women's pain).

A final qualification: biopower is resisted. The HIV/AIDS activism of ACT UP, the body positivity movement, fat liberation, anti-vaccine movements (whatever one thinks of their politics), Indigenous data sovereignty struggles, queer organising against HIV criminalisation — these are biopolitical counter-movements. As Foucault writes, "where there is power, there is resistance" (*History of Sexuality Vol. 1*, p. 95).

My verdict: biopower is one of the most useful concepts contemporary social theory has. It names something we would otherwise miss — the politics of life itself. But its usefulness depends on careful articulation with disciplinary and sovereign forms, with attention to historical change, and with updates for digital and algorithmic contexts. Used carelessly, it explains too much; used carefully, it explains exactly what other vocabularies cannot.

### 2021 (Q10): Do we live in a "disciplinary society"? Use the work of Foucault to explain your response.

We do live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's specific sense — though we also live in a biopolitical and partially sovereign one. My answer is "yes, but not exclusively", and the qualification matters.

To answer the question I need first to clarify what Foucault means by a disciplinary society. The concept is articulated in *Discipline and Punish* (1975) and refers, as the Calhoun reader extract makes clear, to "the formation of a disciplinary society in this movement that stretches from the enclosed disciplines, a sort of social 'quarantine', to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 316). The disciplinary society is one in which the techniques first developed within enclosed institutions — prisons, barracks, hospitals, schools — generalise across the social field, becoming the dominant mode through which conduct is shaped. The crucial features, set out in the lecture, are: power operates through everyday practices, not principally through state edict; it works by surveillance, normalisation, and examination rather than by physical coercion; it produces docile bodies that regulate themselves against invented norms; and it is productive — generating subjects, knowledges, and institutions — not merely repressive.

Now the affirmative case for the contemporary UK and similar societies.

Schools first. The British school, my own and probably the marker's, is a near-perfect Foucauldian institution. Registers, the school timetable, school photos, progress reports, key stages, SATs, GCSEs, A-levels, Ofsted inspections, parents' evenings, league tables — every one of these is an instrument of hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and examination. The pupil is observed continually, measured against age-appropriate norms, and documented in records that follow them through life. "This will go on your permanent record" is the disciplinary motto.

The workplace. HR performance reviews, 360-degree feedback, KPIs, time-sheets, productivity software (Hubstaff, Time Doctor), Slack message audits, Amazon warehouse handheld scanners that track pickers by the second — all disciplinary. Foxconn's worker surveillance, Uber's driver ratings, the gig economy's algorithmic management — all disciplinary.

Public space. CCTV in UK cities (Britain has among the highest CCTV density in the world), automatic number plate recognition, facial recognition trials by the Metropolitan Police, TSA-style body scanners at airports. These are panoptic in Bentham's exact sense: visible (we see the cameras) but unverifiable (we never know whether we are being watched on any given screen).

The body. Fitbit, Apple Watch, MyFitnessPal, Strava, sleep-tracking apps, period-tracking apps, mood-tracking apps. Susan Bordo's analysis of the slender body is now everywhere: "Self-management in consumer culture…becomes more elusive as it becomes more pressing…the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, "Reading the Slender Body", 1992, pp. 202–3). The lecture notes the rise of gym membership in the City of London as a sign of professional self-mastery — that observation has only intensified.

Knowledge and expertise. The medicalisation of mental health, the rise of ADHD diagnoses, the DSM as a documenter of normal and pathological — these are power/knowledge in operation. Psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy, and social work are not innocent sciences; they produce the subjects they describe.

The credit score. A single normalised number, generated by hierarchical observation of every transaction, determines access to housing, employment, and loans. China's Social Credit System makes the logic explicit and universal.

So yes, by every test Foucault would apply, contemporary Britain is a disciplinary society. The lecture's claim that "contemporary society is a 'disciplinary society'" stands.

The qualifications. First, we also live in a biopolitical society. The COVID-19 response, the NHS, vaccination programmes, statins, sugar taxes, climate policy — these target populations, not individuals, and operate by a different (though related) logic. Disciplinary techniques work on bodies; biopolitical techniques work on populations. Both are present.

Second, sovereign power has not vanished. The British state still deports asylum seekers, imprisons, polices, sometimes lethally. The US executes prisoners. Drone strikes kill outside any disciplinary frame. The criminalisation of protest under the Public Order Act 2023 in the UK reasserts a sovereign capacity to forbid. Foucault was always clear that disciplinary power supplements, rather than abolishes, sovereign power.

Third, the disciplinary society is unevenly distributed. Working-class and racialised populations face more intensive surveillance — stop and search, predictive policing, school exclusion rates. The disciplinary society is itself a stratified one.

Fourth, resistance exists. Strikes against algorithmic management at Amazon and Uber, opt-out movements against school SATs, the Black Lives Matter response to predictive policing, anti-surveillance campaigns by groups like Big Brother Watch — these are disciplinary counter-movements. Foucault's insistence that "where there is power, there is resistance" (*History of Sexuality Vol. 1*, p. 95) remains operative.

My answer, then: we live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's sense, but the disciplinary register is one among several. The CCTV camera, the Fitbit, the school register, the credit score, the LinkedIn endorsement, the Ofsted report — all confirm Foucault's thesis. But the deportation flight, the COVID modelling, the welfare sanction — these require biopolitical and sovereign analyses alongside it. The right answer to the exam question is yes, with the right qualifications attached.

### 2021 (Q11): Outline and assess Foucault's discussions of power in terms of the governance of a population.

Foucault's discussions of the governance of a population centre on his concept of biopower, introduced in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) and developed in the Collège de France lectures of the late 1970s, especially *Society Must Be Defended* (1975–76) and *Security, Territory, Population* (1977–78). Biopower names a form of power that takes the population, rather than the individual body, as its object. It complements the disciplinary power Foucault had analysed in *Discipline and Punish* (1975), and together the two constitute what Foucault calls the "two poles" of modern power over life.

I will outline biopower in three steps, then assess it.

First, the historical claim. Foucault argues that from the late eighteenth century, a new form of power emerged alongside discipline. Where the sovereign had exercised the right to "take life or let live" — a negative, episodic, deductive power — modern states began to exercise the right to "make live and let die." Population becomes a problem of government: birth rates, mortality, fertility, longevity, public health, epidemics, hygiene, housing, food supply. The objects of this new power are statistical: not the individual body but the species body. The instruments are not the prison or the school but the census, the actuarial table, the epidemiological survey, the public health campaign, the urban plan.

Second, the conceptual structure. Biopower operates through what Foucault calls "regularising mechanisms": interventions that aim to optimise the vital processes of the population as a whole. Where disciplinary power individualises (each pupil has a transcript), biopower massifies (the cohort has a literacy rate). Where discipline aims at the docile and useful body, biopower aims at the secure and productive population. Knowledge here takes the form of statistics, demography, political economy, and epidemiology. Foucault's quip that "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order, nor for assisting governments in their struggle against their enemies, but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 215) captures the genealogy: the modern "police" originally meant the management of urban life, of which crime control was only one piece.

Third, the link to governmentality. In his late lectures, Foucault generalises biopower into the broader concept of governmentality — the rationalities and techniques by which conduct is conducted. Modern liberal governmentality, Foucault argues, governs not by direct command but by structuring the field of choices so that individuals govern themselves. Neoliberal governmentality, in particular, responsibilises individuals for their own risks (private pensions, private insurance, Fitbit-style self-tracking) while treating populations as aggregates of human capital.

Now the assessment.

Strengths. Biopower captures something traditional theories miss. Mass public health, vaccination, sugar taxes, statins, contact tracing, NHS data analytics, the actuarial logic of insurance, predictive policing, credit scoring, COVID-era population-level interventions — none are well described by either "the ruling class wills it" or "the state forbids it." They are biopolitical: they take populations as objects and optimise them. The HIV/AIDS public health response of the 1980s — its targeting of risk groups, its failures around gay men, sex workers, and African populations — is unthinkable without biopolitical analysis. The reproductive politics of foetal ultrasound, IVF regulation, fertility incentives, and abortion law are biopolitical. So is climate policy, increasingly: carbon budgets, emissions per capita, the management of populations as ecological agents.

A second strength: biopower illuminates the dark side of modern humanitarianism. Mid-century welfare states cared for populations; eugenic movements managed them; the line between care and control is thin. The same statistical techniques that produce universal vaccination produce racial classification.

A third strength: biopower opens onto necropolitics. Achille Mbembe's extension of Foucault — biopower's "let die" pole, exercised at borders, in occupied territories, in slums — captures the violent underside of contemporary governance. Gaza, the Mediterranean, the abandonment of immunocompromised populations during late COVID — these are necropolitical operations.

Weaknesses. First, the concept can be over-extended. If everything that touches life is biopolitical, the term loses purchase. Some critics argue Foucault himself is partly responsible for this elasticity.

Second, biopower must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power. The Amazon warehouse worker faces all three. The COVID lockdown was biopolitical (modelling), disciplinary (police enforcement of household rules), and sovereign (criminalisation of breaches). To pick one is to miss the others.

Third, the concept is gendered and raced in ways Foucault did not fully develop. Feminist scholars like Penelope Deutscher and Jasbir Puar have shown how reproductive biopolitics operates differently across gendered and racialised populations. Black women in US obstetric care, working-class women in UK fertility services, migrant women in detention — biopower acts on them through specific mechanisms Foucault did not theorise.

Fourth, the question of resistance. If populations are constituted by biopower, what is the locus of biopolitical agency? Foucault's answer — "where there is power, there is resistance" — is correct but thin. ACT UP, fat liberation, Indigenous data sovereignty, the right-to-die movement, abortion access campaigns — these are biopolitical counter-movements, but theorising them requires going beyond Foucault.

My assessment: Foucault's discussions of the governance of a population through biopower are essential to contemporary social theory but not sufficient. They illuminate what other vocabularies cannot — the politics of life itself, the statistical management of populations, the link between welfare and control. But they need supplementing with structural analyses of capital, gender, and race, with attention to historical change between welfarist and neoliberal biopolitics, and with updated frameworks for digital and algorithmic biopolitics (Zuboff, Noble, Chun). Used in this way, biopower remains one of the most powerful concepts in the toolkit.

## 8. Mock Exam Questions and Answers

### Mock Q1 (to-what-extent): To what extent is the panopticon an adequate model for understanding surveillance in twenty-first-century societies?

The panopticon, as Bentham designed it and Foucault redescribed it, is an architectural diagram of disciplinary surveillance: a central tower from which the inmates of back-lit cells can be continuously observed, while themselves remaining unable to verify when they are being watched. Foucault treats it as a model for the broader form of modern power. In *Discipline and Punish* he writes that its "major effect" is "to induce in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 315 in Calhoun reader). The visibility is, as Foucault summarises, a trap.

The question is whether this nineteenth-century diagram still captures how surveillance works in the twenty-first. My answer: partially yes, partially no — the panopticon remains useful but needs substantial supplementation.

The panopticon captures a great deal of contemporary surveillance. CCTV in UK cities, with cameras that are visibly mounted but whose monitoring is unverifiable, replicates Bentham's logic exactly. Britain has among the densest CCTV networks in the world; we all behave as if we may be watched, even though most footage is never reviewed. Airport TSA body scanners, automatic number plate recognition, the police use of facial recognition at protests — these are panoptic. The workplace deploys panoptic techniques: Amazon warehouse handheld scanners that track pickers by the second, Foxconn factory surveillance, Hubstaff and Time Doctor software for remote workers, Uber driver dashboards. School registers, exam invigilation cameras, university Turnitin submissions — disciplinary panopticism continues. The Fitbit and Apple Watch turn the wearer into both watcher and watched: a panopticon of one, where the inmate inspects herself.

But the panopticon falls short in several respects. First, contemporary surveillance is largely **data-driven** rather than gaze-based. Shoshana Zuboff's analysis of surveillance capitalism (*The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, 2019) describes a regime in which platforms (Google, Meta, Amazon) accumulate behavioural data through APIs, cookies, and sensors rather than through visible cameras. There is no central tower; there are server farms. The data subject often does not know they are observed and would not recognise the form of observation if they saw it.

Second, contemporary surveillance is **distributed and lateral**, not centralised and vertical. The panopticon has one tower; social media has millions of mutually observing users. Selfies, TikTok, Instagram stories, the "main character" phenomenon — these are not panopticism but what some theorists have called synopticism (the many watching the few) or omnopticism (everyone watching everyone). Hashtag campaigns, doxxing, and cancel culture deploy lateral surveillance.

Third, contemporary surveillance is **algorithmic** and operates through pattern detection rather than continuous gaze. Predictive policing, credit scoring, content moderation, hiring algorithms — these classify and act on bodies without anyone watching. Safiya Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* (2018) and Virginia Eubanks's *Automating Inequality* (2018) describe an opaque, automated surveillance that Bentham could not have imagined.

Fourth, contemporary surveillance is **biopolitical** as well as disciplinary. Contact-tracing apps during COVID, NHS digital records, public health dashboards — these target populations, not individuals, and aggregate without individualising. The panopticon individualises by design; biopolitical surveillance does the opposite.

Fifth, contemporary surveillance is **participatory**. Bentham's inmate did not choose the cell. The Fitbit user chooses the wristband. The Instagram user chooses the post. China's Social Credit System exploits the same logic at scale. Voluntary participation is a feature of contemporary surveillance Foucault did not fully theorise — though Bordo's account of self-monitoring anticipates it: "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1992, pp. 202–3).

So how adequate is the panopticon? Adequate as a description of one strand among several. It captures CCTV, airport security, workplace surveillance, school discipline — the strand where visibility-but-unverifiability operates. It captures the internalisation of the gaze. It captures the link between visibility, knowledge, and power. But it under-describes data-driven, algorithmic, lateral, biopolitical, and participatory surveillance. The most fruitful contemporary scholarship — Zuboff, Chun, Browne (on the racial dimensions of surveillance in *Dark Matters*) — treats the panopticon as one diagram among several rather than the master image.

The question of whether the panopticon still works depends on what we ask it to do. As a diagnostic tool for one mode of disciplinary surveillance, it remains indispensable. As a complete theory of twenty-first-century surveillance, it is inadequate. The right verdict, I think, is to retain Foucault's insight that visibility is a trap while extending the vocabulary to capture the new forms — surveillance capitalism, algorithmic governance, lateral exposure — that operate alongside the older panoptic mode.

### Mock Q2 (single-author): Critically discuss Foucault's claim that "power is productive, not repressive". Illustrate with examples.

Foucault's claim that power is productive rather than merely repressive is one of the most consequential moves in twentieth-century social theory. It marks the decisive break from the liberal model (power as constraint on a free individual), the psychoanalytic model (power as the law of prohibition), and especially the Marxist model (power as ideological repression of class consciousness). For Foucault, power produces — bodies, subjects, knowledges, types of people, kinds of pleasure, kinds of resistance.

I want to set out the claim, illustrate it with several examples, and then assess its plausibility and limits.

The claim itself. In *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) Foucault argues that the Victorian regime around sexuality was not a great "repressive hypothesis" silencing sex but a vast incitement to discourse: confessions, medical treatises, psychiatric diagnoses, sexological classifications. Sexuality was not silenced; it was multiplied. The homosexual, the masturbating child, the hysterical woman, the Malthusian couple — these were not pre-existing categories suppressed by Victorian morality but new "personages" produced by the discursive apparatus. In *Discipline and Punish* (1975) the same logic applies to the prisoner: the modern prison does not just punish a pre-existing criminal but produces the modern delinquent through its examinations, classifications, and documentation. As Foucault puts it elsewhere, power "makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 212) — both subjected to power and constituted as subjects by it.

The lecture captures this with a striking quotation: "We should not forget that…the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order, nor for assisting governments in their struggle against their enemies, but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 215). The point: even the institution we most associate with repression has productive functions.

Examples.

First, the school. The British school does not merely constrain children — it produces them. As "pupils", "gifted children", "SEN children", "EAL children", "high achievers", "underachievers", "school refusers". Each category is generated by examination, observation, and documentation. The "ADHD child" of 2026 is a category produced by psychiatric, pedagogical, and pharmaceutical apparatuses that did not exist in 1980. This is power/knowledge in action.

Second, sexuality. The category "homosexual" was, on Foucault's account, produced in the late nineteenth century by sexology, psychiatry, and law. Before this, same-sex acts existed but the "homosexual person" did not. Foucault writes that the homosexual became "a personage, a past, a case history" — power produced a kind of person. Contemporary parallels: "asexual", "non-binary", "demisexual" — these are not necessarily false categories, but they are categories produced through specific discursive and institutional apparatuses (online communities, clinical guidelines, legal recognition).

Third, the body. Susan Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" (1992) shows how disciplinary power produces particular kinds of bodies: the toned, well-defined, slender, self-controlled body of the late twentieth-century professional. This body is not natural but produced — by gyms, diets, magazines, advertisements, social media — and the producing is itself an exercise of power. The City of London banker who works out is not repressed; she is produced.

Fourth, the worker. Amazon's algorithmic management does not just constrain pickers; it produces the "picker" as a specific kind of subject — a body whose rate, units-per-hour, and rest breaks are measured and optimised. The Uber driver is a category produced by app-based dispatch, ratings, and surge pricing.

Fifth, medical categories. The medicalisation of mental health produces "depressed patients", "BPD patients", "ADHD adults". These categories shape how we understand ourselves; they enrol us in regimes of treatment, medication, and therapy that produce particular subjectivities.

Sixth, public health. Vaccination campaigns produce "the vaccinated population" as a manageable demographic; the unvaccinated become a problematic category. HIV testing produced "the HIV+" subject. COVID produced "the close contact", "the asymptomatic carrier".

Assessment.

Strengths. The productive thesis explains phenomena traditional sociology struggles with. It explains why social categories proliferate even as we claim to liberate ourselves. It explains the link between knowledge production (psychiatry, criminology, sexology) and power. It explains why resistance often takes the form of inventing new categories (queer, non-binary) rather than abolishing them. It gives us a vocabulary for analysing how Fitbits, social media, and self-help apps work — not by repressing us but by producing us as particular kinds of self-monitoring subjects.

Limits. The thesis risks erasing genuinely repressive power. Mass incarceration is productive (it produces "felons", "ex-offenders") but also genuinely repressive (it imprisons, denies the vote, breaks families). The Chinese state's repression of Uyghurs produces categories ("re-education", "vocational training") but is also straightforwardly violent. Privileging the productive register can sound apologist.

A second limit: the thesis can underplay agency. If power produces subjects, are subjects ever the authors of their own lives? Judith Butler in *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997) addresses this by arguing that the same power that subjects us also enables agency, but this remains philosophically contested.

A third limit: not all production is morally equivalent. Foucault sometimes writes as if any production is just one more instance of power. But producing "the loved partner" through care work and producing "the felon" through criminal justice are not the same.

My verdict: the productive thesis is one of Foucault's most powerful insights, and it remains indispensable for understanding contemporary subjectivities, institutions, and categories. But it is not a complete theory of power. Repression still happens; some productions are worse than others; and we still need normative criteria to distinguish them. The right use of the thesis is as a corrective to Marxist and liberal accounts that miss the productive dimension, not as their replacement.

### Mock Q3 (comparison): Compare Foucault's account of power with the Marxist account. Whose analysis do you find more useful for contemporary sociology?

Foucault's account of power and the Marxist account share important commitments — both insist that contemporary societies are deeply structured by power relations, both reject naive liberal claims about individual freedom, and both link knowledge to the conditions of its production — but they differ fundamentally in method, scale, and politics.

I will set out the Marxist account, then Foucault's challenge, then their points of overlap and divergence, before assessing which is more useful for contemporary sociology.

The Marxist account. For Marx and his successors, power in capitalist societies is rooted in ownership of the means of production. The bourgeoisie own the factories, banks, and land; the proletariat own only their labour power. The state, in Marx's *Communist Manifesto* formulation, is "a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie." Ideology, in Marx and especially in later Marxists like Lukács, Gramsci, and Althusser, is the principal mechanism by which the working class is brought to consent to its own subordination. Power is concentrated at the top of a pyramid; it is unequally distributed; it is dangerous because it is unjust; it can in principle be seized and abolished through revolution. Feminist Marxists (Hartmann, Federici) add that capitalism's exploitation is gendered: reproductive labour is unpaid, women are doubly exploited.

Foucault's challenge. Foucault accepts that capitalism is a power-saturated form of society. But he rejects three Marxist commitments. First, he rejects the **top-down model**: power, for Foucault, "comes from below" and is built up from micro-relations of force, not down from class ownership. Second, he rejects the **ideology model**: power is not principally a matter of false consciousness but of bodily training, examination, normalisation. As he puts it in the lecture, power is "much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 102). Third, he rejects the **revolutionary model**: power is omnipresent, so seizing the means of production does not abolish power but redistributes it. His sharpest critique of Marxism is the line we have already encountered: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 116).

Points of overlap. Both traditions insist that power and knowledge are linked, though Marxists tend to call this "ideology" and Foucault calls it "power/knowledge". Both insist on the centrality of the body and labour. Both reject liberal pieties about freedom. Both have a historical method that locates contemporary forms in specific genealogies.

Points of divergence. The Marxist asks "who benefits?" Foucault asks "how does it work?" The Marxist looks for structural causes (capital accumulation); Foucault looks for techniques and tactics (the timetable, the examination). The Marxist sees power as a thing to be seized; Foucault sees power as a relation to be analysed. The Marxist has a normative endpoint (communism, emancipation); Foucault is more reticent, even agnostic.

Which is more useful? I want to argue: both, in combination, but with Foucault adding indispensable tools that Marxism alone lacks.

Marxism remains essential for the macro-questions. Why do Amazon warehouses exist? Because Jeff Bezos accumulated capital under conditions of weak labour protection. Why are British social services collapsing? Because of austerity policies that protected capital and squeezed public spending. Why does climate policy progress so slowly? Because fossil capital wields enormous structural power. These are Marxist questions, and they need Marxist answers.

But Foucault is essential for the micro-questions. **How** is the Amazon warehouse run? Through algorithmic management, handheld scanners, rate metrics, surveillance — disciplinary techniques. **How** are British social services delivered? Through case-management software, risk assessment algorithms, performance dashboards — biopolitical and disciplinary techniques. **How** is climate policy implemented? Through carbon budgets, emissions per capita, individualised offsetting — biopolitical techniques. Marxism alone cannot describe these mechanisms.

The most fruitful contemporary scholarship combines the two. Wendy Brown's *Undoing the Demos* (2015) reads neoliberalism through both Marxist and Foucauldian lenses. Shoshana Zuboff's *Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) names a structural form (capitalism) and a technical mode (surveillance). Achille Mbembe's necropolitics extends Foucault to the violent racial capitalism Marxists have always analysed. Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* (2004) reads early modern witch-hunts as both capital accumulation and disciplinary production of female bodies.

For contemporary sociology, then, I find Foucault more methodologically generative — he tells us how to look — and Marxism more politically clarifying — it tells us who wins. Foucault without Marx risks losing structural critique. Marx without Foucault risks losing the texture of everyday power. Together they enable an analysis adequate to Amazon, Foxconn, the NHS, the British school, and the Fitbit on my wrist.

If I had to choose one, I would choose Foucault — not because he is more right but because he is more often **missing** from contemporary analysis. Marxism's vocabulary remains alive (class, capital, exploitation, accumulation) even in mainstream political discourse. Foucault's vocabulary (disciplinary power, biopower, governmentality, normalisation) is more often missing where it is most needed. The marginal utility of Foucault, in 2026, is higher.

### Mock Q4 (discuss-with-examples): Discuss, with examples, the relationship between power and knowledge in Foucault's work.

For Foucault, power and knowledge are inseparable. He coins the hyphenated term power/knowledge to capture this: there is no neutral knowledge of human beings standing apart from power, and no exercise of power that does not produce and rely on knowledge. The relationship is not external (knowledge serves the powerful) but internal (knowledge and power are mutually constitutive). This is one of Foucault's most influential claims, and I want to explain it carefully and illustrate with examples across psychiatry, criminology, sexology, pedagogy, public health, and contemporary digital systems.

Foucault sets out the claim in several places. In *Discipline and Punish* (1975), the modern prison is not just an institution that punishes; it generates a science — criminology — that classifies, measures, and explains the delinquent. The classification then justifies and refines the punishment. In *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976), the Victorian incitement to discourse about sex produced sexology, psychiatry, and the medical-legal complex around perversion. In *Madness and Civilization* (1961), the production of the modern asylum produced psychiatry as a discipline, and psychiatry in turn produced "the mad" as a recognisable kind of person. Foucault summarises in the lecture's quoted line: power "applies itself to immediate everyday life…imposes a law of truth on [the individual]…it is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 212).

The opening Kember example in the lecture illustrates the link beautifully. Obstetric ultrasound produces knowledge — the foetus as visible image — and this knowledge is itself an exercise of power, reorganising what we see, what we treat as significant, and whose body counts. "The scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, *Virtual Anxiety*, 1998). Knowledge and power coincide in the scan.

Examples.

First, psychiatry. The DSM (now in its fifth edition, with text revision) is a knowledge artefact — a manual of diagnostic categories. It is also a power artefact: insurance reimbursement, school accommodations, criminal responsibility, and self-understanding are all routed through it. The rise of ADHD diagnoses in UK adults since 2020, the broadening of autism spectrum criteria, the controversy over PMDD or BPD as diagnoses — all show the power/knowledge nexus in action. Critics like Allen Frances (former DSM chair) have argued that DSM categories are scientific knowledge **and** institutional power **and** pharmaceutical opportunity simultaneously.

Second, criminology. The "delinquent" of nineteenth-century France was produced by the prison, the case file, and the criminological survey. The contemporary "high-risk offender" is produced by risk assessment instruments like OASys (used in England and Wales). Predictive policing tools (PredPol, used controversially by some US police departments) produce knowledge — crime risk maps — that then directs police deployment, which then produces more recorded crime in the targeted areas, which then justifies the tool. Power/knowledge in a feedback loop.

Third, pedagogy. The British school's apparatus of SATs, GCSEs, A-levels, Ofsted reports, and league tables produces vast amounts of knowledge about pupils, teachers, and schools. This knowledge is itself an exercise of power: it determines university admission, teacher careers, school funding. The "good school" and the "failing school" are categories produced by the apparatus, and these categories then shape the housing market (parents move into catchment areas) and inequality.

Fourth, sexology and gender. Foucault's own primary case. The proliferation of categories — heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, asexual, pansexual, non-binary, trans, cis — is power/knowledge at work. These are not (necessarily) false categories. But they are produced by specific discursive and institutional apparatuses (clinical guidelines like ICD-11, online communities, legal recognition, activist organisations), and they shape who we are and what we can become.

Fifth, public health. Epidemiology is knowledge; it is also power. Risk groups, screening protocols, vaccination priorities — these classify populations and direct interventions. The HIV/AIDS response of the 1980s shows the stakes: classifying gay men as a "risk group" produced both public health knowledge and the stigmatisation that delayed effective response.

Sixth, contemporary digital systems. Credit scores are knowledge (probability of default) and power (access to housing, loans, employment). Facebook's behavioural prediction products are knowledge (what you will buy, vote, share) and power (what you see, who reaches you). The Chinese Social Credit System makes the link maximally explicit.

Critical assessment.

Strengths. The power/knowledge nexus illuminates phenomena that other approaches miss. It explains why the human sciences proliferate even as they claim to liberate. It explains why diagnostic categories shape who we are. It explains why algorithms are not neutral. It gives us tools to critique the knowledge produced by powerful institutions without lapsing into anti-scientific relativism — Foucault is not saying "all truth is power" but "truth has a politics".

Limits. The thesis can be over-extended into a flat relativism. If all knowledge is power, then critical knowledge is also power, including Foucault's own. Foucault was aware of this paradox and tried to address it through the notion of "regimes of truth" — there are different truth-regimes, some more defensible than others — but the philosophical question remains.

A second limit: the thesis is harder to operationalise for natural sciences. Is quantum mechanics power/knowledge in the same way criminology is? Most readers think not. The thesis applies most clearly to the human sciences.

A third limit: the thesis can underplay genuine progress. Antiretroviral therapy is power/knowledge — produced by pharmaceutical capital, regulated by state agencies, marketed selectively. It also keeps millions of HIV-positive people alive. The political critique should not become a cynicism that misses real gains.

My view: the power/knowledge thesis is one of Foucault's most enduring contributions and one of the most useful for contemporary sociology. It demands that we treat the production of knowledge as a political question and the exercise of power as a knowledge-laden practice. From the DSM to the credit score to the Fitbit to the school exam, we live in an apparatus where what we know about ourselves and what is done to us are produced together.

### Mock Q5 (to-what-extent): To what extent can Foucault's account of disciplinary power explain the operations of contemporary digital platforms?

Contemporary digital platforms — Meta, Google, TikTok, Amazon, Uber, the algorithmic systems behind credit scoring, predictive policing, and content moderation — present a test case for Foucault's account of disciplinary power. To what extent can his nineteenth-century framework, articulated in *Discipline and Punish* (1975), illuminate the operations of these twenty-first-century systems?

I will argue that Foucault's account is genuinely illuminating but requires substantial updating. Roughly half of what platforms do is recognisably disciplinary; the other half exceeds Foucault's vocabulary and needs new concepts.

The disciplinary half.

First, platforms deploy **hierarchical observation** in Foucault's sense. Every user is observed by the platform (clicks, scrolls, dwell-time, location, social graph) while the platform itself is largely invisible to users. The user cannot see Meta's servers, cannot audit Meta's algorithms, cannot verify when they are being observed for advertising versus content ranking. This is panopticism in Bentham's sense: visible (we know we're tracked) but unverifiable (we don't know when, how, or by whom).

Second, platforms deploy **normalising judgement**. TikTok's For You algorithm produces an implicit norm of what kinds of content "perform"; creators self-regulate to fit. LinkedIn's endorsement and skills features produce a norm of the marketable professional. Instagram's like-and-follow metrics produce body norms, lifestyle norms, success norms. Users measure themselves against these norms and adjust. As Bordo wrote of body discipline, "bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1992, pp. 202–3) — only now extended to the entire self-presentation.

Third, platforms deploy **examination**. The credit score, the Uber driver rating, the eBay seller rating, the Airbnb host rating, the LinkedIn endorsement, the TikTok engagement rate — all are continuous examinations whose results are documented and follow the subject through life and across platforms. Amazon's algorithmic management of warehouse workers is examination in its purest form: every minute of every shift is timed and rated.

Fourth, platforms produce **docile bodies**. The Uber driver who maintains a 4.8 rating, the Instagram influencer who hits the algorithm's preferred posting frequency, the Amazon picker who exceeds rate, the gig worker who never declines a job — these are docile in Foucault's sense: useful, self-regulating, productive. Crucially, no one forces them. As Foucault writes of disciplinary power, "if power 'shouted at us' all the time we would notice it" — instead, "it invites and incites us to behave in certain ways" (lecture slide).

Fifth, platforms generate the **subject** they describe. Just as the modern prison produced the delinquent, TikTok produces the creator, LinkedIn produces the personal brand, Strava produces the cyclist, MyFitnessPal produces the calorie-counter. These are not categories that pre-existed the platform; they are co-produced with it.

So far, Foucault works.

But the platform exceeds Foucault in important ways.

First, **observation is automated, not gaze-based**. Bentham's tower had a guard (real or fictional). Platforms have no guard — observation is performed by sensors, APIs, cookies, and machine learning models. The "tower" is a server farm, and the observation is not principally for individual disciplining but for population-level prediction. This is closer to biopower than to discipline, and it is closer still to what Shoshana Zuboff calls surveillance capitalism in *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019).

Second, **judgement is algorithmic, not normative**. Foucault's normalising judgement assumed an explicit norm to measure against. Algorithmic systems often produce statistical patterns without an explicit norm — the "engagement-maximising" feed, the "click-through-optimising" ad, the "default-probability-minimising" credit score. These behave normatively but do not articulate norms. Wendy Chun's *Discriminating Data* (2021) and Safiya Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression* (2018) describe this without-norm normativity well.

Third, **the data subject is fractured**. Foucault's subject was an integrated individual to be examined. The platform's subject is a dispersed data shadow — a credit score here, a TikTok account there, a Uber rating elsewhere, a health insurance risk profile somewhere else. The unification, when it happens, is done by the platform, not the subject.

Fourth, **observation is participatory**. Bentham's inmate did not consent. The Instagram user does. The relationship between freedom and discipline is more entangled than Foucault's prison model suggests. Bordo and Butler both point in this direction, but Foucault himself never fully theorised platform participation.

Fifth, **the political economy is capital-driven**. Platforms exist because they make money. The disciplinary techniques are subordinated to revenue extraction. A purely Foucauldian analysis can lose sight of this, which is why Zuboff, Nick Srnicek's *Platform Capitalism* (2017), and McKenzie Wark's *Capital Is Dead* (2019) supplement Foucault with structural critique.

So my answer to the question: Foucault's account of disciplinary power explains a great deal about contemporary digital platforms, but not everything. It captures the surveillance, the self-regulation, the normalising drift, the production of new kinds of subjects. It does not capture the algorithmic without-norm, the data-shadow subject, the participatory consent, or the political economy. The best analyses — Zuboff, Chun, Noble, Browne — combine Foucauldian disciplinary analysis with surveillance capitalism critique, algorithmic studies, and critical race theory.

The right verdict: Foucault is necessary but not sufficient. We need *Discipline and Punish* on the reading list for any course on platforms. We also need much more.

### Mock Q6 (single-author): "Foucault's analysis of power leaves no space for resistance." Discuss.

The accusation that Foucault's analysis of power leaves no space for resistance has been levelled by Marxist critics (Nicos Poulantzas, Frank Pearce), feminist critics (Nancy Hartsock, Lois McNay), and Habermasian critics (Habermas himself, Charles Taylor). The charge is serious because, if true, it would render Foucault's analysis politically sterile — a description of domination with no purchase on emancipation.

I want to argue that the charge is largely wrong, though it points to a real difficulty in Foucault's later work. Resistance is internal to Foucault's account of power, not external to it; but resistance in his sense is more modest, more local, and more entangled than traditional emancipatory politics imagines.

The case for the prosecution.

The critics make three claims. First, Foucault says power is omnipresent — "always already there, one is never outside it" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch). If power is everywhere, then there is no Archimedean point from which to resist. Second, Foucault says power produces the subject — "it is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, "The Subject and Power", 1982, p. 212). If the subject is produced by power, then the subject of resistance is also produced by power, and resistance is just power moving against itself. Third, Foucault refuses normative grounds — he does not tell us why one configuration of power is better than another. Without normative grounds, resistance is just preference.

Hartsock's *Money, Sex, and Power* (1983) puts the feminist version of the charge sharply: if power constitutes the gendered subject, then the feminist subject is constituted by patriarchal power and cannot ground emancipatory critique. Habermas's *Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) puts the universalist version: without normative criteria, Foucault is a "young conservative".

The case for the defence.

First, Foucault explicitly insists resistance is internal to power. The famous formulation in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) is: "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet, or rather consequently, this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power" (Foucault, 1990 ed., p. 95). Resistance is not outside power; it is part of how power works. In a late interview Foucault said: "If there were no possibility of resistance — of violent resistance, of escape, of ruse, of strategies that reverse the situation — there would be no relations of power" (Foucault, 1994). Power without resistance is not power but domination.

Second, the omnipresence of power does not mean its totality. Power is everywhere but not everything. As Lynch summarises: "Power does not 'consolidate everything' or 'embrace everything' or 'answer everything'." There are many relations of force and many resistances within them. The Foucauldian world is plural, not monolithic.

Third, Foucault's later work on ethics and the technologies of the self explicitly addresses how subjects can work on themselves to become other than what power has made them. The late lectures on parrhesia (truth-telling) and the care of the self propose a positive ethics of self-fashioning. Resistance is not just refusing power but actively cultivating different modes of subjectivity.

Fourth, examples of Foucauldian resistance abound and are politically legible. The gay liberation movement that he supported, the prison reform movement (the Groupe d'Information sur les Prisons that Foucault co-founded), the anti-psychiatry movement, queer politics, the body positivity movement, fat liberation, sex worker organising, the Black Lives Matter response to predictive policing — these are Foucauldian resistances. They do not seize state power; they contest specific techniques and reshape specific subjectivities.

Fifth, the normative question. Foucault's refusal of foundationalism is not nihilism. He defends what he calls "specific intellectuals" who intervene in particular struggles without claiming universal authority. He defends freedom as a practice: not a metaphysical property but something we do. "If there are relations of power throughout every social field it is because there is freedom everywhere" (Foucault, 1994).

The genuine difficulty.

That said, the critics point to a real problem. Foucauldian resistance is local, tactical, and reversible. It can win specific battles (gay marriage, prison abolition campaigns, the body positivity movement). It is less good at organising the kind of structural transformation Marxists envision (overthrowing capitalism, abolishing patriarchy). The strategic-not-revolutionary character of Foucauldian resistance disappoints those who want bigger horizons.

A second difficulty: if resistance is internal to power, it can be recuperated. Body positivity becomes a marketing strategy. Queer identities become Pride Month brand opportunities. Mindfulness becomes corporate wellness. The "neoliberal subject" can absorb resistance more effectively than the disciplinary subject did. Foucault's framework gives us tools to see this but does not give us a clear way out.

A third difficulty: Foucault sometimes writes as if he describes resistance from above. He rarely writes about the people doing the resisting. Black, feminist, postcolonial, and disability theorists have had to extend his framework to capture the standpoints of the resisting.

My verdict. The strong claim — Foucault leaves no space for resistance — is wrong. Resistance is constitutive of his account of power, and his political life (GIP, support for gay liberation, solidarity with Iranian revolutionaries, however controversially) shows he believed in resistance. The moderate claim — Foucault's resistance is local, tactical, and reversible rather than revolutionary — is correct. Whether this is a flaw depends on what you want from political theory.

For my own part, I think the local-tactical model is honest about what is possible in conditions where power runs through the smallest details of our bodies and platforms. The struggle against the Fitbit, the credit score, the school exam, the algorithmic feed has to be granular because the power is granular. Foucault gives us tools for that struggle. He does not give us a master narrative of liberation. That is a feature, not a bug.

### Mock Q7 (discuss-with-examples): "Foucault has been more influential on feminist theory than Marx." Discuss with examples.

The claim that Foucault has been more influential on feminist theory than Marx is provocative but defensible — at least for the dominant strands of feminist theory since the 1980s. I will set out the case, name the key feminist Foucauldians and their contributions, acknowledge what Marx and Marxist feminism contribute, and then assess.

The case for Foucault's dominance.

Three waves of feminist Foucauldianism stand out.

First, the body. Susan Bordo's *Unbearable Weight* (1993) and especially "Reading the Slender Body" (1992) take Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power and apply it to the gendered production of bodies. The argument: norms of slenderness, control, and self-mastery are not just aesthetic but disciplinary — they produce "bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1992, pp. 202–3). Eating disorders, cosmetic surgery, exercise culture, the rise of the gym, the wellness industry — Bordo reads all of these as gendered disciplinary apparatuses. Sandra Bartky's earlier "Foucault, Femininity, and the Modernization of Patriarchal Power" (1988) makes a similar argument: women internalise the male gaze as a panoptic discipline.

Second, gender as performative. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993) take Foucault's productive thesis and apply it to gender itself. The argument: gender is not the cultural expression of a natural sex; gender is produced through repeated performances that congeal into the appearance of natural sex. Compulsory heterosexuality, drag, the medicalisation of intersex bodies, the legal recognition of trans identities — all become legible as productive operations of power/knowledge. Butler's later *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997) takes the further step of asking how power forms the subject: "We are used to thinking of power as what presses on the subject from the outside…But if…we understand power as forming the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence…then power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997, pp. 1–2).

Third, sexuality and reproduction. Foucault's *History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976) reframed sexuality not as repressed nature but as discursively produced apparatus. Feminist theorists from Gayle Rubin's "Thinking Sex" (1984) to Lauren Berlant's *The Female Complaint* (2008) developed this. Sarah Kember's *Virtual Anxiety* (1998) read obstetric ultrasound as a power/knowledge operation that "eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus." Reproductive biopolitics — IVF, abortion law, surrogacy, foetal personhood — is now standardly theorised in Foucauldian terms.

Other examples. Linda Alcoff and Nancy Fraser on the politics of epistemology; Jasbir Puar's *Terrorist Assemblages* (2007) on homonationalism and biopolitics; Penelope Deutscher's *Foucault's Futures* (2017) on reproductive biopolitics; the entire field of feminist surveillance studies (Lyon, Browne, Gilliom).

The case for Marx.

But the claim is not undisputed. Marxist feminism has its own genealogy, often pre-dating Foucauldian feminism. Silvia Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* (2004) reads the witch-hunts as primitive accumulation against women's reproductive labour. Selma James and Mariarosa Dalla Costa's wages-for-housework movement (1972) used Marx to analyse unpaid reproductive work. Heidi Hartmann's "The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism" (1979) argued for dual-systems theory. Recent social reproduction feminism (Tithi Bhattacharya, Susan Ferguson, Nancy Fraser herself) has revived Marxist categories for analysing care work, gendered labour, and capitalism's reliance on unpaid reproductive labour.

In Black feminist thought, both traditions appear. Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) draws on Marxist analysis of structural inequality and Foucauldian analysis of power/knowledge. Hortense Spillers's "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe" (1987) reads slavery's destruction of Black motherhood in terms compatible with both traditions.

In materialist feminism (Stevi Jackson, Christine Delphy), Marx remains central. In economic feminism analysing the gender pay gap, the glass ceiling, and the care economy, Marx remains central.

Assessment.

The claim that Foucault is more influential is true for some strands of feminism (queer theory, body studies, biopolitics, surveillance studies) and false for others (materialist feminism, social reproduction theory, economic feminism). The claim's truth depends on which feminism we are talking about.

I would offer a more careful formulation: Foucault has been more influential than Marx on **cultural feminism, queer feminism, and the feminist theory of the body and subjectivity since the 1980s**; Marx remains more influential than Foucault on **economic feminism, social reproduction theory, and labour-focused feminism**. The dominant academic feminism of the past forty years — taught in gender studies departments, published in *differences*, *Signs*, and *GLQ* — has been more Foucauldian than Marxist. But that does not mean Foucault has been more influential on feminism as a whole.

Why Foucault appealed to feminists. Several reasons. He offered a framework for analysing how gendered bodies are produced rather than presupposed; he offered an account of how power works through everyday practices and intimate relations (where patriarchy lives); he challenged the public/private divide that liberal feminism struggled with; and his account of power as productive matched feminist intuitions that patriarchy is not just repression but constitution.

Why Marx remains essential. The gender pay gap, the care economy, women's poverty, women's overrepresentation in precarious labour, the global feminisation of poverty — these are economic phenomena that need economic analysis. Foucauldian feminism can be accused (Hartsock did) of losing sight of these material structures.

My verdict: the claim is half-true. Feminist theory of subjectivity, body, and sexuality has been more Foucauldian; feminist theory of labour, capital, and material reproduction has remained more Marxist. The best feminist theory — Federici, Fraser, Brown, Berlant — combines both. Foucault expanded feminist theory; he did not replace Marx in it.

### Mock Q8 (to-what-extent): To what extent is "the normal" a useful sociological category, in light of Foucault's account?

Foucault's lecture treats the nineteenth-century invention of "the normal" as one of the most consequential developments in modern social organisation. The lecture slide asks pointedly: "Is the concept of the 'normal' one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity?" My answer is qualified yes — "the normal" is a useful sociological category, but only when used critically rather than descriptively.

Foucault's argument. Pre-modern forms of discipline operated by prohibition: "what is forbidden" and "what is allowed". The disciplinary revolution introduces a third term: "what is normal". Normality is not a fact discovered by science but a category produced by the techniques of examination, observation, and statistical aggregation. The bell curve, the growth chart, the IQ test, the BMI, the developmental milestones of childhood — all are nineteenth- and twentieth-century inventions that established "normal" as a measurable, comparable, manageable property of individuals and populations.

The category does enormous work. It produces deviance: where there is normal, there is the abnormal — the deviant, the pathological, the delinquent, the special-needs, the at-risk. It enables intervention: the abnormal can be normalised through education, medicine, therapy, surgery. It internalises judgement: the lecture notes that disciplinary society makes us take "responsibility as individuals for matching up to norms" — "Am I different from the rest? Is everyone like me? Am I a good friend?" Hence, the slide notes, "the prevalence of questionnaires etc."

For Foucault, then, the normal is a technology, not a description. It is one of the principal instruments through which modern populations are managed.

Examples of "the normal" at work.

First, child development. The Health Visitor's growth chart, the developmental milestones (sits at 6 months, walks at 12 months, talks at 18 months), the school's age-appropriate norms — these produce "the normal child" and the "developmentally delayed". Neurodiversity activism has critiqued these norms heavily.

Second, mental health. The DSM and ICD operationalise "normal" mental functioning by listing pathologies. The line between sadness and clinical depression, between shyness and social anxiety disorder, between energy and ADHD — these are productively contested.

Third, sexuality. The category of "the homosexual" was produced as the deviant other to "the heterosexual" norm. Queer theory, drawing on Foucault, has worked to denaturalise the norm rather than expand it.

Fourth, the body. Bordo's analysis of slenderness shows how the "normal" body is a moving target — plump in the late nineteenth century, slim in the late twentieth — and how those who fail to meet it are pathologised as obese, lazy, or undisciplined.

Fifth, the population. Census categories produce "normal" demographic patterns from which "abnormal" deviations (low birth rate, ageing population) become political problems.

Sixth, contemporary algorithmic systems. Credit scoring produces "normal" creditworthiness; algorithmic hiring produces "normal" employability; algorithmic content moderation produces "normal" speech.

Why "the normal" is a useful sociological category.

It captures something other categories miss. "Class", "race", "gender" name social positions; "the normal" names a technology of comparison that operates across all of them. The same disciplinary logic that produces the normal child produces the normal worker, the normal citizen, the normal credit risk. Sociologists who study how categories are made — Ian Hacking's "looping effects of human kinds", Bowker and Star's *Sorting Things Out* (1999), Hacking's *The Taming of Chance* (1990) — find Foucault's analysis indispensable.

It captures the role of expertise. Doctors, teachers, psychologists, statisticians, actuaries — these are the "technicians of behaviour" (as some readings put it) who produce, maintain, and apply norms. The professionalisation of normality is a central feature of modernity.

It captures the self-relation. We do not just have norms imposed on us; we measure ourselves against them, often voluntarily. The Fitbit user comparing their step count to the 10,000-step norm is the contemporary incarnation of Bordo's self-monitoring body.

It captures the historical contingency. What counts as normal has changed dramatically and will change again. This historicity is essential for sociological analysis.

Limits and risks.

The category can flatten differences. The "normal/abnormal" binary can obscure other politically significant distinctions. A Marxist might worry that everyone, even the rich, is "subject to norms" — but the rich are normed differently and have more resources to negotiate norms.

The category can be over-applied. If everything is normalisation, the term loses purchase. Some categories are descriptive ("women earn 90% of men's wages in the UK"), some are normative ("women should earn equal pay"), and some are normalising (the assumption that the "default worker" is a man without caring responsibilities). Not all are the same.

The category can be politically ambiguous. Disability activism, queer activism, fat liberation, neurodiversity all use Foucault to denaturalise norms. But the same Foucauldian framework can be wielded to deny that anyone needs medical care — to refuse the diagnosis of depression, autism, ADHD as "just normalisation". The critical use of "the normal" needs careful political judgement.

My verdict: "the normal" is a useful sociological category, perhaps even an indispensable one, but only when used critically. It names a real and politically consequential technology of modern societies. It is not, however, a substitute for analyses of class, race, gender, or labour. It is a tool that sharpens our perception of how disciplinary and biopolitical power works through the apparently neutral apparatus of comparison and measurement.

For exam purposes, the right move is to use "the normal" to denaturalise contemporary categories — credit risk, child development, body weight, mental health — while remaining alert to how the critique itself can be misused.

### Mock Q9 (comparison): Compare Foucault's "docile bodies" with Bourdieu's "habitus" as accounts of how social order is reproduced.

Foucault's docile bodies and Bourdieu's habitus are the two great mid-twentieth-century French accounts of how social order is reproduced through embodied practice. Both reject voluntarist individualism (the idea that people freely choose what they do) and crude determinism (the idea that they are simply forced). Both insist that bodies are central to social reproduction. Both have shaped contemporary sociology decisively. But they differ in important ways, and the differences matter.

I will set out each, identify points of overlap and divergence, and assess which (if either) is more useful.

Foucault's docile bodies. The concept is introduced in *Discipline and Punish* (1975). Disciplinary power produces "docile bodies" — bodies that are useful, obedient, and self-regulating. The production happens through specific techniques: the timetable, the partition of space, the examination, hierarchical observation, normalising judgement. The school child, the soldier, the patient, the worker, the prisoner are all docile bodies produced by their respective institutions. As the lecture summarises: "Such docile bodies fit well into the modern capitalist economy and work well in factories and offices. They question little and behave well." Crucially, docile bodies are produced rather than presupposed; the docility is the outcome of training, not the nature of the trainee.

Bourdieu's habitus. The concept appears across Bourdieu's work but is developed especially in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1972) and *The Logic of Practice* (1980). Habitus is the durable, embodied dispositions that an individual acquires through socialisation. It is "the system of dispositions that generates practices and perceptions" — the bodily knowledge that lets us play tennis, hold a fork correctly, speak with a particular accent, recognise classical music. Habitus is acquired in childhood, especially in the family, and is reinforced by education, leisure, and professional life. Different classes have different habituses, which is why class reproduces itself: the bourgeois child has bourgeois dispositions and thereby fits bourgeois institutions; the working-class child has working-class dispositions and is filtered out.

Overlaps.

Both authors insist on the body as a site of social reproduction. Both reject Cartesian dualism. Both develop genealogies (Bourdieu of taste in *Distinction*, Foucault of punishment in *Discipline and Punish*). Both treat the production of subjectivity as central. Both insist that what looks like free choice is heavily structured.

Divergences.

First, **the mechanism**. Foucault's docile bodies are produced through explicit institutional techniques — bells, timetables, examinations. Bourdieu's habitus is produced more diffusely — through family meal manners, accent, leisure patterns, exposure to art. Foucault sees institutional engineering; Bourdieu sees socialisation. Of course there is overlap (the school is in both stories), but the emphasis differs.

Second, **class**. Bourdieu's habitus is **structured by class** — different classes produce different habituses, and habituses then reproduce class. Foucault's docile bodies are largely **classless** — the timetable disciplines the bourgeois pupil and the working-class pupil alike (though it may produce them as different kinds of subjects). This is a major divergence and one of the standard Marxist criticisms of Foucault.

Third, **agency**. Bourdieu's habitus retains an account of practical sense — agents improvise within their dispositions, like jazz musicians within a tradition. Foucault's docile body has less obvious room for improvisation; the body is "useful and obedient" but resistance enters from elsewhere (the omnipresent counter-pole of power). This is contested — Foucault's later work on technologies of the self addresses agency more directly — but the early docile-bodies framework leaves agency thin.

Fourth, **power**. Bourdieu speaks of symbolic violence, capital (economic, cultural, social, symbolic), and field. Foucault speaks of power as a multiplicity of force relations. The vocabularies do not map cleanly. Bourdieu's account is more structural; Foucault's is more dispersed.

Fifth, **change**. Bourdieu's habitus is slow to change — it has its own "hysteresis" — and the field continues to reproduce class hierarchies. Foucault's power is in constant transformation through resistance and tactical adjustment. Bourdieu is gloomier about reproduction; Foucault is more alert to historical mutation.

Examples.

For school reproduction. The British grammar school produces docile pupils (Foucault) who have particular habitus (Bourdieu). The working-class child who reaches Oxford finds the institution's habitus alien even as her docile body responds to its timetable. Both frameworks illuminate her experience.

For body discipline. Bordo's slim body is a docile body (Foucault) produced by gendered habitus (Bourdieu). The City of London banker's "well-toned" body is the product of both panoptic gyms (Foucault) and bourgeois embodied dispositions (Bourdieu).

For class taste. Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979) is the better book on why the bourgeoisie like classical music and the working class like pop. Foucault has less to say here.

For surveillance. Foucault's docile-bodies framework is the better tool for analysing Amazon warehouses, CCTV, and the Fitbit. Bourdieu has less to say.

Which is more useful?

For contemporary sociology, I think the most fruitful position is that they are **complementary**. Bourdieu is better on class and on the longue durée of socialisation; Foucault is better on institutions, surveillance, and the production of new kinds of subjects. The best contemporary work uses both — Loïc Wacquant's prison ethnography combines Bourdieu and Foucault; Diane Reay's work on schooling does the same; Beverley Skeggs's *Formations of Class and Gender* (1997) draws on both.

If forced to pick one, my answer depends on the question. For "why does inequality persist?", Bourdieu. For "how does the school work?", both. For "what does the Fitbit do to us?", Foucault. For "why do middle-class children outperform working-class ones at the same school?", Bourdieu. For "how does Amazon manage its warehouse workers?", Foucault.

The right answer is probably to know both and use them where they work, rather than to pledge allegiance to one theorist.

### Mock Q10 (discuss-with-examples): Discuss the role of the body in Foucault's account of power. Use contemporary examples.

The body is the privileged site of power in Foucault's account. This is a central distinguishing feature of his work — Marx focused on labour, Weber on bureaucracy, Durkheim on collective representations, but Foucault focused on bodies: how they are observed, examined, trained, disciplined, normalised, sexualised, medicalised, and managed in populations.

I will outline Foucault's account of the body in three steps — the body as object of disciplinary power, the body as object of biopower, and the body as site of resistance — and illustrate each with contemporary examples.

The body as object of disciplinary power.

In *Discipline and Punish* (1975) Foucault traces the shift from sovereign power's brutal exhibition on the body (the Damiens execution) to disciplinary power's training of the body (the prison, the school, the barracks, the hospital). Disciplinary power partitions space (the cell, the classroom, the bed); times activity (the bell, the timetable, the shift); exercises bodies (drill, gymnastics, posture); and observes bodies continuously. The aim is the docile body — useful, obedient, productive. The lecture summarises: "Schools do this, doctors do this, religion does this, the workplace does this. They all try to create us as 'docile bodies' — ones which are passive and which regulate themselves according to the normalizing judgements of society."

Susan Bordo extends this gendered. Her "Reading the Slender Body" (1992) shows that contemporary norms of slenderness, tone, control, and self-management produce particular bodies — especially women's bodies. "Self-management in consumer culture…becomes more elusive as it becomes more pressing…the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1992, pp. 202–3). The Victorian woman's pale skin and the contemporary woman's flat stomach are both disciplinary products, even if the norms have changed.

Contemporary examples. The Amazon warehouse picker's body is disciplinarily produced: rate-tracked, scanner-monitored, restroom-rationed. The Foxconn worker's body is the canonical case of contemporary industrial discipline. The Uber driver's body is shaped by app metrics, surge pricing, and driver ratings. The school pupil's body is shaped by bells, uniforms, exam halls, PE assessments. The patient's body is shaped by ward rounds, charts, scans, drugs. The Fitbit-wearer's body is voluntarily docile, self-monitoring through 10,000-step norms, sleep tracking, and heart-rate zones. The trans person's body navigating clinical gatekeeping at gender identity clinics is disciplinarily produced — the diagnosis, the assessment, the documentation that produces the "trans subject" capable of accessing hormones and surgery.

The body as object of biopower.

If discipline targets the individual body, biopower targets the species body — the population as a biological collective. Foucault introduces this in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976). Biopower manages birth rates, mortality, public health, hygiene, demography. The body becomes a unit in a statistical aggregate.

Contemporary examples. The COVID-19 pandemic was governed biopolitically: the R number, vaccination by demographic priority, contact tracing, modelling of hospital capacity. Bodies were aggregated into populations and managed accordingly. Statins prescribed at population level treat cardiovascular risk as a demographic problem. The NHS's preventive screening programmes (cervical, breast, bowel) target risk groups. Foetal ultrasound (Kember, *Virtual Anxiety*, 1998) makes the maternal body biopolitically transparent while paradoxically "eliminat[ing] the mother's body from view." The Cambridge Analytica scandal showed bodies aggregated into psychographic clusters for political targeting. Predictive policing aggregates bodies into risk zones. The HIV/AIDS public health response of the 1980s targeted "risk groups" — gay men, drug users, sex workers — biopolitically (with the racialised abandonment of African populations as a necropolitical underside).

The body as site of resistance.

Foucault insists that "where there is power, there is resistance" (*History of Sexuality Vol. 1*, p. 95). Resistance often happens through and on the body.

Examples. Hunger strikes (Bobby Sands, Guantanamo detainees) refuse the prison's nutritional discipline. Self-harm and anorexia (controversially discussed) can be read as resistant practices, though they are also captures of disciplinary norms. The body positivity and fat liberation movements explicitly refuse Bordo's slender-body norm. Queer body practices — drag, leather, BDSM — refuse heteronormative body discipline. The gym subculture (counter-intuitively) can resist body norms by celebrating bodies that exceed them. ACT UP's "die-in" protests during the AIDS crisis used the body as political weapon. Black Lives Matter's "I can't breathe" centred the racialised body as the site of state violence. Disability activism, particularly the social model of disability and the neurodiversity movement, refuses the medicalisation of non-normative bodies.

Judith Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997) and *Gender Trouble* (1990) deepen this analysis. The performative production of gendered bodies through repetition opens space for disruptive repetition — drag, gender non-conformity, queer practice. As Butler puts it: power "form[s] the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence" (Butler, 1997, pp. 1–2). The same power that produces docile bodies also produces resistant ones.

Synthesis.

Foucault's account of the body has three crucial features. First, the body is **produced**, not given. There is no natural body apart from the practices that train it. Second, the body is **invested with power and knowledge**: every examination, scan, measurement, classification of the body is simultaneously knowledge production and power exercise. Third, the body is the **site where power and resistance meet**. The struggle is not over the body, abstractly, but on the body, through specific practices.

Why this matters for contemporary sociology. Bodies are everywhere in twenty-first-century social life — surveilled at airports, optimised on Fitbits, sorted by credit scores, gendered by clinics, exploited at Amazon, mourned by Black Lives Matter, claimed by trans activism, contested by body positivity, scanned for foetuses, vaccinated by states, classified by algorithms. To understand any of this without Foucault is impossible. To understand all of it with only Foucault is also impossible, because the political economy and the colonial history that organise bodies need Marxist and postcolonial supplementation.

But the body as object and site of power is one of Foucault's most enduring contributions. We can no longer pretend, after him, that there is a free natural body waiting under the disciplines, or that bodies are neutral instruments. We are, in his powerful phrasing, "subjects" in both senses — subjected to power and constituted as subjects by it. The work of contemporary politics is to do something with that knowledge.

### Mock Q11 (single-author): "Foucault provides a better account of contemporary power than any of his classical predecessors." Discuss.

The claim that Foucault provides a better account of contemporary power than any of his classical predecessors — Marx, Weber, Durkheim — is bold. I will argue it is half-true: Foucault provides better tools for analysing some dimensions of contemporary power (disciplinary techniques, biopolitical governance, power/knowledge, the production of subjects), but the classical predecessors retain decisive advantages for other dimensions (capital accumulation, bureaucratic rationality, collective effervescence). The right verdict is not Foucault's superiority but his necessity as a supplement.

The contenders.

Marx offers an account of power rooted in ownership of the means of production. The bourgeoisie own; the proletariat are exploited; ideology mystifies the relation; the state serves the ruling class. Power is structural, economic, and concentrated. Weber offers an account of power as the probability that one actor in a social relationship can carry out their will against resistance. He distinguishes traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal authority. Power is plural in source, often bureaucratic in form, and rationalising in tendency. Durkheim offers an account of power as the moral force of society over the individual — the collective conscience that constrains, the institutions that solidarise, the rituals that bind. Power is normative, integrative, and exterior to the individual.

Foucault, as we have seen, offers an account of power as a multiplicity of force relations, capillary in distribution, productive rather than merely repressive, inseparable from knowledge, and operating through everyday practices.

Where Foucault wins.

First, on the analysis of disciplinary techniques. Marx's "ideology" cannot describe the school bell, the Amazon warehouse scanner, the Fitbit, the Foxconn dormitory rules. Weber's "rational-legal authority" can describe the bureaucracy that runs Amazon but not the specific embodied disciplines it imposes. Durkheim's "collective conscience" misses the conflictual, individualising character of disciplinary power. Foucault alone offers the toolkit — hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, examination, the panopticon, docile bodies — to describe what these systems actually do.

Second, on biopolitics. Marx, Weber, and Durkheim wrote before the consolidation of mass public health, mass demography, mass vaccination, and population-level risk management. Foucault's biopower captures these in a way the classics cannot. The COVID-19 pandemic response, the NHS data analytics, the statin programme, the contact-tracing app — Foucault's vocabulary fits.

Third, on the production of subjects. Marx, Weber, and Durkheim tend to take the subject as given (the worker, the bureaucrat, the moral individual). Foucault asks how the subject is produced — by which institutions, through which techniques, against which norms. The medicalisation of mental health, the proliferation of sexual identity categories, the production of the modern criminal — these are Foucauldian phenomena. The classics struggle here.

Fourth, on power/knowledge. Marx had ideology; Weber had rationalisation; Durkheim had collective representations. Foucault's power/knowledge is more specific and more useful. The DSM is power/knowledge; the credit score is power/knowledge; the criminological risk assessment is power/knowledge. None of the classical concepts captures these as crisply.

Fifth, on resistance internal to power. The classics tended to treat power as external to subjects, with resistance arising from outside (the proletariat, charismatic prophets, social movements). Foucault's "where there is power, there is resistance" captures the contemporary fact that resistance and power are deeply entangled — body positivity becomes a marketing strategy, queer becomes a brand, mindfulness becomes corporate wellness. The classics did not see this coming.

Where the classics still win.

First, capital. Foucault is famously evasive about capitalism. Amazon exists because Bezos accumulated capital. Foxconn exists because Apple offshored production for higher profits. The British care crisis exists because successive governments cut public spending to protect capital. These are Marxist questions. Foucault can describe the techniques Amazon uses but cannot adequately explain why Amazon exists.

Second, bureaucracy. Weber's analysis of bureaucratic rationality remains essential for understanding the contemporary state, the contemporary corporation, the contemporary university. The Foucauldian focus on micro-techniques can lose sight of the institutional architecture that organises them.

Third, solidarity and moral life. Durkheim's analysis of collective effervescence captures something Foucault misses — the affective, ritual, collective binding of social life. The COVID clap for the NHS, the Queen's funeral, the World Cup, the protest march — these are Durkheimian moments, not Foucauldian ones (though Foucault can analyse the disciplinary dimensions).

Fourth, structural inequality. Marx, on race-class-extended Marxists like W.E.B. Du Bois, capture durable structural inequalities better than Foucault. Foucault gives us the technique; Marx gives us the structure.

Fifth, religion. All three classics had more to say about religion than Foucault did. Religion remains powerful contemporarily; Foucault's silence is a gap.

Examples where each tradition is essential.

For analysing the gig economy: Marx for the wage relation, Weber for the rationalised app, Foucault for the rating system. All three.

For analysing schools: Bourdieu for class reproduction (extending Durkheim and Marx), Foucault for examination and discipline, Weber for the bureaucratic exam apparatus.

For analysing the prison: Foucault for the disciplinary architecture, Marx for the political economy of mass incarceration, Durkheim for the ritual function of punishment.

For analysing pandemics: Foucault for biopower and surveillance, Weber for the bureaucratic response, Marx for the vaccine apartheid.

For analysing social media: Foucault for self-monitoring and normalisation, Marx for surveillance capitalism, Weber for platform rationality, Durkheim for collective effervescence (viral moments).

My verdict.

The claim that Foucault provides a better account than any classical predecessor is, strictly speaking, false. Foucault provides a better account of some dimensions of contemporary power — those involving capillary techniques, productive subjectivity, knowledge-power, biopolitics — and a worse account of others — those involving capital accumulation, bureaucratic rationality, collective ritual, and structural inequality.

The right verdict is that Foucault is **necessary but not sufficient**. He expanded the toolkit of social theory in ways that cannot be undone. But he did not abolish the toolkit he inherited. The strongest contemporary social theory — Wendy Brown, Achille Mbembe, Shoshana Zuboff, Silvia Federici, Loïc Wacquant — uses all of them. To set Foucault against the classics is to miss the productive composition of his work with theirs.

If the question is "should we add Foucault to the canon?", the answer is unambiguously yes. If the question is "should we replace the canon with Foucault?", the answer is no. The marker, I hope, will appreciate the distinction.

### Mock Q12 (to-what-extent): To what extent does Foucault's account of power require us to abandon the concept of ideology?

The relationship between Foucault and the concept of ideology has been one of the most contested questions in social theory since the 1970s. Foucault's most polemical lines — "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 102), and his critique of Marxists for denouncing class domination without analysing power's mechanics (Foucault, 1980, p. 116) — have been read as a wholesale rejection of ideology. But I want to argue that Foucault requires us to **refine** rather than **abandon** the concept of ideology, and that the refined concept remains useful.

I will set out Foucault's critique of ideology, the case for abandoning the concept, the case for retaining it, and a synthesis.

Foucault's critique.

Foucault objects to the concept of ideology in three ways. First, it presupposes a distinction between truth and falsity — ideology is false consciousness, masking a true reality. Foucault prefers to talk about "regimes of truth" — historically specific configurations of what counts as true — rather than truth vs. ideology. Second, it treats power as primarily epistemic (mystifying minds) rather than as practical (training bodies). For Foucault, the discipline of the school timetable does not deceive pupils about reality; it trains their bodies regardless of what they believe. Third, it locates power's source in a specific group (the ruling class) and a specific function (reproducing class domination). For Foucault, power is dispersed and productive, not concentrated and concealing.

The famous lecture quotation captures the polemic: "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, *Power/Knowledge*, 1980, p. 102). More, because power operates through bodies, practices, and institutions that exceed any cognitive content. Less, because once we see the mechanics of disciplinary and biopolitical power, we no longer need to posit a hidden ideological function.

The case for abandoning ideology.

Several Foucauldians and post-Foucauldians have argued the concept should be retired. Their arguments:

First, "ideology" implies a true reality behind the false appearances, which Foucault's perspectivism rejects. If there are only regimes of truth, then there is no privileged position from which to call one regime "ideological".

Second, "ideology" tends to reduce power to cognition. But the Fitbit, the school exam, the airport scanner, the Foxconn dormitory operate on bodies without primarily working through beliefs.

Third, "ideology" presupposes a subject who could be ideologically deceived. But Foucault and especially Butler argue that the subject is itself produced by power; there is no pre-ideological subject to be deceived.

Fourth, the concept has been politically discredited by association with crude Marxism — the assumption that the working class is dupes who need vanguard parties to enlighten them.

The case for retaining ideology.

Other theorists — including some sympathetic to Foucault — have argued ideology remains essential. Their arguments:

First, **cognitive content matters**. People do hold false beliefs that serve dominant interests: that the gender pay gap is explained by women's choices, that climate change is uncertain, that immigrants drive down wages. These are not just disciplinary techniques; they are cognitively wrong and politically functional. Calling them ideology captures something Foucauldian vocabulary misses.

Second, **structural critique requires it**. Without ideology, we struggle to explain why neoliberalism became hegemonic, why anti-trans politics has spread, why fossil capital remains protected. Wendy Brown's *In the Ruins of Neoliberalism* (2019) and Stuart Hall's earlier work mobilise both Foucault and Gramsci — they need ideology.

Third, **Marxists themselves refined ideology**. The Gramscian concept of hegemony, Althusser's ideological state apparatuses, Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding, and Slavoj Žižek's cynical reason all move ideology beyond the crude "false consciousness" model Foucault attacks.

Fourth, **Foucault's own concept of "regimes of truth" does similar work**. If a regime of truth makes certain statements appear true and others false, then a different regime would make different statements appear true. This is functionally close to ideology — it just renames it.

Synthesis.

I want to argue for a compromise position. The crude version of ideology — false consciousness as direct expression of class interests — is rightly rejected by Foucault and most contemporary critical theorists. The refined version — ideology as discursive and material structures that organise consent to dominant arrangements — is compatible with Foucault and remains useful.

Several contemporary theorists demonstrate the compatibility. Stuart Hall's *Policing the Crisis* (1978) used both Gramsci and Foucault. Wendy Brown combines Foucauldian governmentality with critique of neoliberal ideology. Nancy Fraser's *Cannibal Capitalism* (2022) uses both. Slavoj Žižek's *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989), while Lacanian rather than Foucauldian, develops a sophisticated ideology concept that engages Foucault.

Examples of where ideology remains useful.

The gender pay gap discourse. Disciplinary analysis can describe the techniques (performance reviews, glass ceilings, motherhood penalties). But the **belief** that the gap reflects women's choices rather than structural sexism is ideological — and combating it requires ideological critique alongside structural change.

Climate denial. Foucault can describe the biopolitical management of carbon. But the systematic denial funded by fossil capital is ideological — Naomi Oreskes's *Merchants of Doubt* (2010) is an ideological analysis.

Anti-immigrant politics. Foucault can describe border surveillance. But the belief that immigrants drive down wages or commit more crime is ideological — and contradicts evidence.

Neoliberal common sense. Foucault's governmentality framework describes how neoliberalism operates. But "there is no alternative" is ideology — and contesting it requires ideological critique.

What Foucault adds. Even where ideology remains useful, Foucault adds essential supplements. He shows that beliefs alone do not exhaust power — bodies, practices, and institutions matter independently. He shows that ideology operates through power/knowledge, not just through mystification. He shows that subjects are produced by power, so the critique of ideology cannot rest on a pre-ideological standpoint.

My verdict: Foucault's account requires us to refine, not abandon, the concept of ideology. The refined concept — ideology as discursive and material structures organising consent — remains useful for analysing what Foucauldian vocabulary alone cannot capture, especially the cognitive content of dominant beliefs and the structural function of those beliefs in reproducing inequality. The best contemporary critical theory uses both. To abandon ideology entirely would be to lose tools we need; to retain it crudely would be to ignore Foucault's important critique. The right move is the synthesis.
