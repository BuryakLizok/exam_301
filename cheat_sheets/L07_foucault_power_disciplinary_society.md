# L7 - Foucault on Power and Disciplinary Society

## 1. Lecture Overview

Lecture 7 marks a decisive pivot in SC301: from classical models of power as ideology, repression, or class domination toward Foucault's far more subtle and pervasive account of power as something that operates through everyday practices, knowledges, institutions, and the very formation of the subject. The lecturer opens with Sarah Kember's example of obstetric ultrasound, the way scanning "eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998), to dramatise the central problem: how can we understand what is happening here? An ideology critique (the doctor as agent of patriarchy) captures part of the story but, as Foucault puts it, "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

The lecture's spine is the contrast between **sovereign power** and **disciplinary power**. Sovereign power is the spectacular, corporal, top-down power of the pre-modern monarch, illustrated by the grotesque public execution of Damiens the regicide that opens *Discipline and Punish*. Modern power, since the nineteenth century, looks different: it is dispersed, capillary, productive rather than purely repressive, and works through surveillance, examination, and normalisation rather than through the visible application of force. Bentham's panopticon, with its central watchtower and back-lit cells, becomes the architectural figure of this new regime: a "machine for dissociating the see/being seen dyad" that produces, in the inmate, "a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader).

Three aspects of discipline are unpacked: **hierarchical observation** (we are watched from positions we cannot see); **normalising judgement** (we are measured against an invented standard of "the normal"); and **examination** (the combination of observation and judgement in documented, testable form, such as registers, progress reports, key stages, transcripts). The aim and product of this triad is the **docile body**: passive, self-regulating, useful to capital and to the state.

The body and subjectivity sit at the centre of the second half of the lecture. Susan Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" extends Foucault into feminist territory: norms of slenderness, tone, control, and self-mastery shape bodies, particularly women's, and produce subjects "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). The lecture documents the shift from late-nineteenth-century norms (plump men signalling success, pale women signalling exemption from field work) to contemporary norms (the slender, well-toned body, advertised in titles like *Men's Health* and read off in the City-of-London gym). Judith Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* pushes further: power is "what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Power forms the subject; we cannot stand outside it.

Behind all of this stands Foucault's polemic against traditional sociological theories of power: liberal/Enlightenment (power as a thing possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom) and Marxist (power as class domination operating through ideology, concentrated in the owners of the means of production). Foucault's complaint is consistent: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). His project is to look at **how** power operates, at its capillary, micro-physical level, rather than asking only **who** holds it. The closing slide of the lecture returns to Kember's ultrasound image, now reread through the whole conceptual apparatus, disciplinary power, productive power, normalising judgement, and power/knowledge.

## 2. Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Sovereign power**: pre-modern, spectacular, top-down power that operates through visible force, corporal punishment, and the abuse of the body. Concentrated in a person (the king) and exercised through edict and execution.
- **Disciplinary power**: modern, dispersed, capillary power that works through training, observation, examination, and normalisation. Subtle, productive, everywhere.
- **The panopticon**: Jeremy Bentham's architectural design (central tower, back-lit ring of cells) that allows constant, asymmetric visibility. Foucault treats it as the diagram of disciplinary modernity.
- **Surveillance**: continuous observation that need not be actual to be effective; the possibility of being watched is enough to produce self-regulation.
- **Hierarchical observation**: monitoring conducted from positions of invisibility, with observers themselves unobserved.
- **Normalising judgement**: measurement of individuals against an invented standard of "the normal"; the nineteenth-century invention of "the normal" is "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (lecture slide).
- **Examination**: the ritual combination of observation and judgement that produces documented, comparable knowledge of individuals (registers, progress reports, transcripts, medical records, school photos, key-stage testing).
- **Docile bodies**: passive, self-regulating, useful bodies produced by disciplinary techniques; bodies that fit the modern capitalist economy.
- **Power/knowledge**: power and knowledge are inseparable; the production of knowledge (psychiatry, criminology, demography) is itself an exercise of power, and power produces knowledge.
- **Productive power**: power is not only repressive; it produces subjects, bodies, types of people, institutions, and forms of conduct.
- **Biopower**: power exercised at the level of populations (birth rates, mortality, public health, demographics), managing life rather than threatening death.
- **Governmentality**: the rationalities and techniques through which populations are governed (cross-applied from related lectures).
- **The subject**: the modern individual as both subjected to power and constituted as a subject by power.
- **Regimes of truth**: historically specific configurations of what counts as true and how truth is produced.
- **The disciplinary society**: contemporary society understood as saturated by the techniques of discipline that emerged in the nineteenth century, including schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces.
- **Force relations**: Foucault's basic micro-unit of power; relations that push, urge, or compel, immanent in social interactions (Lynch, 2011).
- **Capillary power**: power that operates at the smallest scale, in the everyday details of bodies and conduct.

Power forms the subject.

## 6. Common Essay Pitfalls

- Treating Foucault as if he simply said "power is bad" or "everything is power"; both miss the productive, omnipresent-but-not-totalising character of his analysis.
- Conflating disciplinary power with sovereign power; failing to mark the historical shift Foucault traces from spectacular punishment to surveillance.
- Reducing the panopticon to "being watched" without explaining the **asymmetry** (visible/unverifiable) and the **internalisation** that produces self-regulation.
- Forgetting that power is **productive**: it creates subjects, knowledges, and types of people, not just constraints.
- Treating biopower and disciplinary power as synonyms. Disciplinary power targets the individual body; biopower targets the population. They overlap but are not identical.
- Ignoring resistance. Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance", so making him sound more deterministic than he is.
- Failing to engage the lecture's explicit foils: liberal/Enlightenment views (Slide 34) treat power as a thing to be seized or shared; Marxist views (Slide 35) reduce power to ownership of the means of production and ideology. Foucault's reply, that "the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116; Slide 36), names the methodological gap.
- Using only one example (the prison) when the lecture and reading insist that disciplinary power runs through schools, hospitals, workplaces, families, and bodies.
- Quoting "panopticon" without naming Bentham, or quoting Bordo/Butler without anchoring them to Foucault.
- Reading "docile bodies" as universal slavery rather than as a tendency produced by specific institutions and techniques.
- Treating Foucault as a "postmodernist" who denies truth; he writes about regimes of truth, not the abolition of truth.
- Forgetting the lecture's opening: ideology is not wrong, but it is "much more and much less" than what Foucault wants to analyse.

## 8. Essay answers

### Past-Year Essays

### 2025 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing do you find his arguments?

Foucault's challenge to traditional sociology is set out polemically in *Discipline and Punish* (1977) and *Power/Knowledge* (1980). The lecture isolates two principal foils. Liberal/Enlightenment accounts treat power as a thing possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom (Slide 34). Marxist accounts treat power as flowing from ownership of the means of production, concentrated in the bourgeoisie, and exercised through ideology (Slide 35). Both, as Lynch summarises, "mischaracterize an accidental feature of power" as "an essential characteristic of power in general" (Lynch, 2011: 19).

Foucault's response to Marxism is biting: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). What is missing is an account of **how** power operates.

Foucault's counter-proposal has six moves. First, **power is dispersed, not concentrated**: it circulates through micro-relations and "comes from below" rather than from a sovereign apex (Lynch, 2011: 25-26). Second, **power is productive**: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of subjects" (Slide 13). Third, **power and knowledge are inseparable**: the Kember scan constitutes knowledge of the foetus and redistributes visibility around the maternal body (Kember, 1998). Fourth, **power operates through everyday practices**: registers, progress reports, key-stage tests (Slide 10), not principally through state edict. Today we could add fitness trackers, social media feeds, and HR performance reviews to the list. Fifth, **modern power forms the subject** rather than merely constraining a pre-existing one. It "makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212), and Butler extends this: power is "what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Sixth, **resistance is internal to power**: "Where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95).

The pay-off is methodological. We should not ask "who has power?" but "how is power exercised?" We look at the panopticon, the school register, the medical scan, the gym, the algorithmic feed, not only at parliament and capital.

How convincing? Largely, with reservations.

Foucault's account illuminates phenomena traditional sociology struggles to explain. Calling the obstetric ultrasound doctor "ideological" or "patriarchal" is not exactly wrong, but it misses how the technology and protocols organise women's bodies in ways that exceed any individual's intent. "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). The same goes for the school: the Marxist asks whose interests mass schooling serves, but Foucault asks how registers, progress reports, photos, and key-stage testing produce the pupil as a particular subject (Slide 10).

The productive thesis is especially convincing. The modern prisoner is not a pre-existing criminal the prison contains; the prison produces him through its panoptic architecture. Bordo's diachronic analysis makes this vivid for gender: the late-nineteenth-century plump-and-prosperous male body and the contemporary slender-and-toned body are two different productions of the gendered subject by different disciplinary configurations (Bordo, 1993; Slides 25, 27). Contemporary facial recognition systems in airports and CCTV networks similarly produce new categories of "risk profile" rather than merely catching pre-existing threats.

The reservations. First, the capillary model risks underplaying spectacularly sovereign power. The lecture itself notes that disciplinary power displaces but does not abolish sovereign power; the public execution of Damiens (Foucault, 1977) is exhibit A. Second, if power constitutes the subject, grounding emancipatory agency becomes harder. Butler's response is that agency is immanent rather than oppositional (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Third, Marxist critics argue Foucault sees the techniques but loses the structure: the disciplinary apparatus of the modern factory makes little sense without the capitalist logic that organises it, including the relation between docile bodies and "the modern capitalist economy" the lecture's Slide 23 invokes ("The bourgeoisie has won!"). Gramsci's account of hegemony as rule "by consent" (Gramsci, 1971: 12) sits productively between the two: where Foucault asks how discipline produces self-regulating subjects, Gramsci asks how a class secures consent through cultural leadership. Hall's reading (Hall, 1980: 36) makes the bridge explicit, treating ideology and discipline as complementary registers rather than rivals. The cost-of-living crisis and gig-economy work reinforce the point.

On balance, I find Foucault's challenge convincing as a supplement, not as a replacement. Lynch's reading is helpful: Foucault supplies the "analytics" the older traditions lacked (Lynch, 2011: 17), not an abolition of the question of who benefits. To ask "how" without abandoning "who benefits" is the best legacy of his intervention.

### 2024 (Q7): How does Foucault conceptualise disciplinary power? Why is this conceptualisation important?

Foucault's notion of disciplinary power names the form of power that emerged in Western societies from the late eighteenth century onwards and came to displace the older logic of sovereign power. Sovereign power was concentrated in the monarch, exercised through visible force, and dramatised in spectacular punishment; the slow public execution of Damiens for regicide is its emblem (Foucault, 1977; Slide 8). Disciplinary power is "subtle", "everywhere", and works not by stopping people but by training them into doing certain things (Slide 10). As Foucault puts it, this is "a type of power, a modality for its exercise, comprising a whole set of instruments, techniques, procedures, levels of application, targets; it is a 'physics' or an 'anatomy' of power" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader).

Four features make up the conceptualisation. First, disciplinary power is **capillary**: it operates in the everyday details of timetables, classrooms, examinations, and records. The school's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10) are paradigmatic; Lynch's academic transcript ("this will go on your permanent record") captures the same logic (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). Second, it is **productive**: it produces new kinds of subjects (the modern prisoner, the schoolchild, the patient) through new institutions (Slide 13). Third, it works through three techniques: **hierarchical observation, normalising judgement, and the examination**. We are observed from positions of asymmetric invisibility (Slide 19), measured against "the normal", which the lecturer calls "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (Slide 22), and documented in rituals fusing the two. Fourth, it produces **docile bodies**: useful, obedient, and crucially self-regulating (Slide 23). "We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!" (Slide 17).

The architectural diagram is Bentham's panopticon: "a supervisor in a central tower" with each cell containing "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). It induces "in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). Because the inmate cannot verify when they are watched, they behave as if always observed. The diagram travels: schools, hospitals, factories, and the police all instantiate "an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). Today CCTV in public spaces and university league tables update the diagram without abandoning its logic.

Why does this matter? Four reasons. First, it reorients sociology from "who holds power?" to "how does power operate?" (Slide 6). We can examine the school register, the ultrasound, or the prison cell without first reducing them to ruling-class will. Second, it explains why modern liberal societies, while claiming to expand freedom, generate ever more intensive conduct-shaping. Disciplinary power is the underside of liberal freedom. Third, it identifies the power/knowledge link. The human sciences (psychiatry, pedagogy, clinical medicine) are not innocent but apparatuses that produce the subjects they claim to describe. The ultrasound makes the foetus visible and the mother disappear (Kember, 1998). Fourth, it opens politics at the micro-level: the examination, the medical scan, the body in the gym become sites of contestation.

The conceptualisation has limitations. Foucault dissolves class and gender into a generalised analytic of conduct; Bordo's account of women "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) supplies the gendered specificity Foucault did not fully develop. Marxist critics argue disciplinary power cannot replace political economy: Slide 23's concluding flourish, that docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy" and "the bourgeoisie has won!", concedes that disciplinary analysis needs pairing with critique of capital. Foucault's account works best as a supplement, not a substitute, for structural analyses. The supplement is essential: a school's regime of registers and key stages is unintelligible if we have only "the ruling class" as our analytic.

Disciplinary power names not a thing but a mode. Its importance lies in letting us see, in the most mundane practices (the school register, the medical scan, the bathroom-mirror appraisal), the political form of our present. Halewood's account of how modern thought freezes social life into bifurcations (Halewood, 2014: 36) is useful here: Foucault's genealogy dismantles the very pairs (sovereign/disciplinary, normal/abnormal, mind/body, observer/observed) on which the older sociology of power relied, and shows them as historical products rather than natural kinds. As Foucault writes, this is "a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212), and that double sense, subjected to and constituted as, is the productive heart of his concept.

### 2024 (Q8): "Contemporary societies are biopolitical." Critically analyse this statement.

The claim that contemporary societies are biopolitical extends Foucault's analysis of power from the disciplined individual body to the managed population. Lynch summarises: where disciplinary power targets individuals (the transcript, the case file), biopower "does not directly address particular individuals, but rather groups of people and populations as a whole" (Lynch, 2011: 16). His illustration is epidemiological: observing which groups contract lung cancer leads to identifying causes such as smoking or asbestos exposure (Lynch, 2011: 16). The rationality is "to save lives" by intervening on the population. Disciplinary and biopolitical power are the "two poles" of modern power over life: body and species (Lynch, 2011: 26).

I argue the statement is largely defensible but with three qualifications: biopolitics layers onto rather than replaces disciplinary and sovereign power; it operates through everyday moral injunctions as much as state apparatus; and its operations remain contested.

The affirmative case. Slide 24 is built around "It is our duty to keep ourselves healthy" and unpacks the biopolitical injunctions: "it is the duty of government to make us reduce our salt level"; "we feel bad if we do not eat 5 portions of fruit/veg per day"; "the government must stop obese children." None of these target a particular individual; all act on the population through dietary norms, public-health campaigns, and the moralisation of self-management. Pandemic contact-tracing apps and population-level vaccination drives extend the same logic. Slide 37 supplies the genealogy: "the police force was not only invented for maintaining law and order... but for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215).

Biopower works through productive moral norms rather than coercion. Bordo's analysis of body norms makes this concrete at the population level. The slender, well-toned body of contemporary culture is not coerced; it is invited and incited by media, advertising, and the self-management that Slide 28 traces through gym membership and the City-of-London expectation that one will "work-out" to "show you 'mean business'." Fitness trackers and calorie-counting apps automate Bordo's diagnosis: "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Biopower sits alongside the productive thesis of disciplinary power. Slide 13: nineteenth-century schools, prisons, and psychiatric hospitals produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)". Schools and hospitals act on bodies one by one but also on populations through statistics, comparisons, and aggregate norms (key stages, health screenings). The Kember scan is a clinical encounter with one mother but also part of a population-level antenatal screening apparatus: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998).

Race is the paradigmatic biopolitical object. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century taxonomies (Linnaeus, 1735; Blumenbach, 1775) sorted human populations into "varieties" and supplied the statistical knowledge biopower then managed through hygiene, sanitation, and reproductive policy. Du Bois' "double consciousness" (Du Bois, 1903: 8) names the embodied effect: the racialised subject is folded into the population as both an object of management and an excluded interiority. Reading L7 alongside L8 shows that biopower is not only about salt and 5-a-day.

Now the qualifications. The statement risks overgeneralisation if read as "contemporary societies are *only* biopolitical." Sovereign power persists wherever visible force on bodies displays political authority, the trace of Damiens (Foucault, 1977). Inside the school, disciplinary techniques (registers, examinations) operate alongside biopolitical ones (5-a-day reaching children through school meals). The statement is best read additively: contemporary societies are biopolitical *as well as* disciplinary and partially sovereign.

Second, resistance. If biopower were total, the contestation of public-health framings, fat-acceptance politics, anti-vaccine movements, and body-positivity discourse would be unintelligible. Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Lynch echoes: power relations "can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Biopolitical injunctions provoke biopolitical contestation.

Third, the conceptual qualification. Lynch warns against using theory to "answer everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13). Biopolitics captures population-level rationalities of health, fertility, and longevity; it does not replace disciplinary analysis or structural analysis of capital.

I find the statement convincing on balance. Contemporary societies are saturated by biopolitical rationalities, from the salt-reduction targets on a soup tin to the 5-a-day banner in a school canteen to the smartphone push-notification reminding you to walk. But "saturated" does not mean "exhausted by". Biopolitics is one rationality among several, unevenly distributed, and constantly contested. Foucault's concept is most useful when it sharpens our perception of these uneven politics of life, not when wielded as a totalising diagnosis.

### 2023 (Q9): How did Foucault challenge traditional sociological approaches to power? How convincing?

Foucault's intervention into the sociology of power was, at root, a question about method. Traditional sociology asked **who** holds power: the state, the ruling class, the king. Foucault asked **how** power operates. Slide 6 paraphrases: "if we want to know what power is, then we need to understand HOW power relations operate in society." The shift sounds modest but reorganises the entire field.

I set out the challenge under three headings (what he rejects, what he proposes, what follows) and then assess it.

What he rejects. The **liberal/Enlightenment** model treats power as a thing, possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom, something to be seized or shared (Slide 34). First-wave feminism shares this structure. The **Marxist** model treats power as flowing from ownership of the means of production, concentrated in a wealthy minority, exercised through ideology (Slide 35). Both mistake "terminal forms" of power for its essence and fail to analyse its **mechanics**. The Marxist version receives Foucault's sharpest rebuke: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116).

What he proposes. Foucault counters with an "analytics of power". Power is a "multiplicity of force relations" (Foucault, 1978: 92, cited in Lynch, 2011: 21). Power is exercised, not possessed. It comes from below, from micro-relations, and aggregates into the larger structures we recognise as states (Lynch, 2011: 25-26). It is intentional but non-subjective: tactics have aims, but no single agent controls overall effects (Lynch, 2011: 26-27). It is omnipresent, "always already there" (Lynch, 2011: 19), so resistance is internal to it. Disciplinary power and biopower are its modern forms. The panopticon is the architectural diagram (Foucault, 1977: 314-316 in Calhoun reader). Power is "much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

What follows. Sociology should examine the school register, the progress report, the medical scan, the body in the mirror, the questionnaire ("Am I different from the rest?", Slide 21), and today the social-media feed and the algorithmic rating. The human sciences (psychiatry, pedagogy, clinical medicine) are part of the apparatus of power/knowledge. The subject is not the starting point but the product.

How convincing? Largely so, with three positives and two reservations.

First, the empirical reach. The lecture lists schools (registers, progress reports, key stages), prisons (the panopticon), the body (Bordo's slender norm, the City gym), the clinic (Kember's ultrasound), and the police (Slide 37). None is well explained by a top-down model. Lynch's transcript "begins from observation at the bottom of the pyramid" (Lynch, 2011: 15); even what to wear is shaped by capillary power (Lynch, 2011: 23). The reach extends to facial recognition, fitness trackers, and HR appraisals.

Second, the productive thesis is original. Slide 13: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people". The modern prisoner is not a pre-existing criminal the prison contains; the prison produces him. Bordo extends this to gender (Bordo, 1993).

Third, the knowledge/power linkage answers a real problem. The Kember scan is at once a knowledge artefact (an image of the foetus) and a power artefact (the maternal body's displacement). Calling this "ideology" misses the point: "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). Mulvey's account of the "male gaze" (Mulvey, 1975: 11) sharpens the same point in a feminist register: visibility is never neutral but always organised by relations of power, and Berger's claim that "men look at women; women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47) anticipates the panoptic structure Foucault later names.

Reservations. First, the diffuse model underplays concentrated, spectacular power. The Damiens execution (Foucault, 1977; Slide 8) is exhibit A for a form of power that has not entirely vanished. Foucauldian analysis must work harder to capture concentrated force than a structural model does. Second, agency. If "power forms the subject" (Butler, 1997: 1-2), what grounds resistance? Foucault answers that resistance is internal to power, and Lynch reinforces this (Lynch, 2011: 28). But the answer remains thinner than a fully developed normative theory.

Overall verdict: Foucault's challenge is convincing as a transformation of what sociology can see, less so as a complete replacement. Slide 23's flourish, that docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy" and "the bourgeoisie has won!", suggests disciplinary analysis benefits from tension with Marxist critique rather than replacing it. Foucault expanded the toolkit; he did not close it.

### 2022 (Q10): Foucault described the power inherent in contemporary society as "disciplinary". What did he mean by this? How useful do you find this term?

When Foucault described contemporary power as disciplinary, he meant a historically new mode emerging in Western societies in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, operating through training rather than force, surveillance rather than spectacle, normalisation rather than prohibition, and producing subjects who regulate themselves.

I set this out under five headings, then assess the term's usefulness.

First, **disciplinary power is contrasted with sovereign power**. The opening of *Discipline and Punish* describes the public execution of Damiens, a spectacle that displayed the king's power over the criminal's body (Foucault, 1977; Slide 8). Within decades came the prison timetable, the school register, the hospital ward round. The shift is not from cruelty to humanity but from one logic of power to another: sovereign power was concentrated, visible, intermittent, and abused the body; disciplinary power is dispersed, subtle, continuous, and trains it (Slide 10).

Second, **disciplinary power is capillary**. It operates not principally through the state but through everyday institutions (schools, hospitals, factories, families, barracks). "Discipline may be identified neither with an institution nor with an apparatus; it is a type of power, a modality for its exercise" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The school's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10) are paradigmatic; Lynch's transcript example ("this will go on your permanent record") captures the same mode (Lynch, 2011: 15-16).

Third, **disciplinary power works through three techniques**: hierarchical observation (Slide 19), normalising judgement against "the normal", "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" (Slide 22), and the examination (the documented combination).

Fourth, **its architectural figure is the panopticon**. Bentham's design places "a supervisor in a central tower" and shuts "in each cell a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader), inducing "a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader). The inmate cannot tell when watched, so internalises the gaze.

Fifth, **its product is the docile body**: useful, obedient, self-regulating. Such bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy and work well in factories and offices. They question little and behave well. And don't resist" (Slide 23). They have been trained, not coerced.

How useful is the term? Very useful, for four reasons, with three limits.

First, it names what traditional sociology missed. Marxist ideology theory and liberal state-power theory cannot describe the school register, the medical scan, the bathroom-mirror appraisal, the Fitbit, the algorithmic feed. All are disciplinary.

Second, it captures contemporary intensifications. Slide 28 reports that in the City of London "it is important to 'work-out'... to show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life", and Bordo's norms of "trim", "well-toned", "well-defined" bodies now reach "into the very insides of our bodies" (Bordo, 1993; Slide 27). The cultural fixation on Andrew Tate and manosphere masculinity and the corresponding female-body discourses are paradigmatic disciplinary phenomena.

Third, it illuminates how we participate in our own discipline. The dieter, gym-goer, and questionnaire-taker (Slide 21) is not coerced; she monitors herself. Bordo's "chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) is the condition of contemporary embodiment, intensified by smartphone tracking and social-media metrics.

Fourth, it provides a vocabulary for resistance. If discipline runs through everyday techniques, those techniques are sites of contestation. Bordo's diachronic historicisation of body norms (Slide 25: plumpness as male success, muscle as working-class, pale skin for women) is implicitly such a contestation.

Limits. First, the term can homogenise; nineteenth-century prison discipline is not the same as a twenty-first-century gym. Second, disciplinary power does not exhaust contemporary power. Slide 24's biopolitical material (salt, 5-a-day, obesity policy) targets the population, and sovereign power has not vanished. The racialised body is the paradigmatic case where disciplinary, biopolitical and sovereign registers converge: Du Bois' "double consciousness" (Du Bois, 1903: 8) and Gilroy's Black Atlantic (Gilroy, 1993: 30) describe subjects formed by discipline (school, police), biopower (population statistics, public health), and visibly sovereign force (stop-and-search, deportation) at once. Third, the term has been popularised into a slogan covering everything anyone disapproves of.

Overall: the term remains one of the most productive in twentieth-century social theory but is most useful when wielded with historical precision and combined with other analytics. Contemporary society is partly disciplinary, partly biopolitical, partly residually sovereign.

### 2022 (Q11): How useful is Foucault's notion of biopower for understanding contemporary society?

Foucault's biopower, identified by Lynch as one of two "closely related modes" of modern power (Lynch, 2011: 13), names a form acting not on individual bodies but on populations as biological collectives. Lynch's illustration is epidemiological: observing which groups contract lung cancer leads to identifying causes such as smoking or asbestos (Lynch, 2011: 16). The rationality is "to save lives" by acting on aggregate populations; biopower "does not directly address particular individuals, but rather groups of people and populations as a whole" (Lynch, 2011: 16).

I argue the concept is highly useful for contemporary society but with three caveats: it must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power; it operates through productive moral norms as much as state institutions; and it requires attention to the everyday register.

The affirmative case. Public-health injunctions are the clearest example. Slide 24 unpacks "It is our duty to keep ourselves healthy" through biopolitical mechanisms: salt-reduction policy; the 5-a-day campaign and the felt failure of missing it; "the government must stop obese children." None of these target a particular individual; all act on the population. Pandemic contact-tracing apps and population-level vaccination campaigns extend the same logic. Slide 37 gives the genealogy: the modern police were instituted not only for law and order but "for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215).

Reproductive medicine is biopolitical par excellence. Kember: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998). Biopolitical visibility renders some bodies hyper-visible (the foetus) while erasing others (the mother). The ultrasound is the lecture's opening and closing example (Slides 2, 38).

Bordo's body norms operate at the population level. Slide 28: "more demands are made on women than men in terms of producing themselves as 'normal'", the rise of gym membership turns body work into population-wide expectation, and consumer culture (Slide 32) "runs on us drinking coffee, eating cakes, take-away food" while demanding "we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves." Fitness trackers, social-media body imagery, and calorie-counting apps automate the biopolitical regime.

Race is biopower's other paradigmatic domain. Eighteenth-century natural-history taxonomies (Linnaeus, 1735; Blumenbach, 1775) classified human populations into "varieties" and supplied the statistical knowledge through which states later managed reproduction, sanitation, and migration. Gilroy's Black Atlantic (Gilroy, 1993: 30) shows the same population-level apparatus organising who counts as modern. Reading biopower against L8 reveals that the management of life has been, from its origin, a racial as much as a medical project.

The caveats.

First, biopower does not stand alone. The school applies disciplinary techniques to each pupil (registers, progress reports) and biopolitical interventions to the cohort (height-and-weight screening, 5-a-day). Lynch: "a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26). Conflating flattens the analysis.

Second, sovereign power persists. The Damiens torture at the opening of *Discipline and Punish* (Slide 8) describes a power that visibly seizes the body. Wherever the state exercises violence visibly on bodies, sovereign power remains operative.

Third, biopower works through productive moral norms as much as state apparatus. Bordo's Slide 29: "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) names how the population is governed through aspirational images and felt failures, not coercion. Butler extends this (Butler, 1997: 1-2): biopolitical operations are felt as our own projects.

A final qualification: biopower is resisted. Foucault: "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Lynch: power relations "can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Biopolitical norms about food, weight, fertility, and health provoke biopolitical contestation, from body-positivity movements to anti-vaccination politics.

Contemporary applications expand the framework. Pandemic contact-tracing apps, mask mandates, vaccination drives, and obesity policy combine population-level statistical knowledge with moralised personal duty. Fitbit-style step-counting, calorie-counting apps, and sleep-tracking devices export biopolitical surveillance into the home as voluntary self-monitoring. Where the public-health director once acted on the population aggregate, the individual now self-administers the same logic.

Verdict: biopower is one of the most useful concepts in contemporary social theory. It names what we would otherwise miss: the politics of life itself, the management of populations through diet, hygiene, antenatal care, and moralised self-management. Its usefulness depends on careful articulation with disciplinary and sovereign forms. Used carelessly, it explains too much; used carefully, it explains exactly what other vocabularies cannot.

### 2021 (Q10): Do we live in a "disciplinary society"? Use the work of Foucault to explain your response.

We do live in a disciplinary society, in Foucault's sense, though also in a biopolitical and partially sovereign one. My answer is "yes, but not exclusively".

A disciplinary society, as the Calhoun extract puts it, is one "stretches from the enclosed disciplines, a sort of social 'quarantine', to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316). Techniques first developed within enclosed institutions (prisons, barracks, hospitals, schools) generalise across the social field. Its features: power operates through everyday practices; it works by surveillance, normalisation, and examination rather than physical coercion; it produces self-regulating docile bodies; and it is productive, not merely repressive (Slides 10, 13, 19-23).

The affirmative case.

Schools. The school is the paradigmatic disciplinary institution. Slide 10 names its instruments: "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages." The pupil is observed continually, measured against age-appropriate norms, and documented in records that follow them through life. Lynch crystallises the logic: the transcript works because "the fact that each one is observed... is what influences students' behaviour" (Lynch, 2011: 16). University league tables extend the diagram into higher education.

The body. Bordo's Slides 25-32 make the body the second canonical site. Body norms have shifted historically: in the late nineteenth century a plump male body signalled success and pale female skin signalled exemption from field labour (Slide 25); thirty years ago the imperative was to diet and "fight the flab"; today it is to be "slender", "in trim", "well-toned" (Slide 27). The disciplinary form persists: body held against a norm, subject self-monitors, failure registers as moral failing. The City of London gym (Slide 28) makes it concrete: one works out "to show you 'mean business' in both your personal and professional life." Fitness trackers, gym apps, and social-media bodies extend the regime.

The medical clinic. The Kember ultrasound is a contemporary disciplinary scene: the foetus rendered visible and the maternal body disappearing, "the question of power and its relation to knowledge" (Slide 3). The scan is normalised, expected, and clinically beneficial: that is what makes it disciplinary.

Knowledge and expertise. Nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)" (Slide 13). The modern prisoner, schoolchild, and psychiatric patient are products of disciplinary apparatuses. Butler extends this: "we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Contemporary society remains saturated by such institutions.

So yes, the lecture's claim that "contemporary society is a 'disciplinary society'" (Slide 10) stands. Add the algorithmic feeds, facial recognition at airports, and CCTV networks, and the diagram extends.

Qualifications. First, we also live in a biopolitical society. Slide 24's injunctions (salt reduction, 5-a-day, anti-obesity policy) target the population. Slide 37 supplies the longer biopolitical arc: "for assuring urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). Disciplinary techniques work on bodies; biopolitical techniques on populations. Both are present.

Second, sovereign power has not vanished (Slide 8). Wherever the state visibly seizes bodies (corporal punishment, capital punishment, military force), sovereign power persists alongside discipline.

Third, the disciplinary society is unevenly distributed. Slide 28: "more demands are made on women than men in terms of producing themselves as 'normal'". Race, class, and gender stratify who is most intensively disciplined.

Fourth, resistance exists. Foucault: "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Lynch: power relations "can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Bordo's diachronic historicisation is itself a disciplinary counter-move: denaturalising the slender norm opens it to challenge, as body-positivity discourse shows.

A productive comparison is with Gramsci's hegemony, "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12). Where disciplinary power works through normalising techniques on the body, hegemony works through consent in the realm of culture and common sense. Hall's reading (Hall, 1980: 36) treats them as two registers of the same problem: how modern societies secure compliance without overt coercion.

My answer: we live in a disciplinary society, but the disciplinary register is one among several. The school register, the medical scan, the body in the gym, the questionnaire (Slide 21), the algorithmic feed, and the fitness tracker all confirm the thesis. The Damiens scene and Slide 24's injunctions require sovereign and biopolitical analyses alongside it. Yes, with qualifications.

### 2021 (Q11): Outline and assess Foucault's discussions of power in terms of the governance of a population.

Foucault's discussions of population governance centre on biopower, one of two "closely related modes" of modern power (Lynch, 2011: 13). Biopower takes the population, not the individual body, as object. It complements disciplinary power; together they form Lynch's "two poles": "a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26).

I outline biopower in three steps, then assess it.

First, the historical claim. From the late eighteenth century, a new form of power emerged alongside discipline. Where the sovereign abused or killed the criminal body (the Damiens execution, Slide 8), modern states began managing the vital processes of populations. Slide 24 catalogues the duties: "it is the duty of government to make us reduce our salt level"; "we feel bad if we do not eat 5 portions of fruit/veg per day"; "the government must stop obese children." Population-level interventions, species body, not individual.

Second, the conceptual structure. Lynch's illustration: observing which groups contract lung cancer leads to identifying causes such as smoking or asbestos exposure (Lynch, 2011: 16). The knowledge is statistical, the intervention aggregate, the rationality "to save lives". Where disciplinary power individualises (each pupil has a transcript), biopower massifies (the cohort has a literacy rate). The modern "police" originally meant management of urban life. Pandemic contact-tracing and population vaccination drives extend the genealogy.

Third, the link to subject formation. Biopower does not bypass the subject; it forms her differently. Bordo's body norms (Slides 25-32) show how biopolitical imperatives are taken up at the embodied self. The City-of-London worker at the gym to "show you 'mean business'" (Slide 28) enacts a biopolitical injunction as personal aspiration. Consumer culture (Slide 32) is biopolitical even when it presents as private choice.

Assessment.

Strengths. First, biopower captures what traditional theories miss. The public-health regime of salt targets, 5-a-day, and anti-obesity policy (Slide 24) is not well described by "the ruling class wills it" or "the state forbids it"; it takes the population as object. The Kember scan renders the antenatal population the unit of governance (Kember, 1998). Bordo's regime organises population-wide aspirations to slenderness (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Second, biopower illuminates the productive side of power. The police "assure urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215). Power produces healthy populations, not merely punished individuals.

Third, biopower clarifies the productive thesis. Slide 13's "new kinds of people" include not just individuals (the prisoner) but population categories (the delinquent, the at-risk, the obese, the metabolically syndromal). Both registers are productive. Race is the paradigm here: eighteenth-century taxonomies (Linnaeus, 1735; Blumenbach, 1775) produced "varieties" of humanity that nineteenth-century states then governed as populations through hygiene, reproductive policy, and migration controls. Du Bois' "double consciousness" (Du Bois, 1903: 8) names the embodied effect for the racialised subject, who is folded into the population as both managed object and excluded interiority. Biopower's history is inseparable from this racial genealogy.

Weaknesses. First, the concept can be over-extended. Lynch warns against using theory to "answer everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13).

Second, biopower must be combined with disciplinary and sovereign power. The school applies disciplinary techniques (registers) alongside biopolitical ones (5-a-day reaching children through meals). The Damiens punishment (Slide 8) is the trace of a sovereign register that persists.

Third, agency. If populations are constituted by biopower, what is the locus of biopolitical agency? Foucault: "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95). Lynch reinforces: "in the relations of power, there is necessarily the possibility of resistance" (Lynch, 2011: 28-29). Bordo's diachronic historicisation is itself biopolitical resistance: showing that today's slender body is no more natural than 1880's plump one (Slide 25) opens it to contestation. Anti-vaccine politics and fat-acceptance movements show biopolitical contestation in action.

Assessment: Foucault's discussions of population governance through biopower are essential to contemporary social theory but not sufficient on their own. They illuminate what other vocabularies cannot: the politics of life itself, the everyday management of populations through diet, hygiene, antenatal care, vaccination, contact-tracing, and the moralisation of bodily self-management. They need supplementing with disciplinary analysis of the individual body and attention to the residual sovereign register. Used this way, biopower remains one of the most powerful concepts in the contemporary social-theoretic toolkit.

The ideology reading captures something but misses more. The Marxist who asks "whose interests does obstetric medicine serve?" gets a partial answer (capital's, patriarchy's), but the question Foucault adds, "how does this scan organise bodies, gazes, and self-conduct?", names what ideology underdescribes. The same applies to the liberal account (Slide 34): power as a thing possessed by specific people, dangerous because it limits freedom. Both treat what Lynch calls "terminal forms" of power as if they were essences (Lynch, 2011: 17), and both miss the capillary level.

The clinical apparatus produces knowledge of the foetus and exercises power on the maternal body in the same artefact. Butler radicalises this: power is "what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2), so the subject of feminist resistance is itself produced by the apparatus she contests.

Asking "how does this apparatus work?" opens specific sites of contestation: the protocol, the screen, the antenatal class, the moralised duty to be screened. Critique can engage the mechanism rather than wait for the revolution. Slide 23's flourish ("the bourgeoisie has won!") concedes that disciplinary apparatuses serve capital, and Marxist critique remains necessary for political-economic analysis the disciplinary toolkit brackets. The right reading is that ideology and the analytics of power are different registers, not rivals. Power is "both much more and much less than ideology" because ideology gets some things right and misses others. Foucault changes the question, not the answer. To ask HOW without abandoning WHO BENEFITS is the legacy of his intervention.

Second, the disciplinary shift is uneven; Bordo notes "more demands are made on women than men" (Slide 28), and L8's analyses of race show that disciplinary, biopolitical, and sovereign registers converge on racialised bodies. Third, "the normal" is contested; the body-positivity movement and contemporary critiques of BMI categories show normalising judgements being denaturalised. Yet on balance, the shift is real and the normal is insidious. Foucault's diagnosis names what otherwise hides in plain sight: the historically invented standards against which we are trained to police ourselves.

The bourgeoisie may not have entirely won, but the apparatus the lecture names is the political form in which contemporary life is lived.

### M8 (was Mock Q1, quote-driven): "Visibility is a trap." Discuss with reference to Foucault.

"Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 315 in Calhoun reader) appears as Foucault unpacks Bentham's panopticon. The trap is not that the inmate is seen (sovereign power saw bodies too, in the spectacle of the scaffold) but that the inmate is visible while the watcher is invisible, so the inmate behaves as if continuously observed even when no one is in the tower. The line is a compressed theory of disciplinary power; taking it seriously means tracking the trap through the panopticon, nineteenth-century institutions, Bordo's gendered body, and the ultrasound that opens the lecture.

CCTV in shopping streets and facial recognition at airports extend the diagram into public space.

Fitness trackers and Instagram bodies update it again.

Kember's ultrasound is the panoptic illustration: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998). The clinical gaze is asymmetric, unverifiable, and produces self-monitoring antenatal conduct. Biopower, Slide 24's salt and 5-a-day injunctions, acts on populations rather than individual cells. Where the field is statistical, the panoptic trap is the wrong diagram. Sixth, the politics. The trap captures the productive character of disciplinary power: it does not just constrain, it makes subjects. The school produces the pupil, the prison the prisoner (Slide 13), the gym the well-toned body. The weakness: if every visibility is a trap, even reciprocal visibility, we lose the distinction between accountability of power and surveillance of the powerless. A *Black Mirror* episode where everyone rates everyone else makes the point: shared visibility is not always panoptic, but sometimes is.

"Visibility is a trap" is correct as a description of the disciplinary mode, indispensable as a diagnosis of self-regulation, and incomplete as a complete theory of contemporary observation. Read alongside Bordo and Kember, the trap remains one of the most useful images in social theory.

### M9 (was Mock Q2, named-work): Critically assess Foucault's argument in *Discipline and Punish*.

*Discipline and Punish* (Foucault, 1977) makes a historical claim (Western societies shifted from sovereign to disciplinary power over the long nineteenth century) and a diagnostic claim (the prison's techniques, articulated in the panopticon, became "an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" running through schools, hospitals, factories, and the army (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader)).

I argue the book is convincing at the level of technique, partially vindicated at the level of subjectivity, and deliberately incomplete at the level of political economy, which Foucault brackets in favour of analytic precision.

Vindication at the level of technique. The school operationalises the three aspects of discipline with paradigmatic clarity. Registers, progress reports, photos, records of achievement, testing, and key stages (Slide 10) instantiate hierarchical observation (asymmetric invisibility), normalising judgement (deviations flagged), and the examination (the documented combination). The same cellular arrangement holds "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). The panopticon is a diagram, not a building.

Lynch captures the same logic compactly. The transcript "begins from observation at the bottom of the pyramid, not from an edict from the top" (Lynch, 2011: 15); "this will go on your permanent record" works because "each one is observed" (Lynch, 2011: 16). Power has its principle "not so much in a person as in a certain concerted distribution of bodies, surfaces, lights, gazes" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The "physics of power" is recognisable wherever observation, normalisation, and examination combine, from school exams to HR performance reviews to fitness-app dashboards.

Vindication of the productive thesis. Slide 13: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)". The modern prisoner, schoolchild, and psychiatric patient are produced by their institutions, not caught by them. Butler extends the thesis to the subject as such: "we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Bordo extends it to gender: the slender, well-toned body is produced by mirror, magazine, gym, and self-talk (Bordo, 1993; Slide 28).

Strain at the level of subjectivity. *Discipline and Punish* assumes a unified subject then examined. Butler's *Psychic Life of Power* (Slide 12) pushes further: power constitutes the subject as such, so resistance cannot stand on a pre-power self. The strain is productive: it forces the reader to think about subject formation more carefully than the early framework demands.

Brackets at the level of political economy. Foucault deliberately brackets "who benefits?" to ask "how does it work?" Slide 23 hints at the cost: docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy and work well in factories and offices. They question little and behave well. And don't resist. Let alone revolt. The bourgeoisie has won!" The disciplinary apparatus serves capital, so the *how* analysis needs pairing with a Marxist *for whom*. Slide 36 frames Foucault's reply: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116). The right reading is complementarity, not rivalry. Cost-of-living and gig-economy precarity make the point: disciplinary analysis without capital is incomplete.

Three further considerations. First, the productive register: Slide 37's police-genealogy quote reminds us discipline also "assures urban supplies, hygiene, health" (Foucault, 1982: 215); it produces useful populations, opening onto biopolitics. Second, resistance: Foucault insists "where there is power, there is resistance" (Foucault, 1978: 95), and Lynch reinforces it (Lynch, 2011: 28). Third, gender: Bordo shows disciplinary norms bear more heavily on women's bodies (Slide 28), a specification *Discipline and Punish* did not develop.

A further genealogical strength is worth noting. Halewood's critique of modern thought as organised through bifurcations (Halewood, 2014: 36) clarifies what Foucault is doing methodologically: *Discipline and Punish* dismantles the very dyads (sovereign/disciplinary, normal/abnormal, observer/observed, mind/body) on which earlier accounts of power relied, and shows them as historical products of specific institutional practices rather than natural kinds. Reading L7 alongside L1 makes Foucault's anti-essentialism intelligible as a sustained refusal of fixed sociological categories.

My verdict: *Discipline and Punish* names what is happening with unique precision in the school, prison, hospital, army, clinic, and body. It is convincing at the level of technique and productive subject formation; deliberately incomplete at the level of political economy; and benefits from being read alongside Bordo and Butler. Foucault gives us the anatomy; the structural critique remains the necessary supplement.

### M10 (was Mock Q3, two-part): What does Foucault mean by power/knowledge, and why does he treat the two as inseparable?

Part one: what does Foucault mean by power/knowledge? The hyphen carries the argument. By power/knowledge Foucault means the production of human-scientific knowledge (psychiatric, pedagogical, clinical, demographic) is itself a power operation, and the exercise of power on bodies and populations cannot proceed without producing knowledge about them. Slide 3: the ultrasound "raises the question of power and its relation to knowledge." Slide 15: in the panopticon "power is linked to visibility and vision and to knowledge." Foucault: this power "imposes a law of truth on him which he must recognize... It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212).

Part two: why are they inseparable? Three reasons.

(i) Bodies cannot be disciplined without being known. The school cannot train pupils without "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" (Slide 10); each is a knowledge instrument and a disciplinary instrument simultaneously. Lynch's transcript is "the record of a student's courses and performance" and an "instrument of power" in the same artefact (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). The hospital cannot treat without the chart; the panopticon "opens up an interiority into which both the prison guard and the inmate can 'look'" (Slide 15).

(ii) Knowledge in the human sciences cannot proceed without observed, classified, normalised subjects. Slide 13: nineteenth-century institutions produced both new knowledges and "new kinds of people". Criminology required prisons; clinical medicine required clinics; pedagogy required schools. The object of human-scientific knowledge is produced by the practices that claim to describe it.

(iii) The truths produced are not false in the Marxist ideology sense. Slide 5: "It is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). Slide 10: power "points out what is normal and invites us to behave in a similar way, to judge ourselves in relation to this 'norm'." Truth has a history and a politics, not because fake but because produced.

Why does this matter? Because it dissolves the Enlightenment picture in which knowledge is a neutral mirror of nature and power is what obscures or applies it. Foucault is not saying all truth is power, only that the human sciences cannot be cleanly separated from the institutions that produced them. Haraway's notion of "situated knowledges" (Haraway, 1988: 581) sharpens this in a different register: knowledge is always knowledge from somewhere, and the disembodied "god-trick" of objective vision is itself a power-effect.

Examples. The Kember scan is the canonical case: a knowledge artefact (image of the foetus) and a power artefact (maternal body displaced) in the same clinical encounter. "The scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998). Slide 4: is it good that doctors visualise the foetus? Could this make the woman seem unimportant? Both answers "could be 'yes'".

Bordo's body norms are power/knowledge in another register. The slender, well-toned body that "means business" (Slide 28) is the product of magazines, advertising, dietary science, and self-help, all knowledge regimes producing bodies and invalidating others. Slide 22 names the effect: is "the normal" one of "the most insidious of all concepts of modernity"? Contemporary BMI categories, calorie counts, and step-targets repeat the logic.

Slide 18 supplies the gathering quote: power "categorizes the individual, marks him by his own individuality, attaches him to his identity, imposes a law of truth on him which he must recognize and which others must recognize in him. It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212). Knowledge of the individual and power over the individual are produced together.

The inseparability is essential to the politics. If knowledge were neutral, critique could appeal to "the science"; if power were merely repressive, resistance could appeal to "the truth". Power/knowledge denies both shortcuts. Critique has to engage with how knowledge is produced and what it does, simultaneously. ChatGPT-generated content circulating as authoritative knowledge is a recent illustration: the same artefact is both a knowledge-claim and a power-effect.

Conclusion: power/knowledge is inseparable by historical analysis, not by definition. The thesis applies most cleanly to the human sciences. Regimes of truth can still be better or worse; Foucault was not a strong relativist. The Kember scan, the school's testing regime, and Bordo's body norms each show the inseparability in action, and the analysis is reactivated every time a new diagnostic category, an examination, or a body ideal is introduced.

### M11 (was Mock Q4, specific-comparison): Compare Foucault's account of disciplinary power with Marx's account of ideology.

This essay compares Marx's ideology (Slide 35) and Foucault's disciplinary power (*Discipline and Punish*, 1977). They operate at different registers and are best used together.

Marx's ideology. Slide 35: "Power is solely related to the ownership of the means of production"; "Power is in the hands of a small, wealthy minority"; "Ideology is one main tool by which power is exercised over the working-class"; "The proletariat need to 'discover' their own power." Ideology is class domination in the realm of ideas: workers share the worldview of the ruling class, exploitation is obscured, socialism's political work is to dispel that obscuring through revolutionary consciousness.

Foucault's disciplinary power. The repressive picture (power as constraint exercised by a ruling group on subordinates) misses what is distinctive about modern power. Disciplinary power operates not through cognition but through bodies: timetable, spatial partition, examination, hierarchical observation, normalising judgement (Slides 19, 21). It produces docile bodies (Slide 23). The schoolchild, soldier, patient, worker, prisoner are all docile bodies; the cell holds "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). Disciplinary power does not need workers to believe in capitalism; it needs them to arrive on time, follow procedure, submit to examination. Power "is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102).

Convergences. Both insist that apparent free choice is heavily structured; both reject liberal autonomy; both link subject formation to historically specific institutions. Slide 23 holds both in view: docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy... The bourgeoisie has won!"

Four differences. First, the register: ideology operates on consciousness, disciplinary power on bodies. The pupil need not believe in the key-stage test; she must sit it. Second, the location: ideology is concentrated in apparatuses controlled by the ruling class; disciplinary power is capillary and "always already there" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch, 2011: 19). Foucault: "Power in Western capitalism was denounced by the Marxists as class domination; but the mechanics of power themselves were never analysed" (Foucault, 1980: 116; Slide 36). Third, the truth: ideology is false consciousness; disciplinary power produces regimes of truth, and Foucault claims no outside standpoint. Fourth, the agent: ideology presupposes a subject who could be undeceived; disciplinary power produces the subject. Butler: power "is not simply what we oppose, but also...what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). No pre-disciplinary subject waits to be liberated.

Worked examples. The school's regime of registers, progress reports, and key stages (Slide 10): ideology asks whose interests mass schooling serves (capital's, in producing a literate and compliant workforce); discipline asks how the school's instruments examine each pupil and normalise her. The Kember ultrasound: ideology asks whether obstetric medicine is patriarchal (Slide 5: "not exactly untrue"); discipline asks how the scan reorganises visibility ("much more and much less than ideology"). Bordo on the slender body (Slides 25-32): ideology asks whose interests women's body discipline serves (capital, the slimming industry, patriarchy); discipline asks how the mirror, magazine, gym, fitness tracker, and Instagram feed produce a subject "habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Foucault did not refute Marx; he changed the question. Marx asks who benefits and how consent is secured. Foucault asks how techniques work and how subjects are produced. The lecture holds both registers: Slide 23 names the capitalist benefit of docile bodies; Slides 35-36 frame Foucault as a methodological supplement to, not a dismissal of, Marxism. Gramsci's account of hegemony is the natural bridge between the two. Hegemony is rule "by consent" (Gramsci, 1971: 12), secured in the everyday institutions of civil society (school, church, press, union) rather than by the state alone, and Hall's reading (Hall, 1981: 227) treats ideological and disciplinary techniques as complementary registers of the same problem: how a modern social order reproduces itself without overt coercion. Lynch's reading reinforces the supplementarity: the analytics of power do not abolish the question of who benefits but make it possible to ask it more carefully. The right move is to keep both: ideology critique remains needed for the manosphere's misogynist worldview or for billionaire-influencer self-help; disciplinary critique remains needed for the gym, the HR review, and the ultrasound. To replace ideology with disciplinary power is to lose tools; to dismiss disciplinary power as Marxist heresy is to miss what makes modern power distinctive.

Foucault's biopower. Where disciplinary power targets the individual body, biopower targets the population. Used this way, biopower remains the most important single concept for thinking about how modern societies govern life itself.

### M13 (was Mock Q6, disjunctive): Is the panopticon a metaphor or a model? Discuss.

The disjunction (metaphor or model) is the key to reading Foucault on the panopticon. The panopticon is best understood as a *diagram*: something between metaphor and model, a schema naming the logical structure of disciplinary surveillance without claiming that any actual institution implements it perfectly, and which therefore travels productively across institutional sites.

The case for "metaphor". A metaphor figures one thing in terms of another. Bentham's prison stands metaphorically for modern surveillance: like the panopticon, contemporary disciplinary arrangements make us visible to an unverifiable watcher. As metaphor, the panopticon is rhetorically powerful but conceptually loose; it breaks down whenever an institution lacks the central tower, individual cell, or human watcher.

The case for "model". A model captures the essential mechanism and can be tested against instances. On this reading, the panopticon is Foucault's model of disciplinary power; particular institutions are evaluated for how closely they implement it. The reformative prison, model school, asylum, factory, and army would be more or less panoptic. As model, the panopticon is rigorous but risks over-extension: if every visibility-asymmetry is panoptic, the model loses discriminating power.

The case for "diagram". The Calhoun extract supplies the framing. The panopticon "automatizes and disindividualizes power. Power has its principle not so much in a person as in a certain concerted distribution of bodies, surfaces, lights, gazes" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). Discipline "may be identified neither with an institution nor with an apparatus; it is a type of power, a modality for its exercise" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). This is the language of diagram, a logical structure that crosses institutional sites. "One can speak of the formation of a disciplinary society in this movement that stretches from the enclosed disciplines... to an indefinitely generalizable mechanism of 'panopticism'" (Foucault, 1977: 316 in Calhoun reader). The diagram generalises; instances vary.

Test cases.

The cell as universal container. The panoptic cell can hold "a madman, a patient, a condemned man, a worker or a schoolboy" (Foucault, 1977: 314 in Calhoun reader). The diagram specifies the conditions (asymmetric visibility, individual confinement, central tower) under which any subject becomes observable, classifiable, and self-regulating.

The school. Slide 10's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages" instantiates the diagram without an architectural tower. A distributed apparatus of records produces the panoptic effect. Lynch's transcript (Lynch, 2011: 15-16) shows the diagram operating capillary-fashion through paperwork.

The body. Bordo (Slides 25-32) shows the diagram operating without a watcher at all: the bathroom mirror becomes the tower, the woman becomes both watcher and watched, and slenderness norms supply the unverifiable standard. "Bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3) are the diagram closed at the level of the embodied self. Berger's earlier formulation already captures the structure: "men look at women; women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47), and Mulvey's "male gaze" (Mulvey, 1975: 11) gives the same diagram its cinematic form. Each instantiates panoptic vision without a literal tower. Selfie culture and fitness-tracker dashboards intensify the closure.

The clinical scene. Kember's ultrasound (Slide 2) is panoptic: asymmetric visibility, unverifiability of clinical evaluation, production of antenatal subjectivity. "The scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has direct access to the image of the foetus" (Kember, 1998).

Contemporary diagrams. Facial recognition at airports, CCTV networks, and social-media follower-counts are all panoptic in form: the watcher need not be present, the watched cannot verify when observed, and self-regulation follows. The diagram has digitised but kept its structure.

Where the diagram strains. The biopolitical injunctions of Slide 24 (salt, 5-a-day, obesity policy) target the population, not the individual in the cell. The panoptic diagram is the wrong figure here; biopower is right. Lynch warns theory should not "answer everything" (Lynch, 2011: 13).

Verdict: the panopticon is a diagram. As metaphor it under-claims; as model it over-claims. As diagram it does just what Foucault wanted: name the disciplinary logic without specifying empirical implementations, and leave room for the biopolitical and sovereign diagrams alongside it. Where asymmetric visibility, unverifiability, and internalisation are in play (school, body, clinic, social-media feed), the panopticon is the right tool; where the population is the target, biopolitics is needed alongside.

Bordo's analysis is the lecture's clearest contemporary illustration of Foucauldian disciplinary power; her diachronic move is decisive for the productive thesis; and her account exposes the gendered character of disciplinary society Foucault did not fully theorise.

The diachronic move. Slide 25: in the late nineteenth century, plump signalled male success, muscle signalled working-class status, and pale skin signalled women's exemption from fieldwork. Slide 27 tracks the inversion: thirty years ago we were "concerned with weight... encouraged to diet, 'fight the flab'. Nowadays, we have to be 'slender'. Adverts talk of being in trim, well-toned, well-defined." Body parts are framed as "in revolt": thighs, stomach, upper arms, calves. The form persists; the content changes.

This move does decisive theoretical work. It instantiates the productive thesis (Slide 13) at the level of the body: different configurations produce different bodies; the body is not the natural ground on which discipline acts but its product. It denaturalises the present: if both 1880's plump masculine body and the contemporary slender feminine body are products of historically specific norms, neither has priority as "natural".

The disciplinary mechanism. Bordo fits the lecture's three aspects of discipline precisely. Hierarchical observation: the woman is watched by the magazine, mirror, advertising image, peer group, and the City-of-London colleague who notices whether one "means business" enough for the gym (Slide 28). Today the Instagram feed and fitness-tracker dashboard add new observers. Normalising judgement: the slender, well-toned norm is the standard against which deviation registers as moral failing ("being fat is equated with being lazy. Of not caring. Of being a failure", Slide 28). Examination: the bathroom scale, calorie count, gym-mirror self-assessment, dressing-room verdict, weekly step-count.

The self-regulatory closure. Slide 17: "If power 'shouted at us' all the time we would notice it. Rather, it invites and incites us to behave in certain ways. We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!" Bordo's clinching formulation: "Self-management in consumer culture...becomes more elusive as it becomes more pressing...lies the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3, Slide 29). No one needs to coerce the dieter; she coerces herself.

The gendered specification. Foucault's analysis was relatively gender-neutral; Bordo supplies the specification, and the lecture endorses it. Slide 28: "More demands are made on women than men in terms of them producing themselves as 'normal'." Slide 32: "Power operates more subtly but more closely in the regulation of women's bodies... Foucault's work on power can help us understand this situation better/more accurately." Not a departure but an application to a domain he did not develop. The contemporary Andrew Tate / manosphere phenomenon partly inverts the gendering, applying intensified body norms to young men, but it does not displace the central asymmetry Bordo identifies.

The intersection with consumer culture. Slide 32 names the tension: "We live in a consumer society, one which runs on us drinking coffee, eating cakes, take-away food etc. But we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals, in control of ourselves, and this means being in control of our bodies!" The subject is hailed as consumer (eat, drink, spend) and as self-disciplinary subject (be slender, controlled). The chasm Bordo names is precisely the gap, producing "bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Bordo's account also has a feminist counter built in. hooks' "oppositional gaze" (hooks, 1992: 116) names a viewing position that refuses identification with the disciplinary norms Bordo describes; the Black woman who looks back at the cinema screen or the magazine cover declines the panoptic invitation to self-monitor. Reading L7 alongside L5 shows that the disciplined body is also a site of contestation, not just normalisation.

Conclusion. Bordo's analysis is the lecture's most fully developed contemporary illustration of disciplinary power and docile bodies. The slender body, the City of London gym (Slide 28), the felt failure of missing a 5-a-day target (Slide 24), the consumer-culture paradox (Slide 32) all instantiate the panoptic diagram at the level of embodied selfhood. Butler (Slide 12) extends the analysis to the subject as such: power forms rather than constrains a pre-existing subject. The disciplinary body and the disciplined subject are produced together; the productive thesis of Slide 13 reaches its sharpest form here.

### M15 (was Mock Q8, hypothetical): How does Lynch's exposition of Foucault help us read the lecture's account of power?

Lynch's chapter "Foucault's theory of power" supplies the conceptual scaffolding the slides rely on without always making explicit. Lynch's exposition supplies four indispensable supplements: a clearer account of what Foucault is rejecting, a clearer account of the micro/macro articulation, a sharper formulation of the "intentional but non-subjective" character of power, and a precise reading of resistance.

Lynch on what Foucault rejects. Slides 34-35 set out the liberal and Marxist foils; Lynch is more fine-grained. He identifies three "mistaken, inadequate or misleading conceptions": the liberal (power as institutions and mechanisms of subservience), psychoanalytic (power as rule and law), and Marxist (power as class domination) (Lynch, 2011: 17). All three share a "juridico-discursive" representation: power as negative, prohibitive, top-down, uniform (Lynch, 2011: 17-19). All mistake "terminal forms" for essence. Lynch makes Slides 34-36 intelligible as a single coherent rejection.

Lynch on micro/macro. The lecture's productive thesis (Slide 13), the panoptic-society generalisation (Slide 10), and the biopolitical material (Slide 24) sit alongside one another without the structural relation being made explicit. Lynch supplies it: "we thus have a micro-level of individuals (disciplinary techniques of the body) and a macro-level of populations (biopolitics)" (Lynch, 2011: 26). The two are not rivals but two poles, the micro constituting the macro through aggregation. Lynch's pyramid analogy (Lynch, 2011: 15) is decisive: power is built "from the bottom of a pyramid" rather than imposed from the top, and the academic transcript is the worked example.

Lynch on "intentional but non-subjective". Power relations are intentional ("imbued, through and through, with calculation: there is no power that is exercised without a series of aims and objectives", Lynch, 2011: 26-27) but non-subjective ("this does not mean that it results from the choice or decision of an individual subject", Lynch, 2011: 27). The school's docile-body production (Slide 23) gestures at this without naming it: nobody decides pupils should be docile, yet the school's tactics aim at it. Lynch: "the effects of the exercise of power reach beyond any individual's (or group's) intentions or control" (Lynch, 2011: 27). This is what makes Slide 23's claim defensible without sliding into conspiracy.

Lynch on resistance. Slide 17's compressed claim risks sounding deterministic. Lynch restores resistance: "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet... this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power" (Foucault, 1978: 95, cited in Lynch, 2011: 28). "Without resistance, without two bodies (or minds) pushing or pulling against each other, there is no power relation. And through resistance, power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). This is essential for reading Bordo: the slender-body norm produces self-monitoring, but the norm is contested (by alternative aesthetics, body-acceptance discourse, historical denaturalisation), confirming disciplinary power is not totalising.

Three illustrations.

First, the Kember example. The ultrasound (Slides 2, 38) raises the question whether the doctor is "part of a patriarchal system" (Slide 5). Ideology critique is "not exactly untrue", but "it is both much more and much less than ideology" (Foucault, 1980: 102). Lynch's micro/macro framing clarifies the surplus: the scan is a micro-encounter aggregating into a macro-apparatus of antenatal screening; the productive effects cannot be reduced to any agent's intent.

Second, the school. Slide 10's list is theoretically intelligible through Lynch's reading of the transcript as a bottom-up instrument (Lynch, 2011: 15-16). "Power develops in the first instance in specific, local, individual choices, behaviours and interactions" (Lynch, 2011: 26).

Third, the body. Bordo (Slides 25-32) is most fully Foucauldian when read through Lynch's resistance clause: the historicisation of body norms (plump in 1880, slender today) is itself a resistive denaturalisation. Lynch's "power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28) keeps Bordo from sliding into pessimism.

A productive cross-reading is with Hall's reception of Gramsci. Hall (Hall, 1980: 36) treats hegemony and disciplinary power as complementary rather than rival accounts: hegemony names how consent is secured in civil society, discipline names how compliant subjects are produced in everyday institutions. Lynch's "micro/macro" framing makes the same move at a different scale.

Verdict: Lynch's exposition is not an alternative to Foucault but the reader's scaffold for the lecture. It clarifies what is rejected, articulates the micro/macro structure, makes the "intentional but non-subjective" claim defensible, and restores the resistance Slide 17 risks losing. The lecture and Lynch are designed to be read together; Lynch is the lecture's theoretical apparatus made explicit.

### M16 (was Mock Q9, single-author how-convincing): How convincing do you find Foucault's claim that "power is everywhere"?

Foucault: "power is everywhere; not because it embraces everything, but because it comes from everywhere" (Foucault, 1978: 93, cited in Lynch, 2011: 25). I find the claim convincing on a careful reading, unconvincing on a careless one, and politically productive only when paired with specifications preventing it from collapsing into cynicism.

The careless reading takes "power is everywhere" to mean every social relation is equally an instance of power, domination is universal, and resistance is futile. This underwrites either nihilistic acquiescence or paranoid maximalism. Neither is what Foucault meant.

The careful reading distinguishes two parts. (i) "Comes from everywhere": power is not a possession concentrated in a state, class, or institution; it is a multiplicity of force relations arising from below and aggregating into larger configurations. Lynch: "power develops in the first instance in specific, local, individual choices, behaviours and interactions" (Lynch, 2011: 26). (ii) "Not because it embraces everything": omnipresence is not totality. Lynch: "power does not 'consolidate everything' or 'embrace everything' or 'answer everything'; power alone may not be adequate to explain all, or every aspect of, social relations" (Lynch, 2011: 14). This saves the claim from totalising determinism.

Three reasons I find the careful claim convincing.

First, explanatory yield. The hypothesis forces us to look at micro-techniques that other vocabularies miss. Slide 10's "registers, progress reports, school photos, records of achievement, testing, key stages", Lynch's transcript, Slide 21's questionnaire, Bordo's bathroom mirror, the Kember ultrasound, and today's fitness trackers and algorithmic feeds are all sites an "elite-domination" framework underdescribes.

Second, the productive thesis. If power "comes from everywhere", it is productive rather than principally repressive. Slide 13: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people". The modern prisoner, schoolchild, and psychiatric patient are products of disciplinary apparatuses. This explanation of category-proliferation is hard to obtain elsewhere.

Third, resistance internal to power. Lynch's reading restores what Slide 17's slogan can miss: "Without resistance, without two bodies (or minds) pushing or pulling against each other, there is no power relation. And through resistance, power relations can always be altered" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Omnipresence is compatible with continuous contestation.

Three reasons I find the claim unconvincing if pushed.

First, the political-economy lacuna. Slide 23: docile bodies "fit well into the modern capitalist economy" and "the bourgeoisie has won!". The disciplinary apparatus serves capital; "comes from everywhere" cannot, alone, explain why it serves the bourgeoisie. The fix is to read Foucault alongside structural analyses, not abandon him. Cost-of-living and gig-economy precarity sharpen the point.

Second, normative reticence. If power is everywhere, the critic needs criteria for distinguishing better and worse configurations. Lynch: "those who understand the claim that individuals are constituted by power relations to constitute a denial of freedom find his vision to be bleak" (Lynch, 2011: 28). His response is that Foucauldian analysis "increase[s] our self-awareness, and hence our own freedom" (Lynch, 2011: 28), but the framework remains thinner than a full normative theory.

Third, the agency problem. If power produces subjects, the subject of resistance is itself produced. Lynch resolves: "to understand subjectivity as constituted (in part) through power relations is not to deny that subjects can act intentionally" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Butler: power "is not simply what we oppose" but "what we depend on for our existence" (Butler, 1997: 1-2; Slide 12).

Worked examples. The school is a site where power "comes from everywhere" (registers, peer comparison, internalised norms): the omnipresence claim fits. Bordo's slender-body norm fits too (mirror, magazine, gym, Instagram). The Kember ultrasound, by contrast, is organised by a specific clinical apparatus; omnipresence must be paired with attention to the medical institution. Slide 24's biopolitical material (salt, 5-a-day, obesity policy) shows power coming from "the government", a specific source; omnipresence must be balanced by attention to the state. Halewood's critique of modern thought as organised through bifurcations (Halewood, 2014: 36) clarifies what the claim is really doing: "power is everywhere" dismantles the inside/outside, state/society, base/superstructure pairs through which earlier theories spatialised power, and replaces them with a topology of force relations.

Verdict: "power is everywhere" is convincing as a description of the dispersed, capillary character of modern power. It is not convincing as a complete theory superseding structural analysis. Read as a methodological injunction (look everywhere) rather than a metaphysical thesis, it is one of the most consequential claims in twentieth-century social theory, and Lynch is the indispensable scaffold for getting it right.

### M17 (was Mock Q10, two-authors): Using the work of at least two authors, evaluate Foucault's account of disciplinary power through the contributions of Bordo and Butler.

Bordo and Butler each contribute a different specification of Foucault's productive thesis. I argue: strengthened at bodily discipline (Bordo), transformed at subject formation (Butler), and qualified by the gendered specification Foucault did not develop.

Foucault's disciplinary power. Slide 10 names contemporary society a "disciplinary society" with its school instruments (registers, progress reports, key stages). Slide 13: nineteenth-century institutions produced "new kinds of people (new kinds of subjects)". Slides 19, 21: hierarchical observation, examination, normalising judgement. The product is the docile body (Slide 23). Power "categorizes the individual, marks him by his own individuality, attaches him to his identity, imposes a law of truth on him... It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212; Slide 18).

Bordo on disciplinary norms. Slides 25-32 trace her three moves. Diachronic (Slide 25): plumpness signalled male success, muscle signalled working-class status, pale skin signalled women's exemption from fieldwork. Norms have inverted (Slide 27) but the form persists. Mechanism (Slides 27-28): contemporary culture demands "slender", "in trim", "well-toned" bodies, and "more demands are made on women than men". The City of London gym is the worked example. Closure (Slide 29): "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3).

Bordo's contribution. She supplies the gendered specification Foucault needed. Slide 32: "Power operates more subtly but more closely in the regulation of women's bodies... Foucault's work on power can help us understand this situation better." Bordo applies Foucault to a domain he did not develop, making the disciplinary register the analytic for the slimming industry, magazine, mirror, gym, and now the fitness tracker and Instagram feed.

Butler on power forming the subject. Slide 12 cites Butler's *Psychic Life of Power*: "We are used to thinking of power as what presses on the subject from the outside [...] But if [...] we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence [...] then power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2).

Butler's contribution. She pushes the productive thesis to its strongest form. Foucault insists power produces subjects (Slides 13, 18); Butler insists it constitutes the very condition of subjectivity. There is no pre-power self. This radicalises (the productive thesis cuts deeper) and complicates (if power forms the subject, the subject of resistance is itself produced by power).

Lynch addresses the complication: "to understand subjectivity as constituted (in part) through power relations is not to deny that subjects can act intentionally" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Resistance is alteration of power relations from within, not return to a pre-power authenticity. Lynch: those who find in Foucault "tools with which to increase our self-awareness, and hence our own freedom, hear wellsprings of hope" (Lynch, 2011: 28).

Convergences. Both agree with Foucault on the productive thesis. Both reject voluntarist individualism and crude determinism. Both insist on the embodied subject as a site of social reproduction. Butler's earlier work in *Gender Trouble* (Butler, 1990: 25), discussed in L5, supplies the missing link: gender is a "stylised repetition of acts" rather than an inner essence, so the disciplinary norms Bordo describes do not regulate a pre-existing gender but constitute it through their citational performance. Reading L7 alongside L5 shows that disciplinary normalisation and gender performativity are two descriptions of the same productive process.

Divergences. Bordo works at disciplinary norms on the body; Butler at subject formation as such. Bordo's tools are the magazine, mirror, and gym; Butler's are citational repetition of gendered acts and psychic interiority. The lecture frames them as complementary.

Worked illustrations. The Kember scan (Slide 2) is the clearest meeting point: disciplinary (Bordo: maternal body rendered visible against a clinical norm) and subject-forming (Butler: antenatal subject constituted through the apparatus). The City gym (Slide 28) is Bordoian. Slide 32's "we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals" is Butlerian.

Verdict: Foucault's disciplinary power is strengthened by Bordo's gendered specification and transformed by Butler's account of subject formation. Both extend Foucault to domains he did not develop, and both expose the productive thesis as more radical than Slide 13's compressed version makes it appear. Reading the lecture without Bordo and Butler is reading Foucault without his most generative heirs.

### M18 (was Mock Q11, can-we): Can Foucault's framework be used to think about resistance, or does it foreclose it?

This repeats one of the oldest charges against Foucault: that his account leaves no room for resistance. I argue: yes, Foucault's framework can be used for thinking about resistance, and Lynch's reading makes the case.

The prosecution case. Three charges. (i) Power is omnipresent: "always already there, one is never outside it" (Foucault, 1980, cited in Lynch, 2011: 19). No outside means no Archimedean point to push back. (ii) Power produces the subject: "It is a form of power which makes individuals subjects" (Foucault, 1982: 212; Slide 18). If the subject of resistance is itself produced by power, resistance is just power moving against itself. (iii) Slide 17 ("We discipline ourselves! We self-regulate!") risks sounding like a verdict of inescapability.

The defence has five elements.

First, Foucault insists resistance is internal to power: "Where there is power, there is resistance, and yet... this resistance is never in a position of exteriority in relation to power" (Foucault, 1990a: 95, cited in Lynch, 2011: 28). Resistance is constitutive of power, not outside it. Without resistance, we have domination, not power.

Second, omnipresence is not totality. Lynch: "power does not 'consolidate everything' or 'embrace everything' or 'answer everything'" (Lynch, 2011: 14). The plurality of force relations is the condition for the plurality of resistances.

Third, power and resistance constitute each other. Foucault 1984: "In the relations of power, there is necessarily the possibility of resistance... if there were no possibility of resistance, of escape, of ruse... there would be no relations of power" (Lynch, 2011: 28-29). "If there are relations of power throughout every social field it is because there is freedom everywhere" (Lynch, 2011: 29).

Fourth, the productive thesis is itself a resource for resistance. Historicising how power produces subjects denaturalises the present and opens it to contestation. Bordo's diachronic move (Slide 25) is the clearest example: showing that the slender body of today is no more natural than the plump body of 1880 opens the norm to challenge. The same applies to the modern prisoner (Slide 14), patient, schoolchild: each is a historical product, so each is reformable. Body-positivity movements and prison-abolitionist politics show contemporary forms.

Fifth, Lynch's reading is hopeful. "Those who understand the claim that individuals are constituted by power relations to constitute a denial of freedom find his vision to be bleak. Those of us... who find in his analyses the tools with which to increase our self-awareness, and hence our own freedom, hear wellsprings of hope" (Lynch, 2011: 28). Resistance requires self-understanding, not an outside.

What Foucauldian resistance does not do. It does not promise final emancipation or a post-power society. Slide 23 ("the bourgeoisie has won!") gestures at the limit: disciplinary analysis without structural critique cannot mount a revolution. For projects of overthrowing capitalism or dismantling patriarchy, Foucault's vocabulary is inadequate alone; the fix is supplementation.

Illustrations. Bordo's historicisation of body norms is a disciplinary resistance: showing the slender norm is contingent opens it to contestation. Butler's "power is not simply what we oppose" but "what we depend on" (Butler, 1997: 1-2; Slide 12) is more radical: it denies an authentic pre-power self but opens immanent contestation. hooks' "oppositional gaze" (hooks, 1992: 116) supplies the worked feminist example: the Black woman who looks back at the screen or the disciplinary norm refuses identification from within, not from an outside. Slide 5's question ("Are doctors part of a patriarchal system?") is itself resistive: raising the question denaturalises the clinical apparatus. Slide 22's framing of "the normal" as "one of the most insidious of all concepts of modernity" is a resistive denaturalisation of the normalising judgement Slide 21 describes. Anti-vaccine politics, fat-acceptance discourse, and contestations of HR rating culture extend the register.

Verdict: Foucault's framework does not foreclose resistance, contrary to the standard prosecution case. What it forecloses is the master-narrative model (the universal subject, the final liberation). What it enables is granular analysis of how power and resistance entangle in specific institutions, bodies, and contested moments. For these tasks, Foucauldian resistance is the right scale of analysis. For structural inequality at scale, Foucault must be supplemented by Marxist and feminist structural critique. The framework opens space for political work; the work itself remains ours.

### M19 (was Mock Q12, specific-comparison): Compare Bordo's reading of disciplinary norms with Butler's account of the subject formed by power.

Bordo and Butler share a Foucauldian commitment to the productive thesis but develop it differently. Bordo's "Reading the Slender Body" (1993) works with disciplinary norms; Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997) works with the formation of the subject by power.

Bordo's disciplinary norms. Slides 25-32 trace her argument. Diachronic move (Slide 25): in the late nineteenth century, plumpness signalled male success, muscle signalled working-class status, and pale skin signalled women's exemption from fieldwork. Body norms are historical products of disciplinary configurations, not natural facts. Contemporary inversion (Slide 27): "30 years ago we were concerned with weight... encouraged to diet, 'fight the flab'. Nowadays, we have to be 'slender'. Adverts talk of being in trim, well-toned, well-defined." Body parts are framed "in revolt": thighs, stomach, upper arms, calves. Gendered specification (Slide 28): "more demands are made on women than men". Self-regulatory closure (Slide 29): "the chasm that produces bodies habituated to self-monitoring and self-normalization" (Bordo, 1993: 202-3). The body is the site at which the three aspects of discipline close as self-regulation.

Butler on power forming the subject. Slide 12: "We are used to thinking of power as what presses on the subject from the outside [...] But if [...] we understand power as *forming* the subject as well as providing the very condition of its existence [...] then power is not simply what we oppose, but also, in a strong sense, what we depend on for our existence and what we harbour and preserve in the beings that we are" (Butler, 1997: 1-2). Butler takes the productive thesis to its strongest form: power constitutes the subject as such.

Convergences. Both reject voluntarist individualism and crude determinism. Both insist on the embodied subject as a site of social reproduction, treat the productive nature of power as central, and link gendered subject-production to historical configurations.

Divergences. First, object: Bordo's is the gendered body as disciplinarily normed; Butler's is the subject as formed. Second, mechanism: Bordo's is disciplinary (observation, examination, internalised normalisation, Slides 19, 21); Butler's is more fundamental, the formation of the condition of subjectivity. Third, temporality: Bordo's is diachronic (Slide 25); Butler's is structural. Fourth, politics of resistance: Bordo opens resistance by denaturalising the present norm; Butler requires resistance be immanent, since there is no pre-power self.

Worked applications. The Kember scan (Slide 2) is the clearest meeting point. In Bordo's terms, the scan is a disciplinary instrument producing the antenatal body against a clinical norm (Slide 4: "could this lead to the woman being viewed as unimportant?"). In Butler's terms, it forms the maternal subject, conferring identity through the apparatus making the foetus visible. The City of London gym (Slide 28) is Bordoian: a paradigmatic scene of normalised body-work. Slide 32's "we have to produce ourselves as normal individuals" is Butlerian: subject formation through the very practices that constitute her. The Instagram filter is both: Bordoian discipline of the face and Butlerian formation of the photographed subject.

Stakes for the lecture's other examples. Slide 24's biopolitical injunctions (salt, 5-a-day, anti-obesity policy) can be read through both: Bordo names them as disciplinary normalisations of the population's bodies; Butler names them as productions of the subject as bearer-of-public-health-duty. The injunction "It is our duty to keep ourselves healthy" produces both a body-shape (Bordo) and a subject-form (Butler). The contemporary practice of running a Fitbit alongside a calorie-tracking app is a disciplinary scene of self-monitoring (Bordo) and a subject-forming scene in which the user becomes the kind of person whose worth is tracked numerically (Butler). Public-health campaigns against smoking, the moralised refusal of alcohol, and the rise of clean-eating discourse all run on both registers simultaneously. hooks' "oppositional gaze" (hooks, 1992: 116) supplies the missing third term: a resistive viewing position from which the disciplinary norms Bordo describes and the subject-formations Butler theorises can be refused without claiming an outside.

Verdict: Bordo and Butler are complementary, not competing. Bordo gives a tool for analysing disciplinary normalisation of gendered bodies; Butler gives a tool for analysing subject formation by power. The lecture endorses both. To force a choice is to lose half the feminist Foucauldian toolkit; to read them together is to get the full picture of the disciplinary subject the lecture describes.
