# SC301 — L4: Language and the World (Words and Things)

> Cheat sheet for revision. Lecture 4 covers three linked questions: (1) the relation between language and thought (the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis); (2) language and gender (Dale Spender, Simone de Beauvoir, Luce Irigaray); (3) language and the legacy of colonialism (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Chinua Achebe). The overriding claim: **language is not a neutral mirror of the world — it classifies, evaluates, and is a mode of power**.

---

## 1. Key concepts and thinkers (≤200 words)

- **Linguistic relativity / the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis** (Edward Sapir, Benjamin Lee Whorf): each language embodies a particular world view; speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968, p. 230).
- **Linguistic determinism**: stronger claim that language *determines* thought — "moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968, p. 231).
- **Man-made language** (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980): English is structured around the male-as-norm; female is "minus male".
- **The Second Sex / "woman is the other"** (Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1949/1988): man is positive and neutral; woman is the negative, the marked term.
- **Imperialism of language / decolonising the mind** (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, 1993): colonial languages were weapons of conquest, "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 37).
- **Africanising English** (Chinua Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997): English as a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, *Transitions*, p. 347).
- **Sexuate subject / sexual difference is not extra-linguistic** (Luce Irigaray).
- **The generic he / chairman vs chair / actor vs actress**: concrete sites where male-as-norm is reproduced.

## 2. Key notes from the presentation

**Three parts to the lecture (slide 2).**

1. *Language, thought and culture* — linguistic relativity and linguistic determinism.
2. *Language and gender* — Spender, de Beauvoir.
3. *Language and the legacy of colonialism* — Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

**Sapir–Whorf (slides 3–6).** Linguistic relativity is "the belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968, p. 230). The strong version, linguistic determinism, holds that the language we speak shapes the very limits of thought. The recurring textbook example is the claim that "Eskimos have over 50 words for snow", which the lecturer marks with scare-quotes to flag two problems: (a) "Eskimo" is itself an inaccurate, externally imposed label (see Samson, *A Way of Life That Does Not Exist*, 2003); (b) the example is built on a single-word fixation that ignores how English speakers also describe many kinds of snow through *phrases* ("good snow for packing"). The slide highlights the load-bearing line: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**" (Lecture 4 slide 4).

**Critique of Sapir–Whorf (slides 7–9).** Any human language renders, rather than reflects, reality; we have lost any "innocent contact" with the world. Worse, the snow story sets up an "us / them" division in which a vaguely "Western" outlook is the benchmark and the so-called indigenous group is the exotic other. This links forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

**Spender on man-made language (slides 10–14, 18).** Spender argues that the structure of English encodes a "**male-as-norm**" classification scheme: humanity is divided into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145–148). Historical evidence: Mr Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, p. 147); John Kirkby's 1746 "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, p. 148). Her programme: "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, p. 162).

**De Beauvoir (slide 15, 28).** "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). The man writes from no particular standpoint; the woman is always a *woman writer*. Crucially, de Beauvoir does **not** fully separate sex from gender — biology and lived societal experience are intertwined.

**Actors / actresses / political correctness (slides 16–17).** David Marsh (*Guardian*) bans "actress": only "actor". This *seems* progressive (women are no longer compared only to other women) but the lecturer warns that occupations were historically male-coded, so calling all women "actors" risks absorbing them into a masculine yardstick rather than dismantling it.

**Grammarians and gender (slide 29).** H.W. Fowler (*A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, 1937 [1926], p. 211) insists that grammatical gender "is a grammatical term only" — derived from Latin *genus* (type, genre). To treat masculine/feminine as biologically sexed is, for him, "a jocularity… or a blunder".

**Ngũgĩ and Achebe (slides 20–25).** Ngũgĩ frames language as a weapon of colonisation: education in the colonial language, suppression of indigenous tongues, "The English language was the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, 2008, p. 33 / lecture cites p. 33). No language of African origin is an official UN language. Achebe responds differently: African writers should keep writing in English/French/Portuguese to reach larger audiences, but should fashion an English "that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). When asked whether an African can ever use English like a native speaker, Achebe says: "I hope not" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347).

**And what of other languages? (slide 26).** Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish — internal colonial casualties of English inside the British Isles.

## 3. Information from the readings

### 3.1 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, Chapter 4: "Imperialism of Language. English, a Language for the World?"

Ngũgĩ opens with a two-aspect view of language: it is both **a means of communication** and **a carrier of culture and history** — what he calls in *Decolonising the Mind* "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 30). These two aspects are inseparable but unbalanced according to the political conditions of encounter.

When languages meet "on terms of equality and independence" (e.g. Scandinavians, Japanese, West Germans learning English), the communicative aspect dominates and English does not substitute for the national language (p. 30–31). When they meet "as oppressor and oppressed, as for instance under imperialism", language becomes a **weapon** alongside "the Bible and the sword" (p. 31). Ngũgĩ quotes David Livingstone's nineteenth-century slogan "Christianity plus 5 percent" and updates it to "Christianity, debt, plus 40 percent in debt servicing" (p. 31).

The capture of minds happens in two complementary moves (p. 31–32):
1. **Suppression of indigenous languages.** They were "experienced as incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (p. 31). Ngũgĩ recalls a Kenyan school hymn — "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom" — where the gloom was the children's own language (p. 32).
2. **Elevation of the conqueror's language.** "Those inducted into the school system… were furnished with new mirrors" (p. 32). The French called this *assimilation*; the English called it *education*. Lugard's blunter name was **indirect rule**: mastery of English was "the measure of one's readiness for election into the band of the elect" (p. 32).

Ngũgĩ describes children punished for speaking African languages — "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'" (p. 33), with pieces of paper from the wastepaper basket stuffed in their mouths. "Humiliation in relation to our languages was the key" (p. 33).

The reading then extends the argument beyond Africa. Citing Lyn Marierid in the Welsh Language Society (*Morning Star*, 21 Oct 1988), Ngũgĩ shows that Welsh is being eroded by capitalist "yuppiefication": "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (p. 36). Equally, the Russians of Novosibirsk learn English because, in Kenneth Baker's words quoted from the *Evening Standard*, "they want to get away from the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (p. 35) — i.e. English carries capitalist modernity.

The consequences in postcolonial Africa: English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and education (p. 37). Yet only a minority speak them, so the working majority "are excluded from centre stage" and from modern scientific knowledge, which is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (p. 37). The elite is "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (p. 38). No African-origin language sits among the official languages of the UN (p. 38).

Ngũgĩ is not against a common world language. He insists that the languages of the world are "our common heritage. A world of many languages should be like a field of flowers of different colours" (p. 39). He demands "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" as the precondition (p. 39). He concludes by proposing Kiswahili — "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (p. 41) — as a candidate world language.

### 3.2 *Gender and Personal Pronouns: She, He, It and They* (Halewood, 2021)

The chapter opens by setting out the sex/gender distinction in sociology, then immediately complicates it. Mead's anthropology shows that gender roles vary cross-culturally, hence they are social. But Halewood, following de Beauvoir and Irigaray, argues that the easy split — biology over here, society over there — itself "bifurcates" language and world.

Fowler's claim that gender "is a grammatical term only… of the male or female sex is either a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1937 [1926], p. 211) is presented as a starting point and then dismantled. If categories really were arbitrary, why not "up, down, middle" instead of "masculine, feminine, neuter"? The specific words carry meaning.

**De Beauvoir's pivot.** "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15).

**Irigaray's grammatical examples.** In French *il* covers "he", impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pluie*), and any unknown subject. A male dog *le chien* is default; *la chienne* requires extra knowledge. The third-person plural *ils* is used for any mixed group, however small the male presence. The Czech example from Tichý — "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses the feminine plural *šly*, but as soon as John joins, the verb flips to masculine *šli* — shows the asymmetry at the level of verbs too. Irigaray: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993, p. 20).

**Gendered English.** Halewood quotes Dewey writing "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his" and Whitehead's "men of science" to show that even Anglophone academic writing carried male defaults. Fowler had wanted *doctress*, *teacheress*, *singeress*, *danceress* — multiplying female forms. Marsh (production editor, *Guardian*) goes the other way: abolish *actress*, use only *actor*.

**Job titles and chairs.** "Chair" derives from Latin *cathedra* — the bishop's seat in a cathedral, and the historical "see" (*sedes*). Professors hold "chairs"; the office carries an authority that Halewood reads as historically male/masculine, tied to the Church. So even apparently neutral words like "chair" are not neutral.

**Pronouns, trans rights, prescriptivism.** Singular "they" is grammatically contested but has a long literary history (Lewis Carroll, Shakespeare, the King James Bible, Thackeray, all cited via Marsh, 2014). Trans writers including Stephen Whittle (2006) argue that "he/she" is exclusionary; some prefer "they" or neologisms (e.g. "per"). The chapter is sympathetic but warns that "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126).

**Haraway's caution.** "All dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991, p. 173, cited p. 130).

The conclusion sides with a *processual* reading: man, woman, trans are *outcomes* of intertwined material and linguistic processes, not pre-given essences.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The thread that holds these readings together is the slogan from the lecture: **"Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world"** (Lecture 4 slide 4). Each thinker tugs on a different end of that rope.

Sapir–Whorf gives us the *epistemic* lever. If "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968, p. 230), then a language is never an innocent code; it is a settlement about what counts. The weak form (language influences thought) is now broadly accepted in cognitive linguistics; the strong form (linguistic determinism) — "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968, p. 231) — is harder to defend, because speakers can stretch their language with phrases, neologisms and translation. The deeper problem the lecturer flags is not whether the strong claim is true, but that the very *example* (Eskimo words for snow) is constructed from a Western "us" looking at an indigenous "them". The classification is already evaluative; the example reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

Spender shows the same asymmetry inside English itself. Her core claim is structural: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145). Historical fragments back this up — Mr Wilson (1553) prescribing the order "male and female" (Spender, p. 147), John Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, p. 148). The generic *he*, the word *mankind*, *chairman*, *manpower*, the older NHS form that asked for a "Mrs / Miss" but only "Mr" for a man — these are the sediment of that prescriptive history.

De Beauvoir radicalises Spender. Her insight is phenomenological: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). The male is the unmarked term in language *because* he is the unmarked term in social ontology. He occupies "both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). This is why I find debates about whether to use *actor* or *actress* irresolvable in purely linguistic terms: tidying the words leaves the social default — the masculine yardstick — untouched.

Irigaray takes this further. "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993, p. 20). The Czech example — *šly* flipping to *šli* as soon as one man joins all the women of the world — is grammatically tiny but ontologically loud: it tells us who counts as the default human.

Ngũgĩ pulls the analysis out to the imperial scale. Language is a "carrier of the history and the culture built into the process of communication over time" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 30). When the encounter is "oppressor and oppressed", language is added to "the Bible and the sword" (p. 31). Suppression plus elevation — children punished with plaques reading "I am stupid" (p. 33), then drilled in "Oxford Readers for Africa" about John and Joan visiting the Thames (p. 34) — produced what Ngũgĩ calls "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (p. 37). His sharpest line is that "the death of many languages should never be the condition for the life of a few" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 39). The point sits squarely on the Sapir–Whorf hinge: kill the language, kill the world that language carries.

Achebe complicates the colonial story. Refusing to abandon English does not equal submission. The African writer should "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). Can the African write English like a native? "I hope not" (Achebe, *Transitions*, p. 347). The world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, *Transitions*, p. 347). This is the position I find most pragmatically defensible: not Ngũgĩ's purism, not the colonial monolith, but a politics of *re-fashioning* the inherited tool.

Across all five thinkers the same lesson appears: language **classifies and evaluates**, and to classify is to distribute power. Sociologists who want to *address* the inequalities Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ and Achebe describe cannot just police vocabulary — Halewood is right that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126) — but they cannot ignore vocabulary either, because, as the lecture insists, problems with inequality in language *are* problems in the world.

## 7. Essay answers

### Essay 1 — 2024 (Q3): *Discuss the links between language and inequality. How can sociologists help to address the problem?*

I want to argue two things. First, language and inequality are linked in three overlapping ways — *classification*, *evaluation*, and *erasure* — and the lecture's slogan that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4) captures this neatly. Second, sociologists can help, but only if they treat language as embedded in the world rather than as a separable surface that can be cleaned up by vocabulary reform alone.

**Language classifies.** This is the core insight of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis as the lecture presents it: speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968, p. 230). Every language carves the world into categories — "doctor" and "patient", "citizen" and "alien", "man" and "woman" — and those categories are unequal in their consequences. Dale Spender shows that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145). The generic *he*, the older default *mankind*, the ladder of titles *Mr / Mrs / Miss*, all encode an asymmetry: the man is the default human, the woman a variation. Medical language is a powerful example: gender-based diagnostic bias means that women presenting with heart attacks were for decades misclassified as anxious because the "textbook" patient was male. The classification scheme is unequal before any individual clinician makes a choice.

**Language evaluates.** Classification almost always smuggles in a hierarchy. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argues that English entered Africa as the language of "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31), at once a label and a verdict. "Eskimo" — the lecture's recurring example — is not just inaccurate; it expresses a relation of power between the namer and the named. The computer science vocabulary of *master* and *slave* drives, or *blacklist* and *whitelist*, evaluates as it classifies. In law, the figure of the "reasonable man" — only re-named "reasonable person" recently — built the male perspective into the very test of negligence.

**Language erases.** Some realities are made unspeakable. Simone de Beauvoir's complaint that "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15) is precisely this: the male perspective passes for the universal, so its particularity disappears from view. Trans and non-binary speakers report a similar erasure in pronoun systems that admit only *he* or *she*. Ngũgĩ describes a more brutal erasure: African children in colonial schools were punished with "plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 33) for speaking their first languages. The Welsh and Gaelic revivals are responses to the same pattern of internal colonial erasure.

**So what can sociologists do?** First, *describe* the patterns with care. Spender's archival work on grammarians from Wilson (1553) to Kirkby (1746) shows that the "man-made" character of English is not a vague impression but a documentable history of prescription. Sociologists can do similar archaeology for medical, legal and technical vocabularies — tracing, say, how DSM categories changed, or how social-media content-moderation algorithms perform much worse on non-English languages, generating new digital inequalities.

Second, *connect* language work to material consequences. De Beauvoir and Irigaray both insist that words alone are not the whole problem. Halewood puts it bluntly: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126). The point of pronoun debates, English-only education policy in former colonies, or campaigns to retire master/slave terminology is not the words as such but the social distributions the words sustain.

Third, sociologists can *amplify counter-voices*. Achebe's strategy of Africanising English — "fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347) — is a model: rather than purifying the language, you refashion it for new uses. Sociologists studying Welsh-medium education, dual-language radio in postcolonial states, or the digital revival of Gaelic show that languages can be repaired and extended.

Fourth, sociologists should be *self-critical* about jargon. The discipline's own language can naturalise inequality (the smooth use of "race" as a variable, "informal economy" as a residue, "third world" as a destination). I think we should follow Haraway's warning that "all dreams for a perfectly true language… are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991, p. 173) — i.e. we should not assume our vocabulary is a neutral grid.

So: language and inequality are linked through classification, evaluation and erasure. Sociologists help when they document the histories, link words to material distributions, support communities refashioning their own languages, and police their own vocabulary as carefully as everyone else's. They fail when they treat language reform as a substitute for redistribution.

### Essay 2 — 2025 (Q4): *"Language, power and modernity are all interrelated." Critically analyse this statement.*

I agree with the statement, but only when "modernity" is read in the specific sense the SC301 module has been building: modernity as a *colonial* project that travelled with particular European languages and that re-described the rest of the world in its own vocabulary. Read that way, language is not an add-on to modernity but one of its load-bearing technologies.

**Power needs a vocabulary.** The lecture's basic move is that language is "not neutral"; it classifies and evaluates and is therefore "a mode of power" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Spender catches this at the level of grammar: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145). De Beauvoir catches it at the level of social ontology: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). The point is the same: power needs a default subject, and the default subject is installed in the words before any explicit argument is made.

**Modernity travels in particular languages.** Ngũgĩ's reading is decisive here. The languages of European modernity — English, French, Portuguese — did not arrive in the Third World "under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31). They came alongside "the Bible and the sword" (p. 31). The mission school, the colonial bureaucracy, the textbook industry ("Oxford Readers for Africa", p. 34) and the Christian hymn ("Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", p. 32) all bundled together a single message: modernity is European, and European is English (or French, or Portuguese). Children caught speaking their own languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 33). The vocabulary of "progress" was so tightly coupled with English that, decades after independence, Kenneth Baker could report Russians in Novosibirsk learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, p. 35). Capitalism is "modern"; everything else is "old-fashioned" — and English is the bridge.

**Power and modernity are inscribed in everyday English.** I find the Halewood chapter useful because it shows that the same logic operates inside the English-speaking core, not just at the imperial periphery. The word "chair", from Latin *cathedra*, encodes the authority of bishops, then of professors, then of committee heads (Halewood, 2021, pp. 128–130). The chairs were literally Church furniture; the office of professor was modelled on that of priest. Replacing "chairman" with "chair" feels modern but preserves a deeper masculine-clerical authority. This is what the lecture means by language *carrying* a history that modernity papers over.

**Two complications.** First, modernity has produced *democratic* vocabularies too. Ngũgĩ is careful to say that English has "a democratic tradition" — Shakespeare, Milton, Blake, Dickens — and is "part of the common heritage of humankind" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 38). The relation between language and power is therefore contested *within* every modern language, not just between languages. Second, Achebe argues that the colonised can refashion the coloniser's language: the world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). Modernity's linguistic infrastructure is more porous than Ngũgĩ sometimes suggests. The rise of "Singlish", Indian English, Caribbean creoles, and Afro-American Vernacular English shows speakers using the modern language to assert non-Anglo identities.

**Examples that pull the three concepts together.**
- *English-only education in former colonies.* Postcolonial governments retaining English as the language of instruction reproduce Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison" (p. 37): scientific and legal knowledge stays accessible only to an Anglophone elite. The modern state inherits the colonial school.
- *Trans rights and pronoun debates.* These are arguments about who counts as a modern subject of rights. The grammatical question is inseparable from the political one.
- *Social-media moderation.* Platforms that moderate well in English and poorly in Burmese or Amharic export the colonial language hierarchy into the digital sphere — a 21st-century instance of Ngũgĩ's complaint that African languages occupy "no place of honour" in international institutions (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 38).
- *Legal "reasonable man" tests* and *medical diagnostic categories*: modernity's flagship institutions — law and medicine — encode male defaults at the level of vocabulary.

**Conclusion.** Language, power and modernity are interrelated because modernity is the historical settlement in which certain European languages became the default vocabulary of progress, science and citizenship. The strong version of this claim risks essentialising European languages as inherently oppressive — Achebe rightly resists that. The weak version, that "the languages of Europe… were taught as if they were our own languages" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 35), is the version I find most useful, and it is enough to vindicate the exam quotation.

### Essay 3 — 2023 (Q4): *Identify and explain how language is tied up with wider questions of power.*

I want to identify four ways language is tied up with power: it (1) classifies who counts; (2) evaluates as it classifies; (3) enforces those evaluations through institutions; and (4) carries the historical sediment of past power even after explicit reform. I will work through each, drawing on Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ, Achebe and the Halewood chapter.

**(1) Classification — who counts as the default.** Spender's claim that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145) is the cleanest statement of this. The generic *he*, the older *mankind*, the asymmetry between *actor* and *actress* — these are not stylistic quirks but classification rules that install a default subject. De Beauvoir generalises: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). The same logic operates in legal English. The "reasonable man" test in tort law set the standard of due care by reference to a masculine ideal type; renaming him "reasonable person" preserved the underlying male yardstick.

**(2) Evaluation — classification is never neutral.** Calling something "primitive", "tribal", "developing" or "indigenous" classifies *and* ranks. Ngũgĩ shows how the colonial school treated African languages as "incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31). The vocabulary itself encoded the verdict: English was light, the African languages were "gloom" (p. 32). The lecturer's caution about the word "Eskimo" makes the same point: the label is not just inaccurate but evaluative — it positions the named group as the curious other against an implicit Western "us".

**(3) Enforcement — institutions police language.** Language is power because someone enforces the vocabulary. Spender notes that the rules were laid down by named grammarians: Wilson in 1553 insisting on "male and female, husband and wife", Kirkby in 1746 ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, pp. 147–148). Ngũgĩ describes the school as the key colonial institution: children were beaten and humiliated for speaking their own languages (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 33). Modern equivalents include English-only requirements in some Anglophone schools for migrant pupils, language tests embedded in citizenship law, and social-media moderation regimes that under-resource non-English languages and so police speech unequally.

**(4) Sedimentation — old power lingers in vocabulary.** Halewood's example of "chair" — from Latin *cathedra*, via the bishop's seat — shows that even apparently neutral terms carry sedimented authority (Halewood, 2021, pp. 128–130). The professor's chair inherits the bishop's chair; the committee chair inherits the professor's; the masculine authority is preserved beneath a neutral surface. The same applies to *master/slave* in computer science, *husbandry* in agriculture, *manhole* in civil engineering, *manning* a station in workplace English. Renaming may help, but the history continues to operate.

**Counter-currents.** Power is not absolute. Achebe shows that the dominated can refashion the dominant tongue: the African writer must use English to "carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347), and indeed should *not* learn to use English like a native ("I hope not", p. 347). Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish revivals are explicit refusals of internal linguistic colonisation. Trans-led pronoun campaigns push English to recognise non-binary subjects, partly through the singular *they* — a usage Marsh (2014) shows has a long literary pedigree from Shakespeare and Lewis Carroll onwards. These counter-currents show that language is contested terrain, not a one-way street.

**The limit case: tinkering versus changing.** It is tempting to think that reforming vocabulary fixes the underlying inequalities. The lecture is sceptical. Marsh's ban on "actress" makes Hattie Jacques comparable to Kenneth Williams as well as to Joan Sims (Halewood, 2021, p. 120) — but, the lecturer warns, "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood draws the broader conclusion: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126).

**Conclusion.** Language is tied up with power in four ways — classification, evaluation, enforcement and sedimentation — and these operate from intimate pronouns (he/she/they) right up to the global hierarchy of UN-recognised languages. The political work language does is uneven and contested, and Achebe's strategy of refashioning is as important to the picture as Ngũgĩ's critique of colonial imposition. The sociologist's job is to make the link between vocabulary and distribution visible without pretending that fixing vocabulary by itself fixes distribution.

### Essay 4 — 2022 (Q4): *Can language create inequality?*

My answer is: language does not create inequality from scratch, but it sustains, amplifies and naturalises inequalities that already have material roots — and in that sense it does much of the *work* of inequality. I will defend that claim across three registers: gender, colonial domination, and contemporary technological power.

**The misleading version of the question.** The question, taken literally, can lead to a bad answer: "language creates inequality" reads as if changing words alone would equalise the world. The lecture warns against this: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126; cf. Lecture 4 slide 17 on actor/actress). Material inequalities — wages, land, citizenship — have material causes, not merely linguistic ones. But that does not mean language is innocent.

**Language sustains gender inequality.** Spender's claim that the structure of English is built on "the male-as-norm" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145) is best read as a sustaining-and-amplifying claim rather than a creating-from-nothing claim. Patriarchy did not begin with English grammar; but English grammar makes male centrality feel natural, so that "we may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, p. 145). De Beauvoir's "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15) makes the same point at the level of the writing subject. The Czech example from Tichý — one man joining all the women of the world flips the verb to masculine *šli* — shows the asymmetry at the level of grammar, not just lexis. Medical language is the place where this stops being a metaphor: the historical absence of female bodies from clinical trial protocols, plus textbook language built around the male body, fed directly into gender-based diagnostic bias. Language does not *invent* the inequality, but it carries it from one institutional setting to the next.

**Language sustains colonial inequality.** Ngũgĩ is uncompromising here. The colonial encounter "was not the encounter between English and most so-called Third World languages… under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31). English, French and Portuguese came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (p. 31). Colonial schools then "elevated the language of the conqueror" (p. 32) by punishing children for using their own. Sixty years after independence, English remains the language of administration, justice and higher education in many African states, which is why Ngũgĩ describes the elite as "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 38). Did English *create* the inequality between Britain and Kenya? No — the inequality is rooted in extraction, conquest and the Berlin Conference. But English language policy keeps it in place after the soldiers have left. That is a real form of creation: language *creates the continuity* of a colonial inequality whose original cause has lapsed.

**Language sustains contemporary technological inequality.** A more recent example. Social-media moderation works much better in English than in, say, Burmese, Tigrinya or Amharic. In the Rohingya crisis, Facebook's failure to moderate hate speech in Burmese contributed directly to incitement to ethnic cleansing. Here, language inequality is not an emanation of colonial schooling; it is the result of corporate decisions about which languages get investment. The result, however, mirrors the colonial pattern: English speakers get a relatively safer platform; non-English speakers do not. Computer-science vocabulary itself — *master/slave*, *blacklist/whitelist* — is being slowly reformed under the same logic.

**Counter-evidence.** There are limits to the claim. Languages get refashioned by their speakers: Achebe insists that English can be "made to carry [the African writer's] peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). The singular *they* has expanded from its older literary use into a tool for non-binary recognition. Welsh and Gaelic are being revived through deliberate language policy. So language does not lock inequality in place; it is contested terrain.

**A subtler creation effect.** I want to defend a *stronger* version of the "language creates inequality" claim in one specific area: when language creates new *categories*, it can create new inequalities. The category "illegal immigrant" — a relatively recent term — does not just describe a pre-existing inequality; it constitutes the migrant as a particular kind of subject with a particular set of legal exposures. The category "informal sector" did similar work in development economics, marking certain types of work as residual and therefore not eligible for the same protections. Here Sapir–Whorf is genuinely useful: the way the language carves the world *partly* shapes which inequalities become administratively and politically possible.

**Conclusion.** Language can create inequality in three precise senses: it can install a default subject (male) so the asymmetry becomes invisible; it can sustain colonial-era inequalities long after their original cause; and it can constitute new categories that produce new inequalities. But it cannot create inequality unaided — the material world is the other half of every story. The most defensible position, with Halewood, is that "language and the world become together" (Halewood, 2021, p. 132), and so do the inequalities each carries.

### Essay 5 — 2021 (Q4): *"Inequalities in language represent inequalities in the world." Critically explain this statement.*

The statement comes almost verbatim from the lecture: "Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). I think it is largely correct, but the word *represent* is doing a lot of work and needs unpacking. Inequalities in language do three different things at once: they *reflect* prior inequalities in the world; they *reproduce* those inequalities by carrying them across time; and in some cases they *constitute* the inequalities they appear merely to describe. I will defend that three-layer reading.

**Reflection.** The simplest reading of "represent" is that language mirrors social hierarchies that exist independently. Spender's history of English grammar offers good evidence. Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 147) and Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" (Spender, p. 148) did not invent the patriarchal society; they *reflected* it. Similarly, the prestige of English in former colonies *reflects* the geopolitical dominance of Britain and the US. Ngũgĩ's contemporary observation that English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and commerce in postcolonial Africa (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 37) is a description of how linguistic prestige reflects political-economic power.

**Reproduction.** Reflection alone is too weak. Language does not just mirror; it *carries* inequalities forward when their original cause has lapsed. This is the bite of Ngũgĩ's argument. By the time he is writing (1993), formal colonialism in Kenya has ended, but the colonial language continues to gatekeep education, science and the law. Knowledge is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 37). De Beauvoir makes the same point for gender: long after legal reform has formally equalised men and women, the unmarked male subject persists in everyday speech — "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). Halewood's "chair" example is the same logic: even as universities formally open chairs to women, the word *chair* carries forward the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's *cathedra* (Halewood, 2021, pp. 128–130). Language reproduces inequalities by making them feel natural and durable.

**Constitution.** The strongest version of the claim is that some inequalities are partly *constituted* in and through language. The Sapir–Whorf insight, in its weak and defensible form, is that the categories a language makes available shape what people can do with the world. Spender's claim is precisely that the category scheme "plus male / minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145) is itself part of how patriarchy is experienced and reproduced. Irigaray pushes further: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993, p. 20). The case of generic *he* is a clean example. Psycholinguistic research has repeatedly shown that readers who encounter *he* in supposedly generic contexts disproportionately picture a man, with downstream effects on, say, who applies for a job advertised with masculine pronouns. Here language is not just reflecting an inequality but doing some of the work that produces it.

**Critical limits.** Three caveats matter.
1. *Language is not the whole story.* Halewood is firm: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126). If we treat language reform as a substitute for material change, we let inequality off the hook.
2. *Counter-currents exist.* Achebe shows that subordinated speakers refashion the dominant language: "fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). Pidgins, creoles, AAVE, Indian English and Singlish are all evidence that linguistic inequality is contested, not simply imposed. The singular *they* and trans neologisms are the same dynamic in gender.
3. *Representation is not always faithful.* Some linguistic inequalities exaggerate or distort the social patterns they purport to represent. The myth of "Eskimo words for snow" — flagged by the lecturer with its "us / them" framing (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8) — represents indigenous people as exotic *others* in a way that the social facts do not support. So a language can misrepresent as well as reflect.

**Concrete examples that bind it together.**
- *Pronoun debates and trans rights*: a place where reflection, reproduction and constitution all overlap.
- *Medical language and gender-based diagnostic bias*: language reproduces the male-bodied default of nineteenth-century medicine into twenty-first century clinics.
- *English-only education in former colonies, and Welsh/Gaelic revival*: showing that linguistic inequality can be reversed by deliberate policy.
- *Computer science master/slave terminology*: a vocabulary inequality that survived the abolition of slavery by a century and a half.

**Conclusion.** The statement is correct in its strong reading: inequalities in language do represent inequalities in the world, where *represent* means simultaneously to reflect, to reproduce and partly to constitute. The danger lies in collapsing the three readings into one. Sociology's job, as Spender, Ngũgĩ, de Beauvoir, Achebe and Halewood all insist in different keys, is to keep them apart while showing how they hang together — and to refuse either the complacent answer that language is "just words" or the over-simple answer that fixing the words fixes the world.

### Essay 6 — Mock Q1: *To what extent does the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis still illuminate the relationship between language and power?*

I will argue that the *weak* version of Sapir–Whorf — linguistic relativity — still illuminates the relation between language and power, but the *strong* version — linguistic determinism — does not. The weak version helps us see how vocabularies route attention, freeze categories and naturalise hierarchies. The strong version overreaches and, ironically, reproduces the colonial "us/them" framing that the lecture is at pains to dismantle.

**The hypothesis, briefly.** The lecture presents Sapir–Whorf in two forms. Linguistic relativity: language "embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968, p. 230); "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968, p. 230). Linguistic determinism: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968, p. 231).

**Why the strong version fails.** Three reasons. First, translation is possible. If language fully determined thought, we could not translate at all; in fact we translate constantly, with friction but with success. Second, language is plastic: English speakers who lack a single-word lexicon for "wet snow" still build the concept out of phrases — "good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing" — as the lecturer notes (Lecture 4 slide 9). Third, the strong version traps us in a Romantic othering. The "Eskimo words for snow" example, taken straight, becomes "they live in a very different world from 'us'" (Lecture 4 slide 8) — exactly the colonial "us/them" framing that Modernity-as-Colonial-Project criticises. Ngũgĩ would find this familiar: the Western outlook is the unmarked benchmark, the indigenous outlook is the marked exotic.

**Why the weak version still helps.** Even if language does not *determine* thought, it powerfully *channels* it. Three illustrations.

1. *Gender pronouns and generic he*. Spender's claim that English carves humanity into "plus male" and "minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145) is exactly a relativity claim: the vocabulary makes certain experiences (the unmarked male subject) easy to express and certain others (a non-marked human subject who is not by default male) harder. Empirical psycholinguistic work supports this — readers do not parse "generic" *he* as truly generic.

2. *Colonial categorisation*. Ngũgĩ shows that mission schools issued African children "new mirrors" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 32). The English vocabulary made certain world-views easy to articulate (the Christian, modern, Anglo-European one) and others — "the world and the history carried by their original languages" — hard to articulate. This is Sapir–Whorf relativity working at the scale of empire.

3. *Trans pronouns and non-binary recognition*. English pronouns *he/she* make binary gender easy to express and non-binary gender hard. The singular *they* — long-attested in Shakespeare and the King James Bible (Halewood, 2021, p. 128) — has been revived precisely because the existing vocabulary channelled thought unequally. Language is not destiny here, but it shapes the friction.

**Where Sapir–Whorf intersects with power.** This is the point of the lecture's bridge from Sapir–Whorf to Spender and Ngũgĩ. Once you accept that language is not a neutral mirror but a categorising instrument, the question becomes: *whose* categories? Spender's answer for English grammar is: male grammarians from Wilson (1553) to Kirkby (1746). Ngũgĩ's answer for the global vocabulary of modernity is: European colonisers, missionaries and Lugard-style administrators. The Sapir–Whorf framing makes the question *who set the categories* feel urgent rather than merely academic, because the categories shape what speakers can easily say.

**Three contemporary applications.**

- *Legal language*: the "reasonable man" test in tort law channels juries toward a male prototype even when rebranded "reasonable person".
- *Medical diagnostic vocabulary*: textbook descriptions of heart attacks ("crushing central chest pain") were built on male case histories; women's atypical presentations were under-recognised, with measurable mortality consequences. Language did not cause the deaths, but it routed clinical attention.
- *Social-media moderation*: platforms that index hate speech using English-language lexicons miss non-English equivalents; this is Sapir–Whorf at the level of code, not just speech.

**Critical caveats.**

- *Speakers are creative.* Achebe's argument that the African writer can fashion an English "able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347) shows that linguistic relativity is not a one-way prison. Speakers refashion their vocabularies under pressure.
- *Material conditions matter independently.* Halewood: "Tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126). Sapir–Whorf can become an alibi for vocabulary politics that ignores distribution.
- *The hypothesis is not a sociology in itself.* It tells us that language and thought are entangled. It does not tell us *which* entanglements matter most. For that we need Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ and the rest.

**Conclusion.** The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis still illuminates the relationship between language and power *in its weak form*, because it identifies the basic mechanism by which vocabularies channel attention, freeze categories and naturalise hierarchies. Its strong form, however, both overreaches empirically and risks reproducing the colonial us/them framing the lecture criticises. The honest verdict is that linguistic relativity is a useful *first* move in any sociological analysis of language and power, but it must be cashed out by historical and political work — which is what Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ and Achebe do.

### Essay 7 — Mock Q5: *Compare Ngũgĩ's and Achebe's positions on whether African writers should write in English.*

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o and Chinua Achebe agree on the diagnosis — English entered Africa as a colonial weapon — and disagree on the prescription. I will argue that the disagreement is real, but smaller than it is sometimes made out to be, and that Achebe's pragmatic refashioning and Ngũgĩ's principled refusal are best read as complementary strategies addressing different audiences.

**Shared diagnosis.** Both writers accept that English in Africa is not a neutral medium. Ngũgĩ states it most starkly: "English, French, and Portuguese came to the Third World to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31). Mission schools elevated the conqueror's language, suppressed African languages, and ordained an elite cut off from its own peoples — "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 38). Achebe does not dispute any of this. He accepts that he is writing in the colonial tongue, and concedes "it is not right for anyone to abandon their first language but he has no choice" (paraphrased from Lecture 4 slide 23; cf. Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997).

**The disagreement: what to do.** Ngũgĩ's response is to *abandon* English (and French) for creative writing, returning to Gĩkũyũ. He proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 41). The case rests on a *carrier* theory of language: language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, p. 30), and writing in English carries English memory, not Gĩkũyũ memory.

Achebe accepts that English is the language of empire but argues that the writer who reaches only Hausa, Igbo or Yoruba speakers reaches a small slice of his potential audience. The African writer should therefore "use English in a way that brings out his message best without altering the language so much that its value as a medium of international exchange will be lost. He should aim at fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). And — crucially — he should not aspire to native-speaker English: "Can he ever learn to use it like a native speaker? I should say, 'I hope not.' It is neither necessary nor desirable for him to be able to do so. The price a world language must be prepared to pay is submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, *Transitions*, p. 347).

**Comparing the two.** Three contrasts and one convergence.

1. *Purity vs hybridity.* Ngũgĩ's strategy is purity-oriented: leave the contaminated language behind. Achebe's strategy is hybridity-oriented: refashion the contaminated language for new uses. Both strategies presuppose that the language carries political baggage; they differ on whether that baggage can be repackaged.

2. *Audience.* Ngũgĩ writes for the rural Gĩkũyũ-speaking masses, hence his project to write novels and plays in Gĩkũyũ. Achebe writes for a continental and global Anglophone audience, including the African elite and Western readers. Each strategy fits its audience.

3. *Theory of change.* Ngũgĩ believes meaningful decolonisation requires linguistic decolonisation — what he calls "decolonising the mind". Achebe believes meaningful decolonisation can proceed *through* a refashioned English; the empire's tools, properly retooled, can serve the colonised.

4. *Convergence.* Both insist that the encounter between African languages and English was unequal and unjust. Neither accepts the view that English is innocently universal. Achebe's "I hope not" is a refusal of cultural assimilation as fierce in spirit as Ngũgĩ's refusal of the language itself.

**Concrete illustrations.**

- *Things Fall Apart* (1958) — the title itself quotes W.B. Yeats, an Irish (post-colonial) poet, and the prose embeds Igbo proverbs into English syntax. The novel performs Achebe's argument: English bends to carry Igbo memory.
- Ngũgĩ's *Devil on the Cross* (1980), written in Gĩkũyũ on toilet paper in detention, performs his counter-argument: the same political content carried by a different language carries different political weight.
- The institutional setting matters. In primary and adult literacy work, Ngũgĩ's position is overwhelmingly more useful: people learn faster and engage politically more deeply in their first language. In high-prestige international publishing, Achebe's position is more practical.

**My evaluation.** I think the disagreement is less stark than it looks, for three reasons.

First, Ngũgĩ himself accepts that some common language for cross-national communication is desirable; he just thinks it should not be a coloniser's language. Achebe accepts that English does need to be "submitted to many different kinds of use" — i.e. de-anglicised at the level of style and idiom.

Second, both authors are writing into a world in which the *option* of pure linguistic separation is largely unavailable. Educated African readers cross between Wolof, Yoruba, Swahili, English, French and Portuguese in daily life. A purist line is hard to maintain.

Third, the strongest synthesis is *both/and*. Ngũgĩ's strategy protects African languages from extinction; Achebe's strategy expands what English can carry. Together they shrink the colonial monopoly on prestige.

**Conclusion.** Ngũgĩ and Achebe agree that the colonial encounter twisted the relation between African languages and English. They disagree on whether the writer's job is to refuse the coloniser's language or to refashion it. I take the disagreement to mark a productive division of labour rather than a contradiction, and I would expect a serious cultural-political programme to need both.

### Essay 8 — Mock Q3: *"Woman is the Other." Critically evaluate Simone de Beauvoir's account of how language positions men as neutral and women as marked.*

I will defend de Beauvoir's account as essentially correct for the languages it analyses, while flagging two limits: it overgeneralises from European languages, and it can pull toward an essentialism it elsewhere resists.

**The claim.** De Beauvoir's argument is most famously condensed in two lines from the Introduction to *The Second Sex*: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). And: "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex; it goes without saying that he is a man… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). The man is the *unmarked* term, the default human; the woman is the *marked* term, the deviation.

**How language encodes this.** Halewood draws out the grammatical mechanics. In French, *il* covers male persons, male dogs, and the impersonal *it* ("*il pluie*" — it is raining). The mixed plural is always masculine *ils*; no word exists in standard French for "they, mixed group". In Czech, Tichý's verb example shows the rule operating in conjugation: "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses feminine *šly*, but adding one man (John) flips the verb to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021, p. 116). The masculine is at once *one* gender and *the* default. English does this more quietly: the generic *he*, the older default *mankind*, the asymmetry between *actor* and *actress*, the residual *Mr* vs *Mrs/Miss*. Spender's archive — Wilson (1553) prescribing "male and female", Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" — shows the rule is not natural but historically prescribed (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, pp. 147–148).

**Why I find the account compelling.** Three reasons.

1. *It predicts experience.* The asymmetry de Beauvoir describes shows up wherever the male is treated as the default human subject: medical research that defaults to male bodies; legal language that defaults to the "reasonable man"; computer science that defaults to a generic *user* whose use cases were modelled on male engineers; recruitment language whose masculine-coded adjectives ("driven", "competitive") track gendered application rates.

2. *It explains the resistance to apparently neutral fixes.* If "actor" is the default, then renaming actresses "actors" leaves the masculine yardstick intact, as the lecture warns (Lecture 4 slide 17). De Beauvoir's framework predicts that gender-neutral language can re-mask rather than dismantle the asymmetry — exactly Irigaray's later worry.

3. *It connects to material inequality.* The unmarked subject in language often coincides with the unmarked subject in policy. Pension systems modelled on uninterrupted male careers, parental-leave defaults that pay primary carers (i.e. women) less, and crash-test dummies modelled on male bodies all share the same default-male logic that de Beauvoir identified in pronouns.

**Two limits.**

*First, it overgeneralises from European languages.* De Beauvoir's evidence is French. Halewood's chapter widens the example to Czech. But many languages organise gender differently. Mandarin spoken Chinese has no gendered third-person pronouns (the written *他/她/它* distinction is a twentieth-century borrowing). Turkish and Persian have a single unmarked third-person pronoun (*o*). Yoruba does not gender pronouns. The de Beauvoir/Irigaray analysis works powerfully *where* grammatical gender is built in, less well where it is not. This does not refute the account — gender hierarchies exist in those societies too — but it means the *linguistic* mechanism cannot be the universal one.

*Second, the framework can drift toward essentialism.* De Beauvoir herself is careful: "When I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 31, emphasis in original). Lived experience, not biology, is the centre of gravity. But the marked/unmarked frame can slide into "woman = the other in general", which Halewood and Whittle (2006) both critique as exclusionary of trans, intersex and non-binary subjects. The risk is that you fix "woman" as the marked term in a way that re-marginalises everyone whose identity is not captured by the binary at all.

**Extensions.**

- *Pronoun debates and trans rights.* The marked/unmarked logic helps explain why trans speakers experience pronouns as politically charged: *he/she* enforces a binary that mirrors the patriarchal default, and *they* is one way of refusing the asymmetry.
- *Workplace English.* "Manager", "chairman", "fireman" — all male-coded defaults that produce concrete patterns of who applies, who is hired, and who gets promoted. De Beauvoir gives the theoretical reading; Spender supplies the historical one.
- *Welsh/Gaelic revival and minority subject positions.* The marked/unmarked frame travels: linguistic minorities are *marked* in the way women are marked. The defaults of the dominant language enforce a default of personhood.

**Conclusion.** "Woman is the Other" is a powerful and broadly correct account of how grammatical and lexical gender position the male as neutral and the female as a derivative. Its limits are real but local: the mechanism is most visible in languages with built-in grammatical gender, and the framework needs updating to accommodate trans and non-binary subjects whom the binary marked/unmarked scheme itself marginalises. With those caveats, de Beauvoir's analysis remains, in my view, the single most useful starting point in this lecture for thinking about language and gender inequality.

### Essay 9 — Mock Q7: *"Tinkering with language alone cannot dismantle inequality." Discuss.*

The quotation is a near-direct paraphrase of Halewood: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126). I agree with the claim, but I want to argue that linguistic reform is also not nothing — it is a necessary but insufficient component of any serious anti-inequality programme.

**Why tinkering alone is insufficient.** Three reasons.

1. *Material inequality has material roots.* The wage gap, residential segregation, citizenship hierarchies and asset distribution are not produced by vocabulary; they are produced by labour markets, planning law, immigration law and inheritance. Renaming "chairman" to "chair" does not redistribute professorial chairs by gender. Ngũgĩ's complaint that the African elite is "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 38) is a complaint about a *political-economic* uprooting that happens to operate through language; renaming the language would not relocate the elite.

2. *Lexical sediment outlasts surface change.* Halewood's "chair" example shows that even when an explicitly male word ("chairman") is replaced with a "neutral" one ("chair"), the underlying authority — bishop's *cathedra*, professor's seat — is preserved (Halewood, 2021, pp. 128–130). The same is true of *actor* replacing *actress*: the masculine yardstick survives the lexical reform, as the lecturer warns (Lecture 4 slide 17). Spender's history of grammarians prescribing male precedence (Wilson 1553, Kirkby 1746) suggests that grammatical patriarchy was *set up* deliberately and cannot be reformed out simply by deleting a few words (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, pp. 147–148).

3. *Apparent neutrality can re-mask asymmetry.* Marsh's ban on "actress" makes the linguistic surface look gender-neutral while the underlying assumption — that the male is the unmarked subject — remains intact. This is the heart of de Beauvoir's worry: "man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). Slapping a neutral label on top does not change the marking; it just hides it.

**Why tinkering still matters.** Equally important is the converse argument: language reform is *necessary*, even if not sufficient.

1. *Language shapes attention and possibility.* The weak Sapir–Whorf claim — language channels thought — is enough to make vocabulary politics consequential. Generic *he* really does affect who readers imagine. Generic *user* in software design really does affect whose use cases get prioritised. Renaming things changes who feels addressed.

2. *Language carries political signals.* Switching computer-science vocabulary from *master/slave* to *primary/replica* or from *blacklist/whitelist* to *blocklist/allowlist* matters not because the underlying servers are now anti-racist but because the change communicates that the field acknowledges its hierarchies. The same is true for honorifics (using *Ms*; the singular *they* for non-binary subjects).

3. *Refusing tinkering hands the field to reaction.* The fight to *avoid* renaming is itself an inequality-preserving move. Public arguments against "political correctness" often defend specific historical asymmetries by claiming they are just words.

**Examples that pull both ways.**

- *Pronoun debates and trans rights.* Insisting on a person's pronouns is the smallest possible linguistic act. It does not address healthcare access, employment discrimination or rates of violence. But it is a precondition for recognition; without it the rest is impossible.
- *English-only education in former colonies.* Reforming the language of instruction (toward Swahili, Yoruba, or local creoles) is necessary for decolonising knowledge. But unless investment in teacher training and textbooks follows, language reform alone deepens inequality by producing graduates with credentials no one will hire.
- *Welsh and Gaelic revival.* The most successful minority-language revivals combine signage, schooling and media. Bilingual signs alone are tinkering; bilingual schooling backed by public funding is structural.
- *Medical language and gender-based diagnostic bias.* Renaming "hysteria" did not by itself fix the under-diagnosis of women's heart attacks; that required redesigning clinical trial protocols, training and research funding. But naming "gender-based diagnostic bias" *as such* was a precondition for redesigning anything.
- *Legal "reasonable man".* Renaming "reasonable person" is symbolic; reforming jury instructions, recruitment and case law is structural. Both are needed.

**A synthesis.** I think Ngũgĩ, Achebe, Spender and de Beauvoir all converge here, though they emphasise different sides. Ngũgĩ wants both a structural change in language policy (replace colonial languages where possible) and a structural change in political economy (independence and equality among nations). Achebe wants a refashioning of English alongside political and cultural autonomy. Spender insists that "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 162), but that linguistic visibility is a precondition for, not a substitute for, structural change. De Beauvoir frames the lived experience of being marked as woman as inseparable from material existence.

**Conclusion.** Tinkering with language alone cannot dismantle inequality, because inequality has material roots that vocabulary alone cannot reach and because lexical reform can re-mask the asymmetries it appears to fix. But language reform is also not optional; vocabularies channel attention, signal recognition and make new political possibilities thinkable. The honest position is that linguistic reform and structural reform are jointly necessary and individually insufficient — and that the sociologist's job is to keep the two yoked together so that neither can be used as an alibi for ignoring the other.

### Essay 10 — Mock Q8: *How do debates over trans rights and personal pronouns illustrate the lecture's claim that language is a "mode of power"?*

Pronoun debates are a textbook illustration of the lecture's claim because they make visible, at the smallest possible linguistic scale, four features the lecture identifies in language more broadly: language *classifies*, *evaluates*, is *prescribed* by institutions, and is *contested* by the speakers it positions.

4. *Schools.* Disputes about teachers using students' chosen pronouns, and parents' rights to override them, foreground the question of who has authority to fix a young person's linguistic identity.

- *Tinkering alone is not enough.* Halewood is explicit: "tinkering with language… is not enough. Pronoun reform without legal recognition, healthcare access and protection from violence is symbolic.

The trans-rights debate is also internally contested over whether *they* is enough, whether new pronouns should be coined, and whether the binary itself should be reformed.

**Conclusion.** Pronoun debates illustrate the lecture's central claim with unusual clarity: at the smallest linguistic scale, we can see classification, evaluation, institutional prescription and speaker contestation all operating simultaneously. The trans-rights case extends de Beauvoir's "Woman is the Other" to a wider set of subject positions — those whose existence the standard inventory does not record. It also vindicates Achebe's intuition that subordinated speakers can refashion the dominant language. The pronoun is not the whole world, but it is one of the places where the lecture's slogan — *problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world* — is most easily verified.

### Essay 11 — Mock Q12: *"The colonial attitude survives in linguistic habits long after political independence." Discuss with reference to at least two thinkers.*

Internal colonial casualties (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish) are produced by the same prestige hierarchy that produces external ones.

3. *Self-description.* The vocabulary of "the developing world", "emerging markets", "fragile states", "global south", "informal sector" is largely produced in English by Anglophone institutions and exported. Even when intended as neutral, these terms carry a comparative measure against an implicit (Western, modern, formal) benchmark. This is the sense in which the colonial attitude survives even in the vocabulary postcolonial writers use to describe their own societies.

**Beyond the African case.** The statement applies more widely.

- *India*: English remains the de facto language of the higher courts, the Supreme Court, and elite higher education; Hindi has not displaced it despite seventy years of independence.
- *Ireland*: Gaelic was officially restored as a national language, but English remains the working language of business and most government. The colonial attitude survives in the linguistic habit, even where the constitution says otherwise.
- *Singapore*: English is the medium of administration. Singlish — a refashioned local English — is precisely the Achebe strategy enacted at the level of a whole population.
- *Algeria*: French remains widespread in elite communication despite political and constitutional emphasis on Arabic.

The masculine yardstick that Spender and de Beauvoir identified survives long after legal equality. Halewood's analysis of "chair" — *cathedra*, bishop's seat — shows that even self-consciously gender-neutral reforms can carry old masculine authority forward (Halewood, 2021, pp. 128–130). The mechanism is the same as the colonial one: a vocabulary outlasts the explicit hierarchies it was built to express.

- *Achebe's refashioning*: subordinated speakers can re-tool the dominant language. AAVE, Caribbean creoles, Indian English, Singlish, Igbo-inflected English in Achebe himself.

**Conclusion.** The colonial attitude survives in linguistic habits long after political independence because language carries — to use Ngũgĩ's phrase — "the history and the culture built into the process of communication over time" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 30). Mission schools, administrative codes and prestige hierarchies installed European languages as the medium of modernity, and that installation has outlasted the soldiers. Ngũgĩ's response is to displace those languages; Achebe's response is to refashion them. Both responses presuppose that the statement is correct: the colonial attitude really does survive in linguistic habit. My own view is that it survives by default and can only be dislodged by deliberate language policy combined with structural reform of the institutions — courts, universities, publishers — that keep European languages prestigious. Until both move together, the colonial habit persists.

### M2: *Discuss Dale Spender's claim that English is a "man-made language", with reference to at least two concrete examples.*

I will defend Spender's claim as essentially correct as a *historical* description of how English came to encode male-as-norm, while arguing that the "man-made" framing slightly understates how the language is continually *re-made* by speakers — including by women, trans people, and refashioning subordinated groups. Across the essay I will work through three concrete examples: the generic *he* and gendered pronouns, the asymmetry of titles (*Mr* / *Mrs* / *Miss*), and the male-coded vocabulary of authority (the "reasonable man", *chairman*, *manpower*).

**Spender's core claim.** Dale Spender's argument in *Man Made Language* (1980) is structural: English is built on a classification scheme that divides humanity "not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145). The male is the default human; the female is a deviation marked by additional morphology (*actress*, *waitress*, *poetess*) or by extra titles (*Mrs* and *Miss* both encode marital status; *Mr* does not). Spender's historical evidence is precise. Mr Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, p. 147). John Kirkby's 1746 "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, p. 148). These are the named human beings who made the rules. The "man-made" label is not metaphorical: it tracks an actual archive of male grammarians prescribing male precedence.

**Example one: the generic *he* and gendered pronouns.** Standard English long held that the third-person singular pronoun for an indeterminate human should be *he* — "every student should bring his book". Spender treats this as the cleanest instance of male-as-norm: the unmarked human is grammatically male. The lecture extends the point with Irigaray's French and Czech examples, but the English case stands on its own. Psycholinguistic studies have repeatedly shown that readers do not parse "generic" *he* as truly generic; they default to picturing a man. This has measurable consequences in job advertisements, scientific writing and legal drafting: women report feeling un-addressed by the masculine default. The contemporary fight over the singular *they* — long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll and the King James Bible (Halewood, 2021, p. 128) — is precisely an attempt to dismantle this male default. That the fight is necessary, more than four centuries after Wilson, is evidence of the depth of Spender's diagnosis.

**Example two: titles and the *Mrs* / *Miss* / *Mr* asymmetry.** Until well into the twentieth century, women's titles in English required them to declare their marital status while men's did not. The older NHS forms required *Mrs* or *Miss* but only *Mr*. *Ms* — introduced precisely to dissolve the asymmetry — was resisted exactly because it interfered with the male-as-norm scheme. Spender's point is that the language was *prescribed* to encode this distinction; it did not arise neutrally. The same is true of *Master* / *Mister* / *Mistress*: the male title moved from a marker of adulthood to an unmarked default while the female equivalent acquired sexual connotations. Spender's slogan applies here: "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 162). *Ms* makes married and unmarried women linguistically equal to men; that is a structural reform, not cosmetic.

**Example three: the male-coded vocabulary of authority.** The "reasonable man" of tort law set the standard of due care by reference to a masculine ideal type; the recent renaming as "reasonable person" preserves the underlying yardstick. *Chairman*, *manpower*, *manning a station*, *manhole*, *husbandry*, *fireman* all encode male-as-default in workplaces. Halewood notes that even apparently neutral *chair* derives from Latin *cathedra* — the bishop's seat — and so carries a male-ecclesiastical authority forward (Halewood, 2021, pp. 128–130). Computer science has its own male-coded sediment: *master* drives, *fork* and *kill* commands, the generic *user* whose use cases were modelled on male engineers. Each of these vocabularies is "man-made" in Spender's sense.

**Counter-argument: the language is also being *re*-made.** Achebe's intuition that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347) applies inside English itself. The singular *they*, the abolition of "actress" (Marsh, 2014), the gender-neutral *flight attendant* and *firefighter*, the move from *master/slave* to *primary/replica* in computing all show that English is plastic. Spender herself is not deterministic: her programme is precisely to *re*-make the language. The "man-made" framing is best read as a diagnosis of the historical archive, not a fatalist verdict.

**Two critical limits.** First, Spender's evidence is anglocentric: her grammarians are English, her examples are English. Languages without grammatical gender (Mandarin, Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Yoruba) cannot be "man-made" in the same morphological sense, though their *lexical* hierarchies often replicate the pattern. Second, the framework can pull toward a binary that re-marginalises trans and non-binary speakers; Halewood's processual reading is the better corrective.

**Conclusion.** Spender's claim that English is a "man-made language" is largely vindicated by the historical archive of male grammarians who *prescribed* male precedence and by the contemporary asymmetries of pronouns, titles and vocabularies of authority. The strongest version of the claim is the structural one: male-as-norm is built into the categorisation scheme, not just the lexicon. Linguistic reform — *Ms*, singular *they*, gender-neutral occupational titles — is necessary but, as Halewood warns, "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126) without structural change in the institutions the vocabulary serves.

### M4: *With reference to Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, explain how colonial education uses language as a "weapon".*

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's central contribution to the SC301 module is the claim that language is not a passive medium of colonial rule but one of its sharpest weapons. In this essay I will reconstruct his argument in three moves — *suppression*, *elevation*, and *uprooting* — and conclude by arguing that the "weapon" metaphor is exact rather than rhetorical, because the long-term effects of colonial language policy reproduce inequalities of access to knowledge and self-representation decades after independence.

**The two aspects of language.** Ngũgĩ opens with a distinction. Language is at once "a means of communication" and "a carrier of culture and history" — what he calls in *Decolonising the Mind* "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 30). When languages meet "on terms of equality and independence" (e.g. Scandinavians learning English commercially), the communicative aspect dominates. When they meet "as oppressor and oppressed, as for instance under imperialism", the carrier aspect becomes decisive: the coloniser's language imports the coloniser's history, religion and economic categories alongside its grammar. Ngũgĩ updates Livingstone's slogan "Christianity plus 5 percent" to "Christianity, debt, plus 40 percent in debt servicing" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31). Language was added to "the Bible and the sword" (p. 31).

**First move: suppression of indigenous languages.** Mission schools and colonial administrations treated African languages as "incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 31). Ngũgĩ recalls a Kenyan school hymn — "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom" — where the "gloom" was the children's own languages (p. 32). Children caught speaking African languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 33), and pieces of paper from the wastepaper basket were stuffed in their mouths. "Humiliation in relation to our languages was the key" (p. 33). The pedagogy was deliberate: to make the child's first language a source of shame, so that the second (English) became a source of pride and aspiration.

**Second move: elevation of the conqueror's language.** "Those inducted into the school system… were furnished with new mirrors" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 32). The French called this *assimilation*; the English called it *education*; Lugard called it *indirect rule*. Mastery of English was "the measure of one's readiness for election into the band of the elect" (p. 32). The textbook industry — Ngũgĩ cites the "Oxford Readers for Africa" with John and Joan visiting the Thames (p. 34) — naturalised English geography, history and religion as the universal. The colonial school was thus weaponising language in a double sense: it suppressed the child's tongue *and* installed the coloniser's tongue as the gateway to economic and political opportunity.

**Third move: uprooting the elite.** The cumulative effect is what Ngũgĩ calls the linguistic prison. In postcolonial Africa, English, French and Portuguese remain "the languages of power" of administration, justice and commerce (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 37). The majority who speak only African languages "are excluded from centre stage" and from modern scientific knowledge, which is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (p. 37). The minority who do speak the colonial language well form an elite "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 38). No African-origin language sits among the official languages of the UN (p. 38). This is the long fuse of the weapon: it continues to detonate long after the soldiers have left.

**Why the metaphor is exact.** Three reasons. First, the harm is measurable. Generations of children who lost fluency in their first languages also lost direct access to oral histories, indigenous legal frameworks and ecological knowledge encoded in those languages. Second, the harm is *intergenerational*: parents whose own education was in the coloniser's language often raise their children in it, accelerating language death. Third, the harm is *institutional*: courts, universities and parliaments continue to operate in the coloniser's language. The weapon does not need to be reloaded; the institutions reload it automatically.

**Concrete extensions.** The mechanism Ngũgĩ identifies applies beyond Africa. Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish were eroded by similar internal-colonial language policy in the British Isles. The Hindi/Urdu split — a single spoken language partitioned by colonial script politics — is another example of language-as-weapon. Boarding schools imposed on Indigenous children in the US, Canada and Australia replicated Ngũgĩ's mission-school regime, including punishment for speaking the first language. The pattern is general.

**Counter-currents.** Ngũgĩ is not despairing. He proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 41). He demands "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" as a precondition for any common world language (p. 39). Welsh and Gaelic revivals show that the weapon can be partly disarmed by deliberate policy.

**Conclusion.** Colonial education used language as a weapon by suppressing the child's first language, elevating the coloniser's language as the medium of opportunity, and producing an uprooted elite cut off from its own peoples. The weapon is precise because it operates through institutions — schools, courts, publishers — that keep firing long after political independence. Reversing the damage, as Ngũgĩ insists, requires not just symbolic gestures but structural language policy: indigenous-language schooling, UN recognition, and the political-economic equality without which any "world language" reproduces the colonial pattern.

### M6: *To what extent are gendered pronouns a sociological — rather than purely grammatical — problem?*

I will argue that gendered pronouns are *primarily* a sociological problem dressed in grammatical clothing. The grammarian's defence — that masculine, feminine and neuter are arbitrary linguistic categories — does not survive scrutiny once we examine which subjects the categories track and what consequences the tracking produces. I will reconstruct the grammatical defence, dismantle it via de Beauvoir, Irigaray and Halewood, and conclude with three contemporary sites where pronouns demonstrate their sociological weight.

**The grammarian's defence.** H.W. Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1937 [1926]) insists that grammatical gender "is a grammatical term only" — derived from Latin *genus* meaning "type" or "genre". To treat masculine and feminine as biologically sexed is, for Fowler, "a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1937 [1926], p. 211). On this view, *he*, *she* and *it* are technical labels indexing morphological class, no more meaningful than the labels "Class A" or "Class B".

**Why the defence fails.** Halewood's chapter dismantles it cleanly. If categories really were arbitrary, why not "up, down, middle" instead of "masculine, feminine, neuter" (Halewood, 2021)? The specific words carry semantic content. Three further reasons make the grammatical defence indefensible.

*First*, the categories track real subjects unevenly. Irigaray's French examples make this concrete: *il* covers male persons *and* impersonal "it" (*il pluie*, "it is raining") *and* any unknown subject; the mixed plural *ils* is used however small the male presence. The Czech verb example from Tichý — "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses feminine *šly*, but as soon as John joins, the verb flips to masculine *šli* — shows the asymmetry operating at the level of verbs, not just nouns (Halewood, 2021, p. 116). The masculine is at once *one* gender and *the* default. That is not a neutral classificatory fact.

*Second*, de Beauvoir generalises the asymmetry. "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). Man is "both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, p. 15). The grammatical fact (masculine = unmarked) corresponds to a social ontology (male = default human). If grammar were sociologically neutral, this correspondence would be coincidence; that it holds across French, English, German, Russian, Czech and many others is too consistent to be coincidence.

*Third*, the categories produce measurable effects. Psycholinguistic studies show that English readers parse "generic" *he* as a man; readers of languages with grammatical gender systematically imagine masculine-marked occupations as held by men. The categories shape what speakers can easily think about, which is the weak Sapir–Whorf claim: "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968, p. 230). Pronouns are part of that agreement.

**Three contemporary sites.**

*First, trans rights.* The standard inventory *he/she/it/they* does not match the range of subject positions speakers occupy. Stephen Whittle and others argue that "personal pronouns are limiting and exclusionary for some of those who identify as 'trans'" (Halewood, 2021, p. 128). The singular *they* — long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray (Marsh, 2014) — has been revived precisely because the existing inventory channelled thought unequally. The dispute is sociological down to its bones: who counts as a subject of address?

*Second, occupational and recruitment language.* "Manager", "chairman", "fireman", and the residual generic *he* in older job descriptions all carry the male-as-default Spender identified in "plus male / minus male" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145). The downstream effects — who applies, who is shortlisted — are measurable and material.

*Third, legal and medical recognition.* Some jurisdictions now allow non-binary markers on passports and birth certificates. The state, by selecting which pronouns it inscribes, is exercising classificatory power directly. In healthcare, being correctly gendered improves access and adherence; being repeatedly misgendered is associated with avoidance of care. The pronoun is the gateway to material consequence.

**Counter-argument and reply.** A reasonable objection: some languages have no gendered pronouns at all (Finnish *hän*, Hungarian *ő*, Turkish *o*, Yoruba), yet gender hierarchies exist in those societies. So the linguistic mechanism cannot be the sole cause of gender inequality. I accept this. The point is not that pronouns *cause* inequality but that, where they exist, they *carry* and *reinforce* it. In genderless-pronoun languages, the inequality runs through different lexical and pragmatic channels — title forms of address, occupational nouns, honorifics — but the sociological work is similar.

**Caveats.** Two. First, Halewood is right that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126); pronoun reform without legal recognition, healthcare access and protection from violence is symbolic. Second, the binary *he/she* framework risks re-marginalising trans and intersex subjects whom the binary itself excludes; the solution is not just additional pronouns but a processual reading of identity.

**Conclusion.** Gendered pronouns are sociological problems wearing grammatical disguise. The Fowler-style defence — "it's just grammar" — fails because the grammatical categories track which subjects count as default human, route attention measurably, and serve as gateways to recognition, employment, healthcare and legal personhood. The work pronouns do is not separate from the work institutions do; it is one of the channels through which institutional power operates.

### M9: *Discuss the politics of language revival, drawing on Welsh, Gaelic, or other minoritised languages.*

Language revival is a political project, not a linguistic one. In this essay I will argue that minority-language revivals — Welsh, Gaelic, Māori, Hawaiian, Catalan and others — are best understood as deliberate reversals of internal colonisation, and that their successes and failures map closely onto the framework Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o sets out for external colonisation. I will work through three claims: revival exposes the political character of language death; revival requires structural, not symbolic, investment; and revival inherits both the strengths and the contradictions of the colonial-attitude critique.

**Language death is political, not natural.** Ngũgĩ cites Lyn Marierid of the Welsh Language Society writing in the *Morning Star* (21 October 1988): "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 36). The framing is decisive. Welsh did not decline because Welsh speakers lost interest; it declined because English-medium administration, schooling, mining capital and tourism — what Ngũgĩ generalises as the "yuppiefication" of the Welsh hills — actively reduced the *utility* of Welsh in everyday economic life (p. 36). The Welsh Not, the wooden token hung around the necks of Welsh-speaking schoolchildren in the nineteenth century, is the British-internal counterpart of the plaques reading "I am stupid" that Ngũgĩ describes from colonial Kenya (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 33). Gaelic in Scotland and Ireland, Cornish in Cornwall, and Manx on the Isle of Man followed similar trajectories. The lecture explicitly groups these as internal-colonial casualties of English (Lecture 4 slide 26). Recognising language death as the residue of policy, not nature, is the first political claim of any revival movement.

**Revival requires structural investment.** Symbolic gestures are not enough — Halewood's warning applies here: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126). The Welsh case is instructive. Bilingual road signs and the establishment of S4C (Sianel Pedwar Cymru, the Welsh-language television channel) in 1982 created visibility; the Welsh Language Act 1993 and the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 created legal infrastructure; but it is the Welsh-medium schooling system (*ysgolion cyfrwng Cymraeg*) that produces new fluent speakers. The 2021 census shows Welsh proficiency holding around 17.8% of the population — neither catastrophic decline nor confident reversal. The lesson is that signage and broadcasting alone are *tinkering*; schooling, public-sector employment in the language, and digital infrastructure are *structural*.

Gaelic revival in Ireland is the cautionary case. Despite constitutional status as the first official language (Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution) and compulsory schooling, daily-use figures are very low. Symbolic restoration without economic incentive produces credentialed non-speakers. The Scottish Gaelic case is intermediate: *Bòrd na Gàidhlig*, BBC Alba, and Gaelic-medium schooling in the Western Isles are slowly rebuilding a daily-use community, but the absolute numbers remain small. Hawaiian and Māori, both nearly extinct in the mid-twentieth century, have been revived more successfully because of immersion schooling — *Pūnana Leo* in Hawai'i, *kōhanga reo* in Aotearoa New Zealand — combined with broadcasting, university programmes and state recognition. The pattern is consistent: revival succeeds when the language becomes economically useful again, fails when it remains only symbolically prestigious.

**Revival inherits the colonial-attitude critique.** Ngũgĩ frames decolonising the mind as the long-term work of language policy: indigenous languages should not just survive but should be allowed to "grow" without growing in the graveyards of others (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 41). Minority-language revivals enact exactly this principle inside Britain, Ireland, the US and the Pacific. They presuppose Ngũgĩ's claim that language is a "collective memory bank" (p. 30) and that losing the language loses the world it carries. Achebe's complementary strategy — refashion the dominant language to carry minority experience (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347) — appears alongside, in the form of Welsh English, Hiberno-English, Scottish English and the rich literary tradition that uses the dominant language to encode a minoritised cultural standpoint.

**Three concrete sites.**

*Schooling.* Welsh-medium and Gaelic-medium schools are the most consequential single site of language revival, because they produce new fluent speakers who can use the language professionally. *Kōhanga reo* (Māori language nests) and *Pūnana Leo* (Hawaiian immersion preschools) are the models often cited. *Education* in Ngũgĩ's terms is the location where the weapon is fired *or* defused.

*Broadcasting and digital.* S4C, BBC Alba, TG4 (Ireland), and a new generation of streaming services in minority languages create the everyday environment for a working language. Without media presence, revival becomes a school-only competence with no out-of-school context to use it.

*Public-sector employment.* Where the state runs services in the minority language — courts, healthcare, local government — the language acquires economic value. This addresses the Welsh Language Society's diagnosis that languages "are lost when the predominant class… has no use for them" (Ngũgĩ, p. 36).

**Critical complications.**

- *Class.* Revival can become a middle-class project that excludes the original first-language communities. Welsh-medium urban schools, prized for their educational outcomes, sometimes serve incomers rather than rural Welsh speakers.
- *Authenticity wars.* Revived languages are inevitably modernised — neologisms for "computer", "vaccine", "spreadsheet" — and purists sometimes contest these innovations. This echoes the Achebe / Ngũgĩ debate at the scale of single lexicons.
- *Risk of essentialism.* Tying ethnic identity to language can re-create the exclusions colonisation produced. A successful revival lets speakers be Welsh or Māori or Hawaiian *without* requiring a single linguistic test of belonging.

**Conclusion.** Language revival vindicates Ngũgĩ's claim that language is political infrastructure: minority languages were killed by policy and can only be brought back by policy. Symbolic restoration is necessary but insufficient; structural investment in schooling, media, employment and digital tooling is decisive. The politics of revival are the politics of de-colonising — inside, as well as outside, the metropolitan core.

### M10: *Using examples from medicine, law and computer science, explain how everyday professional vocabularies carry hierarchies.*

Professional vocabularies are not neutral technical inventories; they are sedimented hierarchies. In this essay I will work through three professional domains — medicine, law and computer science — to show how each carries the male-as-norm pattern Dale Spender identified and the colonial-attitude pattern Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o identified, and how each is now the site of contested reform. The overarching claim, following the lecture, is that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4); professional vocabularies are one of the places where that slogan is most easily tested.

**Medicine.** Medical English carries an old male default. Until the late twentieth century, clinical trials systematically under-recruited women, and textbook descriptions of disease presentation were built on male cases. The result is gender-based diagnostic bias: women presenting with heart attacks were misclassified as "anxious" because the textbook description ("crushing central chest pain radiating to the left arm") tracked male physiology and patient demographics. The atypical presentations more common in women — nausea, jaw pain, fatigue — were coded as "atypical" precisely because the male presentation was the unmarked default. De Beauvoir's framework reads this exactly: "man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1988, p. 15). The medical textbook generalises the male body and marks the female body as a deviation requiring its own chapter.

The vocabulary itself carries hierarchies. "Hysteria" — etymologically from Greek *hystera* (womb) — pathologised women's symptoms as inherently gynecological. The DSM and ICD have slowly renamed such categories, but residues remain in the language of "medically unexplained symptoms" and "functional disorders", which patient advocates argue still disproportionately attach to women and ethnic minorities. The vocabulary of "compliance" (patient does what doctor says) versus "non-compliance" (patient does not) encodes a hierarchical model of clinical authority that more recent vocabularies of "shared decision-making" or "concordance" try to dismantle. Renaming alone, as Halewood warns, "is not enough" (Halewood, 2021, p. 126), but it is also not nothing: the language of "patient" versus "client" versus "service user" routes attention to different aspects of the same encounter.

**Law.** Legal English is the cleanest professional case of male-as-norm. The "reasonable man" test in tort law set the standard of due care by reference to a masculine ideal type — "the man on the Clapham omnibus" — and only relatively recently has been renamed "reasonable person". The renaming is symbolic; the underlying yardstick was male and continues to track male reasoning patterns. Legal drafting until the late twentieth century used the generic *he* throughout statutes, contracts and case reports, embedding a male default into the foundational documents of civil and criminal life. Spender's archive — Wilson (1553) prescribing "male and female", Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, pp. 147–148) — shows that this was prescribed, not natural.

Legal English also carries colonial-attitude hierarchies. In many postcolonial common-law jurisdictions, the higher courts conduct business in English — Ngũgĩ's "languages of power" of "administration, justice and commerce" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 37). The Indian Supreme Court operates in English despite Hindi's constitutional status; Kenyan and Nigerian higher courts likewise. Litigants who do not command English are gatekept out of administrative justice. The vocabulary of "developing", "transitional" and "fragile" jurisdictions, generated in Anglophone law-and-development discourse, carries the same comparative measure against an implicit Western benchmark. The Hindi/Urdu split — one spoken language partitioned by colonial script politics into the de facto languages of Hindu-majority and Muslim-majority legal traditions — shows colonial language policy continuing to structure legal life decades after independence.

**Computer science.** Computer science is the newest professional vocabulary and the most explicitly contested. The terms *master* and *slave* drives, *blacklist* and *whitelist*, the *kill* command, *master* branch in version control, and *fork* / *child* / *parent* process all carry overt or sedimented hierarchies. Recent reforms — moving from *master*/*slave* to *primary*/*replica*, from *blacklist*/*whitelist* to *blocklist*/*allowlist*, from *master* branch to *main* branch — exemplify Achebe's principle that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347). The new field is refashioning its own inventory.

Computer science also carries the male-as-norm pattern. The generic *user* in user-experience design was modelled on male engineers; consumer products from voice assistants (defaulting to female voices) to medical devices (defaulting to male anthropometric data) encode the asymmetry. Social-media moderation works much better in English than in Burmese, Tigrinya or Amharic — a Sapir–Whorf effect at the level of code. Facebook's failure to moderate Burmese-language hate speech contributed directly to incitement to ethnic cleansing in the Rohingya crisis. The corporate decision about which languages to invest in is a language-and-power decision in Ngũgĩ's sense.

**Synthesis.** Across all three professions, the same mechanism operates: the unmarked subject (white male engineer, male body, English speaker) is encoded as the default, and everyone else is encoded as a marked deviation. Spender provides the gender mechanism; Ngũgĩ provides the colonial mechanism; de Beauvoir provides the marked/unmarked logic. The vocabularies are now contested precisely because the marked subjects — women, non-English speakers, racialised minorities — are pushing back.

**Counter-currents.** The reforms in each profession show that vocabulary is plastic. *Ms* replacing *Mrs/Miss*, *reasonable person* replacing *reasonable man*, *main* replacing *master*, *patient-centred* replacing *compliant patient* are all live re-fashionings. None of them, on their own, redistributes resources; but each is a necessary precondition for redistribution to become thinkable.

**Conclusion.** Professional vocabularies are one of the densest sites where the lecture's slogan applies. Medicine carries male-bodied defaults that produce diagnostic inequality; law carries male-as-norm reasoning and colonial-language gatekeeping; computer science carries both old male-as-norm patterns and explicit colonial-era metaphors. Reform of each vocabulary is necessary but, as Halewood insists, must be paired with structural change in the institutions — clinical trials, jury instructions, platform investment — that the vocabulary serves.

### M11: *Critically assess the claim that "linguistic determinism" is a coherent sociological position.*

Linguistic determinism is the strong version of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis: that the language a speaker is born into *fixes* the limits of what that speaker can think. In this essay I will argue that the determinism claim is *not* a coherent sociological position, even though the weaker claim of linguistic relativity remains useful. The determinism position fails on three counts — translation, plasticity, and political risk — but the work it tries to do can be done better by relativity plus an account of institutional power.

**The claim.** The lecture presents linguistic determinism via Roger Brown's formulation: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968, p. 231). The image is decisive: minds take the shape of their mould; what cannot be expressed in the language cannot be thought; speakers of different languages live in different cognitive worlds. The recurring textbook example, flagged by the lecturer with sceptical scare-quotes, is "Eskimos have over 50 words for snow" — therefore Inuit speakers perceive snow with finer discrimination than English speakers (Lecture 4 slide 8).

**First objection: translation works.** If language fully determined thought, translation would be impossible. In fact translation is constantly successful, even between languages with very different grammatical structures (Mandarin and English, Yoruba and German, Finnish and Hindi). It is *frictional* — some concepts require explanation, some idioms cannot be carried over — but the friction is calibrable, not absolute. The very existence of multilingual speakers, who hold multiple "moulds" simultaneously without their minds shattering, undermines the determinism claim. Ngũgĩ himself writes in English and Gĩkũyũ; Achebe writes in English with Igbo idioms; the speakers refute the strong claim by their daily practice.

**Second objection: language is plastic.** English speakers without a single-word lexicon for "wet snow" build the concept out of phrases — "good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing" — as the lecturer notes (Lecture 4 slide 9). Languages absorb new vocabulary continuously: *internet*, *selfie*, *misogynoir*, *cisgender*, *intersectionality*. If language determined thought, neologisms would be impossible — speakers would lack the cognitive machinery to coin words for concepts the language did not already have. The fact that new concepts are coined precisely *because* they were needed shows that thought leads language at least as often as language leads thought. Spender's whole programme — to make women linguistically visible by inventing or repurposing vocabulary (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 162) — presupposes plasticity.

**Third objection: political risk.** This is the lecturer's sharpest move. Taken straight, the determinism claim becomes "they live in a very different world from 'us'" (Lecture 4 slide 8). It sets up an "us / them" division in which the speakers of one language are exotic others compared to a Western "us". Ngũgĩ's analysis applies here: this is the colonial outlook in epistemological dress. The "Eskimo" example is itself an externally imposed label (Samson, *A Way of Life That Does Not Exist*, 2003), and the wonderment at indigenous perception is a continuation of the missionary outlook — the indigenous group as the curious other against the implicit Western benchmark. A sociologically responsible account of language and power cannot rest on a framework that reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

**Why relativity does the work better.** The weak version of Sapir–Whorf — linguistic relativity — keeps the useful insight (language channels attention, shapes categories, makes some thoughts easier than others) without the unsupportable claim that it *fixes* the limits of thought. Spender's "plus male / minus male" classification scheme (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980, p. 145), de Beauvoir's marked/unmarked logic, Ngũgĩ's "carrier" theory of language as "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 30), and Halewood's processual reading all rest on linguistic relativity, not determinism. Each thinker grants that speakers can refashion their inventories — that is precisely how Achebe's Africanising English (Achebe, *Transitions*, 1997, p. 347) and the Welsh and Māori revivals proceed.

**What about strong empirical findings?** Some empirical work — Lera Boroditsky on grammatical gender's effects on object descriptions, on spatial frames of reference in Guugu Yimithirr, on colour categorisation — is sometimes read as supporting determinism. I think this misreads it. The findings show that grammatical categories *bias* perception and memory; they do not show that speakers are *unable* to perceive or describe what their language under-codes. Bias is the language of relativity; impossibility is the language of determinism. The empirical record supports the former, not the latter.

**A residual sense in which "determinism" still bites.** One last steelman of the determinism position. Even if speakers can in principle coin new vocabulary, the institutions that publish, teach and credential a language shape what concepts most speakers will encounter. Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, *Moving the Centre*, p. 37) is a structural form of determinism: not at the level of individual cognition but at the level of *which thoughts are easily public*. I accept this point — but it is properly an account of institutional power, not of cognitive moulding. Better to call it linguistic *gatekeeping* than linguistic determinism.

**Conclusion.** Linguistic determinism is not a coherent sociological position. It is refuted by translation, by linguistic plasticity, and by the political risk of reproducing colonial othering. The useful work the determinism claim wants to do — accounting for how vocabularies shape thought — is done better by the weak version (linguistic relativity) combined with an account of institutional power (Spender on prescription, Ngũgĩ on imperial language policy, Halewood on lexical sedimentation). The honest verdict is that language does not determine thought, but it powerfully channels it; and the channels are set by institutions, not by metaphysical "moulds".
