# SC301 / L4: Language and the World (Words and Things)

> Cheat sheet for revision. Lecture 4 covers three linked questions: (1) the relation between language and thought (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis); (2) language and gender (Dale Spender, Simone de Beauvoir, Luce Irigaray); (3) language and the legacy of colonialism (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Chinua Achebe). The overriding claim: **language is not a neutral mirror of the world**. It classifies, evaluates, and is a mode of power.

---

## 1. Key concepts and thinkers (≤200 words)

- **Linguistic relativity / the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (Edward Sapir, Benjamin Lee Whorf): each language embodies a particular world view; speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230).
- **Linguistic determinism**: stronger claim that language *determines* thought, "moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231).
- **Man-made language** (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980): English is structured around the male-as-norm; female is "minus male".
- **The Second Sex / "woman is the other"** (Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1949/1988): man is positive and neutral; woman is the negative, the marked term.
- **Imperialism of language / decolonising the mind** (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, 1993): colonial languages were weapons of conquest, "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37).
- **Africanising English** (Chinua Achebe, 'English and the African Writer', 1965/1997): English as a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347).
- **Sexuate subject / sexual difference is not extra-linguistic** (Luce Irigaray).
- **The generic he / chairman vs chair / actor vs actress**: concrete sites where male-as-norm is reproduced.

## 2. Key notes from the presentation

**Three parts to the lecture (slide 2).**

1. *Language, thought and culture*: linguistic relativity and linguistic determinism.
2. *Language and gender*: Spender, de Beauvoir.
3. *Language and the legacy of colonialism*: Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

**Sapir-Whorf (slides 3-6).** Linguistic relativity is "the belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230). The strong version, linguistic determinism, holds that the language we speak shapes the very limits of thought. The recurring textbook example is the claim that "Eskimos have over 50 words for snow", which the lecturer marks with scare-quotes to flag two problems: (a) "Eskimo" is itself an inaccurate, externally imposed label (see Samson, 2003); (b) the example is built on a single-word fixation that ignores how English speakers also describe many kinds of snow through *phrases* ("good snow for packing"). The slide highlights the load-bearing line: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**" (Lecture 4 slide 4).

**Critique of Sapir-Whorf (slides 7-9).** Any human language renders, rather than reflects, reality; we have lost any "innocent contact" with the world. Worse, the snow story sets up an "us / them" division in which a vaguely "Western" outlook is the benchmark and the so-called indigenous group is the exotic other. This links forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

**Spender on man-made language (slides 10-14, 18).** Spender argues that the structure of English encodes a "**male-as-norm**" classification scheme: humanity is divided into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145-148). Historical evidence: Mr Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147); John Kirkby's 1746 "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). Her programme: "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**De Beauvoir (slide 15, 28).** "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The man writes from no particular standpoint; the woman is always a *woman writer*. Crucially, de Beauvoir does **not** fully separate sex from gender. Biology and lived societal experience are intertwined.

**Actors / actresses / political correctness (slides 16-17).** David Marsh (*Guardian*) bans "actress": only "actor". This *seems* progressive (women are no longer compared only to other women) but the lecturer warns that occupations were historically male-coded, so calling all women "actors" risks absorbing them into a masculine yardstick rather than dismantling it.

**Grammarians and gender (slide 29).** H.W. Fowler (*A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, 1926: 211) insists that grammatical gender "is a grammatical term only", derived from Latin *genus* (type, genre). To treat masculine/feminine as biologically sexed is, for him, "a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211).

**Ngũgĩ and Achebe (slides 20-25).** Ngũgĩ frames language as a weapon of colonisation: education in the colonial language, suppression of indigenous tongues, "The English language was the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). No language of African origin is an official UN language. Achebe responds differently: African writers should keep writing in English/French/Portuguese to reach larger audiences, but should fashion an English "that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). When asked whether an African can ever use English like a native speaker, Achebe says: "I hope not" (Achebe, 1997: 347).

**And what of other languages? (slide 26).** Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish: internal colonial casualties of English inside the British Isles.

## 3. Information from the readings (slide-by-slide presentation walk-through)

### 3.1 Slide-by-slide presentation summary

- **Slide 1 (Title).** "Language and the World (Words and Things)". The bracketed subtitle frames the whole lecture: the question is the relation between *words* (language) and *things* (the world).

- **Slide 2 (3 parts to the lecture).** Three parts: (1) language and thought (and culture): linguistic relativity, linguistic determinism; (2) language and gender: Spender, de Beauvoir; (3) language and the legacy of colonialism: Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

- **Slide 3 (The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).** Linguistic relativity defined as the "belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230). Members of a society who share a language share "both a language and a specific way of viewing and understanding 'reality'". The pivot quote: "The speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230).

- **Slide 4 (How Many Words for Snow?).** Sets up the textbook example: "Eskimos" have "over 50 words for 'snow'". The lecturer scare-quotes "Eskimos" because the term is "neither accurate nor appropriate" as a name for a group. The slide previews the load-bearing slogan of the whole lecture: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**". References Samson (2003) *A Way of Life That Does Not Exist: Canada and the Extinguishment of the Innu*.
  > 💡 slide-question: *How Many Words for Snow?*

- **Slide 5 (A different world?...).** Spells out the Sapir-Whorf reading of the snow example: a wide range of words for snow is needed "to be able to live"; "Eskimos" therefore see the world differently, and this is "reflected in their language". There is a link "between language, thinking and understanding the world". The greater number of words "points to the fact that they live in a very different world from 'us'". Introduces the names Edward Sapir (1884-1939) and Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897-1941) as the "pioneers" the hypothesis is named after.
  > 💡 slide-question: *A different world?...*

- **Slide 6 (A second aspect of Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).** Introduces **linguistic determinism** as a stronger claim. Not just that language *links* to a way of seeing, but: "How we think of the world is *determined* by the language that we use". The famous Brown image: "The languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231).

- **Slide 7 (This is a powerful argument. But it has some problems).** First critique. Any human language "expresses human thoughts about the world"; each language is a "specific rendering of a specific 'reality'" rather than an "accurate or objective picture". "We" (humans with language) "seem to have lost any innocent contact with the world, as our language always 'gets in the way'".

- **Slide 8 (More Problems).** Second critique, more political. The phrase "they live in a very different world from 'us'" assumes an *us* and a *them*. Who is the "them"? "The 'others'". The risk is "assigning completely different 'realities' or worldviews to speakers of so-called indigenous groups" (Amazon rainforest is the example). This makes the "Western outlook" the implicit benchmark, and "plays down the differences between individual European languages". Connects forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

- **Slide 9 (Another (interesting) point (if we have time)…).** Third critique. Sapir-Whorf assumes single words are the main way of indicating things. If "Eskimo" has three words for snow that do not map onto three English words, the conclusion is supposed to be that "Eskimo" speakers "must think of snow and the world differently". This forgets that English speakers (especially snowball-makers) use *phrases*, not single words: "good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing". English may have fewer single-word labels but has "as many possible phrases for snow as they do".

- **Slide 10 (Dale Spender on "Man-Made Language").** Two quotations from Spender, 1980: 145. First: "Undoubtedly our own meanings are partially hidden from us and it is difficult to have access to them. We may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, 1980: 145). Second: "although it is not always easy to get outside this language trap, to get outside the limitations of one's own language, it is not impossible. There are clues, if one is prepared to look for them" (Spender, 1980: 145).

- **Slide 11 (Something to read (later)).** Linked Guardian article on "Sexism and dictionaries" (theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2019/jul/03/hussy-baggage-bit-filly-dictionary-definitions-woman). Side-reading, not core content.

- **Slide 12 (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*).** The central thesis. The key idea operating in the language is the **male-as-norm**. "One standard or normal human being is a male one". Those who are not part of this standard are "in some way 'deviant'". Quote: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts (if two is to be the significant number) but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980). "The status of the female is derived from the status of the male and on this has been erected many strata of positive and negative classifications".

- **Slide 13 ("Men Before Women": Where do such ideas come from?).** Spender's historical archive. "The first record we appear to have is that of a Mr Wilson in 1553 who insisted that it was more natural to place the man before the woman, as for example in male and female, husband and wife, brother and sister, son and daughter. Implicit in his insistence that males take precedence is the belief that males 'come first' in the natural order, and this is one of the first examples of a male arguing for not just the superiority of males but that this superiority should be reflected in the structure of the language" (Spender, 1980: 147).

- **Slide 14 (More Evidence).** The second historical fragment. "In 1746 John Kirkby formulated his 'Eighty Eight Grammatical Rules'. These rules, the product of Mr Kirkby's own imagination, contained one that indicated the esteem in which he held females: Rule Number Twenty One stated that the male gender was more comprehensive than the female. This represents a significant departure from the simple proposition that males are more important" (Spender, 1980: 148).

- **Slide 15 (Men take themselves and language for granted).** The pivot to de Beauvoir. Men do not have to explain that they are writing "as a man"; it is "seen as normal, as 'natural' even". The famous quote: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Women are "immediately seen as 'women writers'"; their writing is not granted the assumed objectivity that male writing claims.

- **Slide 16 (Actors and Actresses).** The David Marsh case. Marsh (*Guardian* production editor) instructs journalists to use only "actor", never "actress". Reasons: "actress" is limiting (it compares a person only to other female actors), while "actor" can refer to the best at acting in the company overall. The masculine term "can include the female, but not the other way around". "The female has been subsumed into the male (again)". "Actor" also stands in for the neuter when the speaker's gender is unknown. The reform argument: equal title, equal pay.

- **Slide 17 (Marsh's argument seems good, but…).** The lecturer's caution. Most occupations (doctor, teacher, actor) were historically carried out by men; women were "rare, even peculiar". Apparently neutral words like "actor" "provide a yardstick which may not immediately appear to refer to the male, or to be masculine". Risk: "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term". We can "pay lip-service" to the problem while ignoring "the more fundamental problem". This is the "political correctness" debate, with "no easy answer".

- **Slide 18 (What is to be done?).** Spender's prescription. "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible… New symbols will need to be created and old symbols will need to be recycled and invested with new images if the male hold of language is to be broken. As the language structure which has been devised and legitimated by male grammarians exacts ambiguity, uncertainty, and anomie for females, then in the interests of dismantling the muted nature of females, that language structure and those rules need to be defied" (Spender, 1980: 162).

- **Slide 28 (Rethinking "Sex" and Gender (Simone de Beauvoir)).** The deeper read of de Beauvoir. Many sociologists ascribe the sex/gender distinction to *The Second Sex* (1949), but the division "is not as clear-cut in de Beauvoir's work as is sometimes thought". De Beauvoir's stated aim is to ask "what is a woman?" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 13). She insists: "When I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). She "does not reduce the question of what a woman 'is' to the biological ('sex')"; likewise, "the lived societal experience of women ('gender') is not purely 'social'". The two are intertwined.

- **Slide 29 (Gender in language (according to grammarians)).** The Fowler counter-position. In *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, H.W. Fowler states gender "is a grammatical term only. To ask whether genders are "biologically 'sexed'" is, for Fowler, "a mistake, even a joke". The Halewood chapter (next section) treats this slide as a straw man to be dismantled.

### 3.2 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, Chapter 4: 'Imperialism of Language. English, a Language for the World?'

Ngũgĩ opens with a two-aspect view of language: it is both **a means of communication** and **a carrier of culture and history**, what he calls in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). These two aspects are inseparable but unbalanced according to the political conditions of encounter.

When languages meet "on terms of equality and independence" (e.g. Scandinavians, Japanese, West Germans learning English), the communicative aspect dominates and English does not substitute for the national language (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30-31). When they meet "as oppressor and oppressed, as for instance under imperialism", language becomes a **weapon** alongside "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Ngũgĩ quotes David Livingstone's nineteenth-century slogan "Christianity plus 5 percent" and updates it to "Christianity, debt, plus 40 percent in debt servicing" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31).

The capture of minds happens in two complementary moves (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32):

1. **Suppression of indigenous languages.** They were "experienced as incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Ngũgĩ recalls a Kenyan school hymn, "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was the children's own language (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32).
2. **Elevation of the conqueror's language.** "Those inducted into the school system… were furnished with new mirrors" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The French called this *assimilation*; the English called it *education*. Lugard's blunter name was **indirect rule**: mastery of English was "the measure of one's readiness for election into the band of the elect" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32).

Ngũgĩ describes children punished for speaking African languages, "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), with pieces of paper from the wastepaper basket stuffed in their mouths. "Humiliation in relation to our languages was the key" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33).

The reading then extends the argument beyond Africa. Citing Lyn Marierid in the Welsh Language Society (*Morning Star*, 21 Oct 1988), Ngũgĩ shows that Welsh is being eroded by capitalist "yuppiefication": "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Equally, the Russians of Novosibirsk learn English because, in Kenneth Baker's words quoted from the *Evening Standard*, "they want to get away from the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35). English carries capitalist modernity.

The consequences in postcolonial Africa: English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and education (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Yet only a minority speak them, so the working majority "are excluded from centre stage" and from modern scientific knowledge, which is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). The elite is "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). No African-origin language sits among the official languages of the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38).

Ngũgĩ is not against a common world language. He insists that the languages of the world are "our common heritage. A world of many languages should be like a field of flowers of different colours" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). He demands "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" as the precondition (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). He concludes by proposing Kiswahili, "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41), as a candidate world language.

### 3.3 *Gender and Personal Pronouns: She, He, It and They* (Halewood, 2021)

The chapter opens by setting out the sex/gender distinction in sociology, then immediately complicates it. Mead's anthropology shows that gender roles vary cross-culturally, hence they are social. But Halewood, following de Beauvoir and Irigaray, argues that the easy split (biology over here, society over there) itself "bifurcates" language and world.

Fowler's claim that gender "is a grammatical term only… of the male or female sex is either a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211) is presented as a starting point and then dismantled. If categories really were arbitrary, why not "up, down, middle" instead of "masculine, feminine, neuter"? The specific words carry meaning.

**De Beauvoir's pivot.** "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15).

**Irigaray's grammatical examples.** In French *il* covers "he", impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pluie*), and any unknown subject. A male dog *le chien* is default; *la chienne* requires extra knowledge. The third-person plural *ils* is used for any mixed group, however small the male presence. The Czech example from Tichý (2018) ("all the women of the world went for a walk" uses the feminine plural *šly*, but as soon as John joins, the verb flips to masculine *šli*) shows the asymmetry at the level of verbs too. Irigaray: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20).

**Gendered English.** Halewood quotes Dewey writing "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his" and Whitehead's "men of science" to show that even Anglophone academic writing carried male defaults. Fowler had wanted *doctress*, *teacheress*, *singeress*, *danceress*: multiplying female forms. Marsh (production editor, *Guardian*) goes the other way: abolish *actress*, use only *actor*.

**Job titles and chairs.** "Chair" derives from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat in a cathedral, and the historical "see" (*sedes*). Professors hold "chairs"; the office carries an authority that Halewood reads as historically male/masculine, tied to the Church. So even apparently neutral words like "chair" are not neutral.

**Pronouns, trans rights, prescriptivism.** Singular "they" is grammatically contested but has a long literary history (Lewis Carroll, Shakespeare, the King James Bible, Thackeray, all cited via Marsh, 2014). Trans writers including Stephen Whittle (2006) argue that "he/she" is exclusionary; some prefer "they" or neologisms (e.g. "per"). The chapter is sympathetic but warns that "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Haraway's caution.** "All dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130).

The conclusion sides with a *processual* reading: man, woman, trans are *outcomes* of intertwined material and linguistic processes, not pre-given essences.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The thread that holds these readings together is the slogan from the lecture: **"Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world"** (Lecture 4 slide 4). Each thinker tugs on a different end of that rope.

Sapir-Whorf gives us the *epistemic* lever. If "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230), then a language is never an innocent code; it is a settlement about what counts. The weak form (language influences thought) is now broadly accepted in cognitive linguistics; the strong form (linguistic determinism), "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231), is harder to defend, because speakers can stretch their language with phrases, neologisms and translation. The deeper problem the lecturer flags is not whether the strong claim is true, but that the very *example* (Eskimo words for snow) is constructed from a Western "us" looking at an indigenous "them". The classification is already evaluative; the example reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

Spender shows the same asymmetry inside English itself. Her core claim is structural: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). Historical fragments back this up: Mr Wilson (1553) prescribing the order "male and female" (Spender, 1980: 147), John Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The generic *he*, the word *mankind*, *chairman*, *manpower*, the older NHS form that asked for a "Mrs / Miss" but only "Mr" for a man: these are the sediment of that prescriptive history.

De Beauvoir radicalises Spender. Her insight is phenomenological: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The male is the unmarked term in language *because* he is the unmarked term in social ontology. He occupies "both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). This is why the lecturer treats the Marsh debate about whether to use *actor* or *actress* as irresolvable in purely linguistic terms: tidying the words leaves the social default (the masculine yardstick) untouched (Lecture 4 slide 17).

Irigaray takes this further. "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). The Czech example (*šly* flipping to *šli* as soon as one man joins all the women of the world) is grammatically tiny but ontologically loud: it tells us who counts as the default human.

Ngũgĩ pulls the analysis out to the imperial scale. Language is a "carrier of the history and the culture built into the process of communication over time" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). When the encounter is "oppressor and oppressed", language is added to "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Suppression plus elevation: children punished with plaques reading "I am stupid" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), then drilled in "Oxford Readers for Africa" about John and Joan visiting the Thames (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34), produced what Ngũgĩ calls "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). His sharpest line is that "the death of many languages should never be the condition for the life of a few" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). The point sits squarely on the Sapir-Whorf hinge: kill the language, kill the world that language carries.

Achebe complicates the colonial story. Refusing to abandon English does not equal submission. The African writer should "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Can the African write English like a native? "I hope not" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). This is the position I find most pragmatically defensible: not Ngũgĩ's purism, not the colonial monolith, but a politics of *re-fashioning* the inherited tool.

Across all five thinkers the same lesson appears: language **classifies and evaluates**, and to classify is to distribute power. Sociologists who want to *address* the inequalities Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ and Achebe describe cannot just police vocabulary. Halewood is right that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). But they cannot ignore vocabulary either, because, as the lecture insists, problems with inequality in language *are* problems in the world.

### Essay 1 / 2025 (Q4): *"Language, power and modernity are all interrelated." Critically analyse this statement.*

I agree with the statement, but only when "modernity" is read in the specific sense the SC301 module has been building: modernity as a *colonial* project that travelled with particular European languages and that re-described the rest of the world in its own vocabulary. Read that way, language is not an add-on to modernity but one of its load-bearing technologies.

**Power needs a vocabulary.** The lecture's basic move is that language is "not neutral"; it classifies and evaluates and is therefore "a mode of power" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Spender catches this at the level of grammar: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir catches it at the level of social ontology: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The point is the same: power needs a default subject, and the default subject is installed in the words before any explicit argument is made.

**Modernity travels in particular languages.** Ngũgĩ's reading is decisive here. The languages of European modernity (English, French, Portuguese) did not arrive in the Third World "under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). They came alongside "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The mission school, the colonial bureaucracy, the textbook industry ("Oxford Readers for Africa", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34) and the Christian hymn ("Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) all bundled together a single message: modernity is European, and European is English (or French, or Portuguese). Children caught speaking their own languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). The vocabulary of "progress" was so tightly coupled with English that, decades after independence, Kenneth Baker could report Russians in Novosibirsk learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35). Capitalism is "modern"; everything else is "old-fashioned", and English is the bridge.

**Power and modernity are inscribed in everyday English.** I find the Halewood chapter useful because it shows that the same logic operates inside the English-speaking core, not just at the imperial periphery. The word "chair", from Latin *cathedra*, encodes the authority of bishops, then of professors, then of committee heads (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). The chairs were literally Church furniture; the office of professor was modelled on that of priest. Replacing "chairman" with "chair" feels modern but preserves a deeper masculine-clerical authority. This is what the lecture means by language *carrying* a history that modernity papers over.

**Two complications.** First, modernity has produced *democratic* vocabularies too. Ngũgĩ is careful to say that English has "a democratic tradition" (Shakespeare, Milton, Blake, Shelley, Dickens, Conrad, Bernard Shaw, Graham Greene) and is "part of the common heritage of humankind" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). The relation between language and power is therefore contested *within* every modern language, not just between languages. Second, Achebe argues that the colonised can refashion the coloniser's language: the world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). His own *Things Fall Apart* (1958) takes its title from W.B. Yeats, an Irish poet (slide 22): a small instance of how modernity's linguistic infrastructure is more porous than Ngũgĩ sometimes suggests.

**Examples that pull the three concepts together.**

- *Postcolonial language of instruction.* The retention of English, French and Portuguese as "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) reproduces the colonial school after the colonial flag has come down.
- *No African-origin language at the UN.* "Among the official languages at the United Nations there is not a single language of African origin" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). The institutional grammar of "modern" international politics is European.
- *English as escape from "backwardness".* Kenneth Baker's report that the Russians of Novosibirsk learn English to flee "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) is the lecture's clearest case of English coupled to "modern" capitalism.
- *Pronouns, occupational titles and the bishop's chair.* From the generic *he* and Marsh's actor/actress reform (Halewood, 2021: 120) to "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, Halewood, 2021: 128-130), modernity's professional vocabulary carries earlier male and ecclesiastical authority into ostensibly neutral usage.

**Conclusion.** Language, power and modernity are interrelated because modernity is the historical settlement in which certain European languages became the default vocabulary of progress, science and citizenship. The strong version of this claim risks essentialising European languages as inherently oppressive (Achebe rightly resists that). The weak version, that "the languages of Europe… were taught as if they were our own languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35), is the version I find most useful, and it is enough to vindicate the exam quotation.

### Essay 2 / 2024 (Q3): *Discuss the links between language and inequality. How can sociologists help to address the problem?*

I want to argue two things. First, language and inequality are linked in three overlapping ways (*classification*, *evaluation*, and *erasure*) and the lecture's slogan that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4) captures this neatly. Second, sociologists can help, but only if they treat language as embedded in the world rather than as a separable surface that can be cleaned up by vocabulary reform alone.

**Language classifies.** This is the core insight of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis as the lecture presents it: speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230). Every language carves the world into categories ("man" and "woman", *actor* and *actress*, *chien* and *chienne*) and those categories are unequal in their consequences. Dale Spender shows that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The generic *he*, the older default *mankind*, the ladder of titles *Mr / Mrs / Miss*, all encode an asymmetry: the man is the default human, the woman a variation. Halewood, drawing on Irigaray, extends the same point to French, where the default noun for a doctor (*le médecin*) takes the masculine form, while *cafetière* (the feminine of *café-owner*) is already occupied by an object, the coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119). The classification scheme is unequal before any individual speaker makes a choice.

**Language evaluates.** Classification almost always smuggles in a hierarchy. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argues that English entered Africa as the language of "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31), at once a label and a verdict. "Eskimo", the lecture's recurring example, is not just inaccurate; it expresses a relation of power between the namer and the named (Lecture 4 slide 4; Samson, 2003). Spender's archive of grammarians is overtly evaluative too: Kirkby's "Rule Number Twenty One" pronounced that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148), embedding a moral hierarchy in a grammatical rule.

**Language erases.** Some realities are made unspeakable. Simone de Beauvoir's complaint that "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) is precisely this: the male perspective passes for the universal, so its particularity disappears from view. Ngũgĩ describes a more brutal erasure: African children in colonial schools were punished with "plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) for speaking their first languages; their school hymn sang of being led "kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was their own tongue (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). Welsh is decribed by Lyn Marierid in the *Morning Star* as being eroded by "yuppiefication" once "the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36): a different mode of the same erasure, inside the British Isles.

**So what can sociologists do?** First, *describe* the patterns with care. Spender's archival work on grammarians from Wilson (1553) to Kirkby (1746) shows that the "man-made" character of English is not a vague impression but a documentable history of prescription (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Halewood does a similar genealogy for the word "chair", tracing it from Latin *cathedra* and the bishop's *sedes* through the Lucasian Chair held by Newton and Hawking to the contemporary committee chair (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Sociologists' first task is this kind of careful etymological and institutional archaeology.

Second, *connect* language work to material consequences. De Beauvoir and Irigaray both insist that words alone are not the whole problem. Halewood puts it bluntly: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The Marsh debate (Halewood, 2021: 120) is the cleanest case: insisting on "actor" for all sounds equalising, but if the masculine yardstick is preserved beneath the neutral surface, the reform is symbolic. Sociologists need to keep both the words and the distributions in view.

Third, sociologists can *amplify counter-voices*. Achebe's strategy of Africanising English ("fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347)) is a model: rather than purifying the language, you refashion it for new uses. Ngũgĩ's complementary move is to insist that the "lives of many languages should add life to whichever language emerges as the transnational or universal language" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39) and to propose Kiswahili because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41).

Fourth, sociologists should be *self-critical* about jargon. The discipline's own language can naturalise inequality. Halewood ends his chapter with Haraway's warning that "all dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130). Even the move from "coloureds" or "Asians" to "ethnic communities" can be tokenistic if "the way they think and act in the world" has not changed (Halewood, 2021: 131). Sociologists should police their own vocabulary as carefully as everyone else's.

So: language and inequality are linked through classification, evaluation and erasure. Sociologists help when they document the histories (Spender's archive, Halewood's etymologies, Ngũgĩ's account of the colonial school), connect words to material distributions, support communities refashioning their own languages (Achebe), and police their own vocabulary as carefully as everyone else's. They fail when they treat language reform as a substitute for the change in "thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126) the analysis demands.

### Essay 3 / 2023 (Q4): *Identify and explain how language is tied up with wider questions of power.*

I want to identify four ways language is tied up with power: it (1) classifies who counts; (2) evaluates as it classifies; (3) enforces those evaluations through institutions; and (4) carries the historical sediment of past power even after explicit reform. I will work through each, drawing on Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ, Achebe and the Halewood chapter.

**(1) Classification: who counts as the default.** Spender's claim that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) is the cleanest statement of this. The generic *he*, the older *mankind*, the asymmetry between *actor* and *actress*: these are not stylistic quirks but classification rules that install a default subject. De Beauvoir generalises: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The same logic operates beyond English. Halewood notes that in French *il* covers both "he" and impersonal *it* (*il fait chaud*), and *ils* serves any mixed plural however small the male presence (Halewood, 2021: 114-115); Tichý's Czech example shows that as soon as one man joins all the women of the world, the verb flips from feminine *šly* to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116).

**(2) Evaluation: classification is never neutral.** Ngũgĩ shows how the colonial school treated African languages as "incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The vocabulary itself encoded the verdict: English was light, the African languages were "gloom" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The lecturer's caution about the word "Eskimo" makes the same point: the label is "neither accurate nor appropriate" and it positions the named group as the curious other against an implicit Western "us" (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8; Samson, 2003). The example reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

**(3) Enforcement: institutions police language.** Language is power because someone enforces the vocabulary. Spender notes that the rules were laid down by named grammarians: Wilson in 1553 insisting on "male and female, husband and wife", Kirkby in 1746 ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Ngũgĩ describes the school as the key colonial institution: children were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) for speaking their own languages. Lugard's bluntly named "indirect rule" produced "more faithful 'chiefs'" through education than through political appointment (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). Even in supposedly neutral contemporary settings, David Marsh's editorial fiat at *The Guardian* (no "actress", only "actor") is a prescriptive moment of the same kind (Halewood, 2021: 120), with the polarity reversed.

**(4) Sedimentation: old power lingers in vocabulary.** Halewood's example of "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, via the bishop's seat) shows that even apparently neutral terms carry sedimented authority (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). The professor's chair inherits the bishop's *cathedra*; the Lucasian Chair (founded 1663, held by Newton and Hawking) sits inside Christian Church traditions that have, "until recently", been male (Halewood, 2021: 129). The committee chair inherits the professor's authority; the masculine-ecclesiastical authority is preserved beneath a neutral surface. Renaming "chairman" as "chair" may help, but the history continues to operate.

**Counter-currents.** Power is not absolute. Achebe shows that the dominated can refashion the dominant tongue: the African writer must use English to "carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347), and indeed should *not* learn to use English like a native ("I hope not", Achebe, 1997: 347). The lecture's brief slide on "other languages" (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish; slide 26) names the same kind of refusal inside the British Isles. The singular *they* has a long literary pedigree from Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, cited via Marsh (Halewood, 2021: 128); reviving it for trans, intersex and "trans" speakers who find "he/she" "limiting and exclusionary" (Whittle, 2006, cited Halewood, 2021: 128) is the same kind of structural intervention Spender called for. These counter-currents show that language is contested terrain, not a one-way street.

**The limit case: tinkering versus changing.** It is tempting to think that reforming vocabulary fixes the underlying inequalities. The lecture is sceptical. Marsh's ban on "actress" makes Hattie Jacques comparable to Kenneth Williams as well as to Joan Sims (Halewood, 2021: 120 fn). But, the lecturer warns, "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood draws the broader conclusion: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Conclusion.** Language is tied up with power in four ways (classification, evaluation, enforcement and sedimentation) and these operate from intimate pronouns (he/she/they) right up to the absence of African-origin languages among the official UN languages (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). The political work language does is uneven and contested, and Achebe's strategy of refashioning is as important to the picture as Ngũgĩ's critique of colonial imposition. The sociologist's job is to make the link between vocabulary and distribution visible without pretending that fixing vocabulary by itself fixes distribution.

### Essay 4 / 2022 (Q4): *Can language create inequality?*

My answer is: language does not create inequality from scratch, but it sustains, amplifies and naturalises inequalities that already have material roots, and in that sense it does much of the *work* of inequality. I will defend that claim across three registers: gender, colonial domination, and contemporary technological power.

**The misleading version of the question.** The question, taken literally, can lead to a bad answer: "language creates inequality" reads as if changing words alone would equalise the world. The lecture warns against this: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126; cf. Lecture 4 slide 17 on actor/actress). Material inequalities (wages, land, citizenship) have material causes, not merely linguistic ones. But that does not mean language is innocent.

**Language sustains gender inequality.** Spender's claim that the structure of English is built on "the male-as-norm" (Spender, 1980: 145) is best read as a sustaining-and-amplifying claim rather than a creating-from-nothing claim. Patriarchy did not begin with English grammar; but English grammar makes male centrality feel natural, so that "we may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir's "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) makes the same point at the level of the writing subject. Halewood's Czech example from Tichý (one man tagging along with all the women of the world is enough to flip the verb to masculine *šli*) shows the asymmetry at the level of conjugation, not just lexis (Halewood, 2021: 116). Halewood's later treatment of "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat) shows the same logic in Anglophone authority terms (Halewood, 2021: 128-130): the office is opened to women, but the masculine-ecclesiastical history operates beneath the renaming. Language does not *invent* gender inequality, but it carries it from one institutional setting to the next.

**Language sustains colonial inequality.** Ngũgĩ is uncompromising here. "The encounter between English and most so-called Third World languages did not occur under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). English, French and Portuguese came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Colonial schools then "elevated the language of the conqueror" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) by punishing children for using their own; the morning hymn in Ngũgĩ's school was "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). Decades after independence, English remains the language of administration, justice and higher education across many African states, which is why Ngũgĩ describes the elite as "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Did English *create* the inequality between Britain and Kenya? No. The inequality is rooted in extraction, conquest, and the carve-up of Africa at the Berlin Conference of 1884 (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). But English language policy keeps it in place after the soldiers have left. That is a real form of creation: language *creates the continuity* of a colonial inequality whose original cause has lapsed.

**Language sustains inequality inside the metropolitan core.** Ngũgĩ extends the same diagnosis beyond Africa. He cites Lyn Marierid of the Welsh Language Society writing in the *Morning Star*: rural areas have been "completely Anglicised as ordinary working-class people have been systematically priced out of their native areas" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Languages "are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The lecture's bare list of Welsh, Gaelic, and Cornish (slide 26) marks the same pattern: a working language gets pressed out when capital and administration no longer reward its use. Language inequality is not generated from nothing here either, but the loss of a language is the loss of "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and that loss is itself a substantive inequality.

**Counter-evidence.** There are limits to the claim. Languages get refashioned by their speakers: Achebe insists that the African writer must "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). His "I hope not" (to native-speaker assimilation, Achebe, 1997: 347) is a refusal that operates in style rather than in choice of language. Halewood notes that the singular *they* has a long literary pedigree (Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible, Thackeray, cited via Marsh, in Halewood, 2021: 128), so its revival is not the imposition of something new but the rediscovery of something prescribed out by the same kind of grammarians Spender's archive names. So language does not lock inequality in place; it is contested terrain.

**A subtler creation effect.** Halewood's "chair" example defends a *stronger* version of the "language creates inequality" claim in one specific area: when a category carries an institution forward (the bishop's *cathedra* becomes the professor's chair becomes the committee chair, Halewood, 2021: 128-130), the linguistic form sustains a kind of authority that the original institutional setting (the Christian Church) no longer formally enforces. Here Sapir-Whorf is genuinely useful in its weak form: the way the language carves the world *partly* shapes which inequalities remain administratively durable.

**Conclusion.** Language can create inequality in three precise senses: it can install a default subject (male) so the asymmetry becomes invisible; it can sustain colonial-era inequalities long after their original cause; and it can carry sedimented institutional authority (the bishop's chair) into apparently neutral contemporary usage. But it cannot create inequality unaided. The material world is the other half of every story. The most defensible position, with Halewood, is that "language and the world become together" (Halewood, 2021: 132), and so do the inequalities each carries.

### Essay 5 / 2021 (Q4): *"Inequalities in language represent inequalities in the world." Critically explain this statement.*

The statement comes almost verbatim from the lecture: "Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). I think it is largely correct, but the word *represent* is doing a lot of work and needs unpacking. Inequalities in language do three different things at once: they *reflect* prior inequalities in the world; they *reproduce* those inequalities by carrying them across time; and in some cases they *constitute* the inequalities they appear merely to describe. I will defend that three-layer reading.

**Reflection.** The simplest reading of "represent" is that language mirrors social hierarchies that exist independently. Spender's history of English grammar offers good evidence. Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, 1980: 147) and Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" (Spender, 1980: 148) did not invent the patriarchal society; they *reflected* it. Similarly, the prestige of English in former colonies *reflects* the geopolitical dominance of Britain and the US. Ngũgĩ's contemporary observation that English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and commerce in postcolonial Africa (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) is a description of how linguistic prestige reflects political-economic power.

**Reproduction.** Reflection alone is too weak. Language does not just mirror; it *carries* inequalities forward when their original cause has lapsed. This is the bite of Ngũgĩ's argument. By the time he is writing (1993), formal colonialism in Kenya has ended, but the colonial language continues to gatekeep education, science and the law. Knowledge is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). De Beauvoir makes the same point for gender: long after legal reform has formally equalised men and women, the unmarked male subject persists in everyday speech, "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Halewood's "chair" example is the same logic: even as universities formally open chairs to women, the word *chair* carries forward the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's *cathedra* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Language reproduces inequalities by making them feel natural and durable.

**Constitution.** The strongest version of the claim is that some inequalities are partly *constituted* in and through language. The Sapir-Whorf insight, in its weak and defensible form, is that the categories a language makes available shape what people can do with the world. Spender's claim is precisely that the category scheme "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) is itself part of how patriarchy is experienced and reproduced. Irigaray pushes further: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). Halewood's Tichý example is the clean illustration: in Czech, "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses feminine *šly*, but as soon as one man joins them the verb flips to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116). The grammatical rule does not simply describe an inequality; it tells the speaker who counts as the default human every time the verb is conjugated.

**Critical limits.** Three caveats matter.

1. *Language is not the whole story.* Halewood is firm: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). If we treat language reform as a substitute for material change, we let inequality off the hook.
2. *Counter-currents exist.* Achebe shows that subordinated speakers refashion the dominant language: "fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Halewood's account of singular *they* (long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, Halewood, 2021: 128) shows the same dynamic in gender: prescribed-out usages can be revived.
3. *Representation is not always faithful.* Some linguistic inequalities exaggerate or distort the social patterns they purport to represent. The myth of "Eskimo words for snow", flagged by the lecturer with its "us / them" framing (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8; Samson, 2003), represents indigenous people as exotic *others* in a way that the social facts do not support. So a language can misrepresent as well as reflect.

**Concrete examples that bind it together.**

- *The actor/actress reform* (Halewood, 2021: 120): a place where reflection, reproduction and constitution all overlap, and where "tinkering" is not enough (Halewood, 2021: 126).
- *Job titles and grammatical gender in French*: *le médecin* default-masculine, *la médicine* feminine, *cafetière* (the female café-owner) already occupied by the coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119). Language reproduces the male-as-default of professional life.
- *Colonial school plaques* ("I am stupid", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), and the Welsh decline tracked by Lyn Marierid (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36): showing that linguistic inequality can be enforced *and* that it can be reversed when the political-economic conditions change.
- *The bishop's chair* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130): an authority inequality reproduced silently in everyday committee language.

**Conclusion.** The statement is correct in its strong reading: inequalities in language do represent inequalities in the world, where *represent* means simultaneously to reflect, to reproduce and partly to constitute. The danger lies in collapsing the three readings into one. Sociology's job, as Spender, Ngũgĩ, de Beauvoir, Achebe and Halewood all insist in different keys, is to keep them apart while showing how they hang together, and to refuse either the complacent answer that language is "just words" or the over-simple answer that fixing the words fixes the world.

### Essay 6 / Mock Q1: *"Decolonising the mind requires decolonising the language" (Ngũgĩ). Discuss.*

I take Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's slogan seriously and want to defend it, but with a twist. Decolonising the mind does require decolonising the language, but "decolonising the language" itself names two different projects: a *displacement* project (swap out the coloniser's tongue for an African one, as Ngũgĩ urges) and a *refashioning* project (rewire the coloniser's tongue from inside, as Achebe urges). My claim is that the slogan is correct, and that both projects are needed.

**Why language carries the colonial mind.** Ngũgĩ's argument in *Moving the Centre* (1993) and *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) is that European languages arrived in Africa "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The mission school did the slow work: children caught speaking African languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Humiliation was the pedagogical method. The point was not just communication; the colonial attitude (that the European tongue is the language of modernity, knowledge and progress) was installed at the level of the child's nervous system. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and that memory bank was systematically replaced.

**What survives independence.** This is where the slogan bites. Political flags changed, but English, French and Portuguese remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Ngũgĩ's diagnosis is that the elite is therefore "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Decolonising the mind is impossible while the everyday operating vocabulary of the state remains the coloniser's, because the categories of governance, science, and legal personhood remain those installed by mission school. This is what I mean by epistemic violence as a continuing condition rather than a past event.

**Three concrete sites where the colonial mind hides in the language.** First, *the curriculum and the textbook*. Ngũgĩ remembers learning English from "Oxford Readers for Africa", reading the story of John and Joan, learning about Oxford and Reading and the river Thames before he knew the names of any other town in Kenya (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34). Knowledge "in the arts and sciences" became something that lived in English (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Second, *the official languages of power*. English, French and Portuguese remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Litigants and students who do not command them are gatekept out of administrative justice and modern science alike. Third, *the international institutional grammar*. "Among the official languages at the United Nations there is not a single language of African origin" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). The colonial attitude (that *real* knowledge happens in English) has been globalised: Kenneth Baker's "amazed" report that Russians in Novosibirsk learn English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) shows the same logic projected past the formal empire.

**Ngũgĩ's displacement strategy.** Ngũgĩ's answer is structural. He proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language precisely because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). African-origin languages currently occupy no place of honour among the official languages of the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38), and that absence is itself a form of cognitive colonisation. The lecture's bare list of Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish (slide 26) enacts the same principle inside the British Isles: where the predominant class "has no use for them" (Welsh Language Society, cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36), languages die, and with them the worlds they carry.

**Achebe's refashioning strategy.** Achebe disagrees on tactics, agrees on diagnosis. His position is that the African writer must use "an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The price a world language must pay is "submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Achebe's "I hope not" (to the question of whether the African should learn English like a native, Achebe, 1997: 347) is a refusal of assimilation expressed in style rather than in choice of tongue. The slide-note that the title of *Things Fall Apart* quotes W.B. Yeats, an Irish poet (slide 22), is itself a small Achebean move: an English-language work refashioned through a non-English nationalist tradition.

**Why both are needed.** Pure displacement risks leaving the colonial mind intact in everyone outside the indigenous-language community. Pure refashioning risks naturalising the prestige of English. Decolonising the mind requires (i) institutional space for indigenous languages in schooling, courts, and international forums; *and* (ii) writers and speakers who refashion English/French/Portuguese to carry non-Anglophone experience. Ngũgĩ's closing position is that the two are complementary: "the lives of many languages should add life to whichever language emerges as the transnational or universal language" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39), provided there is "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39).

**Conclusion.** The slogan is correct, and stronger than it looks. Decolonising the mind requires decolonising the language, because the colonial attitude lives in the everyday categories of knowledge and governance, not just in flags and constitutions. The displacement and refashioning strategies are complementary tools for the same job: prising open the categories so that a non-colonial mind becomes thinkable in the same world the colonial mind built.

### Essay 7 / Mock Q2: *Critically assess Spender's argument in Man Made Language with reference to Halewood's chapter on gender and personal pronouns.*

Dale Spender's *Man Made Language* (1980) is the most ambitious structural account of English-as-patriarchal in the SC301 reading list. Halewood's chapter on personal pronouns develops, extends, and qualifies the argument in three ways: it transposes the diagnosis to French and Czech grammar via de Beauvoir and Irigaray; it traces the same logic through Anglophone job titles and the word "chair"; and it warns against treating language reform as a substitute for the change in "thinking, speaking and being" the analysis demands (Halewood, 2021: 126). I will argue that Halewood's chapter both vindicates Spender's structural claim and refines it.

**Spender's argument, briefly.** Spender's notion of man-made language is structural: English divides humanity "not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The male is the default human; the female is a deviation marked by additional morphology (*actress*, *waitress*) or by extra honorifics (*Mrs* and *Miss* mark marital status; *Mr* does not). The archive is historical and named: Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, 1980: 147), Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The "man-made" label tracks an actual paper trail of male grammarians who prescribed male precedence. Spender's programme follows: "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**Where Halewood vindicates Spender.** Three points of convergence. *First*, Halewood shows that the structural pattern Spender names in English is not anglocentric quirk but operates across grammars. In French, *il* covers male persons and the impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pleut*); *ils* serves any mixed plural, however small the male presence (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). Tichý's Czech example makes the point at the level of conjugation: "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses feminine *šly*, but John tagging along flips the verb to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116). The "plus male / minus male" classification is reproduced at the level of the verb. *Second*, Halewood reinforces Spender's historical archive with Fowler's *Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, which insisted that gender is "a grammatical term only" (Fowler, 1926: 211) while elsewhere proposing the multiplication of female forms ("doctress, teacheress, singeress and danceress", cited Halewood, 2021: 120). Fowler is a grammarian of exactly the Wilson-Kirkby type, proving Spender's "man-made" label tracks a continuing tradition. *Third*, the Marsh case (Halewood, 2021: 120) shows Spender's analysis at work in contemporary editorial practice: banning *actress* makes Hattie Jacques comparable to Kenneth Williams, not only to Joan Sims, but the masculine yardstick (since most occupations were historically male) is preserved beneath the apparently neutral term.

**Where Halewood refines Spender.** Three refinements. *First*, Halewood, with Irigaray, points out that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Spender's slogan that women must become "linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162) is a precondition for, not a substitute for, structural change. Halewood is firmer: "what is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). *Second*, Halewood extends Spender's logic to vocabularies of authority. The word "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat, related to *sedes*, "see") carries male-ecclesiastical authority forward into the office of professor and committee head (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). All bishops "until recently" have been men, and the kind of authority "incorporated in a professorship models a traditional view of masculinity" (Halewood, 2021: 130). Spender catches the prescriptive moment of grammar; Halewood catches the etymological sediment of office. *Third*, Halewood notes that Whittle (2006) reports that "personal pronouns are limiting and exclusionary for some of those who identify as 'trans'", who sometimes prefer *they* or neologisms such as "per" (cited Halewood, 2021: 128). This is one direction in which Spender's "minus male" frame is too narrow: the patriarchal structure she names produces marked subjects beyond the man/woman pair.

**A grammarian's counter-position.** Fowler insists that grammatical gender is "a grammatical term only", derived from Latin *genus*, and that to talk of biologically sexed gender is "either a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211). Halewood reports the position and then dismantles it: if the categories were really arbitrary, why are they not "up, down, middle"? And if they were "up, down, middle", would they not still carry hierarchy ("where 'up' is taken to be better than 'down'")? (Halewood, 2021: 112). The Fowler defence is exactly the "purely descriptive" approach Spender's archive of named prescriptive grammarians already refutes.

**The Irigarayan extension.** Irigaray, summarised by Halewood, pushes the structural claim deeper. "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). In French, the default noun for a doctor (*le médecin*) is masculine while the realm of medicine (*la médicine*) is feminine; the specifically female café-owner (*une cafetière*) is already occupied by a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119, citing Irigaray, 1993: 128 and 1994: 50). Spender's grammatical archive is reinforced by Irigaray's lexical one: the patriarchal structure operates not only in pronouns but in the very nouns assigned to jobs and to objects.

**Conclusion.** Spender's *Man Made Language* is a structural diagnosis that Halewood's chapter substantially vindicates and modestly refines. Vindicates: the male-as-norm pattern operates across French, Czech and English, across pronouns and job titles, across the Wilson-Kirkby grammarians and the Fowler defence. Refines: Spender's binary needs widening to recognise trans subjects (Whittle, 2006); her grammatical focus needs supplementing by Halewood's etymological one (the bishop's chair); and her programme of linguistic visibility needs joining to "a change in thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The diagnosis survives the refinement; the binary scaffolding alone does not.

### Essay 8 / Mock Q3: *What does de Beauvoir mean by "woman is the Other", and why does she treat that othering as linguistically structured?*

The question has two parts and I will take them in order. First, what does de Beauvoir's woman-as-other actually claim? Second, why is the othering inseparable from the structure of language? My answer: the claim is that woman exists as the *marked* term against an unmarked male default, and de Beauvoir treats this as linguistically structured because the asymmetry is built into the grammar and lexicon before any individual speaker chooses a word.

**Part one: what "woman is the Other" means.** De Beauvoir's argument in the Introduction to *The Second Sex* (1949) is condensed in two famous lines. "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). And: "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex; it goes without saying that he is a man… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The man is the *unmarked* term (the default human, the one whose particularity disappears from view); the woman is the *marked* term (the deviation that has to be named to enter discourse).

The "Other" is not just "another person" or "a different category". It is the *constitutive outside* of the universal subject: man defines himself in opposition to the woman whose particularity he has externalised. De Beauvoir is careful to add (and this matters for the second part of the answer) that "when I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). Woman-as-other is a *position* in a structure, not an essence. Lived experience, not biology, is the centre of gravity.

**Part two: why de Beauvoir treats the othering as linguistically structured.** Three reasons, in order of depth.

*First, the asymmetry is encoded in grammar.* Halewood draws out the mechanics. In French, *il* covers male persons, male dogs and the impersonal *it* (*il pleut*, "it is raining"); *ils* covers any mixed plural, however small the male presence. In Czech, Tichý's example shows the rule operating in conjugation: "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses feminine *šly*; add John, and the verb flips to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116). The masculine is at once *one* gender and *the* default. English does it more quietly with the generic *he*, with *mankind*, with *actor / actress*, with the residual *Mr* versus *Mrs / Miss*. The structure exists prior to any speaker's intention. To speak French, Czech or pre-reform English is to use a grammar that has already done the othering before the speaker arrives.

*Second, the asymmetry is historically prescribed, not natural.* This is where de Beauvoir's argument benefits from Dale Spender's archive. Wilson (1553) insisted that it was "more natural to place the man before the woman"; Kirkby (1746) ruled that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). The grammar de Beauvoir analyses was *made* by named human beings (male grammarians) who enforced male precedence. Spender's notion of man-made language is exactly the historical complement to de Beauvoir's structural one. The structure has authors.

*Third, the asymmetry is preserved by apparently neutral reforms.* If "actor" is the unmarked default, renaming actresses "actors" leaves the masculine yardstick intact, as the lecture warns (Lecture 4 slide 17). De Beauvoir's framework predicts that gender-neutral language can *re-mask* rather than dismantle the asymmetry (Irigaray's later worry). Halewood's example of "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat) shows the same thing for authority: replacing "chairman" with "chair" preserves an underlying masculine-clerical authority forward (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Language reform that does not touch the marked/unmarked structure simply hides the marking.

**Why this matters for sociology.** The linguistic structuring of woman-as-other matters because the unmarked subject in language tends to coincide with the unmarked subject in institutions. Halewood works through several cases. Job titles in French route the worker (*le médecin*) through the masculine while routing the realm of work (*la médicine*) through the feminine (Halewood, 2021: 118, citing Irigaray, 1993: 128). The female café-owner (*une cafetière*) is grammatically blocked because the word already names a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119). In English, the historical use of generic *he* (Dewey: "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his") and the residue of "men of science" (Whitehead, 1964: 29, cited Halewood, 2021: 119) show the same default at work in academic prose. Halewood's chair example collects the institutional version: the *cathedra* that the bishop sat in becomes the chair that "makes someone a professor" (Halewood, 2021: 129); the office carries a "masculine authority" forward even when "chairman" is renamed "chair" (Halewood, 2021: 130).

**Two limits worth flagging.** *First*, de Beauvoir's evidence is French; the analysis travels best to languages with grammatical gender. Halewood's Tichý example (Czech *šly* flipping to *šli* with one man's arrival, Halewood, 2021: 116) extends the case to a Slavic language. But the morphology is local; the mechanism is the more general claim that the unmarked default coincides with the male. *Second*, the marked/unmarked frame can re-marginalise trans and intersex subjects whom the binary man/woman scheme does not capture. Halewood notes that Whittle (2006) reports "personal pronouns are limiting and exclusionary for some of those who identify as 'trans'" who sometimes prefer "they" or neologisms such as "per" (cited Halewood, 2021: 128). Halewood himself argues that "man and woman are not ideas or entities that exist continually. They come to be at different points and different times" (Halewood, 2021: 132). The marked/unmarked grid needs to be processual, not fixed.

**Conclusion.** "Woman is the Other" names a structural position, not a property. De Beauvoir treats the othering as linguistically structured because the asymmetry is encoded in grammar, prescribed by male grammarians (Spender's archive), and preserved by apparently neutral reforms (Marsh's "actor", "chair"). Halewood's chapter shows the analysis travelling from de Beauvoir's French pronouns through Tichý's Czech conjugation to the Latin etymology of "chair", and ending with the warning that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The analysis is structural, comparative, and historical; the only revision the contemporary inheritance demands is to keep the marked/unmarked grid open beyond the man/woman pair de Beauvoir originally analysed.

### Essay 9 / Mock Q4: *Compare Ngũgĩ's argument for African-language writing with Achebe's defence of Africanised English.*

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o and Chinua Achebe agree on the diagnosis (English in Africa is not a neutral medium) and split on the prescription. Ngũgĩ argues for *displacement*: African writers should write in African languages. Achebe argues for *refashioning*: African writers should bend English to carry African experience. I will set out both positions on their own terms, identify the genuine disagreement between them, and argue that the disagreement is less zero-sum than it looks and that a serious decolonial-cultural programme needs both.

**The shared diagnosis.** Both writers accept that European languages entered Africa as instruments of empire. Ngũgĩ states it most starkly: "English, French, and Portuguese came to the Third World to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Mission schools elevated the conqueror's language, suppressed African languages, and produced an elite "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Achebe does not dispute this history. He accepts that he writes in the coloniser's tongue. There is no innocence in the medium. The disagreement is what to do next.

**Ngũgĩ's argument for African-language writing.** Ngũgĩ's position rests on a *carrier* theory of language. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), so writing in English carries English memory (English categories, English religion, English economic concepts) not African memory. The colonial school made this concrete: children were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), and were drilled in textbooks ("Oxford Readers for Africa") that taught them about John and Joan, Oxford, Reading, and the river Thames before the names of other towns in Kenya (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34). The result, Ngũgĩ argues, is an elite "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). The remedy he proposes is structural: write in African languages, and elevate one of them (he proposes Kiswahili because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41) as a candidate world language. The condition for any genuine "world" language, Ngũgĩ adds, is "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). Without that prior equality, the world language is just a colonial one wearing a global mask.

**Achebe's defence of Africanised English.** Achebe's position is pragmatic and stylistic. Writing in one of the official Nigerian languages (Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba) reaches a smaller group; many Africans, as the lecture slide notes, are acquiring English or French as their first language (slide 23). The writer should therefore "use English in a way that brings out his message best without altering the language so much that its value as a medium of international exchange will be lost. He should aim at fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). And, crucially, he should not aspire to native-speaker English: "Can he ever learn to use it like a native speaker? I should say, 'I hope not.' It is neither necessary nor desirable for him to be able to do so. The price a world language must be prepared to pay is submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The slide note that the title of *Things Fall Apart* (1958) quotes W.B. Yeats, an Irish poet (slide 22), illustrates the refashioning move at the level of intertext: a coloniser's language carrying a different post-colonial tradition.

**The genuine disagreement.** Three real splits. *Purity versus hybridity.* Ngũgĩ's strategy is to leave the contaminated language for its original site of use; Achebe's is to refashion it from inside. *Audience.* Ngũgĩ writes for African-language-speaking masses; Achebe writes for a continental and global Anglophone audience that includes the African elite. *Theory of change.* Ngũgĩ believes decolonising the mind requires linguistic decolonisation at the level of medium. Achebe believes meaningful decolonisation can proceed *through* a refashioned English; the empire's tools, properly retooled, serve the colonised.

**Where they converge.** Both insist that the colonial encounter was unequal and unjust. The slide note that Achebe "is not against a common language… he just thinks it should not be one with a history of inequality and colonialism" (slide 21, on Ngũgĩ) applies to Achebe too: neither accepts the view that English is innocently universal. Achebe's "I hope not" is a refusal of cultural assimilation as fierce in spirit as Ngũgĩ's refusal of the language itself. And both recognise that some lingua franca is needed for cross-national communication. They disagree only on whether it should be a coloniser's language refashioned (Achebe) or an African language elevated (Ngũgĩ).

**My evaluation: both/and.** Three reasons the disagreement is less zero-sum than it looks. *First*, the institutional setting matters. In primary schooling and the recovery of Ngũgĩ's "collective memory bank" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), Ngũgĩ's position is overwhelmingly more useful. In international publishing and pan-African political debate, Achebe's position is more practical. *Second*, Ngũgĩ himself accepts the democratic side of English: it has "a democratic tradition" carried by "Shakespeare, Milton, Blake, Shelley, Dickens, Conrad, Bernard Shaw, Graham Greene", and is "part of the common heritage of humankind" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). He is therefore not absolutist about displacement, just insistent on conditions of equality. *Third*, the strongest synthesis is genuinely additive. Ngũgĩ's strategy protects African languages from extinction; Achebe's expands what English can carry. Together they shrink the colonial monopoly on prestige.

**Internal-metropolitan applications.** Ngũgĩ's framework explicitly travels to Britain. He cites Lyn Marierid of the Welsh Language Society on the "yuppiefication" of rural Wales (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36) and concludes that "languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The lecture's three-name slide (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish, slide 26) is the same point in the most minimal form. Achebe's complementary move (the dominant language must "submit to many different kinds of use", Achebe, 1997: 347) applies inside the British Isles too, at the interface between revived languages and English.

**Conclusion.** Ngũgĩ and Achebe disagree on whether the writer's job is to refuse the coloniser's language or to refashion it. The disagreement marks a productive division of labour rather than a contradiction. A serious decolonial-cultural programme needs the African-language schooling and elevation Ngũgĩ demands *and* the refashioned English Achebe defends, and neither alone is enough to dislodge the colonial attitude from its linguistic infrastructure.

### Essay 10 / Mock Q5: *"Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Critically explain this slogan with reference to at least two authors.*

The slogan is the load-bearing line of the whole lecture, repeated on slides 4 and (in Halewood's chapter) at 116. It claims a tight coupling between linguistic structure and worldly distribution. I will argue that the slogan is correct in three precise senses (reflection, reproduction, and constitution), and I will defend it across two authors in detail (Spender and Ngũgĩ) with supporting moves from Halewood, de Beauvoir, and Achebe.

**The slogan unpacked.** The lecturer's framing is structural rather than empirical. Language is "not a neutral mirror of the world"; it "classifies, evaluates, and is a mode of power" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Halewood gives the slogan a complementary formulation: "language is part of the world. Problems with inequality in language are also problems in the world" (Halewood, 2021: 116). The two formulations sit together: language is not external to the world, so inequalities encoded in language are part of the world's inequalities.

**Spender on the male-as-norm.** Spender's *Man Made Language* (1980) is the clearest application of the slogan in the lecture. English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145), and this grammatical asymmetry corresponds to a social asymmetry between man (the default human) and woman (the marked deviation). Spender's archive is named and dated. Wilson (1553) insisted that it was "more natural to place the man before the woman, as for example in male and female, husband and wife, brother and sister, son and daughter" (Spender, 1980: 147). Kirkby (1746) formulated his "Rule Number Twenty One": "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The slogan reads cleanly: the prescriptive male precedence in grammar is an enforcement of male precedence in social life, and reform of one cannot be separated from reform of the other.

**Ngũgĩ on the imperial language.** Ngũgĩ's *Moving the Centre* makes the slogan colonial. "The encounter between English and most so-called Third World languages did not occur under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31); English, French and Portuguese came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Two complementary mechanisms enforced the inequality: suppression of indigenous languages (children "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) and elevation of the conqueror's language (the school hymn "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was the children's own languages, Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The slogan applies precisely: humiliation enforced through language was humiliation enforced through the world; the elevation of English in the curriculum tracked the elevation of English-speaking institutions in the political economy.

**Three senses of the slogan.** Halewood's processual conclusion ("language and the world become together", Halewood, 2021: 132) lets us distinguish three readings of the slogan, all of which the texts defend.

*Reflection.* Linguistic asymmetries mirror social ones that already exist. Spender's grammarians did not invent patriarchy; they prescribed a male precedence whose social conditions of force lay outside the grammar. Ngũgĩ's English carried "racist and negative images of the conquered nation, particularly in its literature" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) because British imperial power had produced those images first.

*Reproduction.* Linguistic asymmetries carry social asymmetries forward when their original cause has lapsed. Halewood's "chair" example is the cleanest: even after universities formally open chairs to women, the word *chair* carries forward the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's *cathedra* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Ngũgĩ's diagnosis that the English language remains "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) decades after independence is the same logic: English keeps the colonial inequality in place after the soldiers have left.

*Constitution.* Some inequalities are partly produced through language. Tichý's Czech case (the feminine plural *šly* flipping to masculine *šli* the moment one man joins all the women of the world, Halewood, 2021: 116) is "absurd" precisely because it shows the grammar telling us who counts as a default human every time we conjugate. Irigaray's claim that "sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20) is the deepest version of this reading.

**Counter-currents.** The slogan is not a counsel of despair. Achebe insists that the world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347), and his "I hope not" (to native-speaker English, Achebe, 1997: 347) is a refusal of assimilation. Ngũgĩ proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language precisely because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). Halewood's account of singular *they* (long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, cited via Marsh, Halewood, 2021: 128) shows that prescribed-out usages can be revived. These counter-currents demonstrate that "problems in language" can be acted upon, even if (per Halewood) "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Critical limits.** Two caveats matter. *First*, the slogan should not collapse into "fix the words and you fix the world". Halewood is firm: "what is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The Marsh "actor" reform (Halewood, 2021: 120) shows the danger of treating vocabulary reform as a substitute for changing the social default (which was historically masculine). *Second*, the slogan should not produce a strong-Whorfian othering. The lecturer is careful that "Eskimo" is itself the wrong name (slide 4; Samson, 2003), and that the wonderment at indigenous "different worlds" reproduces a Western "us / them" division (slide 8). The slogan must travel without becoming colonial in its turn.

**Conclusion.** "Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" is correct in its strong reading. Inequalities in language reflect, reproduce, and partly constitute inequalities in the world. Spender shows this for gender; Ngũgĩ shows this for empire; Halewood shows this for the etymology of authority; Irigaray shows this at the level of sexual difference. The sociologist's task, as all four insist in different keys, is to keep the three readings analytically apart while showing how they hang together, and to refuse both the complacent answer that language is "just words" and the simplistic answer that fixing the words fixes the world.

### Essay 11 / Mock Q6: *Is linguistic determinism a coherent thesis or a category error?*

My short answer is: linguistic determinism is *not* a coherent thesis. It is a category error, a metaphysical claim about cognitive moulding masquerading as an empirical claim about language use. The category error can be diagnosed precisely, and once it is diagnosed the useful work the thesis was meant to do is taken over by linguistic relativity plus an account of institutional power. I will defend this position in five moves.

**The thesis.** Linguistic determinism is the strong version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. The lecture's framing is decisive: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231). The image presents a strict implication: what a language fails to encode, its speakers cannot think. The textbook example ("Eskimos have 50 words for snow", therefore Inuit speakers perceive snow with finer discrimination than English speakers, Lecture 4 slide 8) is the determinism reading.

**Move one: translation refutes the strong claim.** If language fully determined thought, translation would be impossible. Ngũgĩ describes how European languages "have always communicated with one another such that today it is possible to read nearly all the classics of Russian, French, or German literature and philosophy in any of those languages, thanks to the art of translation" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 40), even as he complains that translation between African languages is structurally under-resourced. Translation is not metaphysically impossible; it is institutionally under-funded. The very existence of multilingual speakers, who hold several "moulds" simultaneously without their minds shattering, undermines the determinism claim. Ngũgĩ writes (in this very chapter) in Gĩkũyũ and English; Achebe writes in English with the explicit aim of carrying "his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347) in it.

**Move two: plasticity refutes it again.** English speakers without a single-word lexicon for "wet snow" build the concept out of phrases ("good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing") as the lecturer notes (Lecture 4 slide 9). The slide is the strongest internal refutation of the strong claim: if language determined thought, English speakers should be unable to discriminate kinds of snow, and the slide shows they plainly can. Spender's whole programme (to make women linguistically visible by inventing or repurposing vocabulary, Spender, 1980: 162) presupposes the same plasticity. So does Halewood's account of singular *they*, prescribed out by the grammarians but attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, and contested in current usage (cited via Marsh, Halewood, 2021: 128).

**Move three: the political risk diagnoses the category error.** This is the heart of the case. Taken straight, determinism becomes "they live in a very different world from 'us'" (Lecture 4 slide 8). It sets up an "us / them" division in which the speakers of one language are exotic others compared to a Western "us". Ngũgĩ's framework reads this directly: this is the colonial attitude in epistemological dress. The "Eskimo" example is itself an externally imposed label (Samson, 2003), and the wonderment at indigenous perception is a continuation of the missionary outlook: the indigenous group as the curious other against the implicit Western benchmark. The category error is that determinism *describes a political relation in cognitive vocabulary*. A sociologically responsible account of language and power cannot rest on a framework that reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

**Move four: relativity does the useful work.** The weak version of Sapir-Whorf (linguistic relativity) keeps the useful insight (language channels attention, shapes categories, makes some thoughts easier than others) without the unsupportable claim that it *fixes* the limits of thought. Spender's notion of man-made language and the "plus male / minus male" classification scheme (Spender, 1980: 145), de Beauvoir's woman-as-other and the marked/unmarked logic, Ngũgĩ's decolonising the mind and the "carrier" theory of language as "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and Halewood's processual reading all rest on linguistic relativity, not determinism. Each thinker grants that speakers can refashion their inventories: that is precisely how Achebe's Africanised English (Achebe, 1997: 347) and the Welsh case Ngũgĩ describes through Marierid (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36) proceed.

**Move five: institutional gatekeeping, not cognitive moulding.** One last steelman of the determinism position. Even if speakers can in principle coin new vocabulary, the institutions that publish, teach and credential a language shape what concepts most speakers will encounter. Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) names a real structural constraint: African-origin languages occupy no place of honour at the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38); scientific publishing and creative arts achievement are "stored in those languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). I accept this point, but it is properly an account of institutional power, not of cognitive moulding. Better to call it linguistic *gatekeeping* than linguistic determinism. The gatekeeping is real and reformable by policy; the moulding is metaphysical and unreformable by design. Spender's archive of Wilson and Kirkby (Spender, 1980: 147-148) is a gender-gatekeeping case in the same idiom.

**What does the lecture's own example show?** The "Eskimo words for snow" example is the strongest case the lecture entertains for determinism, and the lecture itself dismantles it on three grounds (slides 4, 8, 9). First, "Eskimo" is "neither accurate nor appropriate" as a label (slide 4; Samson, 2003): the example is constructed by the namer, not the named. Second, the wonderment at "a very different world from 'us'" assumes a Western "us" against an exoticised "them" (slide 8), reproducing colonial othering. Third, English speakers use *phrases* ("good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing", slide 9) where Eskimo speakers are imagined to use single words; the supposed cognitive gap is an artefact of how the comparison is framed.

**Contemporary applications inside the materials.** *Marsh's "actor" reform* (Halewood, 2021: 120) is a clean test case for the institutional reading: the editorial fiat changes vocabulary without (alone) changing the social default of who counts as a worker in theatre and cinema. *Halewood's "chair" example* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) shows institutional sedimentation operating beneath a renaming. *The colonial school* (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32-33) shows gatekeeping at the level of pedagogy: it forced the swap of memory bank, not the elimination of any cognitive faculty.

**Conclusion.** Linguistic determinism is a category error: it dresses a political and institutional relation in the language of cognitive impossibility, and in doing so reproduces the colonial othering the lecturer flags on slide 8. The useful work the determinism claim wants to do is done better by linguistic relativity plus an account of institutional power: Spender on prescription, Ngũgĩ on imperial language policy, Halewood on lexical sedimentation. Language does not determine thought, but it powerfully channels it; and the channels are set by institutions, not by metaphysical moulds.

### M7: *How does Halewood use the figure of the "chair" to extend de Beauvoir and Irigaray into Anglophone usage?*

Halewood introduces the "chair" example to answer a specific objection: the claim that de Beauvoir's and Irigaray's analyses of grammatical gender apply only to languages like French or Czech, with their explicit masculine/feminine morphology, and not to English (Halewood, 2021: 119). The "chair" example shows that English carries the same patriarchal structure beneath an apparently neutral vocabulary, and that the structure operates through institutional history rather than through morphology alone. I will set out the example, locate it in the de Beauvoir / Irigaray argument, and assess what it adds.

**The de Beauvoir / Irigaray argument, briefly.** De Beauvoir's Introduction to *The Second Sex* contains the foundational claim: "man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). A man does not have to announce that he writes "as a man"; a woman is "immediately seen as a 'woman writer'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Irigaray takes the analysis into grammar: *il* covers both "he" and impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pleut*); *ils* serves any mixed plural (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). In Czech, Tichý's example shows the masculine *šli* taking over the feminine *šly* the moment one man joins all the women of the world (Halewood, 2021: 116). Job titles follow the same pattern: the default noun for a doctor (*le médecin*) is masculine, and the would-be female café-owner (*une cafetière*) is already a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119).

**The Anglophone objection.** "Irigaray's (and perhaps de Beauvoir's) analysis might have some force when analysing Francophone societies, but it has limited relevance to the Anglophone world" (Halewood, 2021: 119). Halewood needs an English-language case to refute this. He gives two. First, gendered residue in academic English: Dewey's "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his", and Whitehead's "men of science" (cited Halewood, 2021: 119). These are residual, however, and a defender of the objection might say English has moved on. The second case is the "chair", and it is the structural one.

**The "chair" example.** "Chair" comes from Latin *cathedra*, the seat of a bishop, related to *sedes* (the "see", the area over which a bishop has authority) (Halewood, 2021: 128). "Chairs and seats are tied up with the authority of the Christian Church" (Halewood, 2021: 129). Professors hold "chairs": Stephen Hawking held the Lucasian Chair in Mathematics at the University of Cambridge, founded in 1663 by Henry Lucas, and previously held by Isaac Newton (Halewood, 2021: 129). The professor's chair "is its derivation, in that Western universities were originally institutions within the Christian Church, designed to further theological knowledge and train *men* for their roles in the Church" (Halewood, 2021: 129). The cloisters and quadrangles of Oxford and Cambridge are "reminders of this" (Halewood, 2021: 129). The committee chair inherits the professorial chair. So even though "chairman" has been retired in favour of "chair", "in one important sense, the authority held by professors is male *and* masculine" (Halewood, 2021: 130).

**What the example adds.** Three things.

*First, the example transposes the Irigarayan structure into English without needing French-style grammatical gender.* The patriarchal structure operates here not through the morphology of the noun but through the etymology and the institutional history. The unmarked English word *chair* carries an unmarked masculine authority; renaming "chairman" as "chair" does not dismantle the asymmetry.

*Second, the example shows that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126).* The reform from "chairman" to "chair" is precisely the kind of vocabulary reform that the Marsh case (actress to actor, Halewood, 2021: 120) exemplifies. In both cases, the apparently neutral term carries the masculine yardstick forward. Reform of the word without reform of the institution is symbolic.

*Third, the example warns against the "perfectly true language" of language reform.* Halewood quotes Haraway: "all dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130). The point is that each instance of language-and-world entanglement "needs to be dealt with on its own merits" (Halewood, 2021: 130). The "chair" is not a generic case of patriarchal language; it is the specific case of a specific institutional history.

**Spender's complement.** Spender's archive of prescriptive grammarians (Wilson 1553, Kirkby 1746, Fowler 1926) tracks moments at which male precedence was *explicitly* legislated into English grammar (Spender, 1980: 147-148; Fowler, 1926: 211, cited Halewood, 2021: 111). Halewood's "chair" example tracks the *silent* version: not a moment of prescription but a long historical inheritance from the bishop's seat to the committee head. Together, the two cases cover the visible and invisible faces of male-as-norm.

**Ngũgĩ's complement.** Halewood's etymology of "chair" runs through the Christian Church, the same institution Ngũgĩ identifies as the partner of "the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) in colonial language policy. The "chair" example is therefore a small but tight bridge between the gender argument (Spender, de Beauvoir, Irigaray) and the colonial argument (Ngũgĩ, Achebe): both inheritances arrive in contemporary English through Church-mediated authority, even when the speaker is unaware of it.

**A processual reading.** Halewood ends the chapter with a processual conclusion: "language and the world become together; in doing so, they express those elements that later come to be called natural and social. The perspective needs to be shifted" (Halewood, 2021: 132). The "chair" example is a vivid case of this becoming-together: the bishop's authority and the professor's chair become together; the committee's chair and the committee's authority become together. None is fixed by nature; all are produced by the joint history of language and the world.

**Conclusion.** Halewood's "chair" example is a precision instrument. It refutes the objection that de Beauvoir and Irigaray are Francophone-specific by showing that English carries the same patriarchal structure through institutional history rather than morphology. It shows what "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126) means concretely: rename "chairman" to "chair" and the masculine-ecclesiastical authority comes along for the ride. And it links the gender and colonial arguments through the Christian Church as the shared historical-institutional carrier of both.

### M8: *Critically explain how the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is presented and qualified by the lecturer.*

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis structures the first third of Lecture 4 (slides 3-9). The lecturer presents it in two forms (linguistic relativity and linguistic determinism), illustrates both through the "Eskimo words for snow" example, and then dismantles the strong reading on three grounds. I will work through the presentation slide by slide and assess what survives the lecturer's own qualifications.

**Presentation.** The lecturer introduces linguistic relativity through Brown (slide 3): "the belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230), so that "speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230). The example follows (slide 4): "Eskimos" are said to have "over 50 words for 'snow'", suggesting that they see and live in a different world. Slide 5 spells the relativity reading out: the larger snow lexicon "points to the fact that they live in a very different world from 'us'", and this is named the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis after Edward Sapir (1884-1939) and Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897-1941). Slide 6 introduces the stronger version, linguistic determinism: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231). The strong reading is that what a language fails to encode, its speakers cannot think.

**First qualification: language is rendering, not reflection (slide 7).** "Any use of human language expresses human thoughts about the world." Each language is "a specific rendering of a specific 'reality'", not "an accurate or objective picture". Humans with language "seem to have lost any innocent contact with the world, as our language always 'gets in the way'". The point is that *every* language refracts reality; no language is the world's natural mirror. So the Eskimo-vs-English comparison is not between a language that grasps snow correctly and one that does not, but between two specific renderings.

**Second qualification: us-and-them framing (slide 8).** The lecturer flags the political risk of the original example. "They live in a very different world from 'us'" assumes an "us" and a "them". Who is the "them"? "The 'others'". The danger is "assigning completely different 'realities' or worldviews to speakers of so-called indigenous groups (in the Amazon rainforest, for example)". The Western outlook becomes the implicit benchmark, and the so-called indigenous group is the exotic other. The slide explicitly connects this to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project. The slogan on slide 4 that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" is doing double duty here: the inequality is inside the example itself.

**Third qualification: words versus phrases (slide 9).** The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis "assumes that single words are the main way of indicating the things of the world". The lecturer points out that English speakers use *phrases* ("good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing") where Eskimo speakers are imagined to use distinct words. English may have fewer single-word labels but has "as many possible phrases for snow as they do" (slide 9). The supposed cognitive gap is an artefact of the unit of analysis.

**Fourth qualification: the label itself is wrong (slide 4 + Samson, 2003).** Before any of the above qualifications land, the lecturer has already scare-quoted "Eskimos": "neither accurate nor appropriate" as a name for a group. This is "a first indication of the power inherent in language to try to 'capture' groups of people" (slide 4). The example is named by the namer, not the named. Samson's (2003) *A Way of Life That Does Not Exist: Canada and the Extinguishment of the Innu* is the suggested reading.

**What survives.** The weak version of the hypothesis (linguistic relativity) survives all four qualifications. Speakers of a shared language do live in an agreement to perceive the world in a certain way (Brown, 1968: 230); language carves the world into categories, and those categories are unequal in consequence. This weak version is what Spender uses ("plus male / minus male", Spender, 1980: 145) and what Ngũgĩ uses (language as "the collective memory bank of a people", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). The strong version (determinism) does not survive: speakers can build phrases (slide 9), they can translate across languages (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 40), and the strong example reproduces colonial othering (slide 8).

**How the rest of the lecture uses the hypothesis.** The qualified-relativity reading underwrites the Spender section: English's "plus male / minus male" classification (Spender, 1980: 145) is not a fixed mould but a prescriptively-installed agreement that has produced an asymmetry between man (the default human) and woman (the marked deviation). It also underwrites the Ngũgĩ section: the colonial school's elevation of English and suppression of African languages (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-33) produced a real change in the agreement-to-perceive without producing a metaphysical cognitive impossibility. And it underwrites the actor/actress critique (slide 17): treating *actor* as universal is itself a kind of "agreement to perceive" that hides the masculine yardstick beneath an apparently neutral term.

**Halewood's reformulation.** Halewood gives the slogan its sharpest form: "language is part of the world. Problems with inequality in language are also problems in the world" (Halewood, 2021: 116). This is the weak-relativity reading taken structurally: language is not external to the world it describes, so inequalities encoded in it are part of the world's inequalities, not a separate problem to be solved later by tidying the vocabulary.

**Conclusion.** The lecturer presents Sapir-Whorf in order to retain its useful insight (language channels attention and classifies) while dismantling its dangerous extension (determinism plus colonial othering). The four qualifications (language renders rather than reflects; us/them framing reproduces inequality; single-word focus misses phrasing; the very label "Eskimo" is an external imposition) reduce the hypothesis to its defensible weak form. That weak form is exactly what Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ, Achebe and Halewood rely on for the rest of the lecture.

### M9: *How convincing do you find Spender's claim that English is structurally patriarchal?*

I find Spender's claim broadly convincing (*as a structural claim about the prescriptive history of standard written English and its inherited categories*) but only partially convincing as a claim about English as a whole. Three qualifications matter: the claim is anglocentric, it under-weights speaker plasticity, and it presupposes a binary gender frame that contemporary trans politics has rightly contested. I will set out Spender's argument, mark what I find compelling, and identify where the case needs supplementing.

**Spender's claim, briefly.** Dale Spender's notion of man-made language is structural rather than anecdotal. The core thesis from *Man Made Language* (1980) is that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The male is the default human; the female is a deviation marked by additional morphology (*actress*, *waitress*, *poetess*) or by extra honorifics (*Mrs* and *Miss* mark marital status; *Mr* does not). Spender's historical evidence is specific. Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147). Kirkby (1746) in his "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The "man-made" label is not metaphorical: it tracks an archive of male grammarians prescribing male precedence. Her programme follows: "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**Why I find the core claim convincing.** Three reasons.

*First, the historical archive is decisive.* Spender's case does not rest on a hunch about contemporary English. It rests on a paper trail. Named grammarians, dated rules, and explicit justifications for male precedence ("more comprehensive than the female") are evidence of prescription, not of natural drift. The "man-made" label is descriptively accurate: English was made, at the level of standardised grammar, by men who prescribed male precedence. Halewood adds Fowler to that archive: Fowler's call for "doctress, teacheress, singeress and danceress" (cited Halewood, 2021: 120) is the same prescriptive impulse two centuries on, this time multiplying female forms rather than absorbing them, but operating on the same male-as-norm assumption.

*Second, the contemporary asymmetries persist.* The Marsh case (Halewood, 2021: 120) is the cleanest case: even after "actress" is retired in favour of "actor", the masculine yardstick (most occupations were historically male) is preserved beneath the apparently neutral term, so we end up "lumping [women] in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood's "chair" example (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat, Halewood, 2021: 128-130) shows the same sedimentation in a vocabulary of authority: rename "chairman" to "chair" and the masculine-ecclesiastical history carries forward unchecked.

*Third, de Beauvoir's social ontology fits.* De Beauvoir's woman-as-other (man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15)) is exactly the social complement to Spender's grammatical case. If grammar were sociologically arbitrary, the correspondence between grammatical unmarkedness (masculine) and social unmarkedness (male as default human) would be a coincidence. Halewood's Tichý example (Czech *šly* flipping to *šli* with one man's arrival, Halewood, 2021: 116) and Irigaray's French examples (*il pleut*, *il fait chaud*, *le médecin*, *cafetière*, Halewood, 2021: 114-119) show that the correspondence holds across multiple gendered languages.

**Three qualifications that limit the claim's convincingness.**

*First, anglocentrism.* Spender's evidence base is overwhelmingly English. Halewood acknowledges the objection that "Irigaray's (and perhaps de Beauvoir's) analysis might have some force when analysing Francophone societies, but it has limited relevance to the Anglophone world" (Halewood, 2021: 119). His "chair" example is the structural refutation of that objection; without it, Spender's anglocentric framing would carry the same weight as Irigaray's French-centric one. So this is a real limit on the claim *as Spender herself states it*, even if Halewood plugs the gap.

*Second, speaker plasticity.* Spender's framing slightly understates how the language is being continuously *re*-made. Achebe's intuition that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) applies inside English itself. The singular *they* (long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, Halewood, 2021: 128) has been revived. The Marsh abolition of "actress" (Halewood, 2021: 120) is a contestation in the opposite direction. Spender herself is not deterministic (her programme is precisely to "defy" the rules and "recycle" the symbols, Spender, 1980: 162) but the "structurally patriarchal" framing can sound more fixed than her own argument allows.

*Third, the binary frame.* Spender's "plus male / minus male" scaffolding presupposes binary gender. Halewood reports Whittle's (2006) observation that "personal pronouns are limiting and exclusionary for some of those who identify as 'trans'" who sometimes prefer "they" or neologisms such as "per" (cited Halewood, 2021: 128). Halewood's own conclusion is processual: "man and woman are not ideas or entities that exist continually. They come to be at different points and different times" (Halewood, 2021: 132). The patriarchal structure Spender names operates not on a man/woman pair but on a marked-versus-unmarked grid; trans and intersexed subjects (Halewood, 2021: 123, 132) are also marked. The diagnosis survives the qualification; the binary scaffolding does not.

**Three internal-to-the-materials tests.** *The actor/actress case* (Halewood, 2021: 120; Lecture 4 slides 16-17) is the cleanest contemporary case: Spender's structural claim correctly predicts both the reform impulse and the difficulty of the reform. *The Wilson and Kirkby archive* (Spender, 1980: 147-148) is the historical case: the prescription is overt, named, and dated, exactly as Spender claims. *The "chair" etymology* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) is the institutional-history case: the word carries the bishop's seat into the committee, beneath an apparently neutral surface.

**Caveats from Halewood and Ngũgĩ.** Halewood is right that "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Spender's slogan that women must become "linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162) is a precondition for, not a substitute for, structural change. And Ngũgĩ's framework reminds us that patriarchy is one of several hierarchies operating through linguistic structure; the colonial-attitude one operates by similar mechanisms (suppression plus elevation, Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32), and the two intersect in any institution carried by a language with both colonial and patriarchal histories.

**Conclusion.** Spender's claim that English is structurally patriarchal is convincing as a structural-historical diagnosis. The qualifications (anglocentrism, speaker plasticity, binary scaffolding) do not refute the case; they refine it, with Halewood doing most of the refinement. The strongest version is: standard English was prescriptively patriarchalised by named grammarians, the prescription continues to shape contemporary defaults (actress/actor, chair), and the structure is reformable by deliberate intervention provided the world changes alongside the word. Less than that understates the historical archive; more than that overstates the rigidity of language.

### M10: *Using the work of at least two authors, explain how language operates as a vehicle of inequality.*

I will draw on Dale Spender and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, with supporting moves from de Beauvoir, Achebe, and Halewood, to argue that language operates as a vehicle of inequality through a single mechanism: it encodes an unmarked default subject (the male; the European) and makes everyone else a marked deviation. The mechanism runs through two domains in parallel (gender and empire) and through three sites within each (grammar, vocabulary, institutional history). Reforming the language requires both vocabulary change and the change in "thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126) that vocabulary reform alone cannot deliver.

**The shared mechanism.** Spender identifies the structural template. English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir generalises in the language of social ontology: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Ngũgĩ extends the template from gender to empire. European languages came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) and remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). The unmarked male and the unmarked European are produced by the same logic.

**Site one: grammar.** The clearest gender case is grammatical. Spender's archive shows Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" and Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Halewood adds the cross-linguistic cases. In French, *il* covers both "he" and impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pleut*); *ils* serves any mixed plural (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). In Czech, Tichý's example shows the feminine plural *šly* flipping to masculine *šli* the moment one man tags along with all the women of the world (Halewood, 2021: 116). The grammar tells the speaker who counts as the default human every time the verb is conjugated.

**Site two: vocabulary.** The clearest gender case is the actor/actress reform that David Marsh imposed at *The Guardian* (Halewood, 2021: 120; Lecture 4 slides 16-17). On its face, retiring "actress" treats female actors as equals. But, as the lecturer warns on slide 17, most occupations "such as that of doctor, teacher, actor were carried out by men", so the apparently neutral "actor" provides "a yardstick which may not immediately appear to refer to the male, or to be masculine"; "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term". The vocabulary reform pays "lip-service to the problem… but might also ignore the more fundamental problem" (slide 17). The colonial analogue is Ngũgĩ's account of English in postcolonial Africa: continuing to use English in administration, commerce and justice (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) is the institutional equivalent of Marsh's reform: the apparently neutral medium carries the historical hierarchy beneath it.

**Site three: institutional history.** Halewood's "chair" example is the case. "Chair" derives from Latin *cathedra* (the bishop's seat) and is related to *sedes* (the diocese, the "see") (Halewood, 2021: 128). Professors hold "chairs" (Stephen Hawking held the Lucasian Chair, founded 1663 and previously held by Isaac Newton, Halewood, 2021: 129); the committee chair inherits this authority. "All bishops (until recently) have been men", and the kind of authority "incorporated in a professorship models a traditional view of masculinity" (Halewood, 2021: 130). So renaming "chairman" to "chair" preserves a male-ecclesiastical authority beneath an apparently neutral surface. Ngũgĩ's colonial analogue is the imperial school. The school instilled English as "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), with children "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" for using their own languages (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Decades after independence, the curriculum still routes knowledge through the coloniser's tongue.

**Why two authors are enough.** Spender supplies the gender mechanism (male-as-norm encoded at the level of grammar, vocabulary, and prescriptive history). Ngũgĩ supplies the colonial mechanism (European-languages-as-norm encoded at the level of institutional history, education, and international forums). The two patterns are not parallel; they intersect. Halewood's "chair" example bridges them: the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's seat is *both* a gender story and a Christian-imperial story; the Western university was "originally [an] institution within the Christian Church, designed to further theological knowledge and train *men* for their roles in the Church" (Halewood, 2021: 129).

**Counter-currents.** Vocabulary is plastic. Spender herself calls for women to be "linguistically visible" by creating new symbols and recycling old ones (Spender, 1980: 162). Halewood's discussion of singular *they* (long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, via Marsh, Halewood, 2021: 128) shows that prescribed-out usages can be revived. Achebe's principle that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) applies to refashioning English at every level. But Halewood's warning is decisive: "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Conclusion.** Language operates as a vehicle of inequality through one mechanism (the unmarked default) applied in three sites (grammar, vocabulary, institutional history) and across two registers (gender and empire). Spender and Ngũgĩ, supplemented by de Beauvoir, Achebe and Halewood, supply the analytic vocabulary. Reforming the words is necessary but must be paired with structural change in the institutions the words serve.

### M11: *Can sociology be done in English without reproducing the colonial mind?*

I will argue: only partially, and only if it is done self-consciously. Sociology in English is a contradiction the discipline cannot fully escape but can practise more or less honestly. My position is closer to Achebe's than to Ngũgĩ's: the right strategy is to refashion English sociological writing from inside while acknowledging that the limit case (a fully decolonised sociology) requires institutional changes the discipline cannot deliver alone.

**Why the question is sharp.** Ngũgĩ's claim is that European languages came to the Third World "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) and that even after independence they remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Sociology, as an academic discipline taught and published mostly in English, is part of this linguistic infrastructure. Its categories ("society", "modernity", "the state", "race", "class", "informal economy", "third world", "global south") were largely forged in English (and French and German) and they carry English (and French and German) assumptions about the social. Doing sociology in English is, on Ngũgĩ's account, doing sociology in the language of the master-discourse the discipline claims to analyse.

**Three ways the colonial mind enters English-language sociology.**

*First, the conceptual vocabulary.* The discipline's foundational categories assume a European trajectory. The Ngũgĩ reading's most pointed line on this is that English in colonial Africa became "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), so that even when independence came, "the results of our research into science, technology and of our achievements in the creative arts are stored in those languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Sociology inherits this storage. When English-language sociology measures other societies, it measures them against an unmarked Anglo-European benchmark, mirroring de Beauvoir's woman-as-other at the scale of nations.

*Second, the prestige economy of publishing.* Anglophone publishing dominates citation, hiring and promotion. Ngũgĩ's complaint that the African elite is "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38) reads precisely as a diagnosis of disciplinary publishing: the path to the citing centre runs through English. Kenneth Baker's report on the Russians of Novosibirsk learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) extends the pattern: English is the language of escape from "backwardness", and "backwardness" is anything not-English. The discipline's own institutions enforce the colonial attitude.

*Third, the everyday register of academic English.* Spender's archive of male grammarians prescribing male precedence (Spender, 1980: 147-148) has a sociological-prose descendant. Halewood's analysis of "chair" (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) generalises: the chairs of sociology in Western universities inherit the bishop's *cathedra*, an authority whose ecclesiastical-male history operates beneath apparently neutral academic vocabulary. The Dewey and Whitehead residue ("houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his"; "men of science", cited Halewood, 2021: 119) shows that academic English carried male defaults into the twentieth century.

**Three things sociology in English can still do honestly.** Three reasons I do not draw a despairing conclusion.

*First, Achebe's refashioning strategy applies inside the discipline.* The African writer must use "an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Sociologists can do the same: write English sociology that refuses to flatten local categories into English ones, and insists on Achebe's "I hope not" answer to whether non-native speakers should aspire to native-like academic English (Achebe, 1997: 347). The "submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) Achebe demanded of a world language is achievable inside sociological writing.

*Second, self-critical attention to vocabulary.* The lecturer's caution about "Eskimo" (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8) generalises: every English-language sociological term should be interrogated for its colonial sediment. Halewood's processual conclusion is the right meta-rule: "language and the world become together" (Halewood, 2021: 132), and Haraway's warning that "all dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130) blocks the assumption that a cleansed vocabulary is achievable in principle. We can refuse the pretence that the current vocabulary is neutral. Halewood's example of the move from "coloureds" or "Asians" to "ethnic communities" makes the point sharply: the renaming is real but it does not mean that "the way they think and act in the world has changed" (Halewood, 2021: 131).

*Third, structural reforms inside the discipline.* The work that vocabulary reform cannot do, scholarly infrastructure can. Ngũgĩ's call for the "art of translation and interpretation" to be "an integral subject in schools" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39-40) translates into a disciplinary programme of cross-language translation, support for non-English journals, and recognition of non-Anglophone publishing in hiring. These follow from his demand that "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39) precede any genuine universal medium.

**Where the limits bite.** Ngũgĩ's stronger claim (that the medium of knowledge production matters intrinsically, not just instrumentally) means that some forms of sociological knowledge cannot be produced fully in English. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and the memory banks of speech communities outside the Anglosphere cannot be fully translated into Anglophone analytic categories. Halewood's warning applies: "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The structural fix requires multilingual sociology, not just self-critical Anglophone sociology.

**Sites inside the materials.** Ngũgĩ's account of the colonial school (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32-34) generalises directly to the discipline's training. Just as the "Oxford Readers for Africa" taught children about John and Joan visiting the Thames before they knew the names of other towns in Kenya, the canonical sociology curriculum teaches students about Durkheim's Paris and Weber's Heidelberg before introducing African or Asian theorists. The "elevation of the language of the conqueror" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) is reproduced at the level of the reading list. Halewood's "chair" example (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) catches the institutional setting: the chairs of sociology in Western universities inherit the bishop's *cathedra*, an authority whose ecclesiastical-male history operates beneath apparently neutral hiring vocabulary.

**Conclusion.** Sociology cannot be done in English without reproducing *some* of the colonial mind. It can be done in English without reproducing *all* of it, if practitioners take Achebe's refashioning strategy seriously, interrogate the vocabulary, and push for structural multilingual infrastructure inside the discipline. The honest position is that English-language sociology is part of the problem it studies, and being self-conscious about that is not a substitute for changing the institutional conditions, but it is a precondition.

### M12: *"Language is not a vehicle but a battleground." Discuss with reference to the lecture's account of Welsh, Gaelic, and Cornish.*

The slogan reframes language away from the metaphor of transport (language carries meaning from speaker to hearer) toward the metaphor of contested ground (language is fought over by competing political projects). I find the reframe productive but exaggerated, and I want to defend a more careful version: language is *both* vehicle and battleground. The lecture's brief slide on Welsh, Gaelic, and Cornish (slide 26), read together with Ngũgĩ's reading on Welsh, is the cleanest case in the course materials for the battleground reading.

**Why "vehicle" is insufficient.** The vehicle metaphor treats language as a neutral medium that conveys meaning between users. The lecture's slogan that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4) explicitly refuses this. Ngũgĩ's carrier theory does the structural work: language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30); losing the language loses the world it carries. If language were just a vehicle, the question of *which* language gets driven would be aesthetic or convenience-based. Minority-language revival would make no sense: speakers could simply switch to whichever language was most convenient.

**Why "battleground" captures what is missed.** Three reasons. *First*, language death is the residue of policy, not nature. Ngũgĩ cites Lyn Marierid of the Welsh Language Society writing in the *Morning Star* (21 October 1988): "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Welsh did not decline because Welsh speakers lost interest; it declined because, in Marierid's words, "rural areas… have become completely Anglicised as ordinary working-class people have been systematically priced out of their native areas" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The "yuppiefication" of rural Wales (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36) is the same kind of structural pressure on language as the colonial school's "I am stupid" plaques in Kenya (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), just operating through market mechanisms rather than corporal punishment. *Second*, the colonial mechanism Ngũgĩ identifies (suppression of indigenous languages plus elevation of the conqueror's language, Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32) applies internally to the British Isles. *Third*, language carries identity claims and a "collective memory bank" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30) that other vehicles cannot.

**The lecture's three names.** Slide 26 lists only three names: Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish. The slide is deliberately minimal: the names alone do the work of generalising Ngũgĩ's analysis from Africa to the Anglophone core. Each name marks a different position in the battleground. Welsh is the case Ngũgĩ discusses at length (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36): under pressure from English-medium administration and capital but contested by a Welsh Language Society and a structural argument that Welsh is necessary "to be aware of [the working class's] past in order to be able to shape their future" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Gaelic and Cornish are the names alone: the lecture leaves the reader to fill in the contestation each represents.

**Ngũgĩ's analysis applied inside the Anglophone core.** Minority-language revival enacts Ngũgĩ's decolonising-the-mind agenda inside Britain. It presupposes Ngũgĩ's claim that language is a "collective memory bank" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30) and that losing the language loses the world it carries. The two captivity mechanisms Ngũgĩ identifies translate directly. Suppression: Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish were pushed out of administration, schooling and economic life by Anglophone institutions. Elevation: English became "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) inside the British Isles just as in colonial Africa. Marierid's case for Welsh is internalist Ngũgĩ: keep the language because the working class needs its own memory bank (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36).

**Ngũgĩ's preconditions for a world language.** Ngũgĩ's proposal that Kiswahili could serve as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41) translates into a precondition for any minority-language revival: the revival language must be allowed to *grow* (to acquire new vocabulary, new domains of use, new generations of speakers) rather than be preserved in museum form. The political-economic precondition is just as strict: "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39).

**Achebe's strategy alongside.** Achebe's complementary move (refashion the dominant language to carry minority experience, Achebe, 1997: 347) operates at the interface between revived and dominant languages. The slide note that the title of *Things Fall Apart* quotes W.B. Yeats (an Irish post-colonial poet, slide 22) is itself an instance of this: an English title carrying an Irish cultural argument. The battleground has two fronts: defending the indigenous language *and* refashioning the dominant one.

**Critical complications.** Three. *Halewood's warning.* "Tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Signage and broadcasting reform alone is tinkering; what is required is "a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). *Class.* Marierid's argument is explicitly class-based: Welsh is to be preserved because "for generations, the Welsh working class was utterly dependent on the Welsh language and culture" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Revival without working-class participation risks becoming a credentialing exercise. *Ngũgĩ's wider point.* "The death of many languages should never be the condition for the life of a few" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39): the battleground is about the survival of plural memory banks, not the championship of a single revival.

**Conclusion.** "Language is not a vehicle but a battleground" overstates the case if read as denying the carrier function of language. Read as supplementing the vehicle metaphor with a recognition that language is *fought over*, the slogan is exactly right. The lecture's three-name slide (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish) and Ngũgĩ's discussion of the Welsh Language Society together show that minority languages inside the Anglophone core were not lost by attrition; they were defeated by policy ("yuppiefication" included), and they can only be revived by policy. The politics of revival are the politics of decolonising, fought on internal-colonial terrain.
