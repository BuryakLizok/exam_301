# SC301 / L4: Language and the World (Words and Things)

> Cheat sheet for revision. Lecture 4 covers three linked questions: (1) the relation between language and thought (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis); (2) language and gender (Dale Spender, Simone de Beauvoir, Luce Irigaray); (3) language and the legacy of colonialism (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Chinua Achebe). The overriding claim: **language is not a neutral mirror of the world**. It classifies, evaluates, and is a mode of power.

---

## 1. Key concepts and thinkers (≤200 words)

- **Linguistic relativity / the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (Edward Sapir, Benjamin Lee Whorf): each language embodies a particular world view; speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230).
- **Linguistic determinism**: stronger claim that language *determines* thought, "moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231).
- **Man-made language** (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980): English is structured around the male-as-norm; female is "minus male".
- **The Second Sex / "woman is the other"** (Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1949/1988): man is positive and neutral; woman is the negative, the marked term.
- **Imperialism of language / decolonising the mind** (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, 1993): colonial languages were weapons of conquest, "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37).
- **Africanising English** (Chinua Achebe, 'English and the African Writer', 1965/1997): English as a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347).
- **Sexuate subject / sexual difference is not extra-linguistic** (Luce Irigaray).
- **The generic he / chairman vs chair / actor vs actress**: concrete sites where male-as-norm is reproduced.

## 2. Key notes from the presentation

**Three parts to the lecture (slide 2).**

1. *Language, thought and culture*: linguistic relativity and linguistic determinism.
2. *Language and gender*: Spender, de Beauvoir.
3. *Language and the legacy of colonialism*: Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

**Sapir-Whorf (slides 3-6).** Linguistic relativity is "the belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230). The strong version, linguistic determinism, holds that the language we speak shapes the very limits of thought. The recurring textbook example is the claim that "Eskimos have over 50 words for snow", which the lecturer marks with scare-quotes to flag two problems: (a) "Eskimo" is itself an inaccurate, externally imposed label (see Samson, 2003); (b) the example is built on a single-word fixation that ignores how English speakers also describe many kinds of snow through *phrases* ("good snow for packing"). The slide highlights the load-bearing line: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**" (Lecture 4 slide 4).

**Critique of Sapir-Whorf (slides 7-9).** Any human language renders, rather than reflects, reality; we have lost any "innocent contact" with the world. Worse, the snow story sets up an "us / them" division in which a vaguely "Western" outlook is the benchmark and the so-called indigenous group is the exotic other. This links forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

**Spender on man-made language (slides 10-14, 18).** Spender argues that the structure of English encodes a "**male-as-norm**" classification scheme: humanity is divided into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145-148). Historical evidence: Mr Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147); John Kirkby's 1746 "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). Her programme: "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**De Beauvoir (slide 15, 28).** "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The man writes from no particular standpoint; the woman is always a *woman writer*. Crucially, de Beauvoir does **not** fully separate sex from gender. Biology and lived societal experience are intertwined.

**Actors / actresses / political correctness (slides 16-17).** David Marsh (*Guardian*) bans "actress": only "actor". This *seems* progressive (women are no longer compared only to other women) but the lecturer warns that occupations were historically male-coded, so calling all women "actors" risks absorbing them into a masculine yardstick rather than dismantling it.

**Grammarians and gender (slide 29).** H.W. Fowler (*A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, 1926: 211) insists that grammatical gender "is a grammatical term only", derived from Latin *genus* (type, genre). To treat masculine/feminine as biologically sexed is, for him, "a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211).

**Ngũgĩ and Achebe (slides 20-25).** Ngũgĩ frames language as a weapon of colonisation: education in the colonial language, suppression of indigenous tongues, "The English language was the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). No language of African origin is an official UN language. Achebe responds differently: African writers should keep writing in English/French/Portuguese to reach larger audiences, but should fashion an English "that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). When asked whether an African can ever use English like a native speaker, Achebe says: "I hope not" (Achebe, 1997: 347).

**And what of other languages? (slide 26).** Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish: internal colonial casualties of English inside the British Isles.

## 3. Information from the readings (slide-by-slide presentation walk-through)

### 3.1 Slide-by-slide presentation summary

- **Slide 1 (Title).** "Language and the World (Words and Things)". The bracketed subtitle frames the whole lecture: the question is the relation between *words* (language) and *things* (the world).

- **Slide 2 (3 parts to the lecture).** Three parts: (1) language and thought (and culture): linguistic relativity, linguistic determinism; (2) language and gender: Spender, de Beauvoir; (3) language and the legacy of colonialism: Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

- **Slide 3 (The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).** Linguistic relativity defined as the "belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230). Members of a society who share a language share "both a language and a specific way of viewing and understanding 'reality'". The pivot quote: "The speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230).

- **Slide 4 (How Many Words for Snow?).** Sets up the textbook example: "Eskimos" have "over 50 words for 'snow'". The lecturer scare-quotes "Eskimos" because the term is "neither accurate nor appropriate" as a name for a group. The slide previews the load-bearing slogan of the whole lecture: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**". References Samson (2003) *A Way of Life That Does Not Exist: Canada and the Extinguishment of the Innu*.
  > 💡 slide-question: *How Many Words for Snow?*

- **Slide 5 (A different world?...).** Spells out the Sapir-Whorf reading of the snow example: a wide range of words for snow is needed "to be able to live"; "Eskimos" therefore see the world differently, and this is "reflected in their language". There is a link "between language, thinking and understanding the world". The greater number of words "points to the fact that they live in a very different world from 'us'". Introduces the names Edward Sapir (1884-1939) and Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897-1941) as the "pioneers" the hypothesis is named after.
  > 💡 slide-question: *A different world?...*

- **Slide 6 (A second aspect of Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).** Introduces **linguistic determinism** as a stronger claim. Not just that language *links* to a way of seeing, but: "How we think of the world is *determined* by the language that we use". The famous Brown image: "The languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231).

- **Slide 7 (This is a powerful argument. But it has some problems).** First critique. Any human language "expresses human thoughts about the world"; each language is a "specific rendering of a specific 'reality'" rather than an "accurate or objective picture". "We" (humans with language) "seem to have lost any innocent contact with the world, as our language always 'gets in the way'".

- **Slide 8 (More Problems).** Second critique, more political. The phrase "they live in a very different world from 'us'" assumes an *us* and a *them*. Who is the "them"? "The 'others'". The risk is "assigning completely different 'realities' or worldviews to speakers of so-called indigenous groups" (Amazon rainforest is the example). This makes the "Western outlook" the implicit benchmark, and "plays down the differences between individual European languages". Connects forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

- **Slide 9 (Another (interesting) point (if we have time)…).** Third critique. Sapir-Whorf assumes single words are the main way of indicating things. If "Eskimo" has three words for snow that do not map onto three English words, the conclusion is supposed to be that "Eskimo" speakers "must think of snow and the world differently". This forgets that English speakers (especially snowball-makers) use *phrases*, not single words: "good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing". English may have fewer single-word labels but has "as many possible phrases for snow as they do".

- **Slide 10 (Dale Spender on "Man-Made Language").** Two quotations from Spender, 1980: 145. First: "Undoubtedly our own meanings are partially hidden from us and it is difficult to have access to them. We may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, 1980: 145). Second: "although it is not always easy to get outside this language trap, to get outside the limitations of one's own language, it is not impossible. There are clues, if one is prepared to look for them" (Spender, 1980: 145).

- **Slide 11 (Something to read (later)).** Linked Guardian article on "Sexism and dictionaries" (theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2019/jul/03/hussy-baggage-bit-filly-dictionary-definitions-woman). Side-reading, not core content.

- **Slide 12 (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*).** The central thesis. The key idea operating in the language is the **male-as-norm**. "One standard or normal human being is a male one". Those who are not part of this standard are "in some way 'deviant'". Quote: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts (if two is to be the significant number) but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980). "The status of the female is derived from the status of the male and on this has been erected many strata of positive and negative classifications".

- **Slide 13 ("Men Before Women": Where do such ideas come from?).** Spender's historical archive. "The first record we appear to have is that of a Mr Wilson in 1553 who insisted that it was more natural to place the man before the woman, as for example in male and female, husband and wife, brother and sister, son and daughter. Implicit in his insistence that males take precedence is the belief that males 'come first' in the natural order, and this is one of the first examples of a male arguing for not just the superiority of males but that this superiority should be reflected in the structure of the language" (Spender, 1980: 147).

- **Slide 14 (More Evidence).** The second historical fragment. "In 1746 John Kirkby formulated his 'Eighty Eight Grammatical Rules'. These rules, the product of Mr Kirkby's own imagination, contained one that indicated the esteem in which he held females: Rule Number Twenty One stated that the male gender was more comprehensive than the female. This represents a significant departure from the simple proposition that males are more important" (Spender, 1980: 148).

- **Slide 15 (Men take themselves and language for granted).** The pivot to de Beauvoir. Men do not have to explain that they are writing "as a man"; it is "seen as normal, as 'natural' even". The famous quote: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Women are "immediately seen as 'women writers'"; their writing is not granted the assumed objectivity that male writing claims.

- **Slide 16 (Actors and Actresses).** The David Marsh case. Marsh (*Guardian* production editor) instructs journalists to use only "actor", never "actress". The masculine term "can include the female, but not the other way around". "The female has been subsumed into the male (again)". "Actor" also stands in for the neuter when the speaker's gender is unknown. The reform argument: equal title, equal pay.

- **Slide 17 (Marsh's argument seems good, but…).** The lecturer's caution. Most occupations (doctor, teacher, actor) were historically carried out by men; women were "rare, even peculiar". Apparently neutral words like "actor" "provide a yardstick which may not immediately appear to refer to the male, or to be masculine". Risk: "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term". We can "pay lip-service" to the problem while ignoring "the more fundamental problem". - **Slide 18 (What is to be done?).** Spender's prescription. As the language structure which has been devised and legitimated by male grammarians exacts ambiguity, uncertainty, and anomie for females, then in the interests of dismantling the muted nature of females, that language structure and those rules need to be defied" (Spender, 1980: 162).

- **Slide 28 (Rethinking "Sex" and Gender (Simone de Beauvoir)).** The deeper read of de Beauvoir. Many sociologists ascribe the sex/gender distinction to *The Second Sex* (1949), but the division "is not as clear-cut in de Beauvoir's work as is sometimes thought". She insists: "When I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). She "does not reduce the question of what a woman 'is' to the biological ('sex')"; likewise, "the lived societal experience of women ('gender') is not purely 'social'". The two are intertwined.

- **Slide 29 (Gender in language (according to grammarians)).** The Fowler counter-position. In *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, H.W. Fowler states gender "is a grammatical term only. ### 3.2 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, Chapter 4: 'Imperialism of Language. English, a Language for the World?'

Ngũgĩ opens with a two-aspect view of language: it is both **a means of communication** and **a carrier of culture and history**, what he calls in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). These two aspects are inseparable but unbalanced according to the political conditions of encounter.

When languages meet "on terms of equality and independence" (e.g. Scandinavians, Japanese, West Germans learning English), the communicative aspect dominates and English does not substitute for the national language (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30-31). When they meet "as oppressor and oppressed, as for instance under imperialism", language becomes a **weapon** alongside "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Ngũgĩ quotes David Livingstone's nineteenth-century slogan "Christianity plus 5 percent" and updates it to "Christianity, debt, plus 40 percent in debt servicing" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31).

The capture of minds happens in two complementary moves (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32):

1. **Suppression of indigenous languages.** They were "experienced as incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Ngũgĩ recalls a Kenyan school hymn, "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was the children's own language (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32).
2. **Elevation of the conqueror's language.** "Those inducted into the school system… were furnished with new mirrors" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The French called this *assimilation*; the English called it *education*. Lugard's blunter name was **indirect rule**: mastery of English was "the measure of one's readiness for election into the band of the elect" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32).

Ngũgĩ describes children punished for speaking African languages, "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), with pieces of paper from the wastepaper basket stuffed in their mouths. "Humiliation in relation to our languages was the key" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33).

The reading then extends the argument beyond Africa. Citing Lyn Marierid in the Welsh Language Society (*Morning Star*, 21 Oct 1988), Ngũgĩ shows that Welsh is being eroded by capitalist "yuppiefication": "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Equally, the Russians of Novosibirsk learn English because, in Kenneth Baker's words quoted from the *Evening Standard*, "they want to get away from the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35). English carries capitalist modernity.

The consequences in postcolonial Africa: English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and education (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Yet only a minority speak them, so the working majority "are excluded from centre stage" and from modern scientific knowledge, which is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). The elite is "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). No African-origin language sits among the official languages of the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38).

Ngũgĩ is not against a common world language. He insists that the languages of the world are "our common heritage. A world of many languages should be like a field of flowers of different colours" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). He demands "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" as the precondition (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). He concludes by proposing Kiswahili, "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41), as a candidate world language.

### 3.3 *Gender and Personal Pronouns: She, He, It and They* (Halewood, 2021)

The chapter opens by setting out the sex/gender distinction in sociology, then immediately complicates it. Mead's anthropology shows that gender roles vary cross-culturally, hence they are social. But Halewood, following de Beauvoir and Irigaray, argues that the easy split (biology over here, society over there) itself "bifurcates" language and world.

Fowler's claim that gender "is a grammatical term only… of the male or female sex is either a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211) is presented as a starting point and then dismantled. If categories really were arbitrary, why not "up, down, middle" instead of "masculine, feminine, neuter"? The specific words carry meaning.

**De Beauvoir's pivot.** "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15).

**Irigaray's grammatical examples.** In French *il* covers "he", impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pluie*), and any unknown subject. A male dog *le chien* is default; *la chienne* requires extra knowledge. The third-person plural *ils* is used for any mixed group, however small the male presence. The Czech example from Tichý (2018) ("all the women of the world went for a walk" uses the feminine plural *šly*, but as soon as John joins, the verb flips to masculine *šli*) shows the asymmetry at the level of verbs too. Irigaray: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20).

**Gendered English.** Halewood quotes Dewey writing "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his" and Whitehead's "men of science" to show that even Anglophone academic writing carried male defaults. Fowler had wanted *doctress*, *teacheress*, *singeress*, *danceress*: multiplying female forms. Marsh (production editor, *Guardian*) goes the other way: abolish *actress*, use only *actor*.

**Job titles and chairs.** "Chair" derives from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat in a cathedral, and the historical "see" (*sedes*). Professors hold "chairs"; the office carries an authority that Halewood reads as historically male/masculine, tied to the Church. So even apparently neutral words like "chair" are not neutral.

**Pronouns, trans rights, prescriptivism.** Singular "they" is grammatically contested but has a long literary history (Lewis Carroll, Shakespeare, the King James Bible, Thackeray, all cited via Marsh, 2014). Trans writers including Stephen Whittle (2006) argue that "he/she" is exclusionary; some prefer "they" or neologisms (e.g. "per"). The chapter is sympathetic but warns that "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Haraway's caution.** "All dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130).

The conclusion sides with a *processual* reading: man, woman, trans are *outcomes* of intertwined material and linguistic processes, not pre-given essences.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The thread that holds these readings together is the slogan from the lecture: **"Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world"** (Lecture 4 slide 4). Each thinker tugs on a different end of that rope.

Sapir-Whorf gives us the *epistemic* lever. If "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230), then a language is never an innocent code; it is a settlement about what counts. The weak form (language influences thought) is now broadly accepted in cognitive linguistics; the strong form (linguistic determinism), "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231), is harder to defend, because speakers can stretch their language with phrases, neologisms and translation. The deeper problem the lecturer flags is not whether the strong claim is true, but that the very *example* (Eskimo words for snow) is constructed from a Western "us" looking at an indigenous "them". The classification is already evaluative; the example reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

Spender shows the same asymmetry inside English itself. Her core claim is structural: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). Historical fragments back this up: Mr Wilson (1553) prescribing the order "male and female" (Spender, 1980: 147), John Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The generic *he*, the word *mankind*, *chairman*, *manpower*, the older NHS form that asked for a "Mrs / Miss" but only "Mr" for a man: these are the sediment of that prescriptive history.

De Beauvoir radicalises Spender. Her insight is phenomenological: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The male is the unmarked term in language *because* he is the unmarked term in social ontology. He occupies "both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). This is why I find debates about whether to use *actor* or *actress* irresolvable in purely linguistic terms: tidying the words leaves the social default (the masculine yardstick) untouched.

Irigaray takes this further. "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). The Czech example (*šly* flipping to *šli* as soon as one man joins all the women of the world) is grammatically tiny but ontologically loud: it tells us who counts as the default human.

Ngũgĩ pulls the analysis out to the imperial scale. Language is a "carrier of the history and the culture built into the process of communication over time" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). When the encounter is "oppressor and oppressed", language is added to "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Suppression plus elevation: children punished with plaques reading "I am stupid" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), then drilled in "Oxford Readers for Africa" about John and Joan visiting the Thames (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34), produced what Ngũgĩ calls "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). His sharpest line is that "the death of many languages should never be the condition for the life of a few" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). The point sits squarely on the Sapir-Whorf hinge: kill the language, kill the world that language carries.

Achebe complicates the colonial story. Refusing to abandon English does not equal submission. The African writer should "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Can the African write English like a native? "I hope not" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). This is the position I find most pragmatically defensible: not Ngũgĩ's purism, not the colonial monolith, but a politics of *re-fashioning* the inherited tool.

Across all five thinkers the same lesson appears: language **classifies and evaluates**, and to classify is to distribute power. Sociologists who want to *address* the inequalities Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ and Achebe describe cannot just police vocabulary. Halewood is right that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). But they cannot ignore vocabulary either, because, as the lecture insists, problems with inequality in language *are* problems in the world.

### Essay 1 / 2025 (Q4): *"Language, power and modernity are all interrelated." Critically analyse this statement.*

I agree with the statement, but only when "modernity" is read in the specific sense the SC301 module has been building: modernity as a *colonial* project that travelled with particular European languages and that re-described the rest of the world in its own vocabulary. Read that way, language is not an add-on to modernity but one of its load-bearing technologies.

**Power needs a vocabulary.** The lecture's basic move is that language is "not neutral"; it classifies and evaluates and is therefore "a mode of power" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Spender catches this at the level of grammar: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir catches it at the level of social ontology: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The point is the same: power needs a default subject, and the default subject is installed in the words before any explicit argument is made.

**Modernity travels in particular languages.** Ngũgĩ's reading is decisive here. The languages of European modernity (English, French, Portuguese) did not arrive in the Third World "under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). They came alongside "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The mission school, the colonial bureaucracy, the textbook industry ("Oxford Readers for Africa", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34) and the Christian hymn ("Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) all bundled together a single message: modernity is European, and European is English (or French, or Portuguese). Children caught speaking their own languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). The vocabulary of "progress" was so tightly coupled with English that, decades after independence, Kenneth Baker could report Russians in Novosibirsk learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35). Capitalism is "modern"; everything else is "old-fashioned", and English is the bridge.

**Power and modernity are inscribed in everyday English.** I find the Halewood chapter useful because it shows that the same logic operates inside the English-speaking core, not just at the imperial periphery. The word "chair", from Latin *cathedra*, encodes the authority of bishops, then of professors, then of committee heads (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). The chairs were literally Church furniture; the office of professor was modelled on that of priest. Replacing "chairman" with "chair" feels modern but preserves a deeper masculine-clerical authority. This is what the lecture means by language *carrying* a history that modernity papers over.

**Two complications.** First, modernity has produced *democratic* vocabularies too. Ngũgĩ is careful to say that English has "a democratic tradition" (Shakespeare, Milton, Blake, Dickens) and is "part of the common heritage of humankind" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). The relation between language and power is therefore contested *within* every modern language, not just between languages. Second, Achebe argues that the colonised can refashion the coloniser's language: the world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Modernity's linguistic infrastructure is more porous than Ngũgĩ sometimes suggests. The rise of "Singlish", Indian English, Caribbean creoles, and Afro-American Vernacular English shows speakers using the modern language to assert non-Anglo identities.

**Examples that pull the three concepts together.**

- *English-only education in former colonies.* Postcolonial governments retaining English as the language of instruction reproduce Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37): scientific and legal knowledge stays accessible only to an Anglophone elite. The modern state inherits the colonial school.
- *Trans rights and pronoun debates.* These are arguments about who counts as a modern subject of rights. The grammatical question is inseparable from the political one.
- *Social-media moderation.* Platforms that moderate well in English and poorly in Burmese or Amharic export the colonial language hierarchy into the digital sphere: a 21st-century instance of Ngũgĩ's complaint that African languages occupy "no place of honour" in international institutions (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38).
- *Legal "reasonable man" tests* and *medical diagnostic categories*: modernity's flagship institutions (law and medicine) encode male defaults at the level of vocabulary.

**Conclusion.** Language, power and modernity are interrelated because modernity is the historical settlement in which certain European languages became the default vocabulary of progress, science and citizenship. The strong version of this claim risks essentialising European languages as inherently oppressive (Achebe rightly resists that). The weak version, that "the languages of Europe… were taught as if they were our own languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35), is the version I find most useful, and it is enough to vindicate the exam quotation.

### Essay 2 / 2024 (Q3): *Discuss the links between language and inequality. How can sociologists help to address the problem?*

I want to argue two things. First, language and inequality are linked in three overlapping ways (*classification*, *evaluation*, and *erasure*) and the lecture's slogan that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4) captures this neatly. Second, sociologists can help, but only if they treat language as embedded in the world rather than as a separable surface that can be cleaned up by vocabulary reform alone.

**Language classifies.** This is the core insight of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis as the lecture presents it: speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230). Every language carves the world into categories ("doctor" and "patient", "citizen" and "alien", "man" and "woman") and those categories are unequal in their consequences. Dale Spender shows that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The generic *he*, the older default *mankind*, the ladder of titles *Mr / Mrs / Miss*, all encode an asymmetry: the man is the default human, the woman a variation. Medical language is a powerful example: gender-based diagnostic bias means that women presenting with heart attacks were for decades misclassified as anxious because the "textbook" patient was male. The classification scheme is unequal before any individual clinician makes a choice.

**Language evaluates.** Classification almost always smuggles in a hierarchy. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argues that English entered Africa as the language of "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31), at once a label and a verdict. "Eskimo", the lecture's recurring example, is not just inaccurate; it expresses a relation of power between the namer and the named. The computer science vocabulary of *master* and *slave* drives, or *blacklist* and *whitelist*, evaluates as it classifies. In law, the figure of the "reasonable man", only re-named "reasonable person" recently, built the male perspective into the very test of negligence.

**Language erases.** Some realities are made unspeakable. Simone de Beauvoir's complaint that "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) is precisely this: the male perspective passes for the universal, so its particularity disappears from view. Trans and non-binary speakers report a similar erasure in pronoun systems that admit only *he* or *she*. Ngũgĩ describes a more brutal erasure: African children in colonial schools were punished with "plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) for speaking their first languages. The Welsh and Gaelic revivals are responses to the same pattern of internal colonial erasure.

**So what can sociologists do?** First, *describe* the patterns with care. Spender's archival work on grammarians from Wilson (1553) to Kirkby (1746) shows that the "man-made" character of English is not a vague impression but a documentable history of prescription. Sociologists can do similar archaeology for medical, legal and technical vocabularies, tracing, say, how DSM categories changed, or how social-media content-moderation algorithms perform much worse on non-English languages, generating new digital inequalities.

Second, *connect* language work to material consequences. De Beauvoir and Irigaray both insist that words alone are not the whole problem. Halewood puts it bluntly: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The point of pronoun debates, English-only education policy in former colonies, or campaigns to retire master/slave terminology is not the words as such but the social distributions the words sustain.

Third, sociologists can *amplify counter-voices*. Achebe's strategy of Africanising English ("fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347)) is a model: rather than purifying the language, you refashion it for new uses. Sociologists studying Welsh-medium education, dual-language radio in postcolonial states, or the digital revival of Gaelic show that languages can be repaired and extended.

Fourth, sociologists should be *self-critical* about jargon. The discipline's own language can naturalise inequality (the smooth use of "race" as a variable, "informal economy" as a residue, "third world" as a destination). I think we should follow Haraway's warning that "all dreams for a perfectly true language… are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173). We should not assume our vocabulary is a neutral grid.

So: language and inequality are linked through classification, evaluation and erasure. Sociologists help when they document the histories, link words to material distributions, support communities refashioning their own languages, and police their own vocabulary as carefully as everyone else's. They fail when they treat language reform as a substitute for redistribution.

### Essay 3 / 2023 (Q4): *Identify and explain how language is tied up with wider questions of power.*

I want to identify four ways language is tied up with power: it (1) classifies who counts; (2) evaluates as it classifies; (3) enforces those evaluations through institutions; and (4) carries the historical sediment of past power even after explicit reform. I will work through each, drawing on Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ, Achebe and the Halewood chapter.

**(1) Classification: who counts as the default.** Spender's claim that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) is the cleanest statement of this. The generic *he*, the older *mankind*, the asymmetry between *actor* and *actress*: these are not stylistic quirks but classification rules that install a default subject. De Beauvoir generalises: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The same logic operates in legal English. The "reasonable man" test in tort law set the standard of due care by reference to a masculine ideal type; renaming him "reasonable person" preserved the underlying male yardstick.

**(2) Evaluation: classification is never neutral.** Calling something "primitive", "tribal", "developing" or "indigenous" classifies *and* ranks. Ngũgĩ shows how the colonial school treated African languages as "incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The vocabulary itself encoded the verdict: English was light, the African languages were "gloom" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The lecturer's caution about the word "Eskimo" makes the same point: the label is not just inaccurate but evaluative. It positions the named group as the curious other against an implicit Western "us".

**(3) Enforcement: institutions police language.** Language is power because someone enforces the vocabulary. Spender notes that the rules were laid down by named grammarians: Wilson in 1553 insisting on "male and female, husband and wife", Kirkby in 1746 ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Ngũgĩ describes the school as the key colonial institution: children were beaten and humiliated for speaking their own languages (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Modern equivalents include English-only requirements in some Anglophone schools for migrant pupils, language tests embedded in citizenship law, and social-media moderation regimes that under-resource non-English languages and so police speech unequally.

**(4) Sedimentation: old power lingers in vocabulary.** Halewood's example of "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, via the bishop's seat) shows that even apparently neutral terms carry sedimented authority (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). The professor's chair inherits the bishop's chair; the committee chair inherits the professor's; the masculine authority is preserved beneath a neutral surface. The same applies to *master/slave* in computer science, *husbandry* in agriculture, *manhole* in civil engineering, *manning* a station in workplace English. Renaming may help, but the history continues to operate.

**Counter-currents.** Power is not absolute. Achebe shows that the dominated can refashion the dominant tongue: the African writer must use English to "carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347), and indeed should *not* learn to use English like a native ("I hope not", Achebe, 1997: 347). Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish revivals are explicit refusals of internal linguistic colonisation. Trans-led pronoun campaigns push English to recognise non-binary subjects, partly through the singular *they*: a usage Marsh (2014) shows has a long literary pedigree from Shakespeare and Lewis Carroll onwards. These counter-currents show that language is contested terrain, not a one-way street.

**The limit case: tinkering versus changing.** It is tempting to think that reforming vocabulary fixes the underlying inequalities. The lecture is sceptical. Marsh's ban on "actress" makes Hattie Jacques comparable to Kenneth Williams as well as to Joan Sims (Halewood, 2021: 120). But, the lecturer warns, "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood draws the broader conclusion: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Conclusion.** Language is tied up with power in four ways (classification, evaluation, enforcement and sedimentation) and these operate from intimate pronouns (he/she/they) right up to the global hierarchy of UN-recognised languages. The political work language does is uneven and contested, and Achebe's strategy of refashioning is as important to the picture as Ngũgĩ's critique of colonial imposition. The sociologist's job is to make the link between vocabulary and distribution visible without pretending that fixing vocabulary by itself fixes distribution.

### Essay 4 / 2022 (Q4): *Can language create inequality?*

My answer is: language does not create inequality from scratch, but it sustains, amplifies and naturalises inequalities that already have material roots, and in that sense it does much of the *work* of inequality. I will defend that claim across three registers: gender, colonial domination, and contemporary technological power.

**The misleading version of the question.** The question, taken literally, can lead to a bad answer: "language creates inequality" reads as if changing words alone would equalise the world. The lecture warns against this: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126; cf. Lecture 4 slide 17 on actor/actress). Material inequalities (wages, land, citizenship) have material causes, not merely linguistic ones. But that does not mean language is innocent.

**Language sustains gender inequality.** Spender's claim that the structure of English is built on "the male-as-norm" (Spender, 1980: 145) is best read as a sustaining-and-amplifying claim rather than a creating-from-nothing claim. Patriarchy did not begin with English grammar; but English grammar makes male centrality feel natural, so that "we may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir's "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) makes the same point at the level of the writing subject. The Czech example from Tichý (one man joining all the women of the world flips the verb to masculine *šli*) shows the asymmetry at the level of grammar, not just lexis. Medical language is the place where this stops being a metaphor: the historical absence of female bodies from clinical trial protocols, plus textbook language built around the male body, fed directly into gender-based diagnostic bias. Language does not *invent* the inequality, but it carries it from one institutional setting to the next.

**Language sustains colonial inequality.** Ngũgĩ is uncompromising here. The colonial encounter "was not the encounter between English and most so-called Third World languages… under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). English, French and Portuguese came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Colonial schools then "elevated the language of the conqueror" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) by punishing children for using their own. Sixty years after independence, English remains the language of administration, justice and higher education in many African states, which is why Ngũgĩ describes the elite as "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Did English *create* the inequality between Britain and Kenya? No. The inequality is rooted in extraction, conquest and the Berlin Conference. But English language policy keeps it in place after the soldiers have left. That is a real form of creation: language *creates the continuity* of a colonial inequality whose original cause has lapsed.

**Language sustains contemporary technological inequality.** A more recent example. Social-media moderation works much better in English than in, say, Burmese, Tigrinya or Amharic. In the Rohingya crisis, Facebook's failure to moderate hate speech in Burmese contributed directly to incitement to ethnic cleansing. Here, language inequality is not an emanation of colonial schooling; it is the result of corporate decisions about which languages get investment. The result, however, mirrors the colonial pattern: English speakers get a relatively safer platform; non-English speakers do not. Computer-science vocabulary itself (*master/slave*, *blacklist/whitelist*) is being slowly reformed under the same logic.

**Counter-evidence.** There are limits to the claim. Languages get refashioned by their speakers: Achebe insists that English can be "made to carry [the African writer's] peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The singular *they* has expanded from its older literary use into a tool for non-binary recognition. Welsh and Gaelic are being revived through deliberate language policy. So language does not lock inequality in place; it is contested terrain.

**A subtler creation effect.** I want to defend a *stronger* version of the "language creates inequality" claim in one specific area: when language creates new *categories*, it can create new inequalities. The category "illegal immigrant" (a relatively recent term) does not just describe a pre-existing inequality; it constitutes the migrant as a particular kind of subject with a particular set of legal exposures. The category "informal sector" did similar work in development economics, marking certain types of work as residual and therefore not eligible for the same protections. Here Sapir-Whorf is genuinely useful: the way the language carves the world *partly* shapes which inequalities become administratively and politically possible.

**Conclusion.** Language can create inequality in three precise senses: it can install a default subject (male) so the asymmetry becomes invisible; it can sustain colonial-era inequalities long after their original cause; and it can constitute new categories that produce new inequalities. But it cannot create inequality unaided. The material world is the other half of every story. The most defensible position, with Halewood, is that "language and the world become together" (Halewood, 2021: 132), and so do the inequalities each carries.

### Essay 5 / 2021 (Q4): *"Inequalities in language represent inequalities in the world." Critically explain this statement.*

The statement comes almost verbatim from the lecture: "Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). I think it is largely correct, but the word *represent* is doing a lot of work and needs unpacking. Inequalities in language do three different things at once: they *reflect* prior inequalities in the world; they *reproduce* those inequalities by carrying them across time; and in some cases they *constitute* the inequalities they appear merely to describe. I will defend that three-layer reading.

**Reflection.** The simplest reading of "represent" is that language mirrors social hierarchies that exist independently. Spender's history of English grammar offers good evidence. Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, 1980: 147) and Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" (Spender, 1980: 148) did not invent the patriarchal society; they *reflected* it. Similarly, the prestige of English in former colonies *reflects* the geopolitical dominance of Britain and the US. Ngũgĩ's contemporary observation that English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and commerce in postcolonial Africa (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) is a description of how linguistic prestige reflects political-economic power.

**Reproduction.** Reflection alone is too weak. Language does not just mirror; it *carries* inequalities forward when their original cause has lapsed. This is the bite of Ngũgĩ's argument. By the time he is writing (1993), formal colonialism in Kenya has ended, but the colonial language continues to gatekeep education, science and the law. Knowledge is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). De Beauvoir makes the same point for gender: long after legal reform has formally equalised men and women, the unmarked male subject persists in everyday speech, "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Halewood's "chair" example is the same logic: even as universities formally open chairs to women, the word *chair* carries forward the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's *cathedra* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Language reproduces inequalities by making them feel natural and durable.

**Constitution.** The strongest version of the claim is that some inequalities are partly *constituted* in and through language. The Sapir-Whorf insight, in its weak and defensible form, is that the categories a language makes available shape what people can do with the world. Spender's claim is precisely that the category scheme "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) is itself part of how patriarchy is experienced and reproduced. Irigaray pushes further: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). The case of generic *he* is a clean example. Psycholinguistic research has repeatedly shown that readers who encounter *he* in supposedly generic contexts disproportionately picture a man, with downstream effects on, say, who applies for a job advertised with masculine pronouns. Here language is not just reflecting an inequality but doing some of the work that produces it.

**Critical limits.** Three caveats matter.

1. *Language is not the whole story.* Halewood is firm: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). If we treat language reform as a substitute for material change, we let inequality off the hook.
2. *Counter-currents exist.* Achebe shows that subordinated speakers refashion the dominant language: "fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Pidgins, creoles, AAVE, Indian English and Singlish are all evidence that linguistic inequality is contested, not simply imposed. The singular *they* and trans neologisms are the same dynamic in gender.
3. *Representation is not always faithful.* Some linguistic inequalities exaggerate or distort the social patterns they purport to represent. The myth of "Eskimo words for snow", flagged by the lecturer with its "us / them" framing (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8), represents indigenous people as exotic *others* in a way that the social facts do not support. So a language can misrepresent as well as reflect.

**Concrete examples that bind it together.**

- *Pronoun debates and trans rights*: a place where reflection, reproduction and constitution all overlap.
- *Medical language and gender-based diagnostic bias*: language reproduces the male-bodied default of nineteenth-century medicine into twenty-first century clinics.
- *English-only education in former colonies, and Welsh/Gaelic revival*: showing that linguistic inequality can be reversed by deliberate policy.
- *Computer science master/slave terminology*: a vocabulary inequality that survived the abolition of slavery by a century and a half.

**Conclusion.** The statement is correct in its strong reading: inequalities in language do represent inequalities in the world, where *represent* means simultaneously to reflect, to reproduce and partly to constitute. The danger lies in collapsing the three readings into one. Sociology's job, as Spender, Ngũgĩ, de Beauvoir, Achebe and Halewood all insist in different keys, is to keep them apart while showing how they hang together, and to refuse either the complacent answer that language is "just words" or the over-simple answer that fixing the words fixes the world.

### Essay 6 / Mock Q1: *"Decolonising the mind requires decolonising the language" (Ngũgĩ). Discuss.*

I take Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's slogan seriously and want to defend it, but with a twist. Decolonising the mind does require decolonising the language, but "decolonising the language" itself names two different projects: a *displacement* project (swap out the coloniser's tongue for an African one, as Ngũgĩ urges) and a *refashioning* project (rewire the coloniser's tongue from inside, as Achebe urges). My claim is that the slogan is correct, and that both projects are needed.

**Why language carries the colonial mind.** Ngũgĩ's argument in *Moving the Centre* (1993) and *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) is that European languages arrived in Africa "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The mission school did the slow work: children caught speaking African languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Humiliation was the pedagogical method. The point was not just communication; the colonial attitude (that the European tongue is the language of modernity, knowledge and progress) was installed at the level of the child's nervous system. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and that memory bank was systematically replaced.

**What survives independence.** This is where the slogan bites. Political flags changed, but English, French and Portuguese remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Ngũgĩ's diagnosis is that the elite is therefore "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Decolonising the mind is impossible while the everyday operating vocabulary of the state remains the coloniser's, because the categories of governance, science, and legal personhood remain those installed by mission school. This is what I mean by epistemic violence as a continuing condition rather than a past event.

**Three concrete sites where the colonial mind hides in the language.** First, *the curriculum*. Postcolonial primary and secondary schooling in many Anglophone African states still uses English as the medium of instruction from a young age; children encounter physics, biology and history first in a non-native tongue. Knowledge becomes something that lives in English. Second, *higher courts and administrative law*. Litigants who do not command English are gatekept out of justice; constitutional debates happen in the language of empire. Third, *prestige labour markets*. Kenneth Baker, cited by Ngũgĩ, was "amazed" to find Russians learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35). The colonial attitude has been globalised: English is the language of escape from "backwardness", and "backwardness" is anything not-English. The recent rise of ChatGPT and similar LLMs deepens the pattern: generative AI's English bias means that the very tools claimed to democratise knowledge route attention through an Anglophone prior.

**Ngũgĩ's displacement strategy.** Ngũgĩ's answer is to *write in Gĩkũyũ*. *Devil on the Cross* was drafted on toilet paper in detention precisely because the political content required a Gĩkũyũ form. He proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). The argument is structural: African-origin languages currently occupy no place of honour among the official languages of the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38), and that absence is itself a form of cognitive colonisation. Welsh, Gaelic and Māori revivalism inside the Anglophone metropolitan core enacts the same principle: where the predominant class "has no use for them" (Welsh Language Society, cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36), languages die, and with them the worlds they carry.

**Achebe's refashioning strategy.** Achebe disagrees on tactics, agrees on diagnosis. His position is that the African writer must use "an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The price a world language must pay is "submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Achebe's "I hope not" (to the question of whether the African should learn English like a native, Achebe, 1997: 347) is a refusal of assimilation expressed in style rather than in choice of tongue.

The Hindi/Urdu split (one spoken language partitioned into two by colonial script politics) is a cautionary case: contesting the coloniser's categories in their own vocabulary, without addressing the structural inequalities of script and state, can reproduce colonial divisions in new forms.

Decolonising the mind requires decolonising the language, because the colonial attitude lives in the everyday categories of knowledge and governance, not just in flags and constitutions. ### Essay 7 / Mock Q2: *Critically assess Spender's argument in Man Made Language in light of contemporary trans-pronoun debates.*

Dale Spender's *Man Made Language* (1980) is the most ambitious structural account of English-as-patriarchal in the SC301 reading list. The contemporary trans-pronoun debate stress-tests the argument in two directions: it vindicates Spender's central diagnosis (English grammar encodes hierarchy, not neutrality) and exposes the limits of her binary framing (the patriarchal scheme she names produces marked subjects beyond the man/woman pair). I will argue that the trans debate strengthens the deep argument while requiring us to drop its binary scaffolding.

**Spender's argument, briefly.** Spender's notion of man-made language is structural: English divides humanity "not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The male is the default human; the female is a deviation marked by additional morphology (*actress*, *waitress*) or by extra honorifics (*Mrs* and *Miss* mark marital status; *Mr* does not). The archive is historical and named: Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, 1980: 147), Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The "man-made" label tracks an actual paper trail of male grammarians who prescribed male precedence. Spender's programme follows: "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**Where the trans debate vindicates Spender.** Three points of confirmation. First, the contemporary fight over the singular *they* is exactly the kind of structural reform Spender's diagnosis predicts is needed. Marsh (2014), cited by Halewood, shows that singular *they* is attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray (Halewood, 2021: 128). It was *prescribed out* of formal English by grammarians of exactly the Wilson-Kirkby type, in favour of "generic" *he*. Reviving it is the same kind of structural intervention Spender called for with *Ms*: making linguistically visible a subject the prescriptive tradition occluded. Second, the *intensity* of the trans-pronoun backlash (workplace policies, op-ed columns, parliamentary debates) is itself evidence that pronouns are not "just grammar". The grammarian's defence ("masculine and feminine are arbitrary technical labels", in the spirit of Fowler, 1926: 211) collapses on contact with the political stakes. Third, the social-ontology mechanism Spender identified (that the unmarked subject corresponds to the default human) generalises beyond men/women. Whittle and colleagues, summarised by Halewood, observe that "personal pronouns are limiting and exclusionary for some of those who identify as 'trans'" (Halewood, 2021: 128). The trans subject is twice-marked: by gender and by departure from binary morphology.

**Where the trans debate exposes Spender's limits.** Spender's binary scaffolding ("plus male / minus male") works as long as the political subject is "woman" understood in opposition to "man". But contemporary trans, non-binary, intersex and genderqueer politics queer that scaffolding in precisely the way de Beauvoir already warned: "When I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). Spender's argument needs to be re-cast: not "minus male" versus "plus male", but a *grid* of marked categories against a single unmarked default. The trans speaker is not a third gender position alongside man and woman; they are a third type of marking, one that destabilises the binary itself.

A second limit. Spender's evidence base is overwhelmingly English. Languages with no gendered pronouns (Finnish *hän*, Hungarian *ő*, Turkish *o*, Yoruba), or with grammatical gender systems unlike English (Mandarin's twentieth-century 他/她/它 split, Bantu noun classes), do not fit the "man-made" template cleanly. Yet trans politics in those linguistic environments still encounter analogous hierarchies (in title forms of address, in occupational nouns, in honorifics). The *mechanism* Spender names is right; the *morphology* she names is local to languages like English.

**Three contemporary applications.** First, *workplace recognition*. Pronoun fields in email signatures and HR systems are the most everyday site where Spender's structural critique meets daily working life. Critics call it compelled speech; supporters call it basic recognition. Both sides are operating on Spender's terrain. Second, *legal personhood*. Some jurisdictions now allow non-binary markers on passports and birth certificates; the state is selecting which pronouns it inscribes in its records, which is exactly the prescriptive moment Spender's grammarians performed three centuries ago, only with the polarity reversed. Third, *AI and chatbot defaults*. Generative AI systems default to gendered pronouns in many translation tasks even when the source language is genderless ("the doctor said *he* will see you now"), recreating the male-as-norm pattern Spender documented. ChatGPT's English bias and the gendered default of voice assistants both extend Spender's archive into the algorithmic age.

**Critical caveats.** Halewood's warning is decisive: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Pronoun reform without legal recognition, healthcare access and protection from violence is symbolic. And the binary masculine/feminine framework (which Spender shares with Irigaray) risks re-marginalising the very trans subjects whose recognition is at stake. The trans debate is internally contested over whether *they* suffices, whether neopronouns (*per*, *xe*) extend the inventory enough, and whether the marked/unmarked logic should be retired altogether.

**Conclusion.** Spender's *Man Made Language* is a structural diagnosis that contemporary trans-pronoun debates largely vindicate: English grammar encodes hierarchy, and that hierarchy can be reformed by deliberate intervention. The binary scaffolding of "plus male / minus male" needs replacing with a marked-versus-unmarked grid, and the anglocentric evidence base needs widening. With those revisions, Spender's argument is more relevant now than when she wrote it.

### Essay 8 / Mock Q3: *What does de Beauvoir mean by "woman is the Other", and why does she treat that othering as linguistically structured?*

The question has two parts and I will take them in order. First, what does de Beauvoir's woman-as-other actually claim? Second, why is the othering inseparable from the structure of language? My answer: the claim is that woman exists as the *marked* term against an unmarked male default, and de Beauvoir treats this as linguistically structured because the asymmetry is built into the grammar and lexicon before any individual speaker chooses a word.

**Part one: what "woman is the Other" means.** De Beauvoir's argument in the Introduction to *The Second Sex* (1949) is condensed in two famous lines. "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). And: "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex; it goes without saying that he is a man… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The man is the *unmarked* term (the default human, the one whose particularity disappears from view); the woman is the *marked* term (the deviation that has to be named to enter discourse).

The "Other" is not just "another person" or "a different category". It is the *constitutive outside* of the universal subject: man defines himself in opposition to the woman whose particularity he has externalised. De Beauvoir is careful to add (and this matters for the second part of the answer) that "when I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). Woman-as-other is a *position* in a structure, not an essence. Lived experience, not biology, is the centre of gravity.

**Part two: why de Beauvoir treats the othering as linguistically structured.** Three reasons, in order of depth.

*First, the asymmetry is encoded in grammar.* Halewood draws out the mechanics. In French, *il* covers male persons, male dogs and the impersonal *it* (*il pleut*, "it is raining"); *ils* covers any mixed plural, however small the male presence. In Czech, Tichý's example shows the rule operating in conjugation: "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses feminine *šly*; add John, and the verb flips to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116). The masculine is at once *one* gender and *the* default. English does it more quietly with the generic *he*, with *mankind*, with *actor / actress*, with the residual *Mr* versus *Mrs / Miss*. The structure exists prior to any speaker's intention. To speak French, Czech or pre-reform English is to use a grammar that has already done the othering before the speaker arrives.

*Second, the asymmetry is historically prescribed, not natural.* This is where de Beauvoir's argument benefits from Dale Spender's archive. Wilson (1553) insisted that it was "more natural to place the man before the woman"; Kirkby (1746) ruled that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). The grammar de Beauvoir analyses was *made* by named human beings (male grammarians) who enforced male precedence. Spender's notion of man-made language is exactly the historical complement to de Beauvoir's structural one. The structure has authors.

*Third, the asymmetry is preserved by apparently neutral reforms.* If "actor" is the unmarked default, renaming actresses "actors" leaves the masculine yardstick intact, as the lecture warns (Lecture 4 slide 17). De Beauvoir's framework predicts that gender-neutral language can *re-mask* rather than dismantle the asymmetry (Irigaray's later worry). Halewood's example of "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat) shows the same thing for authority: replacing "chairman" with "chair" preserves an underlying masculine-clerical authority forward (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Language reform that does not touch the marked/unmarked structure simply hides the marking.

**Why this matters for sociology.** The linguistic structuring of woman-as-other matters because the unmarked subject in language tends to coincide with the unmarked subject in institutions. Medical research that defaults to male bodies produces gender-based diagnostic bias: textbook descriptions of heart attacks ("crushing central chest pain") were built on male case histories, and women's atypical presentations were classified as "anxious" and under-treated. Legal language defaults to the "reasonable man" (only recently renamed "reasonable person") and the renaming did not change the underlying male yardstick built into jury instructions and case law. Computer science defaults to a generic *user* whose use cases were modelled on male engineers; crash-test dummies have been modelled on male anthropometric data; voice assistants default to female voices for subservient functions and male voices for authoritative ones. The same default-male logic that de Beauvoir identified in pronouns runs through pensions modelled on uninterrupted male careers and through parental-leave defaults that pay primary carers (still mostly women) less.

**Two limits worth flagging.** First, de Beauvoir's evidence is French; the analysis travels best to languages with grammatical gender (French, Czech, Spanish, German). Languages without gendered pronouns (Finnish, Turkish, Yoruba, Mandarin in speech) reproduce gender hierarchy through other channels: honorifics, occupational nouns, titles. The mechanism generalises; the morphology does not. Second, the marked/unmarked frame can re-marginalise trans, intersex and non-binary subjects whom the binary man/woman scheme does not capture. The trans-pronoun debate is precisely the contemporary inheritance of de Beauvoir's question: who else is being othered by the unmarked default?

**Conclusion.** "Woman is the Other" names a structural position, not a property. De Beauvoir treats the othering as linguistically structured because the asymmetry is encoded in grammar, prescribed by male grammarians, and preserved by apparently neutral reforms. The contemporary applications (medical diagnostic bias, legal language, AI defaults, trans-pronoun politics) vindicate the analysis while requiring us to extend its marked/unmarked grid beyond the man/woman pair de Beauvoir originally analysed.

### Essay 9 / Mock Q4: *Compare Ngũgĩ's argument for African-language writing with Achebe's defence of Africanised English.*

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o and Chinua Achebe agree on the diagnosis (English in Africa is not a neutral medium) and split on the prescription. Ngũgĩ argues for *displacement*: African writers should write in African languages. Achebe argues for *refashioning*: African writers should bend English to carry African experience. I will set out both positions on their own terms, identify the genuine disagreement between them, and argue that the disagreement is less zero-sum than it looks and that a serious decolonial-cultural programme needs both.

**The shared diagnosis.** Both writers accept that European languages entered Africa as instruments of empire. Ngũgĩ states it most starkly: "English, French, and Portuguese came to the Third World to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Mission schools elevated the conqueror's language, suppressed African languages, and produced an elite "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Achebe does not dispute this history. He accepts that he writes in the coloniser's tongue. There is no innocence in the medium. The disagreement is what to do next.

**Ngũgĩ's argument for African-language writing.** Ngũgĩ's position rests on a *carrier* theory of language. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), so writing in English carries English memory (English categories, English religion, English economic concepts) not Gĩkũyũ memory. He proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). The practical commitment matters: *Devil on the Cross* (1980) was drafted in Gĩkũyũ on toilet paper in detention precisely because the political content required a Gĩkũyũ form. The audience-and-politics argument follows: writing in Gĩkũyũ addresses the rural Gĩkũyũ-speaking masses; writing in English addresses an Anglophone elite Ngũgĩ has already identified as uprooted. The condition for any genuine "world" language, Ngũgĩ adds, is "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). Without that prior equality, the world language is just a colonial one wearing a global mask.

**Achebe's defence of Africanised English.** Achebe's position is pragmatic and stylistic. The African writer must reach a continental and global audience, and the writer who reaches only Hausa, Igbo or Yoruba speakers reaches a small slice. The writer should therefore "use English in a way that brings out his message best without altering the language so much that its value as a medium of international exchange will be lost. He should aim at fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). And, crucially, he should not aspire to native-speaker English: "Can he ever learn to use it like a native speaker? I should say, 'I hope not.' It is neither necessary nor desirable for him to be able to do so. The price a world language must be prepared to pay is submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The strategy is enacted in *Things Fall Apart* (1958): the title quotes Yeats, an Irish (post-colonial) poet, and the prose embeds Igbo proverbs into English syntax. English bends to carry Igbo memory.

**The genuine disagreement.** Three real splits. *Purity versus hybridity.* Ngũgĩ's strategy is purity-oriented: leave the contaminated language behind. Achebe's is hybridity-oriented: refashion it from inside. *Audience.* Ngũgĩ writes for the rural Gĩkũyũ-speaking masses; Achebe writes for a continental and global Anglophone audience including the African elite and Western readers. *Theory of change.* Ngũgĩ believes decolonising the mind requires linguistic decolonisation at the level of medium. Achebe believes meaningful decolonisation can proceed *through* a refashioned English; the empire's tools, properly retooled, serve the colonised.

**Where they converge.** Both insist that the colonial encounter was unequal and unjust. Neither accepts the view that English is innocently universal. Achebe's "I hope not" is a refusal of cultural assimilation as fierce in spirit as Ngũgĩ's refusal of the language itself. And both recognise that some lingua franca is needed for cross-national communication. They disagree only on whether it should be a coloniser's language refashioned (Achebe) or an African language elevated (Ngũgĩ).

**My evaluation: both/and.** Three reasons the disagreement is less zero-sum than it looks. *First*, the institutional setting matters. In primary schooling and adult literacy, Ngũgĩ's position is overwhelmingly more useful: people learn faster and engage politically more deeply in their first language. In international publishing and pan-African political debate, Achebe's position is more practical. *Second*, the educated African reader already crosses between Wolof, Yoruba, Swahili, English, French and Portuguese in daily life; pure linguistic separation is largely unavailable. The strategies coexist in practice. *Third*, the strongest synthesis is genuinely additive. Ngũgĩ's strategy protects African languages from extinction; Achebe's expands what English can carry. Together they shrink the colonial monopoly on prestige.

**Contemporary applications.** Singlish, Indian English, AAVE, Caribbean creoles and Hiberno-English all enact Achebe's strategy at the scale of whole speech communities. The Welsh and Māori revivals (*ysgolion cyfrwng Cymraeg*, *kōhanga reo*) enact Ngũgĩ's strategy inside the Anglophone metropolitan core. The contemporary debate over generative AI's English bias deepens the case for both: tools trained on Anglophone corpora make Achebe's refashioning project harder and Ngũgĩ's displacement project more urgent.

**Conclusion.** Ngũgĩ and Achebe disagree on whether the writer's job is to refuse the coloniser's language or to refashion it. I take the disagreement to mark a productive division of labour rather than a contradiction. A serious decolonial-cultural programme needs the African-language schooling Ngũgĩ demands *and* the refashioned English Achebe defends, and neither alone is enough to dislodge the colonial attitude from its linguistic infrastructure.

### Essay 10 / Mock Q5: *How does the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis help us think about generative AI's English-language bias?*

I will argue that the *weak* version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (linguistic relativity) is exactly the framework we need to understand generative AI's English bias, and that the *strong* version (linguistic determinism) should be retired for the same reasons it was retired in classical sociolinguistics. The weak version explains why a system trained mostly on English texts routes attention through Anglophone categories without "thinking" of itself as doing so. The strong version overreaches and risks turning a corporate-engineering problem into a metaphysical one.

**The hypothesis, briefly.** The lecture presents the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis in two forms. Linguistic relativity: "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230). Linguistic determinism: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231). The lecture's "Eskimo words for snow" example flags the determinism reading as both empirically weak and politically risky. "They live in a very different world from 'us'" (Lecture 4 slide 8) reproduces colonial othering.

**Where AI training data sits in the Sapir-Whorf frame.** Large language models such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are trained on text corpora dominated by English. Recent estimates put English at over 90% of the training data for major Anglophone-built systems, despite English being the first language of only about 5% of the world's population. The system is not "born" into a language the way a child is; it is *assembled out of* texts. But the relativity claim transfers: the model's representational space is "an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230), where the perceiving partners are now English-language Wikipedia editors, Reddit users, news writers and academic publishers. Whatever world that corpus carries, the model carries. This is linguistic relativity at the scale of code.

**Concrete manifestations of the bias.** Four. *First*, factual accuracy and reasoning quality degrade outside English. Benchmarks consistently show major LLMs performing best in English, with measurable drops in low-resource languages such as Amharic, Burmese, Welsh and Gĩkũyũ. *Second*, translation defaults to gendered pronouns in target languages even when the source is genderless: "the doctor said *he* will see you now" is a notorious case, recreating Dale Spender's "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) at the scale of automated translation. *Third*, ethical and normative reasoning anchors on Anglo-American frames: what counts as a fair example, a reasonable response, or an offensive prompt is calibrated to Anglophone moderator priors. The "reasonable man" of legal English now has an algorithmic descendant: the *reasonable user* whose objections the safety classifier models. *Fourth*, social-media moderation (partly delegated to similar models) works far better in English than in Burmese, Tigrinya or Amharic; Facebook's failure to moderate Burmese-language hate speech contributed directly to incitement during the Rohingya crisis.

**How the weak Sapir-Whorf reading does the work.** Linguistic relativity tells us *why* this matters. The model's training corpus is the agreement-to-perceive that organises its outputs. English-language priors make some queries easy (the model fluently discusses Shakespeare, US politics, Anglo-American case law) and others hard (it stumbles on Yoruba proverbs, Hindi grammar, Quechua syntax). The asymmetry is not a metaphysical "different world": it is a routing of attention by training data. Ngũgĩ's framework extends naturally: just as colonial schools "elevated the language of the conqueror" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) and produced an elite "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38), AI corpora elevate Anglophone vocabulary and produce a generation of digital tools uprooted from the linguistic majority of the world. The colonial attitude has been globalised at the level of training data.

**Why the strong reading should be retired.** Three reasons, mirroring the classical objections. First, translation is calibrable, not absolute: multilingual LLMs already do non-trivial translation work, and the friction is engineering-tractable rather than metaphysically fixed. Second, models are plastic: fine-tuning, retrieval augmentation and multilingual training expand the inventory continuously. Spender's programme of making subjects linguistically visible has a direct computational analogue. Third, the strong reading recreates the colonial othering the lecture warns against. To say "speakers of low-resource languages live in a different cognitive world" repeats the "Eskimo words for snow" mistake (Lecture 4 slide 8) at the level of AI commentary. Better to say: corporate investment decisions have produced unequal digital tools, and the inequality can be reformed by policy.

**Contemporary applications.** *Translation in healthcare and legal settings.* When AI is used to translate medical consent forms or asylum interviews, the gendered-default and English-prior biases become safety-critical: misgendering a patient or mistranslating a refugee claim has direct material consequences. *Educational tools.* AI tutors trained on Anglophone curricula reproduce the Anglo-American canon and crowd out local pedagogical traditions: the digital descendant of the "Oxford Readers for Africa" Ngũgĩ describes (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34). *Code-switching in diaspora communities.* Multilingual users report that LLMs flatten code-switching into monolingual outputs, erasing the linguistic creativity Achebe celebrated when he called for the "submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347).

**Critical caveats.** Halewood's warning applies here: "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Adding non-English data to training corpora without changing the upstream extraction relations (who owns the data, who labels it, who is paid) reproduces the colonial pattern in new clothes. And Sapir-Whorf alone is too thin a theory; we need Ngũgĩ on imperial language policy, Spender on prescriptive grammar, and de Beauvoir on the unmarked default to make sense of which language biases matter most.

**Conclusion.** The weak Sapir-Whorf hypothesis helps us see generative AI's English bias as a structural routing of attention by training data, not a quirk of engineering. The strong version should be left in the classical-sociolinguistics graveyard. Decolonising the digital mind requires the same work decolonising the analogue mind required: structural investment in non-English corpora, indigenous-language tooling, and refusal of the assumption that the Anglophone training set is the unmarked default.

### Essay 11 / Mock Q6: *Is linguistic determinism a coherent thesis or a category error?*

My short answer is: linguistic determinism is *not* a coherent thesis. It is a category error, a metaphysical claim about cognitive moulding masquerading as an empirical claim about language use. The category error can be diagnosed precisely, and once it is diagnosed the useful work the thesis was meant to do is taken over by linguistic relativity plus an account of institutional power. I will defend this position in five moves.

**The thesis.** Linguistic determinism is the strong version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. The lecture's framing is decisive: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231). The image presents a strict implication: what a language fails to encode, its speakers cannot think. The textbook example ("Eskimos have 50 words for snow", therefore Inuit speakers perceive snow with finer discrimination than English speakers, Lecture 4 slide 8) is the determinism reading.

**Move one: translation refutes the strong claim.** If language fully determined thought, translation would be impossible. In practice translation succeeds constantly (between Mandarin and English, Yoruba and German, Finnish and Hindi) with calibrable friction rather than metaphysical absence. The very existence of multilingual speakers, who hold several "moulds" simultaneously without their minds shattering, undermines the determinism claim. Ngũgĩ writes in English and Gĩkũyũ; Achebe writes in English with Igbo idioms embedded in the syntax; bilingual diasporic communities code-switch fluently. These speakers refute the strong claim by their daily practice.

**Move two: plasticity refutes it again.** English speakers without a single-word lexicon for "wet snow" build the concept out of phrases ("good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing") as the lecturer notes (Lecture 4 slide 9). Languages absorb new vocabulary continuously: *internet*, *selfie*, *misogynoir*, *cisgender*, *intersectionality*. If language determined thought, neologisms would be impossible: speakers would lack the cognitive machinery to coin words for concepts the language did not already encode. The fact that new concepts are coined precisely *because* they were needed shows thought leads language at least as often as language leads thought. Dale Spender's whole programme (to make women linguistically visible by inventing or repurposing vocabulary, Spender, 1980: 162) presupposes plasticity. So does the contemporary contest over the singular *they* and the trans-pronoun neologisms (*per*, *xe*).

**Move three: the political risk diagnoses the category error.** This is the heart of the case. Taken straight, determinism becomes "they live in a very different world from 'us'" (Lecture 4 slide 8). It sets up an "us / them" division in which the speakers of one language are exotic others compared to a Western "us". Ngũgĩ's framework reads this directly: this is the colonial attitude in epistemological dress. The "Eskimo" example is itself an externally imposed label (Samson, 2003), and the wonderment at indigenous perception is a continuation of the missionary outlook: the indigenous group as the curious other against the implicit Western benchmark. The category error is that determinism *describes a political relation in cognitive vocabulary*. A sociologically responsible account of language and power cannot rest on a framework that reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

**Move four: relativity does the useful work.** The weak version of Sapir-Whorf (linguistic relativity) keeps the useful insight (language channels attention, shapes categories, makes some thoughts easier than others) without the unsupportable claim that it *fixes* the limits of thought. Spender's notion of man-made language and the "plus male / minus male" classification scheme (Spender, 1980: 145), de Beauvoir's woman-as-other and the marked/unmarked logic, Ngũgĩ's decolonising the mind and the "carrier" theory of language as "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and Halewood's processual reading all rest on linguistic relativity, not determinism. Each thinker grants that speakers can refashion their inventories: that is precisely how Achebe's Africanised English (Achebe, 1997: 347) and the Welsh, Gaelic and Māori revivals proceed.

**Move five: institutional gatekeeping, not cognitive moulding.** One last steelman of the determinism position. Even if speakers can in principle coin new vocabulary, the institutions that publish, teach and credential a language shape what concepts most speakers will encounter. Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) names a real structural constraint: African-origin languages occupy no place of honour at the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38); generative AI is overwhelmingly trained on English; scientific publishing happens in English. I accept this point, but it is properly an account of institutional power, not of cognitive moulding. Better to call it linguistic *gatekeeping* than linguistic determinism. The gatekeeping is real and reformable by policy; the moulding is metaphysical and unreformable by design.

**What about strong empirical findings?** Some empirical work (Lera Boroditsky on grammatical gender's effects on object descriptions, on spatial frames of reference in Guugu Yimithirr, on colour categorisation) is sometimes read as supporting determinism. I think this misreads it. The findings show that grammatical categories *bias* perception and memory; they do not show that speakers are *unable* to perceive or describe what their language under-codes. Bias is the language of relativity; impossibility is the language of determinism. The empirical record supports the former, not the latter.

**Contemporary applications.** *Generative AI* is the cleanest contemporary test. Models trained on Anglophone corpora exhibit a clear linguistic-relativity profile (Anglo-American categories are easier to invoke, low-resource languages produce degraded outputs) without any cognitive "moulding" in the determinist sense. The bias is corporate-engineering, not metaphysical. *Medical diagnostic categories* show the same pattern: textbook descriptions of heart attacks bias attention toward male presentations without making female presentations cognitively impossible. *Legal "reasonable man" tests* bias jury reasoning without making other reasoning unthinkable.

**Conclusion.** Linguistic determinism is a category error: it dresses a political and institutional relation in the language of cognitive impossibility, and in doing so reproduces colonial othering. The useful work the determinism claim wants to do is done better by linguistic relativity plus an account of institutional power: Spender on prescription, Ngũgĩ on imperial language policy, Halewood on lexical sedimentation. Language does not determine thought, but it powerfully channels it; and the channels are set by institutions, not by metaphysical moulds.

### M7: *Decolonising the mind in the age of large language models.*

Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) was written for a world in which the colonial language was the medium of school, court and parliament. We now live in a world in which a new institution (the large language model) has joined those three as a producer of the everyday categories of knowledge. I will argue that decolonising the mind in the age of LLMs requires extending Ngũgĩ's framework in three ways: from human pedagogy to algorithmic pedagogy, from political-economic gatekeeping to data-economic gatekeeping, and from mission-school humiliation to the subtler humiliation of degraded multilingual performance.

**Ngũgĩ's framework, briefly.** Ngũgĩ's argument is that European languages entered Africa "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Mission schools elevated the conqueror's language and suppressed African languages; children caught speaking their own languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Decades after independence, the colonial languages remain "the languages of power" of administration, justice and commerce (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37), and the elite is "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30); the colonial school replaced the memory bank, and the postcolonial state has not restored it.

**First extension: algorithmic pedagogy.** LLMs such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are now the default tutoring infrastructure for millions of students globally. Their training corpora are overwhelmingly English: recent estimates put English at over 90% of pre-training data for major Anglophone-built systems, despite English being the first language of only about 5% of the world's population. This is mission-school logic at industrial scale. A Kenyan undergraduate using ChatGPT to study physics or political theory receives the same English-language pedagogy Ngũgĩ describes for the "Oxford Readers for Africa" with John and Joan visiting the Thames (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34), but now mediated by a model that fluently produces Anglo-American examples and stumbles on local ones. The model has, in Ngũgĩ's phrase, furnished students with "new mirrors" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32): algorithmic ones this time.

**Second extension: data-economic gatekeeping.** Ngũgĩ's complaint that knowledge is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) generalises to training-data economics. Low-resource languages (Amharic, Tigrinya, Burmese, Welsh, Gĩkũyũ, Quechua) are systematically under-represented in pre-training; the LLMs that result perform substantially worse in those languages on factual recall, reasoning and translation. The pattern echoes the corporate decisions of social-media platforms: Facebook's failure to moderate Burmese-language hate speech contributed directly to incitement during the Rohingya crisis. The colonial attitude (that real knowledge happens in English) has been encoded in the cost-curve of model training. African-origin languages occupy no place of honour at the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38); they occupy a similar lack of place in the training corpora that increasingly mediate global knowledge.

**Third extension: degraded performance as humiliation.** Ngũgĩ's "I am stupid" plaque (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) has a subtle LLM-era descendant. When a Gĩkũyũ speaker asks ChatGPT a question in Gĩkũyũ and receives a hallucinated, ungrammatical or non-responsive answer, the implicit message is similar: your language is not a serious language; switch to English to receive serious help. This is not pedagogical violence in Ngũgĩ's literal sense, but it is epistemic violence of a recognisable kind: the speaker learns to internalise the model's incompetence as their own linguistic inadequacy.

**Three counter-currents.** Decolonising the mind in the age of LLMs is not a counsel of despair. *First*, Ngũgĩ's proposal that Kiswahili could serve as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41) translates directly into a training-data programme: deliberate over-investment in African-origin languages, indigenous-language fine-tunes, and African-led model development (the Masakhane and Lelapa AI initiatives are concrete examples). *Second*, Achebe's strategy of refashioning English (Achebe, 1997: 347), bending the dominant language to carry African experience, applies inside LLM prompting itself. Speakers who interleave Igbo proverbs, Yoruba syntax or AAVE rhythm into prompts can produce outputs that resist the Anglo-American default. The "submission to many different kinds of use" Achebe demanded of a world language (Achebe, 1997: 347) is enacted prompt by prompt. *Third*, Welsh, Gaelic and Māori revival projects offer templates: where the predominant class "has no use for [a language]" (Welsh Language Society, cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36), structural investment can restore use. Bòrd na Gàidhlig, *kōhanga reo* and the BBC's Welsh and Scots digital divisions show that revival can be funded into existence.

**Critical complications.** Halewood's warning applies with extra force here: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Adding non-English data to training corpora without changing the upstream extraction relations (who owns the data, who labels it, whose attention is monetised) reproduces the colonial pattern in new clothes. The Hindi/Urdu split is a useful historical analogy: a single spoken language partitioned by colonial script politics into two literary standards is a precedent for how technical infrastructure can carve linguistic communities into administrative categories that do not match their actual speech. We should be alert to LLM tokenisation schemes that do similar carving.

**Synthesis with Spender, de Beauvoir and Halewood.** The LLM bias is not only colonial. Spender's notion of man-made language predicts (and inspections confirm) that LLMs default to male pronouns in translation, encode male-as-norm in occupational descriptions, and reproduce the "reasonable user" of safety classification as a male-coded prior. De Beauvoir's woman-as-other is enacted automatically when models gender ambiguous referents as male. Halewood's processual reading reminds us that LLM outputs are not fixed: they are continuously remade by fine-tuning, RLHF and prompting. The decolonising-the-mind agenda runs alongside the de-patriarchalising one.

**Conclusion.** Decolonising the mind in the age of large language models requires Ngũgĩ's full framework, extended from school to server, from missionary pedagogy to training-data economics, from "I am stupid" plaques to degraded multilingual outputs. The strategies remain the ones the SC301 reading list offers: structural investment in indigenous languages, refashioning the dominant language from inside, and refusing the assumption that the Anglophone default is universal.

### M8: *Does the rise of multilingual AI translation tools strengthen or undermine Ngũgĩ's argument?*

I will argue that the rise of multilingual AI translation tools *strengthens* Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's argument on balance, not because the tools liberate indigenous languages but because the way they fail and the way they succeed both confirm Ngũgĩ's diagnosis. The optimistic case (translation tools democratise multilingual knowledge) turns out to misread what Ngũgĩ was complaining about; the pessimistic case (translation tools deepen English dominance) tracks his argument more closely. I will set out both readings, defend my verdict, and identify where translation tools *could* be redesigned to genuinely advance the decolonising-the-mind agenda.

**The optimistic reading, briefly.** A first-glance argument is that Google Translate, DeepL, ChatGPT translation and emerging multilingual LLMs democratise access across linguistic boundaries. A Gĩkũyũ speaker can now read scientific articles in English; an Amharic speaker can communicate with a Tamil speaker; literary works circulate faster across language pairs. If Ngũgĩ's complaint was that knowledge is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37), translation tools appear to unlock the prison.

**Why the optimistic reading misreads Ngũgĩ.** Ngũgĩ's argument is not principally about access to translation. It is about which language is the medium in which knowledge is produced, taught, and credentialed. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30); the "carrier" aspect of language imports the coloniser's history, religion and economic categories alongside its grammar. Translation tools that move texts from English into Gĩkũyũ do not return the production of knowledge to Gĩkũyũ. They consolidate English as the originating language and the African language as the translation target. The grammar of the relationship (what gets produced first, and in what language) is the colonial grammar Ngũgĩ identified.

**Three ways translation tools strengthen Ngũgĩ's argument.** *First, asymmetric quality*. Translation between English and other high-resource European languages (French, German, Spanish) works far better than translation between English and low-resource African or Indigenous languages. Translation pairs that do not involve English at all are catastrophically worse: Yoruba to Welsh, Quechua to Maori. This recreates Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) at the level of code: English is the implicit hub through which other languages are routed. The colonial geography of language has been reproduced as the engineering geography of multilingual NLP.

*Second, gendered and cultural defaults.* When source languages are genderless, translation tools default to gendered English pronouns: "the doctor said *he* will see you now", "the nurse said *she* will help". This recreates Dale Spender's "plus male / minus male" classification (Spender, 1980: 145) automatically in every translation. Cultural references default to Anglo-American examples: idioms get translated through American mid-Atlantic priors. The "Oxford Readers for Africa" with John and Joan visiting the Thames (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34) has a digital descendant in the default examples LLMs produce when asked to illustrate concepts.

*Third, training-data asymmetry as new gatekeeping.* The big-tech corporations that fund multilingual translation invest disproportionately in languages with the largest commercial markets (Mandarin, Spanish, Hindi, Arabic) and under-invest in lower-commercial-value languages even where speaker populations are substantial. Kenneth Baker's "amazed" discovery that Russians in Novosibirsk were learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) has a translation-tool descendant: low-resource-language speakers learn that the path to AI-mediated knowledge runs through English regardless. African-origin languages occupy "no place of honour" at the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38); they occupy an analogous lack of place in the training data of multilingual translation tools.

**Where the optimistic reading partially survives.** Three concessions. *First*, deliberate African-led projects (Masakhane, Lelapa AI, the Niger-Congo language model initiatives) show that the tools *can* be retooled. This is Achebe's refashioning strategy applied to translation infrastructure: "the price a world language must be prepared to pay is submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347), now applied to the world's translation backbone. *Second*, real practical access is real. A community health worker in rural Kenya who can translate medical instructions into Gĩkũyũ via an LLM is doing genuine work. The tool is not zero-sum; it can be both colonial and useful. *Third*, translation tools make linguistic asymmetries *visible*. Degraded performance in low-resource languages produces a measurable record of the colonial geography. This visibility is a precondition for reform.

**Concrete sites of the dilemma.** *Healthcare interpretation.* AI-mediated translation of consent forms and clinical interviews has been deployed widely; misgendering, mistranslation of symptoms and false confidence have produced real harms. *Asylum interviews.* Translation tools deployed in immigration contexts deepen the power asymmetry between Anglophone administrative systems and non-Anglophone applicants. *Social-media moderation.* Translation underpins automated moderation; Facebook's failure to moderate Burmese hate speech during the Rohingya crisis is the textbook case of inadequate translation deepening structural violence. *Educational tools.* AI tutors translating Anglo-American curricula reproduce the colonial pedagogy Ngũgĩ describes, only faster.

**What would actually advance the decolonising agenda?** Three requirements, all in Ngũgĩ's idiom. *First*, structural investment in African-origin languages as *source* languages, not just *target* languages, so that texts originate in Gĩkũyũ, Yoruba, Quechua and are translated outward. *Second*, the political-economic precondition Ngũgĩ insisted on: "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). Translation tools cannot decolonise without the upstream extractive relations changing (who owns the data, who labels it, who is paid). *Third*, refusal of the assumption that the Anglophone default is universal. Halewood's warning applies: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Conclusion.** Multilingual AI translation tools strengthen Ngũgĩ's argument: they reproduce the colonial geography of language at the level of code, they automate Spender's male-as-norm pattern in every translation, and they consolidate English as the implicit hub of multilingual knowledge. The tools could be redesigned to advance the decolonising-the-mind agenda, but the redesign requires structural investment and political-economic equality, not just better engineering. Ngũgĩ's diagnosis travels intact from 1986 to the present; it has only acquired new hardware.

### M9: *How convincing do you find Spender's claim that English is structurally patriarchal?*

I find Spender's claim broadly convincing (*as a structural claim about the prescriptive history of standard written English and its inherited categories*) but only partially convincing as a claim about English as a whole. Three qualifications matter: the claim is anglocentric, it under-weights speaker plasticity, and it presupposes a binary gender frame that contemporary trans politics has rightly contested. I will set out Spender's argument, mark what I find compelling, and identify where the case needs supplementing.

**Spender's claim, briefly.** Dale Spender's notion of man-made language is structural rather than anecdotal. The core thesis from *Man Made Language* (1980) is that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The male is the default human; the female is a deviation marked by additional morphology (*actress*, *waitress*, *poetess*) or by extra honorifics (*Mrs* and *Miss* mark marital status; *Mr* does not). Spender's historical evidence is specific. Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147). Kirkby (1746) in his "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The "man-made" label is not metaphorical: it tracks an archive of male grammarians prescribing male precedence. Her programme follows: "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**Why I find the core claim convincing.** Three reasons.

*First, the historical archive is decisive.* Spender's case does not rest on a hunch about contemporary English. It rests on a paper trail. Named grammarians, dated rules, and explicit justifications for male precedence ("more comprehensive than the female") are evidence of prescription, not of natural drift. The "man-made" label is descriptively accurate: English was made, at the level of standardised grammar, by men who prescribed male precedence.

*Second, the contemporary asymmetries persist.* The generic *he* in pre-reform legal drafting; the *Mr* / *Mrs* / *Miss* asymmetry that marks women's marital status but not men's; the male-coded vocabulary of authority (*chairman*, *manpower*, *manhole*, *husbandry*, *fireman*); the "reasonable man" of tort law (only recently renamed "reasonable person"); the medical textbook default of male anatomy and the resulting gender-based diagnostic bias in heart-attack presentations: all of these track Spender's diagnosis. Halewood's processual reading reinforces it with the example of "chair": from Latin *cathedra* (the bishop's seat), the word carries male-clerical authority forward even after the gendered suffix is removed (Halewood, 2021: 128-130).

*Third, de Beauvoir's social ontology fits.* De Beauvoir's woman-as-other (man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15)) is exactly the social complement to Spender's grammatical case. If grammar were sociologically arbitrary, the correspondence between grammatical unmarkedness (masculine) and social unmarkedness (male as default human) would be a coincidence. That it holds across French, English, German, Russian and Czech is more consistent than coincidence allows.

**Three qualifications that limit the claim's convincingness.**

*First, anglocentrism.* Spender's evidence base is overwhelmingly English. Languages without grammatical gender (Mandarin, Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Yoruba, Persian) cannot be "man-made" in the same morphological sense. Yet gender hierarchies plainly exist in those societies. They operate through other channels (title forms of address, occupational nouns, honorifics). This means the *mechanism* Spender names is local to languages like English; the *general* sociological pattern needs different vocabulary.

*Second, speaker plasticity.* Spender's framing slightly understates how the language is being continuously *re*-made. Achebe's intuition that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) applies inside English itself. The singular *they* (long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, Halewood, 2021: 128) has been revived. The abolition of "actress" (Marsh, 2014), gender-neutral *flight attendant* and *firefighter*, the move from *master/slave* to *primary/replica* in computing all show English is plastic. Spender herself is not deterministic (her programme is precisely to re-make the language) but the "structurally patriarchal" framing can sound more fixed than her own argument allows.

*Third, the binary frame.* Spender's "plus male / minus male" scaffolding presupposes binary gender. Contemporary trans politics has exposed the cost of that scaffolding: Whittle and colleagues, cited by Halewood, argue that "personal pronouns are limiting and exclusionary for some of those who identify as 'trans'" (Halewood, 2021: 128). The patriarchal structure Spender names operates not on a man/woman pair but on a marked-versus-unmarked grid, where trans, non-binary and intersex subjects are also marked. The diagnosis survives the qualification; the binary scaffolding does not.

**Three contemporary tests.** *Trans-pronoun debates* vindicate the structural claim: the intensity of the workplace, school and legal contests is exactly the kind of prescription Spender's archive documented, only with reformers and reactionaries fighting for prescriptive authority. *Generative AI* extends the case: translation tools default to gendered pronouns even when source languages are genderless, automating the male-as-norm pattern at the scale of code. *Medical diagnostic language and the "reasonable man"* of legal English show the male-as-default running through professional vocabularies that handle high-stakes decisions.

**Caveats from Halewood and Ngũgĩ.** Halewood is right that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Spender's slogan that women must become "linguistically visible" is a precondition for, not a substitute for, structural change. And Ngũgĩ's framework reminds us that patriarchy is one of several hierarchies operating through linguistic structure; the colonial-attitude one operates by similar mechanisms, and the two often intersect (trans women of colour in Anglophone systems bear both patterns at once).

**Conclusion.** Spender's claim that English is structurally patriarchal is convincing as a structural-historical diagnosis. The qualifications (anglocentrism, speaker plasticity, binary scaffolding) do not refute the case; they refine it. The strongest version is: standard English was prescriptively patriarchalised by named grammarians, the prescription continues to shape contemporary defaults, and the structure is reformable by deliberate intervention. Less than that understates the historical archive; more than that overstates the rigidity of language.

### M10: *Using the work of at least two authors, explain how everyday professional vocabularies (medical, legal, technical) operate as vehicles of inequality.*

I will draw on Dale Spender, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, and supporting moves from Simone de Beauvoir and Mark Halewood to argue that medical, legal and technical vocabularies operate as vehicles of inequality through a single mechanism: they encode an unmarked default subject (the male, the Anglophone, the European) and they make everyone else a marked deviation requiring its own chapter. The mechanism runs through three professional domains in parallel, and reforming each requires both vocabulary change and the structural change that vocabulary alone cannot deliver.

**The shared mechanism.** Spender's notion of man-made language identifies the structural template. English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir generalises: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Ngũgĩ's framework extends the same template to colonial geography: European languages came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) and remain "the languages of power" of administration, justice and commerce (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Professional vocabularies inherit both templates simultaneously, which is what makes them especially dense sites of inequality.

**Medical English.** The clearest case of male-as-default in a professional vocabulary. Until late in the twentieth century, clinical trials systematically under-recruited women, and medical textbooks built their descriptions of disease presentation on male cases. The result is gender-based diagnostic bias: women presenting with heart attacks were misclassified as "anxious" because the textbook description ("crushing central chest pain radiating to the left arm") tracked male physiology. The atypical presentations more common in women (nausea, jaw pain, fatigue) were coded "atypical" precisely because the male presentation was the unmarked default. De Beauvoir's framework reads this exactly. The vocabulary itself carries the hierarchy: "hysteria" (etymologically from Greek *hystera*, womb) pathologised women's symptoms as inherently gynecological. The DSM and ICD have slowly renamed such categories, but residues remain in "medically unexplained symptoms" and "functional disorders", which patient advocates argue still disproportionately attach to women and ethnic minorities. The vocabulary of "compliance" (patient does what doctor says) versus "non-compliance" encodes a hierarchical model of clinical authority that "shared decision-making" tries to dismantle. Halewood's warning applies: renaming alone "is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126), but the language of "patient" versus "client" versus "service user" routes attention differently. Two authors, one diagnosis.

**Legal English.** The cleanest case of structural male-as-norm. The "reasonable man" of tort law set the standard of due care by reference to a masculine ideal type ("the man on the Clapham omnibus") and only relatively recently was renamed "reasonable person". The renaming is symbolic; the underlying yardstick was male and continues to track male reasoning patterns. Legal drafting until the late twentieth century used the generic *he* throughout statutes, contracts and case reports, embedding a male default in the foundational documents of civil and criminal life. Spender's archive (Wilson (1553) prescribing "male and female", Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" (Spender, 1980: 147-148)) shows this was prescribed, not natural. Legal English carries Ngũgĩ's colonial-attitude pattern too. In Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire the higher courts conduct business in the coloniser's language: Ngũgĩ's "languages of power" of administration and justice (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). The Indian Supreme Court operates in English despite Hindi's constitutional status. Litigants who do not command English are gatekept out of administrative justice. The Hindi/Urdu split (one spoken language partitioned by colonial script politics into the de facto languages of Hindu-majority and Muslim-majority legal traditions) shows colonial language policy continuing to structure legal life decades after independence. The vocabulary of "developing", "transitional" and "fragile" jurisdictions, generated in Anglophone law-and-development discourse, carries the same comparative measure against an implicit Western benchmark.

**Computer-science English.** The newest professional vocabulary and the most explicitly contested. The terms *master* and *slave* drives, *blacklist* and *whitelist*, the *kill* command, *master* branch in version control, and *fork* / *child* / *parent* process all carry overt or sedimented hierarchies. Recent reforms (*master*/*slave* to *primary*/*replica*, *blacklist*/*whitelist* to *blocklist*/*allowlist*, *master* branch to *main*) exemplify Achebe's principle that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Computer science also carries Spender's male-as-norm. The generic *user* in user-experience design was modelled on male engineers; consumer products from voice assistants (defaulting to female voices for subservient functions, male voices for authoritative ones) to medical devices (defaulting to male anthropometric data) encode the asymmetry. Social-media moderation works far better in English than in Burmese, Tigrinya or Amharic: Sapir-Whorf at the level of code. Facebook's failure to moderate Burmese-language hate speech contributed directly to incitement during the Rohingya crisis. Generative AI extends the pattern: ChatGPT's English bias and the gendered defaults in translation reproduce both Spender's and Ngũgĩ's diagnoses in the algorithmic age.

**Why two authors are enough to explain the pattern.** Spender supplies the gender mechanism (male-as-norm encoded at the level of grammar and prescription); Ngũgĩ supplies the colonial mechanism (European-languages-as-norm encoded at the level of professional institutions). The two patterns are not parallel; they intersect. Trans women of colour navigating Anglophone medical, legal and computing systems experience both at once. The professional vocabularies operate as vehicles of inequality precisely because they bundle the patterns together: a single set of words doing the gender-and-empire work simultaneously.

**Counter-currents.** Vocabulary is plastic. *Ms* replacing *Mrs/Miss*, *reasonable person* replacing *reasonable man*, *main* replacing *master*, *patient-centred* replacing *compliant patient* are live re-fashionings. Achebe's strategy applies at the level of each vocabulary: the field can be made to "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). But Halewood's warning is decisive: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Renaming changes who feels addressed but not who gets paid, hired or treated. Vocabulary reform without clinical trial redesign, jury-instruction reform, training-data investment and pay equity is symbolic.

**Conclusion.** Medical English carries male-bodied defaults that produce diagnostic inequality; legal English carries male-as-norm reasoning and colonial-language gatekeeping; computer-science English carries both old male-as-norm patterns and explicit colonial-era metaphors. Spender and Ngũgĩ, supplemented by de Beauvoir and Halewood, supply the analytic vocabulary to see all three as instances of one structural mechanism: the unmarked default. Reforming each vocabulary is necessary but must be paired with structural change in the institutions (clinical trials, jury instructions, platform investment) that the vocabulary serves.

### M11: *Can sociology be done in English without reproducing the colonial mind?*

I will argue: only partially, and only if it is done self-consciously. Sociology in English is a contradiction the discipline cannot fully escape but can practise more or less honestly. My position is closer to Achebe's than to Ngũgĩ's: the right strategy is to refashion English sociological writing from inside while acknowledging that the limit case (a fully decolonised sociology) requires institutional changes the discipline cannot deliver alone.

**Why the question is sharp.** Ngũgĩ's claim is that European languages came to the Third World "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) and that even after independence they remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Sociology, as an academic discipline taught and published mostly in English, is part of this linguistic infrastructure. Its categories ("society", "modernity", "the state", "race", "class", "informal economy", "third world", "global south") were largely forged in English (and French and German) and they carry English (and French and German) assumptions about the social. Doing sociology in English is, on Ngũgĩ's account, doing sociology in the language of the master-discourse the discipline claims to analyse.

**Three ways the colonial mind enters English-language sociology.**

*First, the conceptual vocabulary.* Many of the discipline's foundational categories assume a European trajectory. "Modernity" is the worked-up self-description of the European nineteenth century; "the state" is the post-Westphalian European state; "society" was theorised by Durkheim and Weber in opposition to a "primitive" or "traditional" other. The vocabulary of "developing", "transitional", "fragile" and "informal" all carry an implicit measure against an unmarked Anglo-European benchmark, mirroring de Beauvoir's woman-as-other at the scale of nations. When English-language sociology speaks of "the informal sector", it is the unmarked "formal sector" (i.e. the Northern wage economy) that holds the comparison still.

*Second, the prestige economy of publishing.* Anglophone journals dominate citation counts, hiring committees and promotion criteria. Scholars in non-Anglophone universities must publish in English to be visible to the discipline's "centre", reproducing what Ngũgĩ calls the "linguistic prison" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) in academic form. Kenneth Baker, cited by Ngũgĩ, was "amazed" to find Russians learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35); the equivalent today is the social scientist in Nairobi or Manila publishing in *Sociology* or *American Sociological Review* because the path to citation runs through English. The colonial attitude (that *real* knowledge happens in English) is enforced by the discipline's own institutions.

*Third, the everyday register of academic English.* Spender's archive of male grammarians prescribing male precedence (Spender, 1980: 147-148) has a sociological-prose descendant. Academic English encodes professional defaults (the "reasonable" interlocutor, the "we" of the disciplinary "we", the citation conventions modelled on European canon) that quietly enforce who counts as a serious knower. Halewood's analysis of "chair" (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) generalises: even "neutral" academic vocabulary carries sedimented authority that is not innocent.

**Three things sociology in English can still do honestly.** Three reasons I do not draw a despairing conclusion.

*First, Achebe's refashioning strategy applies inside the discipline.* The African writer must use "an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Sociologists can do the same: write English sociology that interleaves untranslated terms (*ubuntu*, *jeitinho*, *amae*, *guanxi*), refuses to flatten local categories into English ones, and insists on the "I hope not" answer to whether non-native speakers should aspire to native-like academic English. The "submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) Achebe demanded of a world language is achievable inside sociological writing.

*Second, self-critical attention to vocabulary.* The lecturer's caution about "Eskimo" (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8) generalises: every English-language sociological term should be interrogated for its colonial sediment. Haraway's warning ("all dreams for a perfectly true language… are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173)) is the right meta-rule. We will not get to a colonisation-free vocabulary; we can refuse the pretence that the current vocabulary is neutral. Calling racialised categorisations "ethnic origin" instead of "race", or "majority-world" instead of "developing world", will not fix the underlying inequalities but it can stop the language doing some of their work for them.

*Third, structural reforms inside the discipline.* The work that translation tools cannot do, scholarly infrastructure can. Translating major sociological works into and out of Hindi, Yoruba, Swahili, Quechua; supporting non-English-language journals; funding indigenous-language teaching of sociological concepts; recognising non-Anglophone publishing in hiring decisions. These are the disciplinary counterparts of the Welsh, Gaelic and Māori revivals: structural investment, not symbolic gesture.

**Where the limits bite.** Ngũgĩ's stronger claim (that the medium of knowledge production matters intrinsically, not just instrumentally) means that some forms of sociological knowledge cannot be produced fully in English. The sociology of Gĩkũyũ family forms, of Andean reciprocity practices, of Burmese Buddhist ethics is impoverished when squeezed into Anglophone analytic categories. Trans-pronoun debates inside English are a smaller-scale version of the same problem: the available vocabulary forces certain framings. Halewood's warning applies: "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The structural fix requires multilingual sociology, not just self-critical Anglophone sociology.

**Contemporary applications.** Generative AI's English bias is now part of the problem: graduate students using LLMs for literature review and drafting receive an Anglophone-prior version of any field they study. Code-switching in diaspora communities (Yoruba-English, Punjabi-English, AAVE-English) is exactly the sociological resource Anglophone academic writing is least equipped to capture. The Hindi/Urdu split shows that even at the level of a single spoken language, colonial-era classifications continue to structure what counts as a serious knowledge tradition.

**Conclusion.** Sociology cannot be done in English without reproducing *some* of the colonial mind. It can be done in English without reproducing *all* of it, if practitioners take Achebe's refashioning strategy seriously, interrogate the vocabulary, and push for structural multilingual infrastructure inside the discipline. The honest position is that English-language sociology is part of the problem it studies, and being self-conscious about that is not a substitute for changing the institutional conditions, but it is a precondition.

### M12: *"Language is not a vehicle but a battleground." Discuss with reference to the politics of minority-language revival.*

The slogan reframes language away from the metaphor of transport (language carries meaning from speaker to hearer) toward the metaphor of contested ground (language is fought over by competing political projects). I find the reframe productive but exaggerated, and I want to defend a more careful version: language is *both* vehicle and battleground, and minority-language revival is the cleanest demonstration of why the battleground reading must supplement the vehicle reading. I will work through Welsh, Gaelic, Māori and Hawaiian cases, drawing on Ngũgĩ, Achebe and Halewood.

**Why "vehicle" is insufficient.** The vehicle metaphor treats language as a neutral medium that conveys meaning between users. This is the framing the lecture criticises with the phrase "language is not a window on the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). If language were just a vehicle, the question of *which* language gets driven would be aesthetic or convenience-based. Minority-language revival would make no sense: speakers could simply switch to whichever language was most convenient. The fact that Welsh, Gaelic, Māori and Hawaiian speakers fight to preserve and reactivate their languages shows that something more than convenience is at stake. Ngũgĩ's carrier theory captures this: language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30); losing the language loses the world it carries.

**Why "battleground" captures what is missed.** Three reasons. *First*, language death is the residue of policy, not nature. Ngũgĩ cites Lyn Marierid of the Welsh Language Society writing in the *Morning Star* (21 October 1988): "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Welsh did not decline because Welsh speakers lost interest; it declined because English-medium administration, schooling, mining capital and tourism (what Ngũgĩ generalises as the "yuppiefication" of the Welsh hills) actively reduced the *utility* of Welsh in everyday economic life (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The Welsh Not, the wooden token hung around the necks of Welsh-speaking schoolchildren in the nineteenth century, is the British-internal counterpart of the plaques reading "I am stupid" that Ngũgĩ describes from colonial Kenya (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). The battleground framing recognises that this was a fight, not a fading. *Second*, revival is fought against active resistance, not just inertia. Funding decisions, school-medium choices, broadcasting licences, signage policies, civil-service language requirements: each is a contest. *Third*, language carries identity claims that other vehicles cannot. To speak Māori in Aotearoa or Welsh in Wales is to assert a political subjectivity that the dominant language does not encode.

**The Welsh case in detail.** Bilingual road signs and the establishment of S4C (Sianel Pedwar Cymru, the Welsh-language television channel) in 1982 created visibility; the Welsh Language Act 1993 and the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 created legal infrastructure; the Welsh-medium schooling system (*ysgolion cyfrwng Cymraeg*) produces new fluent speakers. The 2021 census shows Welsh proficiency holding around 17.8% of the population: neither catastrophic decline nor confident reversal. Halewood's warning applies: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Signage and broadcasting alone are *tinkering*; schooling, public-sector employment in the language, and digital infrastructure are *structural*. The Welsh battleground is being fought; the outcome is open.

**The Gaelic cases.** Irish Gaelic is the cautionary case. Despite constitutional status as the first official language (Article 8 of the 1937 Constitution) and compulsory schooling, daily-use figures are very low. Symbolic restoration without economic incentive produces credentialed non-speakers. The Scottish Gaelic case is intermediate: *Bòrd na Gàidhlig*, BBC Alba, and Gaelic-medium schooling in the Western Isles are slowly rebuilding a daily-use community, but the absolute numbers remain small. The contrast with Welsh shows that legal recognition is necessary but insufficient; the battleground requires sustained material commitment.

**The Pacific revivals.** Hawaiian and Māori were both nearly extinct in the mid-twentieth century. Both have been revived more successfully because of immersion schooling (*Pūnana Leo* in Hawai'i, *kōhanga reo* in Aotearoa New Zealand) combined with broadcasting, university programmes and state recognition. The pattern is consistent: revival succeeds when the language becomes economically useful again, fails when it remains only symbolically prestigious. The lecture explicitly groups Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish, Manx, Māori and Hawaiian as cases of internal-colonial linguistic casualty (Lecture 4 slide 26).

**Ngũgĩ's framework applied inside the Anglophone core.** Minority-language revival enacts Ngũgĩ's decolonising-the-mind agenda inside Britain, Ireland, the US and the Pacific. It presupposes Ngũgĩ's claim that language is a "collective memory bank" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30) and that losing the language loses the world it carries. Ngũgĩ's proposal that Kiswahili could serve as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41) translates into a precondition for any minority-language revival: the revival language must be allowed to *grow* (to acquire new vocabulary, new domains of use, new generations of speakers) rather than be preserved in museum form.

**Achebe's strategy alongside.** Achebe's complementary move (refashion the dominant language to carry minority experience, Achebe, 1997: 347) appears as Welsh English, Hiberno-English, Scottish English and Hawaiian Pidgin. The "submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) Achebe demanded operates at the interface between revived and dominant languages. The battleground has two fronts: defending the indigenous language *and* refashioning the dominant one.

**Critical complications.** Three. *Class.* Revival can become a middle-class project that excludes the original first-language communities. Welsh-medium urban schools, prized for their educational outcomes, sometimes serve incomers rather than rural Welsh speakers. *Authenticity wars.* Revived languages are inevitably modernised (neologisms for "computer", "vaccine", "spreadsheet") and purists sometimes contest these innovations. This is the Achebe/Ngũgĩ debate at the scale of single lexicons. *Risk of essentialism.* Tying ethnic identity to language can re-create the exclusions colonisation produced. A successful revival lets speakers be Welsh or Māori or Hawaiian *without* requiring a single linguistic test of belonging.

**Contemporary application.** Generative AI complicates the battleground in two directions. Low-resource minority languages perform badly on major LLMs, deepening the prestige gap. But community-led model fine-tunes (Te Reo Māori chatbots, Welsh-language LLM projects) show the digital battleground can be contested. The Hindi/Urdu split is a cautionary case from outside the Anglo-Celtic revival sphere: one spoken language partitioned by colonial script politics shows that revival is not always a single-language project.

**Conclusion.** "Language is not a vehicle but a battleground" overstates the case if read as denying the carrier function of language. Read as supplementing the vehicle metaphor with a recognition that language is *fought over*, the slogan is exactly right. Minority-language revival vindicates the battleground reading: Welsh, Gaelic, Māori and Hawaiian were not lost by attrition; they were defeated by policy, and they can only be revived by policy. The politics of revival are the politics of decolonising, fought on internal-colonial terrain.
