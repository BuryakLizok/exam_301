# SC301 / L4: Language and the World (Words and Things)

> Cheat sheet for revision. Lecture 4 covers three linked questions: (1) the relation between language and thought (the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis); (2) language and gender (Dale Spender, Simone de Beauvoir, Luce Irigaray); (3) language and the legacy of colonialism (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Chinua Achebe). The overriding claim: **language is not a neutral mirror of the world**. It classifies, evaluates, and is a mode of power.

---

## 1. Key concepts and thinkers (≤200 words)

- **Linguistic relativity / the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (Edward Sapir, Benjamin Lee Whorf): each language embodies a particular world view; speakers of a shared language are "partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230).
- **Linguistic determinism**: stronger claim that language *determines* thought, "moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231).
- **Man-made language** (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980): English is structured around the male-as-norm; female is "minus male".
- **The Second Sex / "woman is the other"** (Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, 1949/1988): man is positive and neutral; woman is the negative, the marked term.
- **Imperialism of language / decolonising the mind** (Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, 1993): colonial languages were weapons of conquest, "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37).
- **Africanising English** (Chinua Achebe, 'English and the African Writer', 1965/1997): English as a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347).
- **Sexuate subject / sexual difference is not extra-linguistic** (Luce Irigaray).
- **The generic he / chairman vs chair / actor vs actress**: concrete sites where male-as-norm is reproduced.

## 2. Key notes from the presentation

**Three parts to the lecture (slide 2).**

1. *Language, thought and culture*: linguistic relativity and linguistic determinism.
2. *Language and gender*: Spender, de Beauvoir.
3. *Language and the legacy of colonialism*: Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

**Sapir-Whorf (slides 3-6).** Linguistic relativity is "the belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230). The strong version, linguistic determinism, holds that the language we speak shapes the very limits of thought. The recurring textbook example is the claim that "Eskimos have over 50 words for snow", which the lecturer marks with scare-quotes to flag two problems: (a) "Eskimo" is itself an inaccurate, externally imposed label (see Samson, 2003); (b) the example is built on a single-word fixation that ignores how English speakers also describe many kinds of snow through *phrases* ("good snow for packing"). The slide highlights the load-bearing line: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**" (Lecture 4 slide 4).

**Critique of Sapir-Whorf (slides 7-9).** Any human language renders, rather than reflects, reality; we have lost any "innocent contact" with the world. Worse, the snow story sets up an "us / them" division in which a vaguely "Western" outlook is the benchmark and the so-called indigenous group is the exotic other. This links forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

**Spender on man-made language (slides 10-14, 18).** Spender argues that the structure of English encodes a "**male-as-norm**" classification scheme: humanity is divided into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145-148). Historical evidence: Mr Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147); John Kirkby's 1746 "Rule Number Twenty One" stated that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). Her programme: "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**De Beauvoir (slide 15, 28).** "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The man writes from no particular standpoint; the woman is always a *woman writer*. Crucially, de Beauvoir does **not** fully separate sex from gender. Biology and lived societal experience are intertwined.

**Actors / actresses / political correctness (slides 16-17).** David Marsh (*Guardian*) bans "actress": only "actor". This *seems* progressive (women are no longer compared only to other women) but the lecturer warns that occupations were historically male-coded, so calling all women "actors" risks absorbing them into a masculine yardstick rather than dismantling it.

**Grammarians and gender (slide 29).** H.W. Fowler (*A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, 1926: 211) insists that grammatical gender "is a grammatical term only", derived from Latin *genus* (type, genre). To treat masculine/feminine as biologically sexed is, for him, "a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211).

**Ngũgĩ and Achebe (slides 20-25).** Ngũgĩ frames language as a weapon of colonisation: education in the colonial language, suppression of indigenous tongues, "The English language was the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). No language of African origin is an official UN language. Achebe responds differently: African writers should keep writing in English/French/Portuguese to reach larger audiences, but should fashion an English "that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). When asked whether an African can ever use English like a native speaker, Achebe says: "I hope not" (Achebe, 1997: 347).

**And what of other languages? (slide 26).** Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish: internal colonial casualties of English inside the British Isles.

## 3. Information from the readings (slide-by-slide presentation walk-through)

### 3.1 Slide-by-slide presentation summary

- **Slide 1 (Title).** "Language and the World (Words and Things)". The bracketed subtitle frames the whole lecture: the question is the relation between *words* (language) and *things* (the world).

- **Slide 2 (3 parts to the lecture).** Three parts: (1) language and thought (and culture): linguistic relativity, linguistic determinism; (2) language and gender: Spender, de Beauvoir; (3) language and the legacy of colonialism: Ngũgĩ, Achebe.

- **Slide 3 (The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).** Linguistic relativity defined as the "belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230). Members of a society who share a language share "both a language and a specific way of viewing and understanding 'reality'". The pivot quote: "The speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230).

- **Slide 4 (How Many Words for Snow?).** Sets up the textbook example: "Eskimos" have "over 50 words for 'snow'". The lecturer scare-quotes "Eskimos" because the term is "neither accurate nor appropriate" as a name for a group. The slide previews the load-bearing slogan of the whole lecture: "**Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world**". References Samson (2003) *A Way of Life That Does Not Exist: Canada and the Extinguishment of the Innu*.
  > 💡 slide-question: *How Many Words for Snow?*

- **Slide 5 (A different world?...).** Spells out the Sapir-Whorf reading of the snow example: a wide range of words for snow is needed "to be able to live"; "Eskimos" therefore see the world differently, and this is "reflected in their language". There is a link "between language, thinking and understanding the world". The greater number of words "points to the fact that they live in a very different world from 'us'". Introduces the names Edward Sapir (1884-1939) and Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897-1941) as the "pioneers" the hypothesis is named after.
  > 💡 slide-question: *A different world?...*

- **Slide 6 (A second aspect of Sapir-Whorf hypothesis).** Introduces **linguistic determinism** as a stronger claim. Not just that language *links* to a way of seeing, but: "How we think of the world is *determined* by the language that we use". The famous Brown image: "The languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231).

- **Slide 7 (This is a powerful argument. But it has some problems).** First critique. Any human language "expresses human thoughts about the world"; each language is a "specific rendering of a specific 'reality'" rather than an "accurate or objective picture". "We" (humans with language) "seem to have lost any innocent contact with the world, as our language always 'gets in the way'".

- **Slide 8 (More Problems).** Second critique, more political. The phrase "they live in a very different world from 'us'" assumes an *us* and a *them*. Who is the "them"? "The 'others'". The risk is "assigning completely different 'realities' or worldviews to speakers of so-called indigenous groups" (Amazon rainforest is the example). This makes the "Western outlook" the implicit benchmark, and "plays down the differences between individual European languages". Connects forward to the lecture on Modernity as a Colonial Project.

- **Slide 9 (Another (interesting) point (if we have time)…).** Third critique. Sapir-Whorf assumes single words are the main way of indicating things. If "Eskimo" has three words for snow that do not map onto three English words, the conclusion is supposed to be that "Eskimo" speakers "must think of snow and the world differently". This forgets that English speakers (especially snowball-makers) use *phrases*, not single words: "good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing". English may have fewer single-word labels but has "as many possible phrases for snow as they do".

- **Slide 10 (Dale Spender on "Man-Made Language").** Two quotations from Spender, 1980: 145. First: "Undoubtedly our own meanings are partially hidden from us and it is difficult to have access to them. We may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, 1980: 145). Second: "although it is not always easy to get outside this language trap, to get outside the limitations of one's own language, it is not impossible. There are clues, if one is prepared to look for them" (Spender, 1980: 145).

- **Slide 11 (Something to read (later)).** Linked Guardian article on "Sexism and dictionaries" (theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2019/jul/03/hussy-baggage-bit-filly-dictionary-definitions-woman). Side-reading, not core content.

- **Slide 12 (Dale Spender, *Man Made Language*).** The central thesis. The key idea operating in the language is the **male-as-norm**. "One standard or normal human being is a male one". Those who are not part of this standard are "in some way 'deviant'". Quote: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts (if two is to be the significant number) but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980). "The status of the female is derived from the status of the male and on this has been erected many strata of positive and negative classifications".

- **Slide 13 ("Men Before Women": Where do such ideas come from?).** Spender's historical archive. "The first record we appear to have is that of a Mr Wilson in 1553 who insisted that it was more natural to place the man before the woman, as for example in male and female, husband and wife, brother and sister, son and daughter. Implicit in his insistence that males take precedence is the belief that males 'come first' in the natural order, and this is one of the first examples of a male arguing for not just the superiority of males but that this superiority should be reflected in the structure of the language" (Spender, 1980: 147).

- **Slide 14 (More Evidence).** The second historical fragment. "In 1746 John Kirkby formulated his 'Eighty Eight Grammatical Rules'. These rules, the product of Mr Kirkby's own imagination, contained one that indicated the esteem in which he held females: Rule Number Twenty One stated that the male gender was more comprehensive than the female. This represents a significant departure from the simple proposition that males are more important" (Spender, 1980: 148).

- **Slide 15 (Men take themselves and language for granted).** The pivot to de Beauvoir. Men do not have to explain that they are writing "as a man"; it is "seen as normal, as 'natural' even". The famous quote: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Women are "immediately seen as 'women writers'"; their writing is not granted the assumed objectivity that male writing claims.

- **Slide 16 (Actors and Actresses).** The David Marsh case. Marsh (*Guardian* production editor) instructs journalists to use only "actor", never "actress". Reasons: "actress" is limiting (it compares a person only to other female actors), while "actor" can refer to the best at acting in the company overall. The masculine term "can include the female, but not the other way around". "The female has been subsumed into the male (again)". "Actor" also stands in for the neuter when the speaker's gender is unknown. The reform argument: equal title, equal pay.

- **Slide 17 (Marsh's argument seems good, but…).** The lecturer's caution. Most occupations (doctor, teacher, actor) were historically carried out by men; women were "rare, even peculiar". Apparently neutral words like "actor" "provide a yardstick which may not immediately appear to refer to the male, or to be masculine". Risk: "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term". We can "pay lip-service" to the problem while ignoring "the more fundamental problem". This is the "political correctness" debate, with "no easy answer".

- **Slide 18 (What is to be done?).** Spender's prescription. "For women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible… New symbols will need to be created and old symbols will need to be recycled and invested with new images if the male hold of language is to be broken. As the language structure which has been devised and legitimated by male grammarians exacts ambiguity, uncertainty, and anomie for females, then in the interests of dismantling the muted nature of females, that language structure and those rules need to be defied" (Spender, 1980: 162).

- **Slide 28 (Rethinking "Sex" and Gender (Simone de Beauvoir)).** The deeper read of de Beauvoir. Many sociologists ascribe the sex/gender distinction to *The Second Sex* (1949), but the division "is not as clear-cut in de Beauvoir's work as is sometimes thought". De Beauvoir's stated aim is to ask "what is a woman?" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 13). She insists: "When I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). She "does not reduce the question of what a woman 'is' to the biological ('sex')"; likewise, "the lived societal experience of women ('gender') is not purely 'social'". The two are intertwined.

- **Slide 29 (Gender in language (according to grammarians)).** The Fowler counter-position. In *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, H.W. Fowler states gender "is a grammatical term only. To ask whether genders are "biologically 'sexed'" is, for Fowler, "a mistake, even a joke". The Halewood chapter (next section) treats this slide as a straw man to be dismantled.

### 3.2 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Moving the Centre*, Chapter 4: 'Imperialism of Language. English, a Language for the World?'

Ngũgĩ opens with a two-aspect view of language: it is both **a means of communication** and **a carrier of culture and history**, what he calls in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). These two aspects are inseparable but unbalanced according to the political conditions of encounter.

When languages meet "on terms of equality and independence" (e.g. Scandinavians, Japanese, West Germans learning English), the communicative aspect dominates and English does not substitute for the national language (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30-31). When they meet "as oppressor and oppressed, as for instance under imperialism", language becomes a **weapon** alongside "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Ngũgĩ quotes David Livingstone's nineteenth-century slogan "Christianity plus 5 percent" and updates it to "Christianity, debt, plus 40 percent in debt servicing" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31).

The capture of minds happens in two complementary moves (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32):

1. **Suppression of indigenous languages.** They were "experienced as incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Ngũgĩ recalls a Kenyan school hymn, "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was the children's own language (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32).
2. **Elevation of the conqueror's language.** "Those inducted into the school system… were furnished with new mirrors" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The French called this *assimilation*; the English called it *education*. Lugard's blunter name was **indirect rule**: mastery of English was "the measure of one's readiness for election into the band of the elect" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32).

Ngũgĩ describes children punished for speaking African languages, "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid' or 'I am an ass'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), with pieces of paper from the wastepaper basket stuffed in their mouths. "Humiliation in relation to our languages was the key" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33).

The reading then extends the argument beyond Africa. Citing Lyn Marierid in the Welsh Language Society (*Morning Star*, 21 Oct 1988), Ngũgĩ shows that Welsh is being eroded by capitalist "yuppiefication": "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Equally, the Russians of Novosibirsk learn English because, in Kenneth Baker's words quoted from the *Evening Standard*, "they want to get away from the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35). English carries capitalist modernity.

The consequences in postcolonial Africa: English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" of administration, justice and education (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Yet only a minority speak them, so the working majority "are excluded from centre stage" and from modern scientific knowledge, which is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). The elite is "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). No African-origin language sits among the official languages of the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38).

Ngũgĩ is not against a common world language. He insists that the languages of the world are "our common heritage. A world of many languages should be like a field of flowers of different colours" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). He demands "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" as the precondition (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). He concludes by proposing Kiswahili, "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41), as a candidate world language.

### 3.3 *Gender and Personal Pronouns: She, He, It and They* (Halewood, 2021)

The chapter opens by setting out the sex/gender distinction in sociology, then immediately complicates it. Mead's anthropology shows that gender roles vary cross-culturally, hence they are social. But Halewood, following de Beauvoir and Irigaray, argues that the easy split (biology over here, society over there) itself "bifurcates" language and world.

Fowler's claim that gender "is a grammatical term only… of the male or female sex is either a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211) is presented as a starting point and then dismantled. If categories really were arbitrary, why not "up, down, middle" instead of "masculine, feminine, neuter"? The specific words carry meaning.

**De Beauvoir's pivot.** "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex… man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15).

**Irigaray's grammatical examples.** In French *il* covers "he", impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*, *il pluie*), and any unknown subject. A male dog *le chien* is default; *la chienne* requires extra knowledge. The third-person plural *ils* is used for any mixed group, however small the male presence. The Czech example from Tichý (2018) ("all the women of the world went for a walk" uses the feminine plural *šly*, but as soon as John joins, the verb flips to masculine *šli*) shows the asymmetry at the level of verbs too. Irigaray: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20).

**Gendered English.** Halewood quotes Dewey writing "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his" and Whitehead's "men of science" to show that even Anglophone academic writing carried male defaults. Fowler had wanted *doctress*, *teacheress*, *singeress*, *danceress*: multiplying female forms. Marsh (production editor, *Guardian*) goes the other way: abolish *actress*, use only *actor*.

**Job titles and chairs.** "Chair" derives from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat in a cathedral, and the historical "see" (*sedes*). Professors hold "chairs"; the office carries an authority that Halewood reads as historically male/masculine, tied to the Church. So even apparently neutral words like "chair" are not neutral.

**Pronouns, trans rights, prescriptivism.** Singular "they" is grammatically contested but has a long literary history (Lewis Carroll, Shakespeare, the King James Bible, Thackeray, all cited via Marsh, 2014). Trans writers including Stephen Whittle (2006) argue that "he/she" is exclusionary; some prefer "they" or neologisms (e.g. "per"). The chapter is sympathetic but warns that "tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Haraway's caution.** "All dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130).

The conclusion sides with a *processual* reading: man, woman, trans are *outcomes* of intertwined material and linguistic processes, not pre-given essences.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The thread that holds these readings together is the slogan from the lecture: **"Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world"** (Lecture 4 slide 4). Each thinker tugs on a different end of that rope.

Sapir-Whorf gives us the *epistemic* lever. If "the speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230), then a language is never an innocent code; it is a settlement about what counts. The weak form (language influences thought) is now broadly accepted in cognitive linguistics; the strong form (linguistic determinism), "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231), is harder to defend, because speakers can stretch their language with phrases, neologisms and translation. The deeper problem the lecturer flags is not whether the strong claim is true, but that the very *example* (Eskimo words for snow) is constructed from a Western "us" looking at an indigenous "them". The classification is already evaluative; the example reproduces the asymmetry it claims to describe.

Spender shows the same asymmetry inside English itself. Her core claim is structural: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). Historical fragments back this up: Mr Wilson (1553) prescribing the order "male and female" (Spender, 1980: 147), John Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The generic *he*, the word *mankind*, *chairman*, *manpower*, the older NHS form that asked for a "Mrs / Miss" but only "Mr" for a man: these are the sediment of that prescriptive history.

De Beauvoir radicalises Spender. Her insight is phenomenological: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The male is the unmarked term in language *because* he is the unmarked term in social ontology. He occupies "both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). This is why the lecturer treats the Marsh debate about whether to use *actor* or *actress* as irresolvable in purely linguistic terms: tidying the words leaves the social default (the masculine yardstick) untouched (Lecture 4 slide 17).

Irigaray takes this further. "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). The Czech example (*šly* flipping to *šli* as soon as one man joins all the women of the world) is grammatically tiny but ontologically loud: it tells us who counts as the default human.

Ngũgĩ pulls the analysis out to the imperial scale. Language is a "carrier of the history and the culture built into the process of communication over time" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30). When the encounter is "oppressor and oppressed", language is added to "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Suppression plus elevation: children punished with plaques reading "I am stupid" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), then drilled in "Oxford Readers for Africa" about John and Joan visiting the Thames (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34), produced what Ngũgĩ calls "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). His sharpest line is that "the death of many languages should never be the condition for the life of a few" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39). The point sits squarely on the Sapir-Whorf hinge: kill the language, kill the world that language carries.

Achebe complicates the colonial story. Refusing to abandon English does not equal submission. The African writer should "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Can the African write English like a native? "I hope not" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). This is the position I find most pragmatically defensible: not Ngũgĩ's purism, not the colonial monolith, but a politics of *re-fashioning* the inherited tool.

Across all five thinkers the same lesson appears: language **classifies and evaluates**, and to classify is to distribute power. Sociologists who want to *address* the inequalities Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ and Achebe describe cannot just police vocabulary. Halewood is right that "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). But they cannot ignore vocabulary either, because, as the lecture insists, problems with inequality in language *are* problems in the world.

### Essay 1 / 2025 (Q4): *"Language, power and modernity are all interrelated." Critically analyse this statement.*

I agree with the statement, but only when "modernity" is read in the specific sense the SC301 module has been building: modernity as a *colonial* project that travelled with particular European languages and that re-described the rest of the world in its own vocabulary. Read that way, language is not an add-on to modernity but one of its load-bearing technologies.

**Power needs a vocabulary.** The lecture's basic move is that language is "not neutral"; it classifies and evaluates and is therefore "a mode of power" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Spender catches this at the level of grammar: "Our fundamental classification scheme is one which divides humanity not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir catches it at the level of social ontology: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The point is the same: power needs a default subject, and the default subject is installed in the words before any explicit argument is made.

**Modernity travels in particular languages.** Ngũgĩ's reading is decisive here. The languages of European modernity (English, French, Portuguese) did not arrive in the Third World "under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). They came alongside "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The mission school, the colonial bureaucracy, the textbook industry ("Oxford Readers for Africa", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34) and the Christian hymn ("Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) bundled together a single message: modernity is European, and European is English. Children caught speaking their own languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Said's Orientalism gives the discursive complement: European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9), and the vocabulary in which it managed was European. Kenneth Baker's report that Russians learn English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) projects the same coupling past formal empire.

**Power and modernity are inscribed in everyday English.** Halewood shows the same logic inside the English-speaking core. "Chair", from Latin *cathedra*, encodes the authority of bishops, professors, then committee heads (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Replacing "chairman" with "chair" feels modern but preserves masculine-clerical authority. The same logic appears in AI chatbots like ChatGPT, which default to Anglophone conceptual categories, and in social media moderation across languages.

**Two complications.** First, modernity has produced *democratic* vocabularies too. Ngũgĩ concedes English has "a democratic tradition" (Shakespeare, Dickens) and is "part of the common heritage of humankind" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Second, Achebe argues the colonised can refashion the coloniser's language: it must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). His *Things Fall Apart* takes its title from W.B. Yeats, an Irish poet. Welsh, Gaelic and Maori revivalism extends the point.

**Examples that pull the three concepts together.**

- *Postcolonial language of instruction.* English, French and Portuguese remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37).
- *No African-origin language at the UN.* "Among the official languages at the United Nations there is not a single language of African origin" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38).
- *English as escape from "backwardness".* Kenneth Baker reports Russians learning English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35), the clearest case of English coupled to "modern" capitalism.
- *Pronouns, occupational titles and the bishop's chair.* From the generic *he* and Marsh's actor/actress reform (Halewood, 2021: 120) to "chair" (Halewood, 2021: 128-130), modernity's professional vocabulary carries earlier authority into ostensibly neutral usage.

**Conclusion.** Language, power and modernity are interrelated because modernity is the historical settlement in which certain European languages became the default vocabulary of progress, science and citizenship. The strong version risks essentialising European languages as inherently oppressive (Achebe rightly resists that). The weak version, that "the languages of Europe… were taught as if they were our own languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35), is the version I find most useful, and it vindicates the exam quotation.

### Essay 2 / 2024 (Q3): *Discuss the links between language and inequality. How can sociologists help to address the problem?*

I want to argue two things. First, language and inequality are linked in three overlapping ways (*classification*, *evaluation*, and *erasure*) and the lecture's slogan that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4) captures this neatly. Second, sociologists can help, but only if they treat language as embedded in the world rather than as a separable surface that can be cleaned up by vocabulary reform alone.

**Language classifies.** This is the core insight of Sapir-Whorf as the lecture presents it: speakers share an "agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230). Every language carves the world into categories (*actor*/*actress*, *chien*/*chienne*) and those categories are unequal in consequence. Spender shows that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The generic *he*, *mankind*, the ladder of titles *Mr/Mrs/Miss*, encode an asymmetry: the man is the default human, the woman a variation. Halewood, drawing on Irigaray, extends the point to French, where the default noun for a doctor (*le médecin*) takes the masculine form, while *cafetière* (the feminine of café-owner) is already occupied by a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119). Contemporary debates over trans pronouns extend the same point: the binary marked/unmarked grid is contested by speakers it cannot accommodate.

**Language evaluates.** Classification smuggles in hierarchy. Ngũgĩ argues that English entered Africa as the language of "the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31), at once a label and a verdict. "Eskimo", the lecture's recurring example, expresses a relation of power between namer and named (Lecture 4 slide 4; Samson, 2003). Spender's archive is overtly evaluative: Kirkby's "Rule Number Twenty One" pronounced that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148), embedding a moral hierarchy in a grammatical rule. Debates over "master/slave" terminology in computing show the same logic operating in technical vocabulary.

**Language erases.** Some realities are made unspeakable. De Beauvoir's complaint that "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) catches the universalising erasure of male particularity. Hill Collins extends this to compound erasures: Black feminist thought articulates "a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70) precisely because the matrix of domination renders some subjects doubly unspeakable in the master-vocabulary. Ngũgĩ describes a more brutal erasure: African children in colonial schools wore "plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) for speaking their first languages; their school hymn sang of being led "kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was their own tongue (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). Lyn Marierid describes Welsh being eroded by "yuppiefication" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36): the same erasure inside the British Isles.

**So what can sociologists do?** *First*, describe the patterns with care. Spender's archival work on grammarians from Wilson (1553) to Kirkby (1746) shows "man-made" English is a documentable history of prescription (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Halewood does a similar genealogy for "chair" from *cathedra* through the Lucasian Chair to the committee chair (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). *Second*, connect language work to material consequences. Halewood: "Tinkering with language… is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The Marsh debate (Halewood, 2021: 120) is the cleanest case: insisting on "actor" sounds equalising, but if the masculine yardstick is preserved beneath, the reform is symbolic.

*Third*, amplify counter-voices. Achebe's Africanising English ("fashioning an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience", Achebe, 1997: 347) is a model. Ngũgĩ proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). *Fourth*, sociologists should be self-critical about jargon. Halewood ends with Haraway's warning that "all dreams for a perfectly true language [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130). Even moving from "coloureds" to "ethnic communities" is tokenistic if "the way they think and act in the world" has not changed (Halewood, 2021: 131).

Language and inequality are linked through classification, evaluation and erasure. Sociologists help when they document histories, connect words to material distributions, support refashioning, and police their own vocabulary. They fail when language reform substitutes for "thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

### Essay 3 / 2023 (Q4): *Identify and explain how language is tied up with wider questions of power.*

I want to identify four ways language is tied up with power: it (1) classifies who counts; (2) evaluates as it classifies; (3) enforces those evaluations through institutions; and (4) carries the historical sediment of past power even after explicit reform. I will work through each, drawing on Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ, Achebe and the Halewood chapter.

**(1) Classification: who counts as the default.** Spender's claim that English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) is the cleanest statement. The generic *he*, *mankind*, *actor*/*actress*: these are classification rules that install a default subject. De Beauvoir generalises: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Saussure's structural insight that meaning "arises from difference within a system" (Strinati, 1995: 83) reframes Spender: male/female is a paradigmatic opposition whose hierarchy is built into the *langue* itself. In Czech, Tichý's example shows the verb flipping from *šly* to *šli* as soon as one man joins all the women of the world (Halewood, 2021: 116). Trans-pronoun debates show the binary classification challenged from inside.

**(2) Evaluation: classification is never neutral.** Ngũgĩ shows how the colonial school treated African languages as "incomprehensible noise from the dark Tower of Babel" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). English was light, African languages were "gloom" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The lecturer's caution about "Eskimo" makes the same point: the label is "neither accurate nor appropriate" and positions the named group as the curious other against an implicit Western "us" (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8; Samson, 2003). Contemporary debates over "master/slave" terminology in computing repeat the same evaluative move.

**(3) Enforcement: institutions police language.** Language is power because someone enforces the vocabulary. Spender's rules were laid down by named grammarians: Wilson in 1553 insisting on "male and female, husband and wife", Kirkby in 1746 ruling "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Ngũgĩ describes the colonial school: children were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" for speaking their own languages (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Lugard's "indirect rule" produced "more faithful 'chiefs'" through education than political appointment (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). David Marsh's editorial fiat at *The Guardian* (no "actress", only "actor") is a prescriptive moment of the same kind (Halewood, 2021: 120).

**(4) Sedimentation: old power lingers in vocabulary.** Halewood's "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat) shows that apparently neutral terms carry sedimented authority (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). The professor's chair inherits the bishop's *cathedra*; the Lucasian Chair (held by Newton and Hawking) sits inside Christian Church traditions "until recently" male (Halewood, 2021: 129). The committee chair inherits the professor's authority. Renaming "chairman" as "chair" may help, but the history continues to operate. The legal "reasonable man" of common-law jurisprudence shows the same sedimentation.

**Counter-currents.** Power is not absolute. Achebe shows the dominated can refashion the dominant tongue: the African writer must use English to "carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347), and should *not* learn to use it like a native ("I hope not", Achebe, 1997: 347). The lecture's slide on "other languages" (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish; slide 26) names the same refusal inside the British Isles. Singular *they* has a long literary pedigree from Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray (cited via Marsh, Halewood, 2021: 128); reviving it for trans speakers who find "he/she" "limiting and exclusionary" (Whittle, 2006, cited Halewood, 2021: 128) is the structural intervention Spender called for. Language is contested terrain, not a one-way street.

**The limit case: tinkering versus changing.** Marsh's ban on "actress" makes Hattie Jacques comparable to Kenneth Williams as well as Joan Sims (Halewood, 2021: 120). But "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood draws the broader conclusion: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Conclusion.** Language ties up with power in four ways (classification, evaluation, enforcement, sedimentation), from intimate pronouns to the absence of African-origin languages at the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Achebe's refashioning is as important as Ngũgĩ's critique. The sociologist's job is to make the link between vocabulary and distribution visible without pretending fixing vocabulary fixes distribution.

### Essay 4 / 2022 (Q4): *Can language create inequality?*

My answer: language does not create inequality from scratch, but it sustains, amplifies and naturalises inequalities with material roots, and in that sense does much of the *work* of inequality. I defend the claim across three registers: gender, colonial domination, and the metropolitan core.

**The misleading version of the question.** Taken literally, "language creates inequality" reads as if changing words alone would equalise the world. The lecture warns against this: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126; Lecture 4 slide 17). Material inequalities (wages, land, citizenship) have material causes. But that does not mean language is innocent.

**Language sustains gender inequality.** Spender's claim that the structure of English is built on "the male-as-norm" (Spender, 1980: 145) is a sustaining-and-amplifying claim, not creating-from-nothing. Patriarchy did not begin with English grammar, but English grammar makes male centrality feel natural, so that "we may use the English language our whole lives without ever noticing the distortions and omissions" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir's "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) makes the same point at the level of the writing subject. The grammatical male/female split is one of the bifurcations Halewood identifies as modernity's signature move (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7): a split "into two branches" that "creates a gulf" between an unmarked positive and a marked negative, and the gulf then operates as if natural. Halewood's "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*) shows the same logic in Anglophone authority: the office is opened to women, but the masculine-ecclesiastical history operates beneath the renaming (Halewood, 2021: 128-130).

**Language sustains colonial inequality.** Ngũgĩ is uncompromising. "The encounter between English and most so-called Third World languages did not occur under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). English, French and Portuguese came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Colonial schools "elevated the language of the conqueror" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) by punishing children for using their own; the morning hymn was "Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). Decades after independence, English remains the language of administration, justice and higher education, leaving the elite "in linguistic terms, completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Did English *create* the inequality between Britain and Kenya? No, that is rooted in extraction and conquest. But English language policy keeps it in place after the soldiers leave. Language *creates the continuity* of a colonial inequality whose original cause has lapsed. The same logic appears in generative AI: ChatGPT and translation apps default to Anglophone conceptual categories, extending the colonial dominance of English into machine learning.

**Language sustains inequality inside the metropolitan core.** Ngũgĩ cites Lyn Marierid of the Welsh Language Society: rural areas "completely Anglicised as ordinary working-class people have been systematically priced out of their native areas" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Languages "are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The lecture's bare list of Welsh, Gaelic, and Cornish (slide 26) marks the pattern. Maori revivalism is the counter-instance: a deliberate policy of language reclamation. The loss of a language is the loss of "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), itself a substantive inequality.

**Counter-evidence.** Languages get refashioned by their speakers. Achebe insists the African writer must "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). His "I hope not" to native-speaker assimilation (Achebe, 1997: 347) refuses through style. Singular *they* has a long literary pedigree (Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible, Thackeray, cited via Marsh in Halewood, 2021: 128); its revival is rediscovery of usage prescribed out by Spender's grammarians. Language does not lock inequality in place; it is contested terrain.

**A subtler creation effect.** Halewood's "chair" defends a *stronger* version: when a category carries an institution forward (bishop's *cathedra* to professor's chair to committee chair, Halewood, 2021: 128-130), the linguistic form sustains an authority the original setting no longer formally enforces. Sapir-Whorf in its weak form is genuinely useful here.

**Conclusion.** Language creates inequality in three senses: it installs a default subject (male) so the asymmetry becomes invisible; it sustains colonial-era inequalities; and it carries sedimented institutional authority into neutral contemporary usage. But it cannot create inequality unaided. With Halewood, "language and the world become together" (Halewood, 2021: 132).

### Essay 5 / 2021 (Q4): *"Inequalities in language represent inequalities in the world." Critically explain this statement.*

The statement comes almost verbatim from the lecture: "Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). It is largely correct, but *represent* is doing a lot of work. Inequalities in language do three things at once: they *reflect* prior inequalities; they *reproduce* those inequalities by carrying them across time; and in some cases they *constitute* the inequalities they appear merely to describe. I defend this three-layer reading.

**Reflection.** The simplest reading is that language mirrors social hierarchies that exist independently. Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, 1980: 147) and Kirkby (1746) ruling the male gender "more comprehensive" (Spender, 1980: 148) did not invent patriarchy; they *reflected* it. Similarly, the prestige of English in former colonies reflects the geopolitical dominance of Britain and the US. Ngũgĩ's observation that English, French and Portuguese are "the languages of power" in administration, justice and commerce in postcolonial Africa (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) shows linguistic prestige reflecting political-economic power.

**Reproduction.** Reflection alone is too weak. Language *carries* inequalities forward when their original cause has lapsed. This is the bite of Ngũgĩ's argument. By 1993, formal colonialism in Kenya had ended, but the colonial language continued to gatekeep education, science and law. Knowledge is "locked up in the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). De Beauvoir makes the same point for gender: "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) persists long after legal reform. Halewood's "chair" is the same logic: even as universities formally open chairs to women, the word carries forward the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's *cathedra* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130).

**Constitution.** The strongest version: some inequalities are partly *constituted* through language. The weak Sapir-Whorf insight is that available categories shape what people can do with the world. Spender's "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) scheme is itself part of how patriarchy is experienced. Irigaray pushes further: "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). Fanon's railway-carriage scene shows the same constitution at the colonial scale: the white child's hail "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323) does not merely describe the Black subject; it fixes him, "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). The naming makes the position. Tichý's Czech *šly* flipping to *šli* the moment one man joins the women of the world (Halewood, 2021: 116) is the grammatical miniature of the same move.

**Critical limits.** Three caveats.

1. *Language is not the whole story.* Halewood: "Tinkering with language to avoid or remove words or phrases that are considered inappropriate or offensive is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being; this will involve changing the world" (Halewood, 2021: 126).
2. *Counter-currents exist.* Achebe shows subordinated speakers refashion the dominant language: the world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Singular *they* (long-attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, Halewood, 2021: 128) shows the same: prescribed-out usages can be revived. Welsh, Gaelic and Maori revivalism extend the point.
3. *Representation is not always faithful.* The "Eskimo words for snow" myth, flagged with "us/them" framing (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8; Samson, 2003), represents indigenous people as exotic *others* in a way the social facts do not support. Language can misrepresent as well as reflect.

**Concrete examples.**

- *The actor/actress reform* (Halewood, 2021: 120): where reflection, reproduction and constitution overlap, and "tinkering" is not enough (Halewood, 2021: 126).
- *Job titles in French*: *le médecin* default-masculine, *cafetière* (the female café-owner) already occupied by the coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119).
- *Colonial school plaques* ("I am stupid", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) and the Welsh decline tracked by Marierid (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36): linguistic inequality enforced *and* reversible.
- *The bishop's chair* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130): authority inequality reproduced silently in committee language.

**Conclusion.** The statement is correct in its strong reading: inequalities in language represent inequalities in the world, where *represent* means simultaneously to reflect, to reproduce and partly to constitute. The danger lies in collapsing the three readings. Sociology's job is to keep them apart while showing how they hang together, and to refuse both the complacent answer that language is "just words" and the over-simple answer that fixing the words fixes the world.

### Essay 6 / Mock Q1: *"Decolonising the mind requires decolonising the language" (Ngũgĩ). Discuss.*

I take Ngũgĩ's slogan seriously and defend it, with a twist. Decolonising the language names two projects: a *displacement* project (swap the coloniser's tongue for an African one, as Ngũgĩ urges) and a *refashioning* project (rewire the coloniser's tongue from inside, as Achebe urges). My claim is that the slogan is correct, and that both projects are needed.

**Why language carries the colonial mind.** Ngũgĩ argues in *Moving the Centre* (1993) and *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) that European languages arrived in Africa "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The mission school did the slow work: children caught speaking African languages were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Said's account of Orientalism gives the broader frame: European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9), and the medium of that production was European vocabulary. The colonial attitude (that the European tongue is the language of modernity, knowledge and progress) was installed at the level of the child's nervous system. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), and that memory bank was systematically replaced.

**What survives independence.** Political flags changed, but English, French and Portuguese remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). The elite is "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Decolonising the mind is impossible while the everyday vocabulary of the state remains the coloniser's, because the categories of governance, science, and legal personhood remain those installed by mission school.

**Three concrete sites where the colonial mind hides.** First, *the curriculum*. Ngũgĩ learned English from "Oxford Readers for Africa", reading about Oxford and the Thames before knowing other Kenyan towns (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34). Knowledge "in the arts and sciences" became something that lived in English (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Second, *the official languages of power* (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37): litigants and students who do not command them are gatekept out of justice and science. Third, *the international institutional grammar*. "Among the official languages at the United Nations there is not a single language of African origin" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Kenneth Baker's report that Russians learn English to escape "the old-fashioned totalitarian state-controlled society" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) shows the same logic projected past formal empire. Contemporary translation apps and AI chatbots like ChatGPT extend the pattern: the "default" world is in English.

**Ngũgĩ's displacement strategy.** Ngũgĩ proposes Kiswahili as a candidate world language because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). The lecture's bare list of Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish (slide 26) enacts the same principle inside the British Isles: where the predominant class "has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36), languages die, and with them the worlds they carry. Maori and Gaelic revivalism are contemporary counter-examples.

**Achebe's refashioning strategy.** Achebe disagrees on tactics, agrees on diagnosis. The African writer must use "an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). The world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). His "I hope not" to native-speaker English (Achebe, 1997: 347) refuses assimilation in style rather than choice of tongue.

**Why both are needed.** Pure displacement risks leaving the colonial mind intact in everyone outside the indigenous-language community. Pure refashioning risks naturalising the prestige of English. Decolonising the mind requires (i) institutional space for indigenous languages in schooling, courts, and international forums; *and* (ii) writers refashioning English/French/Portuguese for non-Anglophone experience. Ngũgĩ's closing position: "the lives of many languages should add life to whichever language emerges as the transnational or universal language" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39), provided there is "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39).

**Conclusion.** The slogan is correct and stronger than it looks. Decolonising the mind requires decolonising the language, because the colonial attitude lives in the everyday categories of knowledge and governance, not just flags. Displacement and refashioning are complementary tools for the same job: prising open the categories so that a non-colonial mind becomes thinkable in the same world the colonial mind built.

### Essay 7 / Mock Q2: *Critically assess Spender's argument in Man Made Language with reference to Halewood's chapter on gender and personal pronouns.*

Dale Spender's *Man Made Language* (1980) is the most ambitious structural account of English-as-patriarchal in the SC301 reading list. Halewood's chapter develops, extends and qualifies the argument: it transposes the diagnosis to French and Czech via de Beauvoir and Irigaray, traces the logic through "chair", and warns against language reform as substitute for "thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Halewood's chapter both vindicates Spender and refines her.

**Spender's argument.** English divides humanity "not into two equal parts… but into those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The male is the default human; the female is marked by morphology (*actress*, *waitress*) or honorifics (*Mrs/Miss* mark marital status; *Mr* does not). The archive is named: Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" (Spender, 1980: 147), Kirkby (1746) ruling that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The "man-made" label tracks a paper trail of male grammarians prescribing male precedence. Her programme: "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**Where Halewood vindicates Spender.** *First*, the structural pattern operates across grammars, not as anglocentric quirk. In French, *il* covers male persons and the impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*); *ils* serves any mixed plural (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). Tichý's Czech example: "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses *šly*, but John tagging along flips it to *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116). *Second*, Halewood reinforces the archive with Fowler's *Dictionary of Modern English Usage*, which insisted gender is "a grammatical term only" (Fowler, 1926: 211) while proposing "doctress, teacheress, singeress and danceress" (cited Halewood, 2021: 120). Fowler is exactly a Wilson-Kirkby type. *Third*, the Marsh case (Halewood, 2021: 120) shows Spender at work in contemporary editorial practice: banning *actress* preserves the masculine yardstick (since most occupations were historically male) beneath an apparently neutral term.

**Where Halewood refines Spender.** *First*, with Irigaray, "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Spender's "linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162) is a precondition, not a substitute, for "a change in thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126). *Second*, Halewood extends to authority vocabularies. "Chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat) carries male-ecclesiastical authority into the office of professor and committee head (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Spender catches the prescriptive moment of grammar; Halewood catches the etymological sediment. *Third*, Hartsock's standpoint theory deepens Spender's diagnosis: "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), so the "minus male" position is not just a linguistic deficit but an epistemic vantage. Naming the asymmetry is a precondition for the standpoint from which it can be analysed. Whittle (2006) reports that pronouns are "limiting and exclusionary" for trans speakers (cited Halewood, 2021: 128); Spender's binary needs widening.

**A grammarian's counter-position.** Fowler insists grammatical gender is "a grammatical term only", derived from Latin *genus*; talk of biologically sexed gender is "either a jocularity… or a blunder" (Fowler, 1926: 211). Halewood dismantles this: if the categories were arbitrary, why not "up, down, middle"? And if they were, would they not still carry hierarchy (Halewood, 2021: 112)? The Fowler defence is the "purely descriptive" approach Spender's archive already refutes.

**The Irigarayan extension.** "Sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). In French, the default noun for a doctor (*le médecin*) is masculine while medicine (*la médicine*) is feminine; the female café-owner (*une cafetière*) is already a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119). Spender's grammatical archive is reinforced by Irigaray's lexical one: the patriarchal structure operates in pronouns and in the nouns for jobs and objects.

**Conclusion.** Halewood vindicates: the male-as-norm pattern operates across French, Czech and English, across pronouns and titles, across Wilson-Kirkby and Fowler. Refines: Spender's binary needs widening to recognise trans subjects (Whittle, 2006); her grammatical focus needs supplementing by Halewood's etymological one (the bishop's chair); her programme of linguistic visibility needs joining to "a change in thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The diagnosis survives the refinement; the binary scaffolding alone does not.

### Essay 8 / Mock Q3: *What does de Beauvoir mean by "woman is the Other", and why does she treat that othering as linguistically structured?*

The question has two parts. First, what does de Beauvoir's woman-as-other claim? Second, why is the othering inseparable from language? My answer: woman exists as the *marked* term against an unmarked male default, and de Beauvoir treats this as linguistically structured because the asymmetry is built into grammar and lexicon before any speaker chooses a word.

**Part one: what "woman is the Other" means.** De Beauvoir's argument in the Introduction to *The Second Sex* (1949) is condensed in two lines. "A man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). And: "man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). The man is the *unmarked* term (the default human); the woman is the *marked* term (the deviation that has to be named to enter discourse).

The "Other" is not just "another person". It is the *constitutive outside* of the universal subject: man defines himself in opposition to the woman whose particularity he has externalised. De Beauvoir adds that "when I use the words *woman* or *feminine* I obviously refer to no archetype, no changeless essence" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 31, emphasis in original). Woman-as-other is a *position* in a structure, not an essence. Lived experience, not biology, is the centre of gravity.

**Part two: why the othering is linguistically structured.** Three reasons.

*First, the asymmetry is encoded in grammar.* In French, *il* covers male persons, male dogs and the impersonal *it* (*il pleut*); *ils* covers any mixed plural however small the male presence (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). In Czech, Tichý's example shows the rule in conjugation: "all the women of the world went for a walk" uses *šly*; add John, and the verb flips to masculine *šli* (Halewood, 2021: 116). The masculine is at once *one* gender and *the* default. English does it more quietly with generic *he*, *mankind*, *actor/actress*, *Mr* versus *Mrs/Miss*. The structure exists prior to speaker intention.

*Second, the asymmetry is historically prescribed, not natural.* De Beauvoir benefits from Spender's archive. Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman"; Kirkby (1746) ruled that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). The grammar was *made* by male grammarians who enforced male precedence. The structure has authors.

*Third, the asymmetry is preserved by apparently neutral reforms.* If "actor" is the unmarked default, renaming actresses "actors" leaves the masculine yardstick intact (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood's "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat) shows the same for authority: replacing "chairman" with "chair" preserves masculine-clerical authority (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). Language reform that does not touch the marked/unmarked structure simply hides the marking.

**Why this matters for sociology.** The unmarked subject in language coincides with the unmarked subject in institutions. French job titles route the worker (*le médecin*) through the masculine; *une cafetière* is grammatically blocked because the word names a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119). In English, Dewey's "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his" and Whitehead's "men of science" (cited Halewood, 2021: 119) show the same default. The *cathedra* becomes the chair that "makes someone a professor" (Halewood, 2021: 129); the office carries "masculine authority" forward even when "chairman" is renamed "chair" (Halewood, 2021: 130).

**Two limits.** *First*, de Beauvoir's evidence is French and the analysis travels best to gendered grammars. *Second*, the marked/unmarked frame can re-marginalise other subjects. Hill Collins shows the limit sharply: Black feminist thought articulates "a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70) precisely because the de Beauvoirian woman-as-other was tacitly white. The matrix of domination crosses gender with race and class, so the "minus male" position is itself internally differentiated. Whittle (2006) reports pronouns are "limiting and exclusionary" for trans speakers (cited Halewood, 2021: 128). Halewood: "man and woman are not ideas or entities that exist continually. They come to be at different points and different times" (Halewood, 2021: 132).

**Conclusion.** "Woman is the Other" names a structural position. De Beauvoir treats the othering as linguistically structured because the asymmetry is encoded in grammar, prescribed by male grammarians, and preserved by neutral-sounding reforms (Marsh's "actor", "chair"). The only revision the contemporary inheritance demands is keeping the marked/unmarked grid open beyond the man/woman pair.

### Essay 9 / Mock Q4: *Compare Ngũgĩ's argument for African-language writing with Achebe's defence of Africanised English.*

Ngũgĩ and Achebe agree on the diagnosis (English in Africa is not a neutral medium) and split on the prescription. Ngũgĩ argues for *displacement*: African writers should write in African languages. Achebe argues for *refashioning*: bend English to carry African experience. I argue the disagreement is less zero-sum than it looks and that a serious decolonial programme needs both.

**The shared diagnosis.** Both accept that European languages entered Africa as instruments of empire. Ngũgĩ: "English, French, and Portuguese came to the Third World to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Mission schools elevated the conqueror's language, suppressed African languages, and produced an elite "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). Said's Orientalism gives the broader diagnostic frame: European discourse "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9), and the vocabulary of that management was European. Ngũgĩ and Achebe both accept this; they disagree only on what to do next.

**Ngũgĩ's argument for African-language writing.** Ngũgĩ's position rests on a *carrier* theory: language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), so writing in English carries English memory, not African. The colonial school made this concrete: children were "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), and drilled in "Oxford Readers for Africa" about John and Joan, Oxford, and the Thames before the names of other Kenyan towns (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 34). The remedy: write in African languages, and elevate one (Kiswahili, because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41) as a candidate world language. The condition for any genuine world language is "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39).

**Achebe's defence of Africanised English.** Achebe's position is pragmatic. Writing in Hausa, Igbo or Yoruba reaches a smaller group; many Africans are acquiring English or French as their first language (slide 23). The writer should "fashion an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). He should not aspire to native-speaker English: "I hope not. It is neither necessary nor desirable… The price a world language must be prepared to pay is submission to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). *Things Fall Apart* takes its title from W.B. Yeats: refashioning at the level of intertext.

**The genuine disagreement.** Three splits. *Purity versus hybridity.* Ngũgĩ leaves the contaminated language; Achebe refashions it. *Audience.* Ngũgĩ writes for African-language masses; Achebe for a continental and global Anglophone audience. *Theory of change.* Ngũgĩ believes decolonisation requires displacement of medium; Achebe believes it can proceed *through* refashioned English.

**Where they converge.** Both insist the colonial encounter was unjust. Neither accepts English as innocently universal. Achebe's "I hope not" refuses cultural assimilation as fiercely as Ngũgĩ refuses the language. Both recognise that some lingua franca is needed for cross-national communication. They disagree only on whether it should be a coloniser's language refashioned (Achebe) or an African language elevated (Ngũgĩ).

**My evaluation: both/and.** *First*, institutional setting matters. In primary schooling and the recovery of Ngũgĩ's "collective memory bank" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30), Ngũgĩ is more useful. In international publishing and pan-African political debate, Achebe is more practical. *Second*, Ngũgĩ himself accepts the democratic side of English: it has "a democratic tradition" (Shakespeare, Milton, Blake, Shelley, Dickens) and is "part of the common heritage of humankind" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). He is not absolutist about displacement, just insistent on conditions of equality. *Third*, the synthesis is genuinely additive. Ngũgĩ's strategy protects African languages; Achebe's expands what English can carry. Together they shrink the colonial monopoly on prestige.

**Internal-metropolitan applications.** Ngũgĩ travels to Britain. He cites Lyn Marierid: "languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The lecture's three-name slide (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish; slide 26) is the same point minimally; Maori revivalism extends it. AI translation apps and ChatGPT show why both strategies remain urgent: machine-mediated English extends colonial dominance algorithmically.

**Conclusion.** Ngũgĩ and Achebe disagree on whether the writer refuses or refashions the coloniser's language. The disagreement marks a productive division of labour. A serious decolonial programme needs the African-language schooling Ngũgĩ demands *and* the refashioned English Achebe defends.

### Essay 10 / Mock Q5: *"Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Critically explain this slogan with reference to at least two authors.*

The slogan is the load-bearing line of the lecture, repeated on slide 4 and (in Halewood) at 116. It claims a tight coupling between linguistic structure and worldly distribution. I argue the slogan is correct in three senses (reflection, reproduction, constitution), defending it across two authors (Spender, Ngũgĩ) with supporting moves from Halewood, de Beauvoir and Achebe.

**The slogan unpacked.** Language is "not a neutral mirror of the world"; it "classifies, evaluates, and is a mode of power" (Lecture 4 slide 4). Halewood: "language is part of the world. Problems with inequality in language are also problems in the world" (Halewood, 2021: 116). Language is not external to the world, so inequalities encoded in it are part of the world's inequalities.

**Spender on male-as-norm.** English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145), and the grammatical asymmetry corresponds to a social one. The archive is named: Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147); Kirkby (1746) ruled "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). This is one of the bifurcations Halewood diagnoses as constitutive of modernity (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7): humanity is split into two branches, a "gulf" opens between them, and the asymmetric pair then operates as if natural. Prescriptive male precedence in grammar enforces male precedence in social life; reform of one cannot be separated from reform of the other.

**Ngũgĩ on the imperial language.** "The encounter between English and most so-called Third World languages did not occur under conditions of independence and equality" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31); European languages came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). Two mechanisms enforced inequality: suppression of indigenous languages (children "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'", Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) and elevation of the conqueror's language ("Lead kindly light amidst the encircling gloom", where the gloom was the children's own languages, Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32). The slogan applies: humiliation through language was humiliation through the world.

**Three senses of the slogan.**

*Reflection.* Linguistic asymmetries mirror social ones that already exist. Spender's grammarians did not invent patriarchy; they prescribed a male precedence whose social conditions of force lay outside the grammar. Ngũgĩ's English carried "racist and negative images of the conquered nation" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 35) because British imperial power had produced those images first.

*Reproduction.* Asymmetries carry forward when their original cause has lapsed. Halewood's "chair" is the cleanest: even after universities formally open chairs to women, the word carries the male-ecclesiastical authority of the bishop's *cathedra* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130). English remains "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) decades after independence.

*Constitution.* Some inequalities are partly produced through language. Tichý's Czech case (feminine plural *šly* flipping to *šli* the moment one man joins all the women of the world, Halewood, 2021: 116) shows the grammar telling us who counts as the default human every time we conjugate. Irigaray: "sexual difference cannot [. . .] be reduced to a simple extra-linguistic fact of nature. It conditions language and is conditioned by it" (Irigaray, 1993: 20). The trans-pronoun debate extends this point: speakers who reject "he/she" constitute new linguistic-social positions through usage.

**Counter-currents.** The slogan is not despair. Achebe insists the world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347); his "I hope not" refuses assimilation. Ngũgĩ proposes Kiswahili (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). Singular *they* (Halewood, 2021: 128) shows prescribed-out usages can be revived, as Welsh and Maori revivalism demonstrate.

**Critical limits.** *First*, the slogan should not collapse into "fix the words and you fix the world". Halewood: "what is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126). *Second*, the slogan should not reproduce strong-Whorfian othering. "Eskimo" is the wrong name (slide 4; Samson, 2003); wonderment at indigenous "different worlds" reproduces a Western "us/them" division (slide 8).

**Conclusion.** "Problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" is correct in its strong reading. Inequalities in language reflect, reproduce, and partly constitute inequalities in the world. Spender shows this for gender; Ngũgĩ for empire; Halewood for the etymology of authority; Irigaray for sexual difference. The sociologist's task is to keep the three readings analytically apart while showing how they hang together, and to refuse both the complacent answer that language is "just words" and the simplistic answer that fixing the words fixes the world.

### Essay 11 / Mock Q6: *Is linguistic determinism a coherent thesis or a category error?*

My short answer: linguistic determinism is *not* a coherent thesis. It is a category error, a metaphysical claim about cognitive moulding masquerading as an empirical claim about language use. Once diagnosed, the useful work it was meant to do is taken over by linguistic relativity plus an account of institutional power. I defend this in five moves.

**The thesis.** Determinism is the strong Sapir-Whorf: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231). The strict implication: what a language fails to encode, speakers cannot think. The textbook example (Eskimo words for snow, Lecture 4 slide 8) is the determinism reading.

**Move one: translation refutes the strong claim.** If language fully determined thought, translation would be impossible. Ngũgĩ notes European languages "have always communicated with one another such that today it is possible to read nearly all the classics of Russian, French, or German literature and philosophy in any of those languages, thanks to the art of translation" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 40). Translation is not metaphysically impossible; it is institutionally under-funded. Multilingual speakers hold several "moulds" simultaneously. Ngũgĩ writes in Gĩkũyũ and English; Achebe in English carrying "his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Contemporary translation apps reduce friction further, even where they default to Anglophone categories.

**Move two: plasticity refutes it again.** English speakers without a single-word lexicon for "wet snow" build the concept from phrases ("good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing", Lecture 4 slide 9). If language determined thought, English speakers should be unable to discriminate kinds of snow; they plainly can. Spender's programme (linguistically visible women, Spender, 1980: 162) presupposes plasticity. So does singular *they* (attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, contested in current usage, Halewood, 2021: 128).

**Move three: the political risk diagnoses the category error.** Taken straight, determinism becomes "they live in a very different world from 'us'" (Lecture 4 slide 8): an "us/them" division in which speakers of one language are exotic others compared to a Western "us". This is the colonial attitude in epistemological dress. "Eskimo" is itself an externally imposed label (Samson, 2003), and wonderment at indigenous perception continues the missionary outlook. Determinism *describes a political relation in cognitive vocabulary*. A sociologically responsible account cannot rest on a framework reproducing the asymmetry it claims to describe.

**Move four: relativity does the useful work.** The weak version (linguistic relativity) keeps the useful insight (language channels attention, shapes categories) without claiming it *fixes* thought. Saussure's structural account is the better foundation: meaning "arises from difference within a system" (Strinati, 1995: 83), so a *langue* organises perception relationally without metaphysically moulding minds. Spender's "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145), de Beauvoir's marked/unmarked logic, and Ngũgĩ's "collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30) all rest on this structural-relativist insight. Each thinker grants speakers can refashion inventories: Achebe's Africanised English (Achebe, 1997: 347) proceeds exactly this way.

**Move five: institutional gatekeeping, not cognitive moulding.** Even if speakers can coin vocabulary, institutions that publish, teach and credential shape what concepts most speakers encounter. Ngũgĩ's "linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) names a structural constraint; no African-origin language at the UN (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38). This is institutional power, not cognitive moulding. Better to call it linguistic *gatekeeping*: real and reformable by policy. Spender's Wilson-Kirkby archive is a gender-gatekeeping case. Social media moderation across languages is the contemporary case.

**What does the lecture's own example show?** The lecture dismantles "Eskimo words for snow" on three grounds (slides 4, 8, 9). First, "Eskimo" is "neither accurate nor appropriate" (slide 4; Samson, 2003): the example is constructed by the namer. Second, "a very different world from 'us'" assumes a Western "us" against an exoticised "them" (slide 8). Third, English speakers use *phrases* (slide 9); the supposed cognitive gap is an artefact of comparison-framing.

**Contemporary applications.** *Marsh's "actor" reform* (Halewood, 2021: 120) is a clean institutional case: editorial fiat changes vocabulary without (alone) changing the social default. *Halewood's "chair"* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) shows institutional sedimentation. *The colonial school* (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32-33) shows gatekeeping at the level of pedagogy.

**Conclusion.** Determinism is a category error: it dresses a political-institutional relation in cognitive vocabulary, reproducing colonial othering (slide 8). The useful work is done better by relativity plus institutional power: Spender on prescription, Ngũgĩ on imperial policy. Language channels thought but does not determine it; the channels are set by institutions, not metaphysical moulds.

### M7: *How does Halewood use the figure of the "chair" to extend de Beauvoir and Irigaray into Anglophone usage?*

Halewood introduces "chair" to answer a specific objection: that de Beauvoir's and Irigaray's analyses of grammatical gender apply only to French or Czech, not to English (Halewood, 2021: 119). The example shows English carries the same patriarchal structure beneath neutral vocabulary, operating through institutional history rather than morphology. I set out the example, locate it in de Beauvoir/Irigaray, and assess what it adds.

**The de Beauvoir/Irigaray argument.** De Beauvoir's foundational claim: "man represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). A man does not announce he writes "as a man"; a woman is "immediately seen as a 'woman writer'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Irigaray takes this into grammar: *il* covers "he" and impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*); *ils* serves any mixed plural (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). Tichý's Czech example shows *šli* taking over *šly* the moment one man joins all the women of the world (Halewood, 2021: 116). Job titles follow: the default noun for a doctor (*le médecin*) is masculine; the would-be female café-owner (*une cafetière*) is already a coffee-pot (Halewood, 2021: 118-119).

**The Anglophone objection.** "Irigaray's (and perhaps de Beauvoir's) analysis might have some force when analysing Francophone societies, but it has limited relevance to the Anglophone world" (Halewood, 2021: 119). Halewood gives two refutations. First, residue: Dewey's "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his", and Whitehead's "men of science" (cited Halewood, 2021: 119). A defender might say English has moved on. The structural case is "chair".

**The "chair" example.** "Chair" comes from Latin *cathedra*, the bishop's seat, related to *sedes* (the "see", Halewood, 2021: 128). "Chairs and seats are tied up with the authority of the Christian Church" (Halewood, 2021: 129). Professors hold "chairs": Hawking held the Lucasian Chair, founded 1663, previously Newton's (Halewood, 2021: 129). Western universities were "originally institutions within the Christian Church… designed to train *men* for their roles in the Church" (Halewood, 2021: 129). The committee chair inherits the professorial chair. Even with "chairman" retired, "in one important sense, the authority held by professors is male *and* masculine" (Halewood, 2021: 130).

**What the example adds.**

*First, it transposes the Irigarayan structure into English without French-style grammatical gender.* The patriarchal structure operates through etymology and institutional history. The unmarked English word *chair* carries unmarked masculine authority.

*Second, it shows what "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126) means concretely.* The "chairman"-to-"chair" reform parallels the Marsh case (actress to actor, Halewood, 2021: 120). In both, the apparently neutral term carries the masculine yardstick. Reform of word without reform of institution is symbolic.

*Third, it warns against "perfectly true language" reform.* Halewood quotes Haraway: "all dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130). Each instance "needs to be dealt with on its own merits" (Halewood, 2021: 130). "Chair" is a specific institutional history, not a generic case.

**Spender's complement.** Spender's archive (Wilson 1553, Kirkby 1746, Fowler 1926) tracks moments where male precedence was *explicitly* legislated (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Halewood's "chair" tracks the *silent* version: not a moment of prescription but inheritance from the bishop's seat to the committee head. Together, the cases cover the visible and invisible faces of male-as-norm. Hartsock's standpoint theory deepens the analytic stakes: "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), and the chair example shows the structural difference is carried in the very furniture of authority. Making the *cathedra*-inheritance visible is part of the standpoint move Hartsock prescribes.

**Ngũgĩ's complement.** "Chair" runs through the Christian Church, the institution Ngũgĩ identifies as partner to "the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31). The example bridges gender and colonial arguments. The "reasonable man" of common-law jurisprudence is a parallel case.

**A processual reading.** Halewood: "language and the world become together; in doing so, they express those elements that later come to be called natural and social" (Halewood, 2021: 132). The bishop's authority and the professor's chair become together; none is fixed by nature.

**Conclusion.** Halewood's "chair" is a precision instrument. It refutes the Francophone-specific objection by showing English carries the same patriarchal structure through institutional history rather than morphology. It shows what "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126) means concretely: rename "chairman" to "chair" and the masculine-ecclesiastical authority comes along for the ride. And it links the gender and colonial arguments through the Christian Church as their shared historical-institutional carrier.

*Word count: ~705.*

### M8: *Critically explain how the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is presented and qualified by the lecturer.*

Sapir-Whorf structures the first third of Lecture 4 (slides 3-9). The lecturer presents it in two forms (relativity and determinism), illustrates both through "Eskimo words for snow", and dismantles the strong reading on three grounds. I work through the presentation and assess what survives.

**Presentation.** The lecturer introduces relativity through Brown (slide 3): "the belief that each language embodies and perpetuates a particular world view" (Brown, 1968: 230), so that "speakers of a language are partners to an agreement to perceive and think of the world in a certain way" (Brown, 1968: 230). The example (slide 4): "Eskimos" have "over 50 words for 'snow'", suggesting they live in a different world. Slide 5 names the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis after Edward Sapir (1884-1939) and Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897-1941). Slide 6 introduces determinism: "the languages of the world are so many moulds of varying shape into which infant minds are poured" (Brown, 1968: 231). The strong reading: what a language fails to encode, speakers cannot think.

**First qualification: language is rendering, not reflection (slide 7).** "Any use of human language expresses human thoughts about the world." Each language is "a specific rendering of a specific 'reality'", not "an accurate or objective picture". Humans "seem to have lost any innocent contact with the world". *Every* language refracts reality; no language is the world's natural mirror. The Eskimo-vs-English comparison is between two specific renderings, not between accurate and inaccurate.

**Second qualification: us-and-them framing (slide 8).** "They live in a very different world from 'us'" assumes an "us" and a "them". The danger is "assigning completely different 'realities' or worldviews to speakers of so-called indigenous groups". The Western outlook becomes the implicit benchmark, and the indigenous group is the exotic other. This connects to Modernity as a Colonial Project. The inequality is inside the example itself.

**Third qualification: words versus phrases (slide 9).** Sapir-Whorf "assumes that single words are the main way of indicating the things of the world". English speakers use *phrases* ("good snow for packing", "bad snow for packing") where Eskimo speakers are imagined to use distinct words. English has "as many possible phrases for snow as they do" (slide 9). The supposed cognitive gap is an artefact of the unit of analysis.

**Fourth qualification: the label itself is wrong (slide 4; Samson, 2003).** The lecturer scare-quotes "Eskimos": "neither accurate nor appropriate" as a name. This is "a first indication of the power inherent in language to try to 'capture' groups of people". The example is named by the namer, not the named.

**What survives.** Weak Sapir-Whorf (relativity) survives all four qualifications. Speakers of a shared language live in an agreement to perceive the world in a certain way (Brown, 1968: 230); language carves the world into categories unequal in consequence. Barthes's account of myth is the natural extension: language has a function "to transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140), to make historically specific arrangements appear timeless and obvious. This is what relativity does at the lexical level. Spender's "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) and Ngũgĩ's "collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30) operate on exactly this Barthesian register. Determinism does not survive: speakers can build phrases (slide 9), translate (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 40), and the example itself reproduces colonial othering (slide 8).

**How the rest of the lecture uses the hypothesis.** Qualified-relativity underwrites Spender: "plus male / minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145) is a prescriptively-installed agreement, not a fixed mould. It underwrites Ngũgĩ: the colonial school's elevation of English (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-33) changed the agreement-to-perceive without producing cognitive impossibility. It underwrites the actor/actress critique (slide 17). AI translation apps and ChatGPT extend the point: their "agreement" defaults to Anglophone categories.

**Halewood's reformulation.** Halewood gives the slogan its sharpest form: "language is part of the world. Problems with inequality in language are also problems in the world" (Halewood, 2021: 116). Weak-relativity taken structurally: language is not external to the world it describes, so inequalities encoded in it are part of the world's inequalities, not a separate problem to be solved by tidying vocabulary.

**Conclusion.** The lecturer presents Sapir-Whorf to retain its useful insight (language channels attention and classifies) while dismantling its dangerous extension (determinism plus colonial othering). The four qualifications reduce the hypothesis to its defensible weak form: language renders rather than reflects; us/them framing reproduces inequality; single-word focus misses phrasing; the very label "Eskimo" is an external imposition. That weak form is what Spender, de Beauvoir, Ngũgĩ, Achebe and Halewood rely on for the rest of the lecture.

*Word count: ~700.*

### M9: *How convincing do you find Spender's claim that English is structurally patriarchal?*

I find Spender's claim broadly convincing as a structural claim about the prescriptive history of standard written English, but only partially convincing as a claim about English as a whole. Three qualifications matter: anglocentrism, under-weighted speaker plasticity, and a binary gender frame that contemporary trans politics has contested.

**Spender's claim.** *Man Made Language* (1980) is structural rather than anecdotal. English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). The male is the default human; the female is marked by additional morphology (*actress*, *waitress*) or honorifics (*Mrs/Miss* mark marital status; *Mr* does not). The archive is named: Wilson (1553) insisted it was "more natural to place the man before the woman" (Spender, 1980: 147). Kirkby (1746) ruled that "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 148). The "man-made" label tracks an archive of male grammarians prescribing male precedence. Her programme: "for women to become visible, it is necessary that they become linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162).

**Why I find the core claim convincing.**

*First, the historical archive is decisive.* Spender's case rests on a paper trail. Named grammarians, dated rules, and explicit justifications ("more comprehensive than the female") are evidence of prescription. Halewood adds Fowler: his call for "doctress, teacheress, singeress and danceress" (cited Halewood, 2021: 120) is the same prescriptive impulse two centuries on, operating on the same male-as-norm assumption.

*Second, contemporary asymmetries persist.* The Marsh case (Halewood, 2021: 120) is the cleanest: even after "actress" is retired in favour of "actor", the masculine yardstick is preserved, so we end up "lumping [women] in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (Lecture 4 slide 17). Halewood's "chair" (from Latin *cathedra*, Halewood, 2021: 128-130) shows the same sedimentation in authority vocabulary.

*Third, de Beauvoir's social ontology fits.* Man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) is the social complement to Spender's grammatical case. Tichý's Czech (*šly* flipping to *šli*, Halewood, 2021: 116) and Irigaray's French examples (*il pleut*, *le médecin*, *cafetière*, Halewood, 2021: 114-119) show the correspondence holds across gendered languages.

**Three qualifications.**

*First, anglocentrism.* Spender's evidence is overwhelmingly English. Halewood acknowledges the objection that "Irigaray's (and perhaps de Beauvoir's) analysis might have some force when analysing Francophone societies, but it has limited relevance to the Anglophone world" (Halewood, 2021: 119). Halewood's "chair" plugs the gap, but this is a real limit on Spender's framing as stated.

*Second, speaker plasticity.* Spender's framing slightly understates how language is continuously *re*-made. Achebe's intuition that a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347) applies inside English. Singular *they* (attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, Halewood, 2021: 128) has been revived. Spender herself is not deterministic (her programme is precisely to "defy" the rules, Spender, 1980: 162) but the "structurally patriarchal" framing can sound more fixed than her argument allows.

*Third, the binary frame.* "Plus male / minus male" presupposes binary gender, and presupposes a single, undifferentiated category of "woman" beneath the minus sign. Hill Collins's matrix of domination refuses this: Black feminist thought articulates "a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70) precisely because the unified "woman" of Spender's grammatical critique was tacitly white. Whittle (2006) extends the limit to trans speakers, for whom pronouns are "limiting and exclusionary" (cited Halewood, 2021: 128). Halewood's conclusion is processual: "man and woman are not ideas or entities that exist continually" (Halewood, 2021: 132). The diagnosis survives the qualification; the binary scaffolding does not.

**Three internal tests.** *Actor/actress* (Halewood, 2021: 120; slides 16-17): Spender predicts both the reform impulse and the difficulty. *Wilson-Kirkby archive* (Spender, 1980: 147-148): overt, named, dated prescription. *"Chair"* (Halewood, 2021: 128-130): the institutional-history case.

**Caveats from Halewood and Ngũgĩ.** Halewood: "tinkering with language… is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Spender's "linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162) is a precondition, not substitute. Ngũgĩ reminds us patriarchy is one of several hierarchies; the colonial-attitude operates by similar mechanisms (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32) and the two intersect.

**Conclusion.** Spender's claim is convincing as a structural-historical diagnosis. The qualifications refine rather than refute. The strongest version: standard English was prescriptively patriarchalised by named grammarians, the prescription continues to shape contemporary defaults (actress/actor, chair), and the structure is reformable provided the world changes alongside the word. Less understates the archive; more overstates the rigidity of language.

*Word count: ~725.*

### M10: *Using the work of at least two authors, explain how language operates as a vehicle of inequality.*

I draw on Spender and Ngũgĩ, with supporting moves from de Beauvoir, Achebe and Halewood, to argue language operates as a vehicle of inequality through one mechanism: it encodes an unmarked default subject (the male; the European) and makes everyone else a marked deviation. The mechanism runs through two domains (gender, empire) and three sites within each (grammar, vocabulary, institutional history).

**The shared mechanism.** Spender identifies the template: English carves humanity into "those who are plus male and those who are minus male" (Spender, 1980: 145). De Beauvoir generalises: man "represents both the positive and the neutral [. . .] whereas woman represents only the negative" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). Ngũgĩ extends from gender to empire. European languages came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) and remain "the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Said names the discursive correlate at imperial scale: Orientalism is the apparatus through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). The unmarked male and the unmarked European are produced by the same logic.

**Site one: grammar.** Spender's archive shows Wilson (1553) insisting on "male and female, husband and wife" and Kirkby (1746) ruling "the male gender was more comprehensive than the female" (Spender, 1980: 147-148). Halewood adds cross-linguistic cases. In French, *il* covers "he" and impersonal "it" (*il fait chaud*); *ils* serves any mixed plural (Halewood, 2021: 114-115). In Czech, Tichý's example shows *šly* flipping to *šli* the moment one man tags along (Halewood, 2021: 116). The grammar tells the speaker who counts as the default human every time the verb is conjugated.

**Site two: vocabulary.** The clearest gender case is the actor/actress reform Marsh imposed at *The Guardian* (Halewood, 2021: 120; Lecture 4 slides 16-17). Retiring "actress" seems equalising, but most occupations "such as that of doctor, teacher, actor were carried out by men", so apparently neutral "actor" provides "a yardstick which may not immediately appear to refer to the male"; "by calling all women 'actors' we are lumping them in to what is really a 'masculine' term" (slide 17). The vocabulary reform pays "lip-service" while ignoring "the more fundamental problem". The colonial analogue: continuing to use English in administration, commerce and justice (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37) is Marsh's reform at institutional scale, the neutral medium carrying the hierarchy beneath. "Master/slave" terminology debates in computing are a parallel case.

**Site three: institutional history.** "Chair" derives from Latin *cathedra* (the bishop's seat) and *sedes* (the diocese) (Halewood, 2021: 128). Professors hold "chairs" (Hawking held the Lucasian Chair, founded 1663, previously held by Newton, Halewood, 2021: 129); the committee chair inherits this authority. "All bishops (until recently) have been men", and authority "incorporated in a professorship models a traditional view of masculinity" (Halewood, 2021: 130). Renaming "chairman" to "chair" preserves male-ecclesiastical authority. Ngũgĩ's colonial analogue is the imperial school: English as "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), with children "caned or made to carry plaques inscribed with the words 'I am stupid'" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33). Decades after independence, the curriculum still routes knowledge through the coloniser's tongue.

**Why two authors are enough.** Spender supplies the gender mechanism (male-as-norm in grammar, vocabulary, prescriptive history). Ngũgĩ supplies the colonial mechanism (European-languages-as-norm in institutional history, education, international forums). The patterns intersect. Halewood's "chair" bridges them: male-ecclesiastical authority is *both* a gender story and a Christian-imperial one; the Western university was "originally [an] institution within the Christian Church, designed to further theological knowledge and train *men* for their roles in the Church" (Halewood, 2021: 129).

**Counter-currents.** Vocabulary is plastic. Spender calls for women to be "linguistically visible" (Spender, 1980: 162). Singular *they* (attested in Shakespeare, Lewis Carroll, the King James Bible and Thackeray, Halewood, 2021: 128) shows prescribed-out usages can be revived. Achebe: a world language must "submit to many different kinds of use" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Welsh, Gaelic and Maori revivalism work at language-level. Halewood's warning: "tinkering with language… is not enough. What is required is a change in thinking, speaking and being" (Halewood, 2021: 126).

**Conclusion.** Language operates as a vehicle of inequality through one mechanism (the unmarked default) in three sites (grammar, vocabulary, institutional history) across two registers (gender and empire). Spender and Ngũgĩ, supplemented by de Beauvoir, Achebe and Halewood, supply the analytic vocabulary. Reforming the words is necessary but must be paired with structural change in the institutions the words serve.

*Word count: ~720.*

### M11: *Can sociology be done in English without reproducing the colonial mind?*

My answer: only partially, and only if done self-consciously. Sociology in English is a contradiction the discipline cannot fully escape but can practise more or less honestly. My position is closer to Achebe than Ngũgĩ: refashion English sociological writing from inside while acknowledging that fully decolonised sociology requires institutional changes the discipline cannot deliver alone.

**Why the question is sharp.** Ngũgĩ argues European languages came "to announce the arrival of the Bible and the sword" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31) and remain "the official languages of instruction, of administration, of commerce, trade, justice, and foreign communications. In short, they are the languages of power" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Sociology, taught and published mostly in English, is part of this infrastructure. Its categories ("society", "modernity", "race", "class", "global south") were forged in English (and French and German) and carry European assumptions. Doing sociology in English is doing sociology in the language of the master-discourse it claims to analyse.

**Three ways the colonial mind enters English-language sociology.**

*First, the conceptual vocabulary.* English in colonial Africa became "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), so that "the results of our research into science, technology and of our achievements in the creative arts are stored in those languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37). Said's Orientalism is the deeper diagnosis: the discipline "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9), so sociological categories carry the unmarked Anglo-European benchmark inside them. When English-language sociology measures other societies, it measures them against that benchmark, mirroring de Beauvoir's woman-as-other at the scale of nations.

*Second, the prestige economy of publishing.* Anglophone publishing dominates citation, hiring and promotion. Ngũgĩ's complaint that the African elite is "completely uprooted from the peoples of Africa and tied to the West" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 38) diagnoses disciplinary publishing. AI chatbots like ChatGPT reproduce the same hierarchy algorithmically.

*Third, the everyday register of academic English.* Halewood's "chair" (Halewood, 2021: 128-130) generalises: chairs of sociology inherit the bishop's *cathedra*. Dewey's "houses are usually owned, are mine and yours and his" and Whitehead's "men of science" (cited Halewood, 2021: 119) show male defaults into the twentieth century.

**Three things sociology in English can still do honestly.**

*First, Achebe's refashioning strategy applies.* The African writer must use "an English that is at once universal and able to carry his peculiar experience" (Achebe, 1997: 347). Sociologists can refuse to flatten local categories into English ones, and adopt Achebe's "I hope not" answer to whether non-native speakers should aspire to native-like academic English (Achebe, 1997: 347).

*Second, self-critical attention to vocabulary.* The lecturer's caution about "Eskimo" (Lecture 4 slides 4, 8) generalises. Halewood's processual conclusion is the meta-rule: "language and the world become together" (Halewood, 2021: 132). Haraway's warning that "all dreams for a perfectly true language, of perfectly naming experience [. . .] are totalizing and imperialist" (Haraway, 1991: 173, cited Halewood, 2021: 130) blocks the cleansed-vocabulary fantasy. The move from "coloureds" to "ethnic communities" is real but does not mean "the way they think and act in the world has changed" (Halewood, 2021: 131).

*Third, structural reforms.* Ngũgĩ's call for "the art of translation and interpretation" to be "an integral subject in schools" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39-40) translates into cross-language translation programmes, support for non-English journals, and recognition of non-Anglophone publishing in hiring. These follow from his demand that "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39) precede any genuine universal medium.

**Where the limits bite.** Ngũgĩ's stronger claim (that medium matters intrinsically, not instrumentally) means some sociological knowledge cannot be produced fully in English. Language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30); memory banks outside the Anglosphere cannot be fully translated into Anglophone categories. Halewood: "tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). The structural fix requires multilingual sociology.

**Sites inside the materials.** Just as "Oxford Readers for Africa" taught children about the Thames before other Kenyan towns, the canonical curriculum teaches Durkheim's Paris and Weber's Heidelberg before African or Asian theorists. The "elevation of the language of the conqueror" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 32) is reproduced at the reading list.

**Conclusion.** Sociology cannot be done in English without reproducing *some* of the colonial mind. It can be done without reproducing *all* of it, if practitioners take Achebe's refashioning seriously, interrogate the vocabulary, and push for multilingual infrastructure. Self-consciousness is not a substitute for changing institutional conditions, but it is a precondition.

### M12: *"Language is not a vehicle but a battleground." Discuss with reference to the lecture's account of Welsh, Gaelic, and Cornish.*

The slogan reframes language away from transport (carrying meaning between speakers) toward contested ground (fought over by competing political projects). The reframe is productive but exaggerated; I defend a careful version: language is *both* vehicle and battleground. The lecture's brief slide on Welsh, Gaelic, and Cornish (slide 26), read with Ngũgĩ's discussion of Welsh, is the cleanest case for the battleground reading.

**Why "vehicle" is insufficient.** The vehicle metaphor treats language as a neutral medium. The lecture's slogan that "problems with inequality in language are also problems within the world" (Lecture 4 slide 4) refuses this. Ngũgĩ's carrier theory does the structural work: language is "the collective memory bank of a people" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30); losing the language loses the world it carries. If language were just a vehicle, the question of *which* gets driven would be aesthetic. Minority-language revival would make no sense.

**Why "battleground" captures what is missed.** *First*, language death is the residue of policy, not nature. Ngũgĩ cites Lyn Marierid: "Languages… are lost when the predominant class in society has no use for them" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Welsh declined because "rural areas… have become completely Anglicised as ordinary working-class people have been systematically priced out of their native areas" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). The "yuppiefication" of rural Wales is the same structural pressure as the colonial school's "I am stupid" plaques (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33), through market rather than corporal mechanisms. *Second*, Ngũgĩ's colonial mechanism (suppression plus elevation, Ngũgĩ, 1993: 31-32) applies internally to the British Isles. *Third*, language carries identity claims and a "collective memory bank" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 30) that other vehicles cannot.

**The lecture's three names.** Slide 26 lists only Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish. The names alone generalise Ngũgĩ's analysis from Africa to the Anglophone core. Welsh is the case Ngũgĩ discusses (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36): under pressure from English-medium administration and capital but contested by a Welsh Language Society arguing Welsh is necessary "to be aware of [the working class's] past in order to be able to shape their future" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Maori revivalism extends the same case to the southern hemisphere.

**Ngũgĩ inside the Anglophone core.** Minority-language revival enacts decolonising-the-mind inside Britain. Suppression: Welsh, Gaelic and Cornish were pushed out of administration, schooling and economic life by Anglophone institutions. Elevation: English became "the bearer of all knowledge in the arts and sciences" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 33) inside the British Isles. This is the linguistic face of one of the bifurcations Halewood diagnoses as modernity's signature move (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7): a split between a "real" national language and a "residual" minority one, where the gulf is then naturalised. Marierid's case for Welsh is internalist Ngũgĩ: keep the language because the working class needs its own memory bank (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36).

**Ngũgĩ's preconditions.** Ngũgĩ proposes Kiswahili because "it already has the advantage of never having grown in the graveyard of other languages" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 41). This translates into a precondition for revival: the revival language must be allowed to *grow*, not preserved in museum form. The political-economic precondition is "absolute independence and equality of all nations in the economic, political, and cultural spheres" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39).

**Achebe's strategy alongside.** Achebe's complementary move (refashion the dominant language to carry minority experience, Achebe, 1997: 347) operates at the interface between revived and dominant languages. The battleground has two fronts: defending the indigenous language *and* refashioning the dominant one. AI translation apps and social media moderation are contemporary fronts: minority-language content is under-supported algorithmically.

**Critical complications.** *Halewood's warning.* "Tinkering with language… is not enough" (Halewood, 2021: 126). Signage reform alone is tinkering. *Class.* Marierid's argument is class-based: "for generations, the Welsh working class was utterly dependent on the Welsh language and culture" (cited Ngũgĩ, 1993: 36). Revival without working-class participation risks becoming a credentialing exercise. *Ngũgĩ's wider point.* "The death of many languages should never be the condition for the life of a few" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 39).

**Conclusion.** "Language is not a vehicle but a battleground" overstates if read as denying the carrier function. Read as supplementing the vehicle metaphor with recognition that language is *fought over*, the slogan is right. The three-name slide (Welsh, Gaelic, Cornish) and Ngũgĩ's discussion of the Welsh Language Society show that minority languages inside the Anglophone core were not lost by attrition; they were defeated by policy, and can only be revived by policy. The politics of revival are the politics of decolonising, fought on internal-colonial terrain.
