# L16: Postmodernism (Lyotard, Baudrillard, Jameson)

## §1 Lecture overview

This lecture maps the shift from modernity to postmodernity through three primary theorists, Jean-François Lyotard, Jean Baudrillard and Fredric Jameson, and locates contemporary popular culture inside that diagnosis. Modernism in this account names a confidence in "grand narratives": Enlightenment rationality, Marxist emancipation, Christian providence, scientific progress, the mass media as a unified public sphere, and a single epistemological centre that grounds truth. Postmodernism is the breakdown of this confidence under the pressure of war, the fall of Communism, accelerating technology, ubiquitous media, and the saturation of consumer life. The lecture stresses that postmodernism is not just a periodisation but a condition (Lyotard), a regime of signs (Baudrillard) and a cultural logic of late capitalism (Jameson).

The lecture begins with the relational map between structuralism (single stable meaning, objective reality, an epistemology of sign and referent), post-structuralism (meaning unstable, multiple truths, socially constructed reality, no universal truth) and post-modernism (after modernity, encompassing many movements including post-structuralism, oscillating between resistance and nihilism). It then unpacks Lyotard's report on the postmodern condition (1979), focusing on the end of grand narratives, the rise of language games and performativity, the fragmentation of knowledge into local narratives, and the transformation of the university. The famous slide quotes Lyotard on "eclecticism" as "the degree zero of contemporary general culture: you listen to reggae, you watch a western, you eat McDonald's at midday and local cuisine at night, you wear Paris perfume in Tokyo and retro clothes in Hong Kong" (Lyotard).

Baudrillard's account of simulation in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981) is then introduced through three vectors: the move from Marx's use and exchange value to sign value ("the sign object is neither given nor exchanged: it is appropriated, withheld and manipulated by individual subjects as a sign, that is as a coded difference"); the hypermarket as a site of symbolic exchange that produces spatial and temporal distortion, surveils consumers and yet hosts playful behaviour; and the four orders of simulation, peaking in third-order hyperreality where the distinction between the real and the model dissolves. The slides apply this to Disneyland ("presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest is real, when in fact all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real, but of the order of the hyperreal and simulation"), to political scandals such as Watergate as hyperreal dissimulation, to *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995), and to reality television as the implosion of real and represented, the death of the panoptic gaze and the gospel that "YOU are the content".

Jameson's notion of postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism is then introduced, with his sharp diagnostic that "it's easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism" and that postmodern culture produces an ahistoricism in which texts become styles and history is represented through pastiche. The lecture closes with Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009), the concept of precorporation, Derrida's hauntology as deployed by Fisher, the debate over whether postmodernism is "guilty" or "not guilty" of producing fake news, conspiracy theories and alternative facts, and the essential reading's stocktake of postmodernism in cinema (*Blade Runner*, *Pulp Fiction*), television (*Breaking Bad*, *Only Way Is Essex*), and pop music (mash-ups, sampling).

## §2 Lyotard: the postmodern condition and language games

I treat Lyotard's *The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge* (1979) as the founding text of the lecture. Lyotard's central thesis is condensed in a sentence the slides paraphrase: postmodernity is defined by "a growing disbelief in 'grand narratives'" (Lyotard, 1984: [VERIFY PAGE?]). Grand narratives, sometimes called metanarratives, are the legitimating stories of modernity: Enlightenment rationality (knowledge frees humanity), the Hegelian-Marxist dialectic (history culminates in emancipation), Christian eschatology (history culminates in salvation), the hermeneutic of meaning (the spirit grounds truth). Postmodernity is the condition in which these stories no longer command belief, not because anyone has proved them false, but because the social, technological and economic infrastructure that supported them has fractured.

In their place Lyotard puts language games (a term he borrows from Wittgenstein) and performativity. Knowledge is no longer justified by reference to a universal foundation; it is justified by efficiency, usefulness and local rules, by whether it works inside a given game. Performativity governs media. Knowledge becomes information. The university, the lecture insists, is transformed by this shift: it becomes a producer of marketable skills rather than a custodian of truth, and disciplines fragment into local specialisms whose languages are mutually opaque. The differend names the situation in which two language games meet without a shared rule that could adjudicate between them, so a wrong is done that cannot be expressed inside the rules of the dominant game.

For media, Lyotard's diagnosis is precise. The media no longer transmit a shared worldview. They circulate discrete, competing messages. No single media narrative can claim authority over others. There is no final authority of the media. Media simultaneously support and undermine grand narratives, sometimes within the same broadcast cycle. The famous "eclecticism is the degree zero of contemporary general culture" passage is Lyotard's most quotable diagnosis: consumption itself becomes a montage of references unmoored from any local tradition.

I apply this directly to the cases the slides foreground. The conspiracy material on slide 32 (Pizzagate, David Icke's *Guide to the Global Conspiracy*, the X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the fake "Huge Death Camps, FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston" banner) is not an aberration from a healthy public sphere but a rational symptom of the absence of any authoritative grand narrative; little paranoid narratives substitute for vanished big ones. Slide 31's Trump quotation, "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE", makes sense inside a Lyotardian frame: when no shared rule adjudicates truth, each language game produces its own facts and accuses the others of fabricating theirs. The Ipsos poll on slide 31 (74% across 29 countries think AI makes fake news easier) is itself a survey of audiences who recognise that no media outlet has final authority.

Critics of Lyotard insist that grand narratives have not died at all, nationalism, capitalism itself, scientific naturalism, identity politics, climate science, neoliberal market triumphalism are all alive and authoritative. I take this critique seriously, but I argue with the slides that even where grand narratives persist they have lost their unchallenged legitimating force; they now circulate as one option among others, defended polemically rather than presupposed.

## §4 Jameson, Fisher and the cultural logic of late capitalism

Fredric Jameson reframes postmodernism as a periodising concept tied to a stage of capitalism. Postmodernism is "the cultural logic of late capitalism", late capitalism here meaning Ernest Mandel's third stage of capitalist development, after market and monopoly capitalism, in which capital becomes truly global, financialised and dominant over media and cultural production. Postmodern theory and culture are the primary form of cultural production in this phase, across art, architecture, literature and film.

Their characteristic effect is ahistoricism. Texts become styles; history is represented through pastiche rather than parody (parody implies a normative original to mock, pastiche is blank quotation without critical edge). Strinati's catalogue makes the point concretely: *Body Heat* (1981) is "parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling…the crime thriller of the 1940s" in what Strinati calls "retro-nostalgia" (Strinati, 1995: 218); the *Rocky* and *Rambo* sequels "go no further than recycling the recent past, making films which are merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality" (Strinati, 1995: 218); *Blade Runner* (1982) "clearly mixes styles from different periods", so that "we appear to be in the past, the present and the future at the same time" (Strinati, 1995: 218–219). *The Last of Us* (2023), foregrounded in slides 24–26, converts a video game into a prestige TV pastiche of post-apocalyptic cinema. Alternative trajectories for systems other than capitalism collapse into mere fads, narratives or vague fantasies.

Jameson's most quoted line, deployed by the slides, is that "it's easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism" and that this asymmetry "is due to some weakness in our imaginations" (Jameson, 1994: xii). Our consumption of post-apocalyptic cinema, television and video games, *The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76*, *The Last of Us*, is a parody of our own demise that has the effect of imagining capitalism by way of imagining the end of the world (Jameson, 2003: 76).

Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009) sharpens this further. Capitalism dominates all so that it is "impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it" (Fisher, 2009: 2, author's italics), even post-apocalypse. A "business ontology" (Fisher, 2009: 17) treats everything, schools, hospitals, friendships, as if it were a corporation. Capitalism has an "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6); it can incorporate anything, including its own critique, including the apocalypse. Our desires, aspirations and hopes undergo a process of "precorporation" (Fisher, 2009: 9): before they enter our consciousness they are already framed by the logic of capitalism. Resistance is therefore not censored but pre-emptively absorbed.

Fisher couples this with Derrida's concept of hauntology. Capitalist media is figuratively haunted by the ghosts of former utopias and theorisations: we cannot escape the modernist grand narratives because we keep mourning them. The slide's bust of Marx in a wintry forest is precisely this hauntology made image. The cycle of post-apocalyptic media on slides 24–26 (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76: Burning Springs*, *The Last of Us*) is, for Fisher and Jameson together, the cultural form in which late capitalism stages its own ungraspable end.

## §5 The fake-news debate and the limits of postmodernism

The slides stage an explicit "guilty/not guilty" debate. The case against postmodernism is that by denying grand narratives it makes all truth relative, opens space for alternative political views and counter-narratives, promotes a polyvocal stance in which all voices appear equal, hides behind obtuse language that prevents popular critique, prioritises style over substantive argument and slides into nihilism. There is some evidence, the slides note with healthy scepticism, that US government agencies funded postmodern art and culture as a Cold War alternative to more radical forms of critical theory and Marxism, though this may itself be a "left" conspiracy theory. The case for postmodernism is that it critiques grand narratives precisely as the vehicles of nationalism and conspiracy, provides alternative modalities of critique, emphasises shifting identities and flows rather than authoritarian closure, deflates pomposity with irony, and is explicitly named by authoritarian governments as leading to identity politics and cultural degeneration, which suggests it is doing something right.

The essential reading flags the limits of postmodernism in explaining media. It exaggerates the role of media and commercialism in forming reality; it rehashes Frankfurt School claims about the culture industry without acknowledging the debt; metanarratives are not actually in decline (nationalism, market triumphalism, climate science); the high/low culture divide persists; and popular culture has always used mixtures of styles, so pastiche is nothing new. The reading suggests these limits can be overcome by bringing postmodernism into dialogue with post-structuralism (media as discourse and dialogic) and cultural populism (anti-elitist working-class cultures).

### M1: Map the relation between structuralism, post-structuralism and postmodernism, and explain why postmodernism emerges from the breakdown of "grand narratives, truth through science and investigation, mass media and unified systems of control".

I argue that the three positions form a continuous trajectory of destabilising meaning, not a clean periodisation, and that postmodernism emerges when modernist confidence in grand narratives, scientific investigation and unified mass media is structurally broken by twentieth-century crisis. The teacher summary's section heading 'From Structuralism to post-structuralism and post-modernism' (Postmodernism notes, p. 1) and slide 3 organise the answer.

Structuralism, in Saussure's foundational account, treats meaning as the effect of a stable system of differences. The sign couples signifier and signified, and reality enters analysis only through structured symbolic systems. The position assumes 'single and stable meaning, objective reality, epistemology' (slide 3). Barthes (1957) extended the model from language to images, food and clothing, but his second-order signifying systems and concept of myth began to expose how stable structures naturalise contingent meanings. Structuralism is the closing form of modernist confidence in unified epistemological grounding.

Post-structuralism inherits the Saussurean machinery but withdraws the assumption of stability. Slide 3 captures the shift: 'meaning is not stable, dependent on interpretation, multiple truths and meanings, reality is socially constructed, no universal truth'. Foucault's analyses of discourse and power dismantle the modernist couple of knower and known by showing that subjects and objects are produced together in historical regimes. Derrida's deconstruction shows that any structure rests on exclusions it cannot fully account for. The position retains the structuralist sensitivity to systems while denying their closure. Reality remains socially constructed, but construction is the work of unstable, contested operations rather than of a stable grammar.

Postmodernism, on slide 3, is 'after modernity, many movements including post-structuralism. May suggest post-modern methods of resistance or nihilism'. The relation is not exclusion but inclusion: postmodernism encompasses post-structuralism but extends the diagnosis beyond textual systems to history, politics and culture. The teacher summary's framing makes the structural break explicit: 'Modernism, grand narratives, truth through science and investigation, mass media and unified systems of control. Post-modernism, breakdown of this (crisis, wars, fall of Communism, technology, media)' (Postmodernism notes, p. 1).

This breakdown is not a philosophical preference but a historical condition. Two World Wars, the Holocaust, the collapse of Soviet communism in 1989, the discrediting of high modernist universalisms, the rise of saturating consumer media, and the proliferation of computing and networked information all eroded the legitimating frameworks of modernity. Lyotard's report on the postmodern condition (1979) names the epistemological correlate: 'postmodernity is a condition in which knowledge is fragmented, instrumentalised, and no longer justified by universal truths, but by efficiency, usefulness, and local rules' (Postmodernism notes, p. 1; Lyotard, 1984: [VERIFY PAGE?]).

Lyotard's account of media (Postmodernism notes, p. 2) follows directly from this: 'Media no longer transmit a shared worldview. Instead, they circulate discrete, competing messages. No single media narrative can claim authority over others'. The 'mass media and unified systems of control' of slide 3's modernist column have given way to plural language games. Habermas (1989) is the productive contrast: the bourgeois public sphere required a shared rule of rational-critical debate; under postmodernity, no such rule survives. Trump's 'THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE' on slide 31 is what 'no final authority of the media' looks like in slogan form.

Baudrillard takes the diagnosis further. Where Lyotard remains within a post-structuralist commitment to local rules and language games, Baudrillard's hyperreality (slides 15–16) collapses the gap between sign and referent altogether. Reality and its representation become indistinguishable, and the model precedes the real. This is post-structuralism radicalised into postmodernism proper: where Barthes (1957) still held a 'behind' the myth, Baudrillard's fourth-order simulacrum has no behind at all.

Jameson on slide 22 supplies the political-economic anchor. Postmodern fragmentation is 'the cultural logic of late capitalism'. The breakdown of unified systems of control is not a free-floating epistemic event; it tracks the structural transformation of capitalism into a global, financialised, media-dominated stage. The 'fall of Communism' on slide 3 is the historical moment at which the principal grand-narrative rival to capitalism collapses, leaving capitalism itself as an unchallengeable horizon (Fisher, 2009: 2).

I conclude that the three positions form a continuous trajectory: structuralism gives the model of meaning, post-structuralism dismantles its stability, postmodernism extends the dismantling to history, politics and culture. The slide-3 / teacher-summary diagnosis of 'breakdown' is historically grounded in twentieth-century crises and is most powerfully theorised by Lyotard's language games, Baudrillard's hyperreality and Jameson's cultural logic of late capitalism. The breakdown is real, structural and contested.

### M2: "In architecture, cinema, television, video games. A breakdown of narratives of high / low culture." Discuss postmodern styles across these forms.

I argue that postmodernism is best diagnosed as a style running across architecture, cinema, television and video games, and that its core feature is the breakdown of the high / low culture distinction modernism policed. The teacher summary's heading 'Postmodern styles' (Postmodernism notes, p. 1) and the revision-doc gloss 'In architecture, cinema, television, video games. A breakdown of narratives of high / low culture' (Revision autumn and spring 2026, p. 24) supply the terms.

In architecture the postmodern style is the most visible. Strinati's catalogue runs through Philip Johnson's grandfather-clock-shaped AT&T Building in New York, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia in New Orleans, and Richard Rogers's Lloyds building in the City of London (Strinati, 1995: 216–217). Las Vegas is the popular-architectural radicalisation: 'Las Vegas has been seen as an exemplar of and inspiration for postmodern architecture (Venturi et al. 1977)' (Strinati, 1995: 217). Slide 4 supplements with a Frank Gehry-style sculptural building and a postmodern image-grid of mismatched designer fashion. The unifying feature is pastiche, mixed period signifiers stripped of historical commitment, set against the austere universalism of high-modernist architecture (the International Style). High-modernist purity has given way to playful, eclectic, polystylistic surface.

In cinema the dynamic operates through pastiche and non-linearity. Slide 37 names the iconic cases: 'Blade Runner / Pulp Fiction'. Strinati's catalogue is dense: Body Heat (1981) is 'parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling… the crime thriller of the 1940s', engaging 'retro-nostalgia' (Strinati, 1995: 218); the Rocky and Rambo franchises are 'merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality' (Strinati, 1995: 218); Blade Runner (1982) 'clearly mixes styles from different periods', so that 'we appear to be in the past, the present and the future at the same time' (Strinati, 1995: 218–219); Pulp Fiction organises its narrative as cinephilic citation with 'no central narrator / truth' (slide 40); Who Framed Roger Rabbit? (1988) mixes 'the cartoon strip and the detective story' (Strinati, 1995: 218). The Marvel Cinematic Universe radicalises the pattern as a cinematic universe of comics pastiche, monomyth and genre quotation. Art cinema and pulp genre share a single stylistic vocabulary.

In television the postmodern style is style itself. Slide 37 names Breaking Bad and Only Way Is Essex; slide 40 adds that Breaking Bad asks 'what our real identity is, and whether we even have one'. Strinati's exemplar is Miami Vice, 'heavily reliant upon style and surface' with the 'no earth tones' rule, where 'narrative is parodied and stylised' (Strinati, 1995: 220). Twin Peaks and Wild Palms extend the pattern. Severance, Black Mirror, Squid Game and Stranger Things are the contemporary inheritors. Reality TV pushes the breakdown further: 'YOU are the content' (slide 19).

In video games the postmodern style is more pronounced because the medium is pastiche-generative. The Last of Us (2023) on slide 26 stages 'hyperreal nostalgia for a pre-apocalyptic consumption space'. Fallout 76: Burning Springs (slide 24) is open-world pastiche of 1950s Americana. Open-world franchises like Grand Theft Auto construct pastiche environments quoting cinematic genres. Pop music exemplifies the breakdown in real time: 'mixing, collage constructions, reggae sound systems, rap, house, hip hop and techno' (Strinati, 1995: 222); slide 40 names Girl Talk who mashes up existing songs. Vapourwave, hyperpop and TikTok-bred genre hybrids continue this. The album is now a stylistic anthology.

The theoretical frame is concise. Lyotard's eclecticism quote (slide 9) is the postmodern style as cultural condition: 'Eclecticism is the degree zero of contemporary general culture: you listen to reggae, you watch a western, you eat McDonald's at midday and local cuisine at night, you wear Paris perfume in Tokyo and retro clothes in Hong Kong' (Lyotard, 1984). Baudrillard's hypermarket (slide 14) is the consumption-space equivalent. Jameson's pastiche-against-parody (slide 23) supplies the formal vocabulary.

I qualify the thesis. Strinati notes the high / low distinction has not disappeared so much as been re-managed: 'divide between high / low culture still remains' (slide 38), and 'popular culture has always used mixture of styles' (slide 38). The aesthetic of austerity (IKEA functionalism, Apple minimalism) survives as counter-current. But the dominant tendency is unambiguous.

I conclude that the teacher summary's list, 'architecture, cinema, television, video games', maps a real stylistic configuration whose unifying logic is the breakdown of high / low. The postmodern style is the cultural face of Jameson's late capitalism, the empirical content of Baudrillard's hypermarket, and the texture of Lyotard's incredulity towards grand narratives.

### M3: "Media no longer transmit a shared worldview… No single media narrative can claim authority over others." Discuss Lyotard's account of media with contemporary examples.

I argue that Lyotard's diagnosis on the breakdown of unified media authority is broadly correct, intensifying rather than receding under contemporary platform conditions, and is sharpest when paired with Jameson and Fisher. The teacher summary's section 'Lyotard on media' (Postmodernism notes, pp. 1–2) supplies the anchoring phrases: 'Media no longer transmit a shared worldview. Instead, they circulate discrete, competing messages. No single media narrative can claim authority over others… Media become part of a landscape of plural language games, not a source of consensus. Performativity governs media'.

Lyotard's account begins in The Postmodern Condition (1979) with the breakdown of grand narratives. If knowledge is justified not by universal truth but by 'efficiency, usefulness, and local rules' (Postmodernism notes, p. 1), media authority loses its modernist foundation. The BBC's wartime authority, the New York Times as paper of record, the Walter Cronkite anchor, all presupposed a shared worldview anchored in scientific rationality and a unified national community. Postmodernity dissolves the foundation. What replaces it is a landscape of plural language games. Performativity displaces correspondence: a message works when it satisfies a local rule, not when it tracks a universal referent.

Slide 8 renders this: 'Performativity governs media; knowledge becomes information; media supports / undermines grand-narratives; there is no final authority of the media'. The megaphone-emitting-social-media-icons image captures the proliferation.

Contemporary support is overwhelming. The fragmentation of US cable news into ideologically siloed networks (Fox, MSNBC, CNN), the rise of Substack and newsletter brands, the partisan capture of Twitter under Musk and the emergence of Truth Social all instantiate the diagnosis. No single outlet produces a consensus position on any politically charged question. The Ipsos poll on slide 31 (74% across 29 countries think AI makes fake news easier) registers audiences' own awareness that no outlet has final authority.

Slide 31's Trump quotation 'THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE' is the literal performativity Lyotard predicted. The differend (Lyotard's term for two language games meeting without a shared rule) is the structuring condition of contemporary political discourse: pro-Trump and mainstream-journalistic ecologies operate by incommensurable rules of evidence.

Slide 32's conspiracy material (Pizzagate, David Icke's Guide to the Global Conspiracy, the X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the fake 'FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston' banner) is a predictable output of Lyotard's diagnosis. In a public sphere with a shared rule these claims would be dismissed. In Lyotard's plural-language-game ecology they find communities for whom the local rule accepts them. QAnon is the radicalised case: a community with its own performativity criteria.

Platform infrastructure intensifies this. TikTok's algorithm serves personalised feeds optimised for engagement; Instagram's For You page and YouTube's recommendation engine reproduce the pattern. The platform business model is engagement, performativity in Lyotard's exact sense. Generative AI sharpens the diagnosis at the level of production: ChatGPT-authored articles, AI-generated images and deepfakes (the AI ICE image and AI CCTV-style 'evidence' on slide 31) detach outputs from any referent.

The contrast with Habermas (1989) makes the stakes precise. Habermas's bourgeois public sphere oriented rational-critical debate to consensus under shared norms; refeudalisation by advertising and PR undermined it, but the regulative ideal survived in his account. Lyotard's ecology has neither shared rule nor consensus horizon. The two diagnoses can be read as complementary stages.

I qualify the diagnosis. Some media narratives still command broad consensus. The BBC retains substantial UK authority; climate-science communication produces shared belief among non-MAGA audiences; sports broadcasting commands consensus on facts of the game. Strinati's point applies (slide 38): metanarratives 'are not in decline' so completely as Lyotard suggests. Nationalism, capitalism and market triumphalism continue with substantial authority.

I pair Lyotard with Jameson and Fisher. Lyotard explains why the ecology has no shared narrative; Jameson explains why it produces engagement-maximising content as routine output; Fisher's precorporation explains why media-literacy responses are framed by capitalist logic before they enter consciousness.

I conclude that Lyotard's account is substantially confirmed. 'No single media narrative can claim authority over others' is now infrastructural rather than philosophical, encoded in platform architectures and political ecologies. The diagnosis describes a dominant tendency, not complete displacement. The strongest position takes Lyotard seriously while pairing him with Jameson, Fisher and Habermas on substrate and stakes.

### M4: "The hypermarket creates its own spatial / temporal distortions like a satellite having gravitational pull… consumers are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption. Yet behaviour is playful and whimsical." Evaluate Baudrillard's hypermarket, including "universities as hypermarket".

I argue that Baudrillard's hypermarket is one of the lecture's most powerful conceptual tools, capturing the structural logic of contemporary consumption space and extending naturally from malls to universities, festivals, platforms and theme parks. The teacher summary's section 'Hypermarket' (Postmodernism notes, p. 2) and slide 14 supply the language: spatial and temporal distortion, satellite gravitational pull, screening and surveillance, playful and whimsical behaviour, 'universities as hypermarket'.

Baudrillard's account begins with the migration of the modern shopping centre. The hypermarket is no longer 'in the heart of a community' but 'out of the city', creating 'its own spatial / temporal distortions like a satellite having gravitational pull' (Postmodernism notes, p. 2). The migration is not incidental: it produces a new geographical and experiential space, 'new modes of living'. The hypermarket reorganises movement, schedule, sociability and consumption around itself.

The space operates by paradox. Consumers 'are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption. Yet behaviour is playful and whimsical' (Postmodernism notes, p. 2). The shopper is simultaneously a tested subject under surveillance and a playful agent. This is the key analytic contribution: the hypermarket reconciles disciplinary surveillance and consumer play. Foucault's panopticon describes pure asymmetric visibility; the hypermarket adds the invited gaze of consumption. The data trail and the playful browsing are the same operation.

This is third-order simulation as social space. The slide-14 collage (Next Home, surveillance camera, Lakeside, Download Festival as hypermarket-festival hybrid) gives the density. Lakeside is the British exemplar, an out-of-city satellite mall with gravitational pull on the Thames Gateway. Westfield Stratford and Bluewater function similarly. The festival economy reproduces hypermarket logic: peripheral location, temporal distortion, ticketed entry, surveillance, play. Coachella, Glastonbury and Download all stage the structure.

The slide's 'universities as hypermarket' turn radicalises the diagnosis. The neoliberal marketisation of higher education has converted universities into consumption spaces. Students are screened (admissions tests, NSS surveys), tested and noted (modules, completion rates, employment outcomes), and surveilled (attendance monitoring, learning-analytics platforms), while their behaviour is structured to be playful (extracurricular branding, student-experience marketing, food courts, themed accommodation). Strinati's catalogue of new 'postmodern' occupations (Strinati, 1995: 225) is the labour-market correlate. Fisher's 'business ontology' (Fisher, 2009: 17) is the same diagnosis at another angle.

I extend to four further sites. First, the airport duty-free terminal is hypermarket in pure form: temporal distortion under perpetual fluorescent day, screening and surveillance, playful consumption, branded retail. Second, the theme park: Disneyland (slide 17) is the architectural radicalisation. Third, the streaming platform: Netflix, Amazon Prime and Spotify operate hypermarket logic in digital space, users screened, surveilled and noted while browsing playfully. Fourth, the social-media platform: TikTok, Instagram and X are hypermarket-attention machines whose For You pages reproduce the gravitational structure.

The political stakes are sharp. The shopper 'screened and tested, surveilled and noted' is structurally the same subject as the reality-TV contestant for whom 'YOU are the content' (slide 19). Influencer culture and the creator economy radicalise this: the influencer is the hypermarket-subject made producer.

I qualify the thesis. Strinati's reminder that 'the ability to consume is restricted by economic and cultural inequalities' (Strinati, 1995: 228) anchors the diagnosis in materialism. Hypermarket play is unevenly distributed; the precarious worker on the Lakeside cleaning shift is surveilled differently from the consumer with discretionary spend. The hypermarket model can also flatten variety: the corner shop, farmers' market and public library operate by different logics. The diagnosis describes a dominant tendency.

The connection to Jameson and Fisher closes the analysis. The hypermarket is the spatial form of Jameson's cultural logic of late capitalism. Fisher's 'business ontology' explains why everything (schools, hospitals, universities, friendships) is now hypermarket-shaped. Baudrillard and Fisher are the same diagnosis at different scales.

I conclude that the hypermarket is one of the most powerful concepts the lecture offers. The teacher-summary phrases (spatial / temporal distortion, satellite gravitational pull, screening and surveillance, playful and whimsical) describe the structural logic of contemporary consumption space, and the slide-14 turn to 'universities as hypermarket' is the conceptual move that radicalises it. Qualified by Strinati's reminders about inequality and partial application, the concept remains decisive.

### M5: "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate… to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery." Apply this to simulation, dissimulation and political scandal.

I argue that Baudrillard's account of simulation and dissimulation, framed by the teacher summary's section 'Political scandals' (Postmodernism notes, p. 3), is the lecture's sharpest tool for analysing how political scandal operates inside hyperreality. The armed-robbery example is a structural claim, not a logical curiosity: under hyperreality the gap between pretending and doing has collapsed, so scandal cannot mark an aberration from a non-scandalous baseline because no baseline exists.

Baudrillard's definitions are precise. 'Simulation, to feign / pretend (eg pretending one is sick by making oneself sick), gap between the two collapses. Dissimulation, reveal the pretence. Political scandal is a pretence that government is not scandalous' (Postmodernism notes, p. 3). The armed-robbery example shows that simulation cannot stay within feigning: to brandish a weapon at a teller and demand money is to commit armed robbery whether one intends to keep the money or not. Simulating produces the thing simulated.

The implication for scandal is structural. Watergate appears as an aberration from legitimate government. Baudrillard argues this appearance is itself the simulation: scandal-as-aberration is the dissimulation concealing that government and capitalism are continuously scandalous. The teacher summary captures this: 'Watergate, Baudrillard argues that it is not a scandal but exists as the hyperreal to covers up that Government / capitalism itself is corrupt and a scandal, an aberration. Attempts dissimulation: an imaginary effect, like Disneyland, concealing that reality no more exists outside than inside the bounds of an artificial parameter' (Postmodernism notes, p. 3). The Watergate-as-Disneyland analogy is decisive: both demarcate an artificial zone to validate the surrounding zone as proper, when both operate by the same hyperreal logic.

Slide 18 catalogues the contemporary archive: 'Nixon resigns' front page; Trump calling Americans to join ICE; Prince Andrew with Virginia Giuffre and Ghislaine Maxwell; Epstein with Trump; Mandelson with Starmer; Keir Starmer with British military. Each image stages the deterrence-machine function. The Prince Andrew photograph operates as scandal while concealing the systemic structure of elite impunity that produced it. The Mandelson / Starmer image stages scandal of association while concealing that the political class operates through such associations routinely.

I apply the diagnosis to a contemporary case. The cycle of British political scandals over the past decade (expenses, Partygate, Owen Paterson lobbying, donor scandals, conflict-of-interest disclosures) is reported as exceptional. Outrage cycles through media attention, investigation, resignation or non-resignation, then dissipation. The Baudrillardian reading is that the cycle itself is the dissimulation. The structural condition is continuous: lobbying, donor influence, revolving-door employment and unequal access are systemic. Each scandal stages an aberration to conceal the absence of any non-scandalous baseline. Scandal is not the breakdown of legitimacy but its renewal through theatrical exception.

The armed-robbery example sharpens this. To pretend to be a corrupt politician is to be a corrupt politician inside the simulation. Trump's open transactional politics on slide 18 instantiates this. Boris Johnson's Partygate denials performed the structure: denials of rule-breaking were themselves rule-breaking insofar as they instrumentalised parliamentary statement.

The information ecology radicalises this. The fake Time cover on slide 31 is not a pretence of being on Time; it is an act of self-publication inside an ecology that consumes the fake as real. The AI ICE image and AI CCTV-style 'evidence' on slide 31 are not pretences of evidence; they are evidentiary acts. To circulate a fake 'Huge Death Camps, FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston' banner (slide 32) is to conduct a small act of political violence inside an ecology that cannot adjudicate it.

The connection to the surrounding theory closes the picture. Disneyland (slide 17) is the spatial form: it conceals that 'all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real'. Scandal is the temporal form. The Gulf War (slide 20) is the geopolitical form.

I qualify the thesis. Strinati's reminder applies: 'Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere' (Strinati, 1995: 228). Baudrillard's hyperbole risks denying that reform sometimes follows scandal: Watergate did remove Nixon; Partygate fixed-penalty notices damaged Johnson. The diagnosis describes a dominant tendency, not complete foreclosure. Habermas's regulative ideal survives in the residual operation of investigative journalism and parliamentary accountability.

I conclude that the armed-robbery example is a structural diagnosis. To simulate political legitimacy is to perform it; to simulate scandal-as-aberration is to enact dissimulation. The slide-18 archive confirms the diagnosis: each scandal is the deterrence machine that conceals the continuous scandalousness of the political-economic order. Qualified by Strinati, the diagnosis remains decisive.

### M6: "The gaze is invited and is the 'test', 'YOU are the content', the end of the panopticon… What about social media now?" Answer the slide-19 prompt.

I answer the slide-19 prompt directly. Social media now is the radicalised continuation of the structure Baudrillard diagnosed for reality television: the gaze is no longer panoptic but invited, the subject becomes the content, and visibility is performance offered to a desired audience. The teacher summary's section 'Reality TV' (Postmodernism notes, p. 4) and slide 19 supply the language: 'Real and represented are identical, they implode into one thing, no perceptual distance. The eye of TV is no longer a panoptic gaze, the gaze is invited and is the 'test', 'YOU are the content', the end of the panopticon. Media stages reality'.

The diagnosis on Reality TV terrain is precise. Big Brother (slide 19) stages contestants who volunteer for total visibility; the show's value is constituted by their performance; contestants do not pre-exist the show. The gaze is asked-for. The 'test' is whether the contestant generates content the audience desires. The panopticon is overturned because the central observer is solicited rather than hidden.

Social media now generalises this to whole populations. The Instagram feed, TikTok For You page, YouTube channel and X timeline are arenas where subjects offer themselves for visibility. The selfie is the elementary unit: an image taken for the gaze, by the subject who solicits it. The story format intensifies it as continuous performance. The reel radicalises it: short-form video designed for algorithmic distribution to strangers, where success is measured by performance metrics.

The influencer is the figure in whom the diagnosis is most complete. Slide 37 names 'new occupations (influencer)' as the structural outcome of media saturation. The influencer's feed is their life rather than its representation. Outfit, meal, gym session, friendship, romance, holiday and family event are content-events from the moment they occur, often planned around their content potential. The slide-19 prompt 'What about social media now?' has its answer here: social media now is the universalisation of the influencer structure.

The creator economy radicalises this. OnlyFans, Patreon, Substack and Twitch mean visibility is a livelihood. The Twitch streamer broadcasting hours of everyday life is panopticon-after-its-end: continuous voluntary visibility for a paying audience. Mukbang creators, ASMR creators and vlog families all instantiate the same structure: invited gaze, performative subject, content-as-life.

Baudrillard's implosion framework explains why. There is no perceptual distance between lived and broadcast life because broadcasting has become the dominant mode of living. The TikTok dance is not a representation of dancing; it is dancing-for-TikTok, a new social practice constituted by the platform. The 'photo dump' is not a documentation of a weekend; it is a weekend curated to produce the dump.

The political implications are sharp. Sign value (slides 12–13) operates through this structure: brands accumulate sign value through influencer association, influencers through brand association, audiences through identification. The hypermarket's structure (slide 14, 'screened and tested, surveilled and noted… playful and whimsical') is reproduced inside the platform.

I qualify the diagnosis. The end-of-panopticon claim is too strong if read literally. Panoptic surveillance persists alongside invited self-display. Workplace monitoring, state intelligence harvesting of platform data, school proctoring software and predictive policing all surveil panoptically. The hybrid regime, invited gaze plus persistent panoptic surveillance, is closer to truth than either alone. The user is both content-creator and surveilled data-subject.

Strinati's reminders apply (slide 38). 'Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere' (Strinati, 1995: 228). Audiences read influencer content inside multiple discourses: critical reception, parasocial identification, ironic distance. Hall's (1980) encoding/decoding restores agency: viewers can decode dominantly, negotiatedly or oppositionally.

The Habermasian contrast sharpens the stakes. Habermas's rational-critical public sphere required private individuals oriented to consensus through shared norms. The TikTok / X / Instagram subject enters as a brand-self oriented to engagement. Habermas (1989) on refeudalisation has run further: rational-critical debate has reformatted into content-economy performance. The slide-31 fake-news material is the consequence: when subjects are content-generators rather than rational-critical citizens, fake news is routine output. Slide-32 conspiracy ecologies (QAnon, Pizzagate, Icke) operate inside the same structure: communities of self-displaying subjects producing content for one another.

I conclude that the slide-19 prompt has its answer. Social media now is the radicalised universalisation of the reality-TV structure Baudrillard diagnosed. 'YOU are the content' is no longer a contestant's situation; it is the user's. The end of the panopticon is qualified by platform and state surveillance, but the dominant cultural form is the invited gaze. The influencer is the figure of the postmodern subject; the creator economy is its infrastructure.

### M7: "These produce an ahistoricism where texts become styles and history is represented through pastiche. Alternative (utopian) trajectories for a system other than capitalism collapse into mere fads, narratives, or vague fantasies." Discuss Jameson with the post-apocalyptic cycle.

I argue that Jameson's diagnosis on slide 23, repeated verbatim in the teacher summary's 'Jameson's critique of post-modernism' (Postmodernism notes, p. 4), is the lecture's most concentrated statement of the postmodern condition, and the post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 is its demonstration. Ahistoricism, pastiche and the collapse of utopian trajectories are the cultural form in which late capitalism stages its own ungraspable end.

Jameson's claim has three components. First, postmodern theory and culture are the primary form of cultural production in late capitalism across art, architecture, literature and film. Second, they produce ahistoricism where texts become styles and history is represented through pastiche. Third, alternative utopian trajectories collapse into 'mere fads, narratives, or vague fantasies'.

The first component locates postmodernism inside political economy: Jameson analyses the cultural face of Mandel's third stage of capitalism, global, financialised, media-dominated. Postmodern culture is not external to late capitalism but its self-expression. The line from Adorno and Horkheimer's culture industry (1944) is direct: the culture-industry thesis radicalises into a condition in which cultural production is shaped by financialised media corporations.

The second component identifies the mechanism. Ahistoricism converts history from sequence to stylistic option. Strinati's catalogue is concentrated: Body Heat (1981) recycles the 1940s crime thriller in 'retro-nostalgia' (Strinati, 1995: 218); the Rocky and Rambo franchises are 'merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality' (Strinati, 1995: 218); Blade Runner (1982) mixes styles 'from different periods' so that 'we appear to be in the past, the present and the future at the same time' (Strinati, 1995: 218–219); the MCU is pastiche assembly of comics history, monomyth and genre. Stranger Things, Severance and vapourwave continue this. History becomes a wardrobe of styles.

The third component is the political diagnosis. Substantive alternatives to capitalism degrade into fashion (eco-localism), narrative (utopian science fiction), or fantasy (Star Trek replicator economics). This is Fisher's capitalist realism in advance: it becomes impossible 'even to imagine a coherent alternative' (Fisher, 2009: 2).

The post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 is the demonstration. The Last of Us (HBO 2023), The Walking Dead and Fallout 76: Burning Springs form a coherent phenomenon. Slide 24: 'Our consumption of post-apocalyptic cinema and television (and video games) is a parody of our own demise'. Slide 25: 'Someone once said that it is easier to imagine the end of the world than to imagine the end of capitalism. We can now revise that and witness the attempt to imagine capitalism by way of imagining the end of the world' (Jameson, 2003: 76).

The cycle works in three ways. First, it converts collapse into entertainment, the apocalypse precorporated (Fisher, 2009: 9) as prestige content. Second, it stages capitalist relations as eternal: characters organise around scarcity, exchange, property and self-interest, rendering feral capitalism rather than non-capitalist forms. Third, the abandoned-mall sequence on slide 26 stages hyperreal nostalgia for the pre-apocalyptic consumption space, the third order at work.

This is Jameson's third component exactly. The post-apocalyptic narrative claims to imagine an alternative to capitalism but imagines capitalism continuing in degraded form. The genuine alternative, a coordinated post-capitalist mode of organising scarcity, is unimaginable. Utopian trajectories appear as fads (eco-preppers), narratives (the post-apocalyptic franchise) or fantasies (the cosy-apocalypse aesthetic).

Fisher's hauntology (slide 28) captures the affective texture. Capitalist media is figuratively haunted by ghosts of former utopias; the bust of Marx in a wintry forest is hauntology made image, and the post-apocalyptic cycle is its mourning ritual.

I qualify Jameson. First, the diagnosis can flatten variety: Strinati notes Miami Vice 'parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre' (Strinati, 1995: 220), so pastiche carries residual parodic charge. Second, Jameson sometimes treats culture as a reliable index of the economic base, underweighting the relative autonomy Hall and British Cultural Studies insist on. Third, the third component risks self-fulfilling pessimism; slide-34 'not guilty' resources retain some purchase.

Nevertheless, the thesis explains things rivals do not. It explains why post-apocalyptic cinema, television and video games are dominant; why their affect is nostalgia for the lost consumption space (slide 26); and why imagined collapse stages capitalism continuing. Westworld, Black Mirror, Severance and Squid Game all instantiate the diagnosis.

I conclude that Jameson's slide-23 diagnosis is the lecture's most powerful statement of the postmodern condition. Ahistoricism, pastiche and the collapse of utopian trajectories operate together; the slide-24–26 cycle demonstrates it; Fisher extends it. The qualifications leave the analytic core intact.

### M8: "Lyotard claims that postmodernity is defined by incredulity towards grand narratives." Discuss.

I argue that incredulity towards grand narratives is real and intensifying, but that what has replaced grand narratives is not their disappearance, rather their multiplication into competing language games with media as the privileged stage. Some grand narratives have proven resilient, but even resilient ones now operate inside the postmodern condition rather than against it.

Lyotard's claim in *The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge* (1979) is that the great legitimating stories of modernity, Enlightenment rationality, Marxist emancipation, Christian providence, the unity of science, no longer command belief. Knowledge becomes fragmented and plural, justified by efficiency and local rules rather than universal truth. Language games proliferate; performativity governs media; the university transforms from custodian of truth into producer of marketable competencies. The differend names the asymmetric situation in which two games meet without a shared rule.

Contemporary support is strong. The conspiracy material catalogued on slide 32 (Pizzagate, David Icke's *Guide to the Global Conspiracy*, the X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the fake "FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston" banner) confirms Lyotard. None of these could survive inside a public sphere with a single authoritative truth procedure; they are made possible by the absence of any shared rule for adjudication. The QAnon ecology and Truth Social register the same condition. Trump's "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE" is the literal performativity Lyotard predicted: the criterion is whether the message satisfies a constituency, not whether it corresponds to a referent. ChatGPT-authored content, AI-generated images and deepfakes intensify this by detaching outputs from any referent at the level of production.

Media confirm the diagnosis. There is no final authority because no outlet can produce a consensus position; every outlet is one game among others. The contrast with Habermas (1989) is instructive: where the bourgeois public sphere oriented rational-critical debate to consensus, Lyotard's ecology has neither shared rule nor consensus horizon. What Habermas diagnosed as refeudalisation under advertising and PR is, read through Lyotard, the moment unified deliberation gives way to plural performativity.

The eclecticism Lyotard predicted, "you listen to reggae, you watch a western, you eat McDonald's at midday and local cuisine at night, you wear Paris perfume in Tokyo and retro clothes in Hong Kong" (Lyotard), is now the default of cultural consumption. Strinati confirms it across cinema (Spielberg recycling cartoons and "B feature science-fiction"; *Body Heat*'s retro-nostalgia; *Blade Runner*'s temporal collage), pop music ("art rock" of Talking Heads, Laurie Anderson and the Pet Shop Boys (Strinati, 1995: 221–223)), and television (*Twin Peaks*, *Wild Palms*). Coachella and the festival economy stage the eclecticism as commodified ritual; *Stranger Things* and nostalgia TV layer historical signifiers without commitment to any.

I qualify the thesis in three ways. First, some grand narratives have proven resilient. Nationalism is louder than ever. Capitalism functions as an unchallengeable horizon, which is precisely Jameson's and Fisher's point. Climate science generates a robust grand narrative; identity politics generates new ones. These confirm Lyotard's diagnosis by operating as one game among others, defended polemically. Second, postmodernism risks performative contradiction: "there are no grand narratives" is itself a grand narrative. Lyotard's response is to demote his own claim to a local report, which is consistent but rhetorically fragile. Third, the loss of grand narratives may be class-specific, Western liberal elites lose faith in metanarratives while populations elsewhere continue to live inside thick religious or nationalist frames.

I push back on the political diagnosis. Postmodernism is sometimes blamed for fake news and "alternative facts". The lecture stages a "guilty/not guilty" debate (slides 30–34). The blame is misplaced: the absence of authoritative grand narratives is a precondition of the slide-32 conspiracy material, not its cause. The cause is the political economy of media saturation Strinati diagnoses, the new occupations ("influencer", advertising, marketing, journalism, television production) that produce engagement at any epistemic cost. To blame Lyotard is to confuse a diagnosis with a recommendation.

In conclusion, Lyotard's thesis is substantially confirmed by contemporary media and politics. Incredulity towards grand narratives is real, accelerating and structural. What has replaced grand narratives is a noisy proliferation of competing little narratives, language games and performances, with no shared rule to adjudicate them. The honest response is to take Lyotard seriously without nostalgia for grand narratives that were always already exclusionary, and without celebrating fragmentation in ways that hand the public sphere over to disinformation entrepreneurs.

### M9: Hyperreality and contemporary popular culture

I argue that Baudrillard's account of simulation in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981), and his concept of hyperreality, remains the most powerful lens for analysing contemporary popular culture. I demonstrate this through Disneyland, the hypermarket, reality television, the Gulf War broadcast and *The Last of Us*, and qualify the thesis by recognising that hyperreality can become a totalising frame that loses analytic edge.

Baudrillard distinguishes four orders of simulation. The third order is hyperreality: simulacra produced by simulation that mark the absence of any basic reality, where the distinction between real and model disappears and the model becomes "more real than the real" (slides 15–16; Baudrillard, 1994). Hyperreality is measured by performativity, not by correspondence with any referent. NFTs and crypto art make the point in extreme form: a sign whose value is constituted entirely by coded difference. This is the post-structuralist radicalisation of structuralism: Saussure (1916) cut the signifier from any natural referent, and Barthes (1957) showed how second-order signifying systems naturalise ideology by emptying the sign of history. Baudrillard pushes further: the sign operates as coded difference with no behind at all, so what Barthes diagnosed as bourgeois myth becomes the texture of the economy.

Disneyland is the privileged example. "Disneyland is presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest is real, when in fact all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real, but of the order of the hyperreal and simulation" (Baudrillard, 1994). It operates as a deterrence machine: by marking itself as fantasy it conceals that the rest of America is also simulation. Strinati notes that Las Vegas "has been seen as an exemplar of and inspiration for postmodern architecture (Venturi et al. 1977)" (Strinati, 1995: 217); the casino strip extends Disneyland to an entire built environment.

The hypermarket is the second case (slide 14): temporal and spatial distortion, new geographical and experiential space, consumers tested, playful and whimsical behaviour, "universities as hypermarket". Lakeside, Next Home and the Download Festival reproduce hypermarket logic, peripheral location, distortion, surveillance, playful behaviour. Shoppers are screened and surveilled while behaving playfully. This is third-order simulation as social space.

Reality television is the third case. Real and represented implode with no perceptual distance. The eye of TV is no longer panoptic; the gaze is invited; "YOU are the content" (slide 19). *Big Brother* stages reality and broadcasts the staging as the event; the contestants do not pre-exist the show. Strinati extends this to televisual flow: programmes built from "compilations and surface simulations" (Strinati, 1995: 219). *Miami Vice*, with Michael Mann's "no earth tones" rule and reliance on style and surface (Strinati, 1995: 220), is the exemplar. Influencer culture is the contemporary radicalisation: the feed is the life rather than its representation.

*The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995) is the fourth case. Slide 20 catalogues Peter Arnett in CNN Baghdad, the "Cleared by Iraqi Censors LIVE" caption, night-vision green flashes, bomb-sight crosshairs. The war's "shrinking of perspective" made it "not possible to say if it happened or not". 9/11 is the counter-pole on slide 35: the "pure event" that briefly broke the flow of hyperreality.

*The Last of Us* (2023) is the fifth case, foregrounded across slides 24–26. A video game (already simulation) is adapted into a prestige TV series whose iconic abandoned-mall sequence stages "hyperreal nostalgia for a pre-apocalyptic consumption space". *Severance*, *Squid Game* and *Black Mirror* extend the pattern. This confirms Jameson's pastiche thesis, "Our consumption of post-apocalyptic cinema and television (and video games) is a parody of our own demise" (slide 24).

I qualify the thesis. Hyperreality can become a totalising frame in which every cultural object is reduced to "simulation" and the analytic edge dulls. Strinati makes the parallel point: "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere" (Strinati, 1995: 228). Baudrillard's hyperbole risks denying the reality of Gulf War civilian deaths, which is why Jameson's materialist correction is necessary. Documentary cinema and investigative journalism continue to operate by referential procedures.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's account remains the most powerful lens for contemporary popular culture, from Disneyland to reality TV to the Gulf War broadcast and *The Last of Us*. The concept identifies a structural feature of late capitalism: signs without referents, measured by performativity, that constitute the real rather than represent it.

### M10: Jameson and the cultural logic of late capitalism

I argue that Jameson's notion of postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism is the most important corrective to Baudrillard's nihilism and Lyotard's celebratory pluralism, because it materialises postmodernism inside a specific stage of capitalist development. I defend the thesis through ahistoricism and pastiche, the "end of the world" line, the post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26, and Fisher's extension. I qualify the thesis because Jameson sometimes flattens the variety of postmodern practices.

Jameson's claim is that postmodern theory and culture are the primary form of cultural production in late capitalism. Late capitalism is Mandel's third stage, global, financialised, dominant over media and cultural production. The decisive cultural effect is ahistoricism: texts become styles; history is represented through pastiche rather than parody. The line back to Adorno and Horkheimer (1944) is direct: "cultural logic of late capitalism" extends the culture-industry thesis into a later stage where standardisation mutates into pastiche, and Adorno's (1991) point that the culture industry trains audiences to consume schemata rather than reflect on history is what Jamesonian ahistoricism radicalises.

Parody implies a normative original to mock; pastiche is blank quotation without critical edge. Strinati's textbook examples: *Body Heat* (1981) is "parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling…the crime thriller of the 1940s", engaging in "retro-nostalgia" (Strinati, 1995: 218); the *Rocky* and *Rambo* franchises are "merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality" (Strinati, 1995: 218); Spielberg's *Indiana Jones* and *Back to the Future* sequences point back to cartoons and "'B' feature science-fiction films" (Strinati, 1995: 217–218). The MCU is the contemporary radicalisation: an entire cinematic universe assembled from pastiche of earlier comics, monomyths and genre conventions. *Stranger Things* turns 1980s style into a wardrobe of period signifiers. Tarantino's *Pulp Fiction* organises its narrative as cinephilic citation. History becomes a wardrobe of styles; real historical struggle is converted into surface design.

The crucial Jamesonian line is that "it's easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism" and that this is "due to some weakness in our imaginations" (Jameson, 1994: xii). I treat this not as a complaint about pessimism but as a structural diagnosis. Late capitalism has so thoroughly colonised the imagination that the only alternatives we can picture are apocalyptic: the post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76*, *The Last of Us*) is precisely the symptom. Our post-apocalyptic media is "a parody of our own demise" that achieves what Jameson (2003: 76) calls "the attempt to imagine capitalism by way of imagining the end of the world".

Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009) extends Jameson decisively. Capitalism dominates all so that it is "impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it" (Fisher, 2009: 2, author's italics). A "business ontology" (Fisher, 2009: 17) treats everything as a corporation; the "universities as hypermarket" diagnosis (slide 14) is the Baudrillardian version. Capitalism has an "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6) and can incorporate anything, including its critique, including the apocalypse. Desires, aspirations and hopes undergo "precorporation" (Fisher, 2009: 9): they are framed by the logic of capitalism before they enter consciousness. Slide 26's abandoned mall in *The Last of Us* is the literal image: the consumption space survives as nostalgic ruin because no other social form can be imagined.

I qualify Jameson in three ways. First, the thesis can flatten variety: Strinati notes that *Miami Vice* "did not so much reject narrative as such, but rather parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre" (Strinati, 1995: 220), suggesting pastiche can carry residual parodic charge. Second, Jameson sometimes treats culture as a reliable index of the economic base. Third, "weakness of imagination" risks blaming symptom rather than cause.

Nevertheless, the thesis explains things no rival explains: why post-apocalyptic media is a dominant genre; why recent films recycle their own past; the ahistorical glamour of postmodern architecture, where Philip Johnson's AT&T tower, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia and Frank Gehry's sculptural buildings (Strinati, 1995: 216–217) stand as built versions of the thesis: history available only as style.

In conclusion, Jameson's notion of postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism is the necessary materialist anchor for postmodern theory. It rescues Baudrillard's diagnosis from nihilism by giving it political-economic ground; it rescues Lyotard's account by showing that the absence of metanarratives is itself ideological. It remains the most powerful single concept the lecture offers.

### M11: Is postmodernism responsible for fake news, conspiracy theories and alternative facts?

I argue that postmodernism is not responsible for fake news, conspiracy theories and alternative facts, but that it correctly diagnoses the conditions under which they thrive. I work through the lecture's "guilty/not guilty" framing, defend a qualified "not guilty" verdict, and conclude that the political-economic causes of disinformation lie elsewhere, in platform capitalism, state strategic communication and the collapse of trusted journalism.

The "guilty" case has real force. Postmodernism denies grand narratives, which can flatten the distinction between truth and falsity; if no narrative has authority, then conspiracy and consensus look symmetrical. It denies counter-narratives such as Marxism and so opens space for alternative political views. Polyvocality treats all voices as equal, dangerous on disinformation issues. Obtuse language prevents popular critique. Emphasis on style over substantive argument trains audiences to consume rather than evaluate. Postmodernism slides into nihilism. There is some evidence (possibly itself a leftist conspiracy) that US Cold War agencies funded postmodern art as a depoliticised alternative to Marxist critical theory.

The "not guilty" case (slide 34) is stronger. Postmodernism critiques grand narratives (nationalism, conspiracy); provides alternative modalities of critique; emphasises flow, shifting identities and agencies and "does not replace one form of authoritarianism with another"; deflates pomposity with irony; and is "explicitly named by authoritarian governments as leading to identity politics / cultural degeneration". When authoritarian regimes name a theory as the enemy, it is usually doing something right. Lyotard wrote against the grand narratives of modernity at the moment those narratives were producing their most violent twentieth-century outcomes; his target was the totalising legitimating story, not local truth practice.

The causal explanation for the slide-31 and slide-32 material (Trump's "leaks are real, news is fake", the fake *Time* cover, Pizzagate, David Icke, the X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the "FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston" banner) is political-economic, not philosophical. QAnon, Truth Social and Twitter under Musk are infrastructural conditions, not philosophical effects. Habermas's (1989) account of the structural transformation of the public sphere supplies the longer political-economic genealogy: rational-critical debate was eroded long before postmodern theory existed, by advertising, public relations and the conversion of newspapers into commercial enterprises whose authority is bought rather than argued. The fake-news ecology is the late stage of that transformation, not the consequence of incredulity towards grand narratives, and blaming Lyotard for what Habermas had already diagnosed in 1962 mistakes the diagnostic for the symptom. The structural conditions Strinati already named, "consumerism and media-saturation", "new middle-class occupations" in advertising, marketing, design, journalism and television production, and the erosion of unified identity (Strinati, 1995: 223–226), are sufficient to explain the conspiracy ecology. AI-generated imagery and deepfakes accelerate this at the level of production. None of these causal mechanisms requires postmodern theory to operate.

Baudrillard's account of dissimulation anticipates the dynamics. Watergate as hyperreal scandal conceals that "Government / capitalism itself is corrupt and a scandal" (slide 18 notes); Pizzagate on slide 32 is structurally similar, a distorted little narrative registering the absence of any authoritative big one. "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate, gives the example of simulating an armed robbery (not possible, as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery)". To circulate a fake banner about FEMA death camps is to conduct a small act of political violence inside an information ecology that cannot adjudicate it.

I qualify the "not guilty" verdict. Some postmodern intellectuals have been irresponsible in failing to defend basic referential procedures; the most extreme social-constructivist claims have given rhetorical cover to denialism. Strinati makes the related point that "implosion of reality into the media" is self-undermining: "if reality has really 'imploded' into the media, how would we know it has happened? We could only rely on the media to tell us that it had, but why should we trust them?" (Strinati, 1995: 228). The charge sheet often substitutes intellectual genealogy for the political-economic analysis Strinati already provides.

In conclusion, postmodernism is not guilty as charged. It is the diagnosis, not the disease. The political-economic causes lie in media saturation, the proliferation of new "postmodern" occupations producing engagement at any epistemic cost, and the erosion of unified identities, all of which Strinati names. Postmodernism describes the cultural soil in which these phenomena grow, but the wholesale blaming of Lyotard, Baudrillard and Jameson is a category error.

### M12: Compare and contrast Lyotard and Baudrillard on postmodern media

I argue that Lyotard and Baudrillard share a diagnosis of the breakdown of unified media authority but differ sharply on what replaces it. Lyotard's account turns on language games, performativity and proliferating little narratives; Baudrillard's on simulation, hyperreality and the implosion of representation. I work through three sites, news, reality TV and political scandal, and conclude that both diagnoses are needed.

Both agree that the modernist model of media, a unified public sphere transmitting a shared worldview anchored to an authoritative referent, has collapsed. For Lyotard the collapse is epistemological: the grand narratives that legitimated unified media authority no longer command belief. For Baudrillard the collapse is ontological: the distinction between the real and its representation has imploded, so there is no longer any referent for media to be true or false about. The genealogical relation to structuralism is informative here. Saussure's (1916) sign already cut the signifier from any natural referent, and Barthes's (1957) *Mythologies* showed how second-order signifying systems naturalise ideology by emptying signs of history. Baudrillard pushes this trajectory to its limit: where Barthes still posited an ideological "behind" the myth, Baudrillard's hyperreal sign has no behind at all. Lyotard's incredulity towards grand narratives, by contrast, retains a residual Wittgensteinian commitment to local rules and language games, which keeps his account on the post-structuralist rather than fully Baudrillardian side of the line.

The divergence produces different analyses of news. For Lyotard the news ecosystem is a landscape of plural language games. Different outlets operate by different rules of evidence and "no single media narrative can claim authority over others"; there is no final authority of the media (slide 8). Performativity governs, what matters is whether a story works. For Baudrillard the same landscape is hyperreal: news is a continuous production of events with no existence outside their broadcast. The Gulf War as experienced by audiences was an overwhelming televisual spectacle whose relation to ground events was indeterminate (slide 20).

The accounts converge on reality television. Lyotard would describe reality TV as a language game in which celebrity, intimacy and conflict operate by local rules. Baudrillard is sharper: reality TV is the implosion of real and represented, with no perceptual distance. "YOU are the content"; the eye is no longer panoptic, the gaze is invited. *Big Brother* on slide 19 stages reality as the show. I find Baudrillard more powerful here, but Lyotard explains how reality TV coexists with other media games. Influencer culture and TikTok extend the diagnosis.

On political scandal the accounts diverge again. Lyotard would treat scandal as a clash of language games: different communities apply different rules of culpability. Baudrillard's account is structural: scandal is hyperreal dissimulation, concealing the deeper truth that government and capitalism are continuously scandalous (Watergate analysis, slide 18). Simulation collapses the gap between pretending and doing: "to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery". Slide 18's collage (Nixon's resignation front page, the Prince Andrew–Maxwell–Giuffre photograph, the Mandelson–Starmer image) is the visual archive.

The accounts differ in political mood. Lyotard's tone is broadly affirmative: language games are productive, plurality desirable, the differend names injustice but also the conditions of its articulation. Baudrillard's tone is darker, sometimes nihilist: hyperreality forecloses critique because there is no referent to ground it. *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995) scandalised commentators who took the phrase literally; it is hyperbole, but captures a real condition of indeterminacy.

Both illuminate the slide-31 AI-fabricated imagery (AI-generated ICE images, fake CCTV-style "evidence"). Lyotard would describe these as performativity engines producing outputs that satisfy a constituency without claim to truth. Baudrillard would describe them as fourth-order simulacra: signs bearing no relation to any reality, pure simulation. Deepfakes and ChatGPT-authored content radicalise this. The two converge: there is no referent, only performativity.

I conclude that the two accounts should be read together. Lyotard supplies the epistemological diagnosis: language games and the absence of any meta-rule. Baudrillard supplies the ontological diagnosis: the implosion of representation. Lyotard's frame is better for analysing how different media communities coexist; Baudrillard's is better for analysing how individual media objects function. Used together they remain the most powerful pair of tools for analysing the contemporary media environment, from the Gulf War broadcast to reality TV to AI-fabricated imagery.

### M13: Disneyland and contemporary spaces of consumption

I answer this question by arguing that Baudrillard's analysis of Disneyland as third-order simulation provides the indispensable template for understanding contemporary spaces of consumption, from Las Vegas to the hypermarket to the Download Festival to the abandoned mall of *The Last of Us*. I work through the original analysis, extend it to four sites foregrounded in the lecture's own slides, and qualify it by noting that not every contemporary consumption space is fully hyperreal.

Baudrillard's analysis of Disneyland is one of the lecture's most quoted passages. "Disneyland is presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest is real, when in fact all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real, but of the order of the hyperreal and simulation" (Baudrillard, 1994). Disneyland is third-order simulation: simulacra produced by simulation, where the model is more real than the real. It operates as a deterrence machine: by ostentatiously marking itself as fantasy it conceals that the rest of America is also simulation. The lecture's parallel example is the prison: "Prisons – hide the fact that we are all incarcerated in society. Disneyland exists to conceal the fact that it is the real America" (Lecture 16 notes).

Las Vegas extends Disneyland to an entire built environment. Strinati notes that "Las Vegas has been seen as an exemplar of and inspiration for postmodern architecture (Venturi et al. 1977)" (Strinati, 1995: 217). The casino strip is the architectural realisation of the third order: each casino is a hyperreal version of an elsewhere (Paris, Venice, ancient Egypt, New York), more vivid, more themed, more consumable than the original. The model precedes and exceeds the real. This is the limit of the trajectory Benjamin (1935) opened: Vegas does not reproduce Venice, it produces a Venice whose aura was always already an effect of reproduction.

The hypermarket is the second extension, the lecture's own master case (slide 14). It has migrated "from the heart of a community" to its own out-of-city satellite zone and "creates its own spatial / temporal distortions like a satellite having gravitational pull" (Lecture 16 notes). It generates "new geographical and experiential space (and new modes of living)". Consumers passing through "are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption. Yet behaviour is playful and whimsical" (Lecture 16 notes). The slide includes Next Home, a surveillance camera, Lakeside shopping centre, and the Download Festival as hypermarket-festival hybrid: contemporary festivals reproduce hypermarket logic, peripheral location, temporal distortion, ticketed entry, surveillance, playful behaviour. The shopper is the content; the slide's parenthetical, "Universities as hypermarket", extends the diagnosis to higher education itself.

Reality television, slide 19, completes the consumption analysis. The hypermarket consumer who is screened, tested and noted is structurally the same subject as the reality-TV contestant for whom "YOU are the content". Both spaces invite the gaze and produce the subject through consumption-as-performance.

The Last of Us mall scene in the 2023 TV adaptation, foregrounded on slide 26, is a deliberate citation of the hyperreal: an abandoned mall, a Halloween-store ruin, an abandoned food court. The mall is more affectively powerful in ruin than it was in operation, which is the third order at work, and slide 26's stills function as the lecture's own image of "hyperreal nostalgia for a pre-apocalyptic consumption space".

I qualify the thesis. Not every consumption space is fully hyperreal. Strinati reminds us that consumers are not passive recipients: "the ability to consume is restricted by economic and cultural inequalities" (Strinati, 1995: 228–229). Baudrillard sometimes flattens this variety.

I also note the ethnographic point. Visitors to Disneyland are not duped. They know it is a simulation. The pleasure of the simulation is part of its appeal. Strinati makes the parallel point: "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere" (Strinati, 1995: 228). The simulation is co-produced by knowing participants.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's analysis of Disneyland is the indispensable template for understanding contemporary spaces of consumption from Las Vegas to the hypermarket to the Download Festival to the *Last of Us* abandoned mall. The third order, the model preceding and exceeding the real, is the dominant logic of these spaces. The analysis must be qualified by Strinati's reminders about audience competence and unequal access, but it remains the most powerful single concept the lecture offers for the analysis of consumption.

### M14: The Gulf War did not take place

I argue that Baudrillard's *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995) is not the absurd literal claim that nothing happened in Iraq in 1991. It is the diagnostic claim that the war as available to audiences was a hyperreal televisual spectacle whose relation to ground events was systematically indeterminate. I work through the argument, the iconography of slide 20, and the connections to Baudrillard's surrounding theses on simulation, dissimulation and reality TV.

Baudrillard's argument: "Gulf war was overwhelming strength (like a pre-programmed war), using US technology and broadcast 'live'. The war was a postmodern act to see if war was possible in the current age. Shrinking of perspective (audience were there) and imagery so not possible to say if it happened or not" (Lecture 16 notes). The audience was "there", embedded by CNN in Baghdad: slide 20 lists Peter Arnett, "Cleared by Iraqi Censors LIVE", CNN's night-vision green flashes, the German *Krieg am Golf* news, bomb-sight crosshairs, Douglas Kellner's *The Persian Gulf TV War* as the academic register. Perspective shrank to a televisual fragment whose relation to ground war was unverifiable. Baudrillard called this a postmodern act: a test of whether war was still possible, since war classically required symmetrical encounter and the production of historical meaning, both of which the asymmetric, pre-programmed Gulf operation lacked.

The title is hyperbole, not literal denial. Baudrillard's point is that the event so thoroughly imploded into its broadcast that there was no longer a referent outside it to which the broadcast could correspond. The war did not take place in the sense that it took place only as broadcast. This is slide 19's logic generalised from reality TV to war: "Real and represented are identical, they implode into one thing, no perceptual distance".

I connect the diagnosis to Baudrillard's surrounding theses. Slide 18 catalogues political scandal as hyperreal dissimulation: Nixon's resignation front page, the Prince Andrew–Maxwell–Giuffre photograph, the Mandelson–Starmer image. The Watergate analysis ("Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate, gives the example of simulating an armed robbery, not possible as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery") applies to war too. To broadcast a war is to conduct it as broadcast; the broadcast is the war's primary mode of existence for everyone outside the combat zone. The 9/11 discussion on slide 35, Baudrillard's "pure event…the revenge of the real", is the exception that proves the rule, an event that briefly broke the flow of hyperreality because its televisual scale exceeded available framing.

This does not mean nothing happened. The proper response is not to deny the dead but to insist the gap between televisual representation and ground event is structural. Investigative journalism, the war-studies tradition (Kellner) and Strinati on the limits of postmodernism hold the diagnosis open. The diagnosis intensifies in contemporary televised conflict: drone-strike footage, livestreams, AI war imagery and deepfakes extend the indeterminacy. Habermas (1989) sharpens the stakes: his regulative ideal of a rational-critical public sphere in which war could be argued against shared evidentiary norms is precisely what the Gulf War broadcast forecloses at the infrastructural level.

I qualify the thesis. Baudrillard sometimes flirts with nihilism, implying that since the war is hyperreal there is nothing to be said. Strinati is the corrective: postmodern theory exaggerates "the role of media / commercialism in forming reality" (slide 38), and "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere" (Strinati, 1995: 228). The Gulf War took place; Baudrillard's hyperbole is true at the level of media phenomenology, not ontology.

The diagnosis applies asymmetrically. State actors with broadcasting capacity benefit from hyperreal indeterminacy because they produce the televisual frame within which war is consumed. This is consistent with Jameson's reminder that postmodernism is the cultural logic of late capitalism: hyperreal war is a privilege of the side that owns the cameras.

In conclusion, *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* is not the literal claim that nothing happened but the diagnostic claim that the war as available to audiences was a hyperreal spectacle whose relation to ground events was systematically indeterminate. The diagnosis is structurally linked to Baudrillard's analyses of political scandal (slide 18) and reality TV (slide 19): all describe the implosion of real and represented. Qualified by Strinati, it remains the most powerful single diagnosis the lecture offers of war as media event.

### M15: Reality TV and the end of the panopticon

I argue that Baudrillard's claim that reality television marks the end of the Foucauldian panopticon is broadly correct: the panoptic gaze of disciplinary modernity has been replaced by an invited gaze of self-display in which "YOU are the content". I work through Foucault's panopticon, Baudrillard's revision, *Big Brother*, the influencer (slide 37), and qualify with Strinati.

Foucault's panopticon describes Bentham's prison design as a model for disciplinary modernity. The inmate is potentially always visible to a central observer who is themselves invisible. The asymmetry of visibility produces self-discipline: the inmate internalises the gaze. Slide 19 reproduces the panopticon image as the disciplinary template Baudrillard is overturning.

Baudrillard's claim is that the eye of TV is no longer a panoptic gaze. The gaze is invited; the contestants offer themselves for visibility; "YOU are the content" (slide 19). "The gaze is invited and is the 'test', 'YOU are the content' the end of the panopticon. Media stages reality". This inverts Bentham's design: the observer is distributed and explicitly addressed. The subject performs for a desired gaze rather than internalising an asymmetric one.

*Big Brother*, foregrounded on slide 19, is the chosen example: contestants explicitly volunteer for total visibility, and the show's value is constituted by their performance. The contestants do not pre-exist the show; their personalities are produced by on-camera self-display. There is no perceptual distance between real and represented. *Love Island* and reality TV more widely reproduce the pattern, as does *Squid Game* in fictionalised form: the contest spectacle whose value is constituted by self-display.

The "what about social media now?" prompt on slide 19 generalises the diagnosis. Slide 37 names the structural outcome: media saturation is "exacerbated by…new occupations (influencer)". The influencer is the figure in whom Baudrillard's diagnosis is most complete, the influencer's feed is their life rather than its representation. TikTok, OnlyFans and the broader creator economy register exactly this. Selfie culture and the streamer who broadcasts hours of everyday life are panopticon-after-its-end.

The hypermarket (slide 14) reinforces the point. "Consumers…are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption. Yet behaviour is playful and whimsical". The shopper and the reality-TV contestant are structurally the same subject: surveilled but playful, producing the content their visibility consumes. The end of the panopticon is the general logic of contemporary consumption, not just a reality-TV phenomenon.

I qualify with Strinati. Total implosion is a strong claim: "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere. Of course, if reality has really 'imploded' into the media, how would we know it has happened?" (Strinati, 1995: 228). Audiences enjoy reality TV partly because they retain critical distance from its constructions; contestants often complain that editing distorts their "real" selves. The panopticon may not have ended so much as combined with the invited gaze, producing a hybrid regime. Social-media surveillance still operates panoptically in many registers (workplace monitoring, state intelligence, platform algorithms), so Baudrillard's diagnosis describes a co-existing tendency rather than complete displacement.

The dialogical and discourse-theoretical approaches Strinati flags (Foucault on discourse, Bakhtin/Volosinov on dialogical analysis) supply the corrective. Audiences read reality TV inside multiple discourses; reception is not exhausted by the offered invitation. This restores critical agency without overturning the diagnosis. The Gramscian-Hallian corrective sharpens the same point politically: Hall's (1980) encoding/decoding model treats audience reception as a site of struggle over meaning, and the broader articulation theory drawn from Gramsci (1971) insists that consent is always re-won through everyday cultural practice rather than secured once by an invited gaze. The "YOU are the content" slogan registers a real shift but leaves the hegemonic question open, because what counts as desirable visibility is itself fought over inside reality-TV reception communities.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's claim is broadly correct at the level of phenomenology: the panoptic gaze has been replaced by an invited gaze in which subjects offer themselves as content. The slide-19 "what about social media now?" prompt extends the diagnosis from television to influencer culture. The diagnosis must be qualified by Strinati on audience competence and by the dialogical-discursive corrective: audiences still distinguish staged reality from lived reality, and panoptic surveillance persists alongside invited self-display. The diagnosis describes a tendency rather than a completed displacement.

### M16: Mark Fisher and Capitalist Realism as an extension of Jameson

I argue that Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009) is the most important extension of Jameson's diagnosis, sharpening the "end of the world easier than end of capitalism" line into a fully developed account of how late capitalism forecloses alternative imagination. I work through Fisher's key concepts, capitalist realism, business ontology, precorporation and hauntology, and apply them to contemporary case studies.

Fisher's central claim is that capitalism dominates all so that it is "impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it" (Fisher, 2009: 2, author's italics), even post-apocalypse. This operationalises Jameson's diagnosis: the cultural logic of late capitalism forecloses alternatives at the level of desire.

A "business ontology" (Fisher, 2009: 17) treats everything as if it were a corporation. Slide 27 paraphrases this: "treats everything as a corporation". Schools, hospitals, friendships are reframed in the language of brands, deliverables and stakeholder management. "Universities as hypermarket" on slide 14 is the Baudrillardian version: the knowledge institution reframed as a consumption space that screens, tests and surveils its users.

Capitalism has an "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6); it "can incorporate anything, even the apocalypse" (slide 27). The post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76*, *The Last of Us*) demonstrates this: imagined civilisational collapse reabsorbed as prestige entertainment. *Black Mirror* sells dystopia back as content; the MCU sells endless apocalypse-and-return narratives. The apocalypse is precorporated. This absorbent quality continues Adorno and Horkheimer's (1944) culture-industry tendency, and Adorno's (1991) point that pseudo-individuation supplies the illusion of difference inside standardised production anticipates precorporation.

"Precorporation" (Fisher, 2009: 9) is Fisher's sharpest concept. Desires are framed by capitalism before they enter consciousness: "Before being incorporated into our consciousness they are already framed by the logic of capitalism" (slide 27, paraphrasing Fisher, 2009: 9). The radical critique we might have produced is already neutralised at the point of formation. Strinati's catalogue of new "postmodern" occupations (advertising, marketing, design, journalism, television production: Strinati, 1995: 225) is the labour-market correlate.

Derrida's hauntology, deployed by Fisher (slide 28), captures the cultural phenomenology. Capitalist media is figuratively haunted by ghosts of former utopias. The lecture's image is a bust of Marx in a wintry forest, modernist grand narratives surviving as nostalgic residues that we keep mourning. Hauntology marks the impossibility of either fulfilling or abandoning the modernist promise. Nostalgia TV (*Stranger Things*) and the constant remake economy stage hauntology as marketable mood.

I extend Fisher to four sites. First, education: "Universities as hypermarket" is business ontology in action. Second, media saturation: the slide-37 "new occupations (influencer)" is the human face of precorporation. Coachella and the festival economy sell pre-fabricated authenticity as commodity. Third, apocalypse media: slides 24–26 enact Jameson's diagnosis by making post-apocalypse the only available imagined alternative. Fourth, the slide-26 ruined-mall sequence in *The Last of Us*: even the death of the consumption space becomes hyperreal nostalgia for it. NFTs and crypto art extend the pattern: the most radical-seeming critique-of-art-as-commodity reabsorbed as a speculative market within months.

I qualify Fisher in two ways. First, his tendency to despair: *Capitalist Realism* sometimes reads as if alternatives were genuinely impossible, which risks the political quietism it diagnoses. The slide-34 "not guilty" case lists resources for resistance (alternative spaces of critique, polyvocality, irony, the deflation of authoritarian pomposity) that Fisher's totalising diagnosis can flatten. Second, Strinati's limits apply: Fisher inherits the postmodern tendency to exaggerate "the role of media / commercialism in forming reality" (slide 38), and underattends to the persistent role of work, family and material life that Strinati's limits section flags (Strinati, 1995: 227–229).

I also note Fisher's pedagogical power. *Capitalist Realism* is short, accessible and written without postmodern obscurantism. The slide's chosen quotations from pages 2, 6, 9 and 17 are the operational vocabulary the lecture builds its account of contemporary capitalism around. The book's success is itself evidence that the diagnosis is recognised by readers as describing their lived experience.

In conclusion, Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* is the most important extension of Jameson's diagnosis. The concepts, capitalist realism, business ontology, precorporation, hauntology, give Jameson's "end of the world easier than end of capitalism" line operational purchase. The diagnosis must be qualified by the slide-34 "not guilty" resources and by Strinati's limits, but it remains the most powerful single account the lecture offers of how late capitalism forecloses the imagination of alternatives.

### M17: Pastiche, parody and postmodern culture

I argue that Jameson's distinction between parody and pastiche is one of the lecture's most analytically powerful tools, and that pastiche has indeed replaced parody as the dominant mode of contemporary culture, but not as a complete replacement; certain works recover critical parody inside pastiche-saturated environments.

Jameson's distinction is precise. Parody implies a normative original to mock; it carries critical edge because the parodist has a position from which to satirise. Pastiche is blank quotation: imitation of dead styles without critical purpose, the "wearing of a stylistic mask" in a culture that has lost the conviction that any style is normative. Slide 23 paraphrases: postmodern culture "produces an ahistoricism where texts become styles and history is represented through pastiche".

Strinati's chapter is the lecture's most concentrated catalogue of cinematic pastiche. *Body Heat* (1981) is "parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling…the crime thriller of the 1940s", engaging in "retro-nostalgia" (Strinati, 1995: 218). The *Rocky* and *Rambo* sequels are "merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality" (Strinati, 1995: 218). Spielberg's *Indiana Jones* and *Back to the Future* point back to cartoons and "'B' feature science-fiction films" (Strinati, 1995: 217–218). *Who Framed Roger Rabbit?* (1988) is postmodern through "deliberate use of distinct (cultural and technical) genres: the cartoon strip and the detective story" (Strinati, 1995: 218). Tarantino's *Pulp Fiction* organises its narrative as cinephilic citation. The MCU radicalises the pattern: an entire cinematic universe assembled from pastiche of comics, monomyths and genre conventions.

*Blade Runner* (1982), Strinati's most extended example, exemplifies how pastiche corrodes coherent time: its architecture mixes "contemporary skyscrapers" and "ancient temples", its "'street talk' consists of words and phrases taken from a whole range of distinct languages", its 'replicants' are "almost perfect simulations of human beings", and its genre is "not clear" (Strinati, 1995: 218–219). "We appear to be in the past, the present and the future at the same time".

Television confirms the pattern. *Miami Vice* (1985–1990) was "heavily reliant upon style and surface", with Michael Mann's rule "no earth tones" (Strinati, 1995: 220). *Twin Peaks* (1990–1991) and *Wild Palms* (1993) extend the pattern through surreal collage. Contemporary TV continues it: *Stranger Things* recycles 1980s style; *Severance* layers retro-corporate aesthetic with horror; *Black Mirror* parodies media conventions while remaining inside them. Strinati notes adverts now "send up or parody advertising itself by citing other adverts" (Strinati, 1995: 220–221).

Pop music exemplifies pastiche through sampling and mash-up. Girl Talk (slide 40) "creates music by mashing up existing songs". Strinati's wider catalogue includes "eclectic successions of pop and rock-'n'-roll records, mixing, collage constructions, reggae sound systems, rap, house, hip hop and techno" (Strinati, 1995: 222). Modernist pop fused influences into something new; postmodern pop maintains the separate identities of its sources. The semiotic register is illuminated by Barthes (1957): where Barthesian myth borrows a sign and naturalises ideological meaning behind it, pastiche parks borrowed signs next to one another without producing new myth, which is why pastiche feels flatter than modernist allusion.

Architecture is where Jameson and Strinati both anchor pastiche. Strinati's examples are Philip Johnson's grandfather-clock-shaped AT&T Building in New York, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia in New Orleans, and Richard Rogers's Lloyds in the City of London (Strinati, 1995: 216–217). Slide 4 extends this with a Frank Gehry-style sculptural building. Historical periods become aesthetic options rather than living traditions. The Las Vegas casino strip (Venturi) is the popular-architectural radicalisation.

I qualify the thesis. First, parody is not extinct. Strinati notes that *Miami Vice* "did not so much reject narrative as such, but rather parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre…the conventions of the gangster film were often parodied" (Strinati, 1995: 220). Pastiche can carry residual parodic charge. Second, the distinction is more analytic than empirical. Most contemporary works contain both modes; Jameson's distinction works best as a diagnostic of dominant tendency. Strinati reminds us that "popular culture has always used mixture of styles" (slide 38), so pastiche is not wholly novel.

In conclusion, pastiche has replaced parody as the dominant mode of contemporary culture, from *Body Heat*, *Blade Runner* and the *Rocky*/*Rambo* franchises to *Miami Vice*, *Twin Peaks*, *Stranger Things* and Girl Talk, and from Philip Johnson's AT&T to Frank Gehry. The replacement is not complete (residual parody persists), but the broad Jamesonian claim that contemporary culture is dominated by ahistorical pastiche is well supported.

### M18: Does postmodernism exaggerate the role of media in constituting reality?

I argue that postmodernism does sometimes exaggerate the role of media in constituting reality, and that this is the most defensible criticism of the tradition, but that the exaggeration captures a real structural feature of late modernity that other approaches miss.

The essential reading flags this charge: postmodernism exaggerates the role of media and commercialism in forming reality and rehashes Frankfurt School claims about the culture industry without acknowledging the debt. The charge is fair in three ways. First, Baudrillard's most extreme formulations, that the Gulf War did not take place, that all of America is hyperreal, risk treating media as the sole reality, denying any extra-media referent. Second, the tendency to read everything as text or discourse can flatten the material substrates of social life (labour, food, housing, bodies) into semiotic operations. Third, the methodological focus on media texts underweights ethnographic study of how audiences actually use media.

I defend a qualified version of the postmodern claim. Media do constitute reality in important senses, even if they do not exhaust it. Three case studies confirm the constitutive thesis. First, the hypermarket (slide 14): consumers "are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption" and the space generates "spatial / temporal distortions like a satellite having gravitational pull". The hypermarket is a media-constituted environment whose architecture, signage, surveillance and product display together produce the consumer subject. Lakeside and Next Home are concrete examples. Second, political scandal (slide 18): Watergate "exists as the hyperreal to cover up that Government / capitalism itself is corrupt and a scandal". Third, the Gulf War (slide 20): the broadcast constituted the war as available to audiences.

These cases show the constitutive thesis is not metaphysical idealism. It is a sociological claim that, under media saturation, important domains of social reality are constituted in and through media operations. Strinati makes the same point: "the world…will consist more and more of media screens and popular cultural images, TVs, VDUs, videos, computers, computer games, personal stereos, adverts, theme parks, shopping malls" (Strinati, 1995: 224). Reality TV, influencer culture, the festival economy (Coachella, Download), and AI-generated imagery extend the diagnosis: domains of social reality increasingly produced through media operations.

The proper response is not to abandon the constitutive thesis but to supplement it. Slide 38's bullet names the supplements: "post-structuralism (media as discourse / dialogic), cultural populism (culture as understood through anti-elitism, working class cultures)". The discourse-theoretical approach treats media as discourse rather than totalising simulation; the dialogical approach (after Bakhtin/Volosinov) restores audience agency; cultural populism foregrounds working-class uses of media.

Postmodernism shares the exaggeration with rivals: it "rehashes claims of Frankfurt School" (slide 38). The Frankfurt School, including Adorno and Horkheimer (1944), treated the culture industry as totalising; postmodern theory inherits both that strength and that weakness. Strinati and the British cultural studies tradition under Stuart Hall correct this with attention to active audiences and oppositional readings. Hall's (1980) encoding/decoding model is the operative corrective: media texts encode preferred meanings but audiences decode them dominantly, negotiatedly or oppositionally inside the conjunctures their cultural location supplies. Articulation theory, drawn from Gramsci (1971), further insists that ideological dominance is never simply imposed by media saturation but has to be won through everyday cultural practice. Read together, encoding/decoding and articulation theory show why Baudrillard's strongest implosion claim overshoots: even inside hyperreal media environments, audiences perform interpretive labour that the strong constitutive thesis underdescribes.

The most extreme version of the charge is that postmodernism denies the reality of suffering. This is sometimes levelled at the Gulf War book. The charge can be answered: Baudrillard's hyperbole captures a media-phenomenological truth without denying real deaths. But the charge is right that postmodern rhetoric sometimes courts political quietism. The corrective is Jameson's materialism and Fisher's capitalist realism, both of which retain the constitutive thesis while reconnecting it to political-economic substrates.

In conclusion, postmodernism does exaggerate the role of media in constituting reality, and this is the most defensible criticism. But the exaggeration captures a real structural feature: the increasing extent to which social reality is constituted through media operations. The proper response is to retain the constitutive thesis while supplementing it with materialist political economy, ethnographic study of audiences and recognition of the persistent importance of non-mediated experience.

### M19: Use, exchange and sign value

I argue that Baudrillard's sign value alongside Marx's use value and exchange value is the lecture's most analytically productive single concept, because it captures the dominant logic of contemporary consumer culture in a way that neither classical economics nor classical Marxism can.

Marx's distinction: use value is the qualitative satisfaction a commodity provides; exchange value is the quantitative ratio at which one commodity trades for another. Marx's labour theory of value grounds exchange value in socially necessary labour time. The two are different dimensions of the commodity.

Baudrillard's third term is sign value: the signification a commodity carries within a coded system of social difference. "The sign object is neither given nor exchanged: it is appropriated, withheld and manipulated by individual subjects as a sign, that is as a coded difference" (Baudrillard, 1994). The lecture's master example is the trainer. Slide 12 places Nike trainers next to Marx and Baudrillard. A pair of Nike Jordans has use value (running), exchange value (market price), and sign value (membership in a coded community, demonstration of taste). Sign value is increasingly dominant. The genealogy runs back through Barthes (1957), whose *Mythologies* analysed the Citroën DS and steak-and-chips as second-order signifying systems in which commodities carry ideological meanings beyond their function. Baudrillard generalises this to the entire economy: under late capitalism, the second-order signification *is* the value.

I extend the diagnosis. First, the hypermarket (slide 14): consumers are "screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption" while behaviour remains "playful / whimsical". The shopper consumes signs as much as commodities, and the data trail is itself a sign within the surveillance system. Lakeside and Next Home are concrete sites. Second, the virtual handbag on slide 11: a commodity whose use value is zero and whose exchange value depends entirely on coded difference. NFTs and crypto art are the contemporary radicalisation: pure sign value with no underlying use. Third, Disneyland and Las Vegas: branded experience inside themed environments is sign-valued consumption par excellence; the visitor pays for the sign of having been more than for any "use" of the rides. Coachella and the Download Festival operate the same logic with music as occasion. Fourth, the slide-13 sequence: "Culturally the object exists within the world of other sign objects… the media displays that a miracle may happen… media is the site of the playing out of our desires". Commodity becomes desire becomes mediated experience. Influencer marketing is the contemporary engine of this mediation.

Sign value illuminates Strinati's analysis of consumerism. Once capitalist production is established, "the need for consumption begins to grow, and people need to acquire a leisure or consumer ethic in addition to a work ethic" (Strinati, 1995: 224). The "growth of consumer credit, the expansion of agencies such as advertising, marketing, design and public relations" (Strinati, 1995: 224) is the institutional apparatus that produces sign value as the dominant mode. The new "postmodern" occupations (Strinati, 1995: 225) circulate signs.

I qualify the thesis in two ways. First, sign value does not replace use and exchange value but overlays them. The trainers still support feet; the hypermarket still sells food. The dominance of sign value is a tendency, not an exclusive principle. Strinati notes "the ability to consume is restricted by economic and cultural inequalities" (Strinati, 1995: 228), which materially anchors any sign system. Second, sign value is anchored in real social hierarchies. The new occupational class "exert an important influence over other people's life-styles and values" and their "quest for cultural power leads them towards postmodernism and away from the cultures of other classes" (Strinati, 1995: 226). Coded differences are the distinctions of occupational class fractions.

The political stakes. The dominance of sign value means contemporary consumption is increasingly difficult to evaluate by need. Strinati flags this: postmodern theory "fails to recognise how useful the commodities which people buy are for them" (Strinati, 1995: 228). A serious analysis must hold sign value and use value together.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's sign value alongside Marx's use and exchange value is the lecture's most analytically productive single concept. The dominance of sign value is the structural feature of late capitalist consumption, anchored by the slide-11/12/13 trainer sequence and extended by the hypermarket diagnosis. Qualified by Strinati on material inequality and the persistent usefulness of commodities, the concept remains the most powerful single tool the lecture offers for the analysis of consumption.

### M20: Liberation or nihilism?

I argue that the breakdown of grand narratives is neither uniquely liberating nor uniquely nihilist but produces a contested terrain whose political valence depends on what fills the vacuum. I work through both sides via the slide-33/34 "guilty/not guilty" debate.

The liberation case (slide 34). Postmodernism "critiques grand narratives (nationalism / conspiracy)"; it "provides alternative spaces / modalities of critique"; it is polyvocal; it emphasises "flow, shifting identities and agencies" and "does not replace one form of authoritarianism with another"; it "deflates pomposity with irony"; it is "explicitly named by authoritarian governments as leading to identity politics / cultural degeneration". Grand narratives have historically been exclusionary. Lyotard's critique was aimed at the legitimating stories of modernity, the Enlightenment rationality, Marxism and Christianity Strinati lists (Strinati, 1995: 215) as ideas that "make absolute, universal and all-embracing claims to knowledge and truth". The polyvocality slide 34 celebrates is the precondition of voices that the modernist consensus silenced. The postcolonial argument runs parallel: Said (1978) showed in *Orientalism* that the apparently universal grand narratives of Enlightenment scholarship were, in their dominant form, a discursive apparatus through which Europe produced and dominated the Orient as its constitutive other. Lyotard's incredulity towards grand narratives, read alongside Said, is liberating in precisely this sense: the universalist claims of modernity were never as universal as they presented themselves to be, and dismantling them opens space for the subaltern voices the consensus excluded.

The nihilism case (slide 33). Postmodernism "denies grand narratives, all truth is relative so all theories of equal merit"; it opens space for "alterative political views / facts"; it "promotes plurality / polyvocal, therefore all voices are equal"; "obtuse language prevents a popular critique from forming"; it is "more about style than substantive arguments"; it is "nihilist, denies meaning and is destructive". There is some evidence that "postmodern art / culture was funded by US Government as an alternative to more radical forms of critical theory / Marxism". Slide 32's conspiracy catalogue (Pizzagate, David Icke, the 2024-election X post, the FEMA-death-camps banner) is the public-sphere consequence: a polyvocality in which no game has authority and the worst little narratives proliferate. QAnon and "alternative facts" are the contemporary radicalisation.

I work through the lecture's examples. First, slide 32's conspiracy cluster: the breakdown is conditionally nihilist, because conspiracy little narratives substitute for vanished big ones without shared rule for adjudication. Second, postmodern architecture (slide 4, Strinati on Philip Johnson's AT&T, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia, Richard Rogers's Lloyds, Frank Gehry): the breakdown is unambiguously liberating, allowing for "highly ornate, elaborately designed, contextualised and brightly coloured buildings; a stress on fictionality and playfulness; and the mixing of styles drawn from different historical periods" (Strinati, 1995: 216). Third, postmodern cinema and television (slide 37, Strinati on *Blade Runner*, *Pulp Fiction*, *Breaking Bad*, *Miami Vice*, *Twin Peaks*; *Severance*, *Squid Game*, *Black Mirror* extend the line): the breakdown allows for non-linearity, multiple narratives, pastiche, ambiguity, none intrinsically nihilist. Fourth, slides 11–14 consumption: consumers are "playful and whimsical" but also "screened and tested, surveilled", an ambivalent condition.

The breakdown of grand narratives is liberating in some domains and nihilist in others. The slide-33/34 framing leaves the question productively open.

I qualify further with Strinati. The dichotomy may be misleading because the premise (that grand narratives are in decline) is contestable. Postmodernism's claim "that metanarratives are in decline" is self-undermining: "what is postmodernism, after all, if it is not another metanarrative?…If it is indeed another metanarrative, how can metanarratives be in decline?" (Strinati, 1995: 228–229). Slide 38 formalises this: "Meta-narratives are not in decline". The operative question may be which grand narratives are dominant and to whose benefit.

The slide-34 resources of critique (alternative modalities, polyvocality, irony, the deflation of authoritarian pomposity) supply the answer to the nihilism charge. The postmodern condition does not preclude truth practices; it relocates them inside specific language games and local communities of inquiry.

In conclusion, the breakdown is neither uniquely liberating nor uniquely nihilist. It produces a contested terrain whose political valence depends on what fills the vacuum, with the slide-32 conspiracy cluster as the nihilist symptom and the postmodern aesthetic resources of slides 4 and 37 as the liberating ones. Strinati's "Meta-narratives are not in decline" qualifies the premise, and the slide-34 resources answer the nihilism charge.

### M21: Apply postmodern theory to a current media event

I take as my current media event the slide-31 cluster on fake news and AI-generated imagery: Trump's "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE", the fake *Time* Magazine cover, the AI-generated ICE image with wrong-uniform and missing-windows annotation, and the AI-generated CCTV-style "evidence" images. I work through Lyotard, Baudrillard and Jameson in turn, draw on slide-35's "pure event" prompt, and conclude with a synthesis.

Lyotard's lens is sharpest on the epistemological dimension. The slide-31 cluster presupposes the absence of any authoritative grand narrative about what counts as evidence. In a media ecology with a shared rule, fake covers and AI photographs would be quickly identified and dismissed. In the postmodern condition, "no single media narrative can claim authority over others" and "Media supports / undermines grand-narratives" (slide 8). Audiences inhabit plural language games and know it. Performativity governs: the image works if it satisfies a constituency, regardless of provenance. ChatGPT-authored content and deepfakes intensify the diagnosis at the level of production.

Fact-checking will not eliminate the cluster, because it operates inside one language game (mainstream journalism) while the fakes operate inside others (Truth Social, Twitter under Musk). The games meet only as differend; fact-checks are read in partisan networks as further evidence of mainstream conspiracy. "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE" is the differend made slogan. Habermas's (1989) regulative ideal of a rational-critical public sphere is what the cluster forecloses, since shared evidentiary norms have been dismantled by platform capitalism.

Baudrillard's lens is sharpest on the ontological dimension. The AI-generated ICE image and the AI CCTV-style "evidence" are fourth-order simulacra: signs that "bear no relation to any reality whatsoever; pure simulacrum" (slide 15). The Swedish Fish Oreos figure of slide 16 is the lecture's own miniature image of the fourth order. Deepfakes radicalise this further: a sign so detached from any referent that the question of original and copy becomes incoherent.

Baudrillard's analysis of dissimulation applies sharply. "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate, gives the example of simulating an armed robbery, not possible as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery". The fake *Time* cover and fabricated CCTV are not a pretence of evidence; they are evidentiary acts inside the information ecology that consumes them. The gap between simulating and doing has collapsed.

Slide 35's "pure event" prompt sharpens the analysis. Baudrillard called 9/11 a "pure event…the revenge of the real", an event that temporarily broke the flow of hyperreality. The slide-31 cluster is the opposite: not a pure event but the steady-state hyperreality 9/11 briefly interrupted. Pure events are rare; most of the time, the media environment is dissimulation.

Jameson's lens is sharpest on the political-economic dimension. The cluster is the predictable output of late capitalism. Slide 22: "in this phase of capitalism, capital comes to dominate media and cultural production, and hence the sphere of ideas". Media saturation produces fake-news outputs as routine product; audiences know it.

Fisher's precorporation applies. The responses (watermarking, media-literacy education, individual scepticism) are framed by capitalist logic before they enter consciousness; structural alternatives are precorporated out. The "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6) is visible in how the fake-news ecology itself becomes content, news about fake news, podcasts about misinformation, reaction videos to viral fakes, all of which sustain rather than disrupt the system.

The synthesis. Lyotard explains why the cluster succeeds: there is no authoritative game to adjudicate it. Baudrillard explains what it is: fourth-order simulacra whose simulation is the event. Jameson and Fisher explain why it proliferates: late capitalism's domination of media production yields engagement-maximising fakes as routine output and precorporates the political imagination that could regulate them.

The proper response combines all three. Lyotard suggests building local truth practices that earn authority through method. Baudrillard suggests media-phenomenological literacy: teaching audiences that fourth-order simulacra exist. Jameson and Fisher suggest political-economic intervention: democratic constraints on cultural production. Together they constitute a postmodern response to a postmodern crisis.

### M22: Postmodernism as truth of late capitalism, not its critique

I argue that this is Jameson's deepest insight: postmodernism is the truth of late capitalism rather than its critique, because postmodernism describes the cultural logic that late capitalism actually produces. I distinguish strong and weak versions, defend the strong version, and conclude with a qualification about the possibility of critique inside the postmodern condition.

The thesis is that postmodernism is not external to late capitalism but its characteristic self-expression. Postmodern fragmentation, polyvocality, irony, pastiche and incredulity towards grand narratives are the cultural logic produced by global financialised capitalism. Lyotard's language games are capitalism's dissolution of shared frameworks; Baudrillard's hyperreality is capitalism's production of sign value. The line back to Adorno and Horkheimer (1944) is direct: their culture-industry thesis already held that the apparent autonomy of art and theory under monopoly capitalism was an effect of the system itself, and Jameson's strong thesis updates the claim for the financialised stage.

The weak version is that postmodernism happens to coincide historically with late capitalism. The strong version is that postmodernism is structurally produced by late capitalism. I defend the strong version. The end of Bretton Woods, deregulation of finance, de-industrialisation of the global North, the rise of service and information economies, financialisation of everyday life, these produced the social conditions in which grand narratives collapse, language games proliferate and sign value displaces use value. Lyotard, Baudrillard and Jameson theorise the social form of which they are themselves the cultural symptoms.

The Lyotardian objection is that postmodernism contains a critical edge, as the slide-34 "not guilty" case maintains. I grant the edge but argue it is recuperable. Fisher's "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6) explains why: late capitalism "can incorporate anything, even the apocalypse" (slide 27). Polyvocal celebration of difference, ironic deflation of authoritarian pomposity, and the postmodern aesthetic itself all become marketable identities. Slides 24–26's post-apocalyptic cycle (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76*, *The Last of Us*) illustrates: imagined civilisational critique sold back as prestige entertainment. NFTs and crypto art radicalise this: even the avant-garde gesture against art-as-commodity becomes a speculative market.

The strong version explains things no rival explains. It explains why postmodern theory was so quickly adopted by the academy: universities were being marketised, and postmodernism was the cultural form that legitimated that marketisation. It explains why postmodern aesthetics (pastiche, irony, surface, sampling) became dominant in advertising and entertainment, the MCU and *Stranger Things* are saturated with these forms because they are perfectly adapted to commodity culture. It explains why postmodernism's political effects were weak relative to its theoretical sweep: the theory had no leverage because it was the system's own self-expression.

I qualify the thesis in two ways. First, the strong version risks reductionism. Strinati notes that *Miami Vice* "did not so much reject narrative as such, but rather parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre" (Strinati, 1995: 220); the slide-34 resources (irony, polyvocality, alternative modalities of critique) retain some purchase. The thesis identifies a dominant tendency, not complete capture. Second, the thesis must allow for critique inside the postmodern condition, or it slides into Fisher's despair. Slide 28's hauntology suggests a way out: capitalist media is haunted by ghosts of former utopias, and mourning those utopias can be the seed of new political imagination.

The deepest provocation: if postmodernism is the truth of late capitalism, can it also be its critique? My answer is conditional. Postmodernism can be critique only when supplemented by political-economic analysis (Jameson, Fisher), by Strinati's materialist limits, and by the dialogical and cultural-populist correctives slide 38 names. Postmodernism alone, without these supplements, cannot critique what it is.

The meta-irony: to call postmodernism the truth of late capitalism is itself a grand narrative. The claim performs the contradiction it diagnoses. The postmodern condition does not preclude theoretical work; it requires self-awareness about its conditions of possibility.

In conclusion, postmodernism is the truth of late capitalism rather than its external critique. Its theoretical forms describe the cultural logic late capitalism actually produces. The strong thesis explains the academy's adoption of postmodernism, the dominance of postmodern aesthetics in commodity culture, and postmodernism's political weakness. Qualified by the slide-34 resources and the dialogical and cultural-populist supplementations slide 38 names, the thesis remains decisive. The proper response is to take postmodernism seriously as the truth of our condition without surrendering the effort to think against it.
