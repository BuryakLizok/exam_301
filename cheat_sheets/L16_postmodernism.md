# L16: Postmodernism (Lyotard, Baudrillard, Jameson)

## §1 Lecture overview

This lecture maps the shift from modernity to postmodernity through three primary theorists, Jean-François Lyotard, Jean Baudrillard and Fredric Jameson, and locates contemporary popular culture inside that diagnosis. Modernism in this account names a confidence in "grand narratives": Enlightenment rationality, Marxist emancipation, Christian providence, scientific progress, the mass media as a unified public sphere, and a single epistemological centre that grounds truth. Postmodernism is the breakdown of this confidence under the pressure of war, the fall of Communism, accelerating technology, ubiquitous media, and the saturation of consumer life. The lecture stresses that postmodernism is not just a periodisation but a condition (Lyotard), a regime of signs (Baudrillard) and a cultural logic of late capitalism (Jameson).

The lecture begins with the relational map between structuralism (single stable meaning, objective reality, an epistemology of sign and referent), post-structuralism (meaning unstable, multiple truths, socially constructed reality, no universal truth) and post-modernism (after modernity, encompassing many movements including post-structuralism, oscillating between resistance and nihilism). It then unpacks Lyotard's report on the postmodern condition (1979), focusing on the end of grand narratives, the rise of language games and performativity, the fragmentation of knowledge into local narratives, and the transformation of the university. The famous slide quotes Lyotard on "eclecticism" as "the degree zero of contemporary general culture: you listen to reggae, you watch a western, you eat McDonald's at midday and local cuisine at night, you wear Paris perfume in Tokyo and retro clothes in Hong Kong" (Lyotard).

Baudrillard's account of simulation in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981) is then introduced through three vectors: the move from Marx's use and exchange value to sign value ("the sign object is neither given nor exchanged: it is appropriated, withheld and manipulated by individual subjects as a sign, that is as a coded difference"); the hypermarket as a site of symbolic exchange that produces spatial and temporal distortion, surveils consumers and yet hosts playful behaviour; and the four orders of simulation, peaking in third-order hyperreality where the distinction between the real and the model dissolves. The slides apply this to Disneyland ("presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest is real, when in fact all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real, but of the order of the hyperreal and simulation"), to political scandals such as Watergate as hyperreal dissimulation, to *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995), and to reality television as the implosion of real and represented, the death of the panoptic gaze and the gospel that "YOU are the content".

Jameson's notion of postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism is then introduced, with his sharp diagnostic that "it's easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism" and that postmodern culture produces an ahistoricism in which texts become styles and history is represented through pastiche. The lecture closes with Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009), the concept of precorporation, Derrida's hauntology as deployed by Fisher, the debate over whether postmodernism is "guilty" or "not guilty" of producing fake news, conspiracy theories and alternative facts, and the essential reading's stocktake of postmodernism in cinema (*Blade Runner*, *Pulp Fiction*), television (*Breaking Bad*, *Only Way Is Essex*), and pop music (mash-ups, sampling).

## §2 Lyotard: the postmodern condition and language games

I treat Lyotard's *The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge* (1979) as the founding text of the lecture. Lyotard's central thesis is condensed in a sentence the slides paraphrase: postmodernity is defined by "a growing disbelief in 'grand narratives'" (Lyotard, 1984: [VERIFY PAGE?]). Grand narratives, sometimes called metanarratives, are the legitimating stories of modernity: Enlightenment rationality (knowledge frees humanity), the Hegelian-Marxist dialectic (history culminates in emancipation), Christian eschatology (history culminates in salvation), the hermeneutic of meaning (the spirit grounds truth). Postmodernity is the condition in which these stories no longer command belief, not because anyone has proved them false, but because the social, technological and economic infrastructure that supported them has fractured.

In their place Lyotard puts language games (a term he borrows from Wittgenstein) and performativity. Knowledge is no longer justified by reference to a universal foundation; it is justified by efficiency, usefulness and local rules, by whether it works inside a given game. Performativity governs media. Knowledge becomes information. The university, the lecture insists, is transformed by this shift: it becomes a producer of marketable skills rather than a custodian of truth, and disciplines fragment into local specialisms whose languages are mutually opaque. The differend names the situation in which two language games meet without a shared rule that could adjudicate between them, so a wrong is done that cannot be expressed inside the rules of the dominant game.

For media, Lyotard's diagnosis is precise. The media no longer transmit a shared worldview. They circulate discrete, competing messages. No single media narrative can claim authority over others. There is no final authority of the media. Media simultaneously support and undermine grand narratives, sometimes within the same broadcast cycle. The famous "eclecticism is the degree zero of contemporary general culture" passage is Lyotard's most quotable diagnosis: consumption itself becomes a montage of references unmoored from any local tradition.

I apply this directly to the cases the slides foreground. The conspiracy material on slide 32 (Pizzagate, David Icke's *Guide to the Global Conspiracy*, the X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the fake "Huge Death Camps, FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston" banner) is not an aberration from a healthy public sphere but a rational symptom of the absence of any authoritative grand narrative; little paranoid narratives substitute for vanished big ones. Slide 31's Trump quotation, "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE", makes sense inside a Lyotardian frame: when no shared rule adjudicates truth, each language game produces its own facts and accuses the others of fabricating theirs. The Ipsos poll on slide 31 (74% across 29 countries think AI makes fake news easier) is itself a survey of audiences who recognise that no media outlet has final authority.

Critics of Lyotard insist that grand narratives have not died at all, nationalism, capitalism itself, scientific naturalism, identity politics, climate science, neoliberal market triumphalism are all alive and authoritative. I take this critique seriously, but I argue with the slides that even where grand narratives persist they have lost their unchallenged legitimating force; they now circulate as one option among others, defended polemically rather than presupposed.

## §4 Jameson, Fisher and the cultural logic of late capitalism

Fredric Jameson reframes postmodernism as a periodising concept tied to a stage of capitalism. Postmodernism is "the cultural logic of late capitalism", late capitalism here meaning Ernest Mandel's third stage of capitalist development, after market and monopoly capitalism, in which capital becomes truly global, financialised and dominant over media and cultural production. Postmodern theory and culture are the primary form of cultural production in this phase, across art, architecture, literature and film.

Their characteristic effect is ahistoricism. Texts become styles; history is represented through pastiche rather than parody (parody implies a normative original to mock, pastiche is blank quotation without critical edge). Strinati's catalogue makes the point concretely: *Body Heat* (1981) is "parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling…the crime thriller of the 1940s" in what Strinati calls "retro-nostalgia" (Strinati, 1995: 218); the *Rocky* and *Rambo* sequels "go no further than recycling the recent past, making films which are merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality" (Strinati, 1995: 218); *Blade Runner* (1982) "clearly mixes styles from different periods", so that "we appear to be in the past, the present and the future at the same time" (Strinati, 1995: 218–219). *The Last of Us* (2023), foregrounded in slides 24–26, converts a video game into a prestige TV pastiche of post-apocalyptic cinema. Alternative trajectories for systems other than capitalism collapse into mere fads, narratives or vague fantasies.

Jameson's most quoted line, deployed by the slides, is that "it's easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism" and that this asymmetry "is due to some weakness in our imaginations" (Jameson, 1994: xii). Our consumption of post-apocalyptic cinema, television and video games, *The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76*, *The Last of Us*, is a parody of our own demise that has the effect of imagining capitalism by way of imagining the end of the world (Jameson, 2003: 76).

Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009) sharpens this further. Capitalism dominates all so that it is "impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it" (Fisher, 2009: 2, author's italics), even post-apocalypse. A "business ontology" (Fisher, 2009: 17) treats everything, schools, hospitals, friendships, as if it were a corporation. Capitalism has an "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6); it can incorporate anything, including its own critique, including the apocalypse. Our desires, aspirations and hopes undergo a process of "precorporation" (Fisher, 2009: 9): before they enter our consciousness they are already framed by the logic of capitalism. Resistance is therefore not censored but pre-emptively absorbed.

Fisher couples this with Derrida's concept of hauntology. Capitalist media is figuratively haunted by the ghosts of former utopias and theorisations: we cannot escape the modernist grand narratives because we keep mourning them. The slide's bust of Marx in a wintry forest is precisely this hauntology made image. The cycle of post-apocalyptic media on slides 24–26 (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76: Burning Springs*, *The Last of Us*) is, for Fisher and Jameson together, the cultural form in which late capitalism stages its own ungraspable end.

## §5 The fake-news debate and the limits of postmodernism

The slides stage an explicit "guilty/not guilty" debate. The case against postmodernism is that by denying grand narratives it makes all truth relative, opens space for alternative political views and counter-narratives, promotes a polyvocal stance in which all voices appear equal, hides behind obtuse language that prevents popular critique, prioritises style over substantive argument and slides into nihilism. There is some evidence, the slides note with healthy scepticism, that US government agencies funded postmodern art and culture as a Cold War alternative to more radical forms of critical theory and Marxism, though this may itself be a "left" conspiracy theory. The case for postmodernism is that it critiques grand narratives precisely as the vehicles of nationalism and conspiracy, provides alternative modalities of critique, emphasises shifting identities and flows rather than authoritarian closure, deflates pomposity with irony, and is explicitly named by authoritarian governments as leading to identity politics and cultural degeneration, which suggests it is doing something right.

The essential reading flags the limits of postmodernism in explaining media. It exaggerates the role of media and commercialism in forming reality; it rehashes Frankfurt School claims about the culture industry without acknowledging the debt; metanarratives are not actually in decline (nationalism, market triumphalism, climate science); the high/low culture divide persists; and popular culture has always used mixtures of styles, so pastiche is nothing new. The reading suggests these limits can be overcome by bringing postmodernism into dialogue with post-structuralism (media as discourse and dialogic) and cultural populism (anti-elitist working-class cultures).

## §8 Full mock essays

### Q1: "Lyotard claims that postmodernity is defined by incredulity towards grand narratives." Discuss.

I take Lyotard's report on the postmodern condition (1979) as the founding diagnosis of the lecture and answer the question by arguing that incredulity towards grand narratives is real and intensifying, but that what has replaced grand narratives is not their disappearance but their multiplication into competing language games, with media as the privileged stage. I qualify the thesis by showing that some grand narratives have proven remarkably resilient, but I conclude that even resilient grand narratives now operate inside the postmodern condition rather than against it.

Lyotard's claim, condensed in *The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge* (1979), is that the great legitimating stories of modernity, Enlightenment rationality, Marxist emancipation, Christian providence, the unity of science, no longer command belief. Knowledge becomes fragmented and plural, justified by efficiency and local rules rather than universal truth. Language games proliferate; performativity governs media; the university transforms from a custodian of truth into a producer of marketable competencies. The differend names the asymmetric situation in which two games meet without a shared rule.

I find strong contemporary support for this thesis. The conspiracy material catalogued on slide 32 (Pizzagate protestors, David Icke's *Guide to the Global Conspiracy*, an X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the fake "Huge Death Camps, FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston" banner) all confirm Lyotard. None of these phenomena could survive inside a public sphere with a single authoritative truth procedure; they are made possible by the absence of any shared rule for adjudication. Slide 31's Trump quotation, "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE", together with the *Washington Post* report on Trump's fake *Time* Magazine cover, are the literal performativity that Lyotard predicted: the criterion is whether the message satisfies a constituency, not whether it corresponds to a referent.

Media confirm the diagnosis structurally. The Ipsos finding on slide 31 that 74% across 29 countries think AI makes fake news easier registers what Lyotard already diagnosed: there is no final authority of the media because no media outlet can produce a consensus position; every outlet is one game among others. Media supports and undermines grand narratives, sometimes within the same broadcast cycle (Lecture 16, slide 8).

The eclecticism Lyotard predicted, "you listen to reggae, you watch a western, you eat McDonald's at midday and local cuisine at night, you wear Paris perfume in Tokyo and retro clothes in Hong Kong" (Lyotard), is now the default of cultural consumption. The Strinati catalogue confirms it across cinema (Spielberg's *Indiana Jones* and *Back to the Future* recycling cartoons and "B feature science-fiction"; *Body Heat*'s 1940s crime-thriller retro-nostalgia; *Blade Runner*'s temporal collage), pop music (the "open and extensive mixing of styles and genres" Strinati locates in mash-ups, remixing, reggae sound systems, rap, house, hip hop, techno, and the "art rock" of Talking Heads, Laurie Anderson and the Pet Shop Boys (Strinati, 1995: 221–223)), and television (the surreal collage of *Twin Peaks* and *Wild Palms*). Consumption has lost any local-traditional anchor exactly as Lyotard's quotation predicts.

I qualify the thesis in three ways. First, some grand narratives have proven resilient. Nationalism is louder than ever. Capitalism functions as an unchallengeable horizon, which is precisely Jameson's and Fisher's point. Climate science generates a robust grand narrative about the future of the species. Identity politics generates new grand narratives about justice. These narratives do not undermine Lyotard's diagnosis, they confirm it by operating as one game among others, defended polemically. Second, postmodernism risks performative contradiction: the claim "there are no grand narratives" is itself a grand narrative. Lyotard's response is to demote his own claim to a local report rather than a universal truth, which is consistent but rhetorically fragile. Third, the loss of grand narratives may be a class-specific phenomenon, Western liberal elites lose faith in metanarratives while populations in other parts of the world continue to live inside thick religious or nationalist frames.

I also push back on the political diagnosis. Postmodernism is sometimes blamed for fake news and conspiracy theories. The lecture stages this as a "guilty/not guilty" debate (slides 30–34). I find the blame misplaced: the disappearance of authoritative grand narratives is a precondition of the Pizzagate-style conspiracy material on slide 32, but not its cause. The cause is the political economy of media saturation that Strinati already diagnoses, the new occupations (the slide's "influencer", Strinati's "advertising, marketing, design, architecture, journalism and television production") that have an interest in producing engagement at any epistemic cost. To blame Lyotard for the slide-32 conspiracy cluster is to confuse a diagnosis with a recommendation.

In conclusion, I find Lyotard's thesis substantially confirmed by contemporary media and politics. Incredulity towards grand narratives is real, accelerating and structural. What has replaced grand narratives is not silence but a noisy proliferation of competing little narratives, language games and performances, with no shared rule to adjudicate them. The most intellectually honest response is to take Lyotard's diagnosis seriously without retreating into nostalgia for grand narratives that were always already exclusionary, and without celebrating fragmentation in ways that hand the public sphere over to disinformation entrepreneurs.

### Q2: Hyperreality and contemporary popular culture

I answer this question by arguing that Baudrillard's account of simulation in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981), and specifically his concept of hyperreality, remains the single most powerful lens for analysing contemporary popular culture. I demonstrate this through five case studies, Disneyland, the hypermarket, reality television, the Gulf War as broadcast spectacle, and *The Last of Us*, and I qualify the thesis by recognising that hyperreality can become a totalising frame that loses analytic edge.

Baudrillard distinguishes four orders of simulation. The third order is hyperreality: simulacra produced by simulation that mark the absence of any basic reality, where the distinction between the real and the model disappears and the model becomes "more real than the real" (Lecture 16, slides 15–16; Baudrillard, 1994). Hyperreality is measured by performativity, how well it operates, not by correspondence with any referent.

Disneyland is Baudrillard's privileged example. "Disneyland is presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest is real, when in fact all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real, but of the order of the hyperreal and simulation" (Baudrillard, 1994: [VERIFY PAGE?]). Disneyland operates as a deterrence machine: by marking itself as fantasy it conceals that the rest of America is also simulation. Strinati notes that Las Vegas, in the architectural debate, "has been seen as an exemplar of and inspiration for postmodern architecture (Venturi et al. 1977)" (Strinati, 1995: 217); the casino strip extends Disneyland to an entire built environment of third-order simulation.

The hypermarket is the second case. The lecture's slide 14 catalogues its features: temporal and spatial distortion, triage centres, new geographical and experiential space, consumers tested, playful and whimsical behaviour, hyperreality, "universities as hypermarket". The Westfield-style centre has migrated from the heart of the city to its own peripheral satellite zone, generating spatial distortion "like a satellite having gravitational pull" (Lecture 16 notes). Shoppers are screened, tested and surveilled while behaving playfully. This is third-order simulation as social space.

Reality television is the third case. Real and represented implode into one thing with no perceptual distance. The eye of TV is no longer a panoptic gaze; the gaze is invited; "YOU are the content" (Lecture 16, slide 19). *Big Brother*, foregrounded in slide 19, stages reality and broadcasts the staging as the event. The contestants do not pre-exist the show; their personalities are constituted by their on-camera performance. Strinati's chapter extends the phenomenology to the wider televisual flow: television's "regular daily and night-time flows of images and information bring together bits and pieces from elsewhere, and create its sequences of programmes on the basis of compilations and surface simulations" (Strinati, 1995: 219). *Miami Vice*, with Michael Mann's instruction "no earth tones" and its visual reliance on style and surface (Strinati, 1995: 220), is exemplary of this hyperreal televisual register.

*The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995) is the fourth case. Slide 20 catalogues the imagery: Peter Arnett embedded in CNN Baghdad, the "Cleared by Iraqi Censors LIVE" caption, CNN's night-vision green flashes, German *Krieg am Golf* news, bomb-sight crosshairs. The war was "overwhelming strength (like a pre-programmed war), using US technology and broadcast 'live'" (Lecture 16 notes); the "shrinking of perspective (audience were there) and imagery" made it "not possible to say if it happened or not". The war was a "postmodern act to see if war was possible in the current age" (Lecture 16 notes).

*The Last of Us* (2023) is the fifth case, foregrounded across slides 24–26 of the lecture. A video game (already simulation) is adapted into a prestige TV series whose iconic abandoned-mall sequence stages "hyperreal nostalgia for a pre-apocalyptic consumption space". The HBO show is consumed as if it were more real than the everyday because its production values exceed everyday life. This confirms Jameson's account of pastiche, "Our consumption of post-apocalyptic cinema and television (and video games) is a parody of our own demise" (Lecture 16, slide 24), while remaining within Baudrillard's frame.

I qualify the thesis. Hyperreality can become a totalising frame in which every cultural object is reduced to "simulation" and the analytic edge dulls. Strinati's limits section makes the parallel point: "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere. Of course, if reality has really 'imploded' into the media, how would we know it has happened? We could only rely on the media to tell us that it had, but why should we trust them?" (Strinati, 1995: 228). Baudrillard's hyperbole risks denying the reality of Gulf War civilian deaths and reality-TV contestant suffering, which is why Jameson's materialist correction is necessary.

I also note that some cultural production resists hyperreality. Documentary cinema, investigative journalism and certain forms of literary realism continue to operate by referential procedures. They are minoritarian inside the contemporary cultural economy, but their existence shows that hyperreality is not total.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's account of simulation and hyperreality remains the most powerful analytic lens for contemporary popular culture, from Disneyland to the hypermarket to reality TV to the Gulf War broadcast and *The Last of Us*. The concept identifies a structural feature of cultural production under late capitalism: the production of signs without referents, measured by performativity, that come to constitute the real rather than represent it. The lens must be used with care to avoid totalisation and political quietism, but used carefully it remains indispensable.

### Q3: Jameson and the cultural logic of late capitalism

I answer this question by arguing that Jameson's notion of postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism is the single most important corrective to Baudrillard's nihilism and Lyotard's celebratory pluralism, because it materialises postmodernism inside a specific stage of capitalist development. I defend the thesis through ahistoricism and pastiche (Strinati's catalogue of *Body Heat*, *Rocky*, *Rambo*, Spielberg sequences, *Blade Runner*), the famous "end of the world" line, the post-apocalyptic case studies on slides 24–26, and Fisher's extension via capitalist realism. I qualify the thesis by acknowledging that Jameson sometimes flattens the variety of postmodern practices into a single ideological function.

Jameson's claim is that postmodern theory and culture are the primary form of cultural production in late capitalism, across art, architecture, literature and film. Late capitalism here is Mandel's third stage, global, financialised, dominant over media and cultural production. The decisive cultural effect is ahistoricism: texts become styles; history is represented through pastiche rather than parody.

Parody implies a normative original to mock; pastiche is blank quotation without critical edge. Strinati provides the textbook examples: *Body Heat* (1981) is "parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling…the crime thriller of the 1940s", engaging in "retro-nostalgia" (Strinati, 1995: 218); the *Rocky* and *Rambo* franchises "go no further than recycling the recent past, making films which are merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality" (Strinati, 1995: 218); Spielberg's *Indiana Jones* and *Back to the Future* sequences point back to "earlier forms of popular culture such as cartoons, 'B' feature science-fiction films, and the Saturday morning, movie-house, adventure series people of Spielberg's generation would have viewed in their youth" (Strinati, 1995: 217–218). The result is that history becomes a wardrobe of styles to be drawn on at will. Real historical struggle is converted into surface design.

The crucial Jamesonian line, deployed in the slides, is that "it's easier to imagine the end of the world than an end to capitalism" and that this is "due to some weakness in our imaginations" (Jameson, 1994: xii). I treat this not as a complaint about pessimism but as a structural diagnosis. Late capitalism has so thoroughly colonised the imagination that the only alternatives we can picture are apocalyptic, the post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76: Burning Springs*, *The Last of Us*) is precisely the symptom. Our post-apocalyptic media is a parody of our own demise that achieves what Jameson (2003: 76) calls "the attempt to imagine capitalism by way of imagining the end of the world".

Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009) extends Jameson decisively. Capitalism dominates all so that it is "impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it" (Fisher, 2009: 2, author's italics). A "business ontology" (Fisher, 2009: 17) treats everything, schools, hospitals, friendships, as if it were a corporation. The slide's "universities as hypermarket" diagnosis is the Baudrillardian version of this. Capitalism has an "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6) and can incorporate anything, including its critique, including the apocalypse. Desires, aspirations and hopes undergo "precorporation" (Fisher, 2009: 9): they are framed by the logic of capitalism before they enter consciousness. Slide 26's abandoned shopping mall in *The Last of Us* is the literal image: the consumption space survives as nostalgic ruin even after its apocalyptic destruction, because no other social form can be imagined.

I qualify Jameson in three ways. First, the thesis can flatten variety: not all postmodern art is equally pastiche, and Strinati notes that *Miami Vice* "did not so much reject narrative as such, but rather parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre" (Strinati, 1995: 220), suggesting that pastiche can carry residual parodic charge. Second, Jameson sometimes treats the cultural sphere as a reliable index of the economic base, which underestimates the relative autonomy of cultural production. Third, the "weakness of imagination" diagnosis risks blaming the symptom rather than the cause, the cause is the political-economic transformation of late capitalism itself, not a failure of imaginative effort.

Nevertheless, the thesis explains things no rival explains. It explains why post-apocalyptic media has become a dominant genre. It explains why so many recent films simply recycle their own past (the sequel franchises Strinati lists). It explains the strange ahistorical glamour of postmodern architecture, where Philip Johnson's grandfather-clock AT&T tower in New York, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia in New Orleans and Richard Rogers's Lloyds in the City of London (Strinati, 1995: 216–217) stand as built versions of Jameson's thesis: history available only as style.

In conclusion, Jameson's notion of postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism is the necessary materialist anchor for postmodern theory. It rescues Baudrillard's diagnosis from nihilism by giving it a political-economic ground; it rescues Lyotard's account of grand-narrative collapse by showing that the absence of metanarratives is itself ideological. Used carefully, with attention to the relative autonomy of cultural production and to the actual variety of postmodern practices, it remains the most powerful single concept the lecture offers.

### Q4: Is postmodernism responsible for fake news, conspiracy theories and alternative facts?

I answer this question by arguing that postmodernism is not responsible for fake news, conspiracy theories and alternative facts, but that it correctly diagnoses the conditions under which they thrive. I work through the lecture's "guilty/not guilty" framing, defend a qualified "not guilty" verdict, and conclude that the political-economic causes of disinformation lie elsewhere, in platform capitalism, state strategic communication and the collapse of trusted journalism, even if postmodernism describes the cultural soil in which they grow.

The "guilty" case has real force. Postmodernism denies grand narratives, which can flatten the distinction between truth and falsity; if no narrative has authority, then conspiracy and consensus look symmetrical. Postmodernism denies counter-narratives such as Marxism and so opens space for alternative political views and alternative facts. Promoting polyvocality treats all voices as equal, which on disinformation issues is dangerous. Obtuse postmodern language prevents popular critique from forming. Postmodern emphasis on style over substantive argument trains audiences to consume rather than evaluate. Postmodernism slides into nihilism. There is some evidence, though it may be a leftist conspiracy theory in its own right, that US Cold War agencies funded postmodern art and theory as a depoliticised alternative to Marxist critical theory.

The "not guilty" case (slide 34) is, I argue, stronger. Postmodernism "critiques grand narratives (nationalism / conspiracy)"; it "provides alternative spaces / modalities of critique"; it is "polyvocal and new modalities, alternative spaces of politics"; it emphasises "flow, shifting identities and agencies, does not replace one form of authoritarianism with another"; it "allows space for different ideas to develop"; it "deflates pomposity with irony"; and, decisively, "postmodernism is explicitly named by authoritarian governments as leading to identity politics / cultural degeneration". When authoritarian regimes point at a theory and call it the enemy, it is usually doing something right. Lyotard wrote against the grand narratives of modernity at the moment those narratives were producing their most violent twentieth-century outcomes; his target was the totalising legitimating story, not the local truth practice.

I push the analysis further. The causal explanation for the slide-31 and slide-32 material (Trump's "leaks are real, news is fake" line, the fake *Time* cover, the Ipsos finding on AI-enabled fake news, Pizzagate, David Icke, the X post claiming Democrats stole the 2024 election, the "FEMA gutting Walmarts in Houston" banner) is political-economic, not philosophical. The structural conditions Strinati already named, "consumerism and media-saturation", "new middle-class occupations" in advertising, marketing, design, journalism and "those associated with…television production", and the erosion of unified identity (Strinati, 1995: 223–226), are sufficient to explain the conspiracy ecology. None of these causal mechanisms requires postmodern theory to operate.

Baudrillard's account of dissimulation actually anticipates the dynamics. Watergate as hyperreal scandal conceals the deeper truth that "Government / capitalism itself is corrupt and a scandal" (Lecture 16 notes); the Pizzagate-style conspiracy on slide 32 is structurally similar, a distorted little narrative that registers the absence of any authoritative big one. As the lecture notes put it of simulation: "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate gives the example of simulating an armed robbery (not possible as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery)". To circulate a fake banner about FEMA death camps is to conduct a small act of political violence inside an information ecology that cannot adjudicate it.

I qualify the "not guilty" verdict. Some postmodern intellectuals have been irresponsible in failing to defend basic referential procedures, and the most extreme social-constructivist claims, that there is no such thing as objective reality, have given rhetorical cover to denialism. Strinati's own limits section makes the related point that the "implosion of reality into the media" claim is self-undermining: "if reality has really 'imploded' into the media, how would we know it has happened? We could only rely on the media to tell us that it had, but why should we trust them?" (Strinati, 1995: 228). I do not absolve postmodernism of every charge. But the charge sheet often substitutes intellectual genealogy for the political-economic analysis Strinati already provides.

In conclusion, postmodernism is not guilty as charged. It is the diagnosis, not the disease. The political-economic causes of the slide-31 and slide-32 conspiracy material lie in media saturation, the proliferation of new "postmodern" occupations producing engagement at any epistemic cost, and the erosion of unified identities, all of which Strinati names. Postmodernism describes the cultural soil in which these phenomena grow, and a few postmodernists have been intellectually irresponsible in defending basic referential procedures, but the wholesale blaming of Lyotard, Baudrillard and Jameson is a category error.

### Q5: Compare and contrast Lyotard and Baudrillard on postmodern media

I answer this question by arguing that Lyotard and Baudrillard share a diagnosis of the breakdown of unified media authority but differ sharply on what replaces it. Lyotard's account turns on language games, performativity and the proliferation of little narratives; Baudrillard's account turns on simulation, hyperreality and the implosion of representation. I work through three sites, news media, reality television and political scandal, to show where the accounts converge and where they diverge. I conclude that both diagnoses are needed and that they should be read together.

Both theorists agree that the modernist model of media, a unified public sphere transmitting a shared worldview, anchored to an authoritative referent, has collapsed. For Lyotard, the collapse is epistemological: the grand narratives that legitimated unified media authority no longer command belief. For Baudrillard, the collapse is ontological: the distinction between the real and its representation has imploded, so there is no longer any referent for media to be true or false about.

This divergence produces different analyses of news media. For Lyotard, the news ecosystem is a landscape of plural language games. Different outlets operate by different rules of evidence, different criteria of relevance, different audiences, and "no single media narrative can claim authority over others" (Lecture 16 notes). There is no final authority of the media (slide 8). Performativity governs, what matters is whether a story "works", retains attention. For Baudrillard, the same landscape is a hyperreal: the news is no longer a report on events but a continuous production of events that have no existence outside their broadcast. The Gulf War did not take place, meaning that the Gulf War as experienced by audiences was an overwhelming televisual spectacle whose relation to ground events was indeterminate (slide 20).

The accounts converge on reality television. Lyotard would describe reality TV as a language game in which celebrity, intimacy and conflict operate by local rules without claim to universal truth. Baudrillard's account is sharper: reality TV is the implosion of real and represented into one thing, with no perceptual distance. "YOU are the content". The eye of TV is no longer panoptic, it is invited, and the contestants offer themselves for testing. I find Baudrillard's account more powerful here because it captures the ontological collapse, but Lyotard's frame helps explain how reality TV coexists with other media games without dominating them.

On political scandal the accounts diverge again. Lyotard would treat political scandal as a clash of language games: different communities apply different rules of relevance and culpability to the same actions, so no universal verdict is possible. Baudrillard's account is structural: political scandal is hyperreal dissimulation, "an imaginary effect, like Disneyland, concealing that reality no more exists outside than inside the bounds of an artificial parameter" (Lecture 16 notes; Baudrillard, 1994). It conceals the deeper truth that government and capitalism are themselves continuously scandalous (Lecture 16 notes on Watergate). Simulation collapses the gap between pretending and doing: "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate gives the example of simulating an armed robbery (not possible as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery)" (Lecture 16 notes). Slide 18's collage (Nixon's "Nixon resigns" front page, the Prince Andrew–Maxwell–Giuffre photograph, the Mandelson–Starmer image) is the visual archive of this hyperreal dissimulation.

The accounts also differ in political mood. Lyotard's tone is broadly affirmative: language games are productive, plurality is desirable, the differend names real injustice but also names the conditions of its articulation. Baudrillard's tone is darker, sometimes nihilist: hyperreality is suffocating; the implosion of representation forecloses critique because there is no referent left to ground it. The Gulf War book famously argued that the war "did not take place", which scandalised commentators who took the phrase literally. The phrase is hyperbole, but it captures a real condition of indeterminacy.

Both accounts illuminate the AI-fabricated imagery catalogued on slide 31 (the AI-generated ICE image with wrong-uniform and missing-windows annotation, the AI-generated CCTV-style "evidence" images). Lyotard would describe them as performativity engines: they produce outputs that work, that satisfy a constituency, without claim to truth. Baudrillard would describe them as fourth-order simulacra: signs bearing no relation to any reality whatsoever, pure simulation. The two accounts converge on the diagnosis: there is no referent, only performativity.

I conclude by arguing that the two accounts should be read together. Lyotard supplies the epistemological diagnosis, the proliferation of language games and the absence of any meta-rule. Baudrillard supplies the ontological diagnosis, the implosion of representation and the production of hyperreality. Lyotard's frame is better for analysing how different media communities coexist; Baudrillard's frame is better for analysing how individual media objects function. Used together, they remain the most powerful pair of tools for analysing the contemporary media environment, from the Gulf War broadcast to reality TV to the AI-fabricated imagery on slide 31.

### Q6: Disneyland and contemporary spaces of consumption

I answer this question by arguing that Baudrillard's analysis of Disneyland as third-order simulation provides the indispensable template for understanding contemporary spaces of consumption, from Las Vegas to the hypermarket to the Download Festival to the abandoned mall of *The Last of Us*. I work through the original analysis, extend it to four sites foregrounded in the lecture's own slides, and qualify it by noting that not every contemporary consumption space is fully hyperreal.

Baudrillard's analysis of Disneyland is one of the lecture's most quoted passages. "Disneyland is presented as imaginary in order to make us believe that the rest is real, when in fact all of Los Angeles and the America surrounding it are no longer real, but of the order of the hyperreal and simulation" (Baudrillard, 1994). Disneyland is third-order simulation: simulacra produced by simulation, where the model is more real than the real. It operates as a deterrence machine: by ostentatiously marking itself as fantasy it conceals that the rest of America is also simulation. The lecture's parallel example is the prison: "Prisons – hide the fact that we are all incarcerated in society. Disneyland exists to conceal the fact that it is the real America" (Lecture 16 notes).

Las Vegas extends Disneyland to an entire built environment. Strinati notes that "Las Vegas has been seen as an exemplar of and inspiration for postmodern architecture (Venturi et al. 1977)" (Strinati, 1995: 217). The casino strip is the architectural realisation of the third order: each casino is a hyperreal version of an elsewhere (Paris, Venice, ancient Egypt, New York), more vivid, more themed, more consumable than the original. The model precedes and exceeds the real.

The hypermarket is the second extension, the lecture's own master case (slide 14). It has migrated "from the heart of a community" to its own out-of-city satellite zone and "creates its own spatial / temporal distortions like a satellite having gravitational pull" (Lecture 16 notes). It generates "new geographical and experiential space (and new modes of living)". Consumers passing through "are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption. Yet behaviour is playful and whimsical" (Lecture 16 notes). The slide includes Next Home, a surveillance camera, Lakeside shopping centre, and the Download Festival as hypermarket-festival hybrid: contemporary festivals reproduce hypermarket logic, peripheral location, temporal distortion, ticketed entry, surveillance, playful behaviour. The shopper is the content; the slide's parenthetical, "Universities as hypermarket", extends the diagnosis to higher education itself.

Reality television, slide 19, completes the consumption analysis. The hypermarket consumer who is screened, tested and noted is structurally the same subject as the reality-TV contestant for whom "YOU are the content". Both spaces invite the gaze and produce the subject through consumption-as-performance.

The Last of Us mall scene in the 2023 TV adaptation, foregrounded on slide 26, is a deliberate citation of the hyperreal: an abandoned mall, a Halloween-store ruin, an abandoned food court. The mall is more affectively powerful in ruin than it was in operation, which is the third order at work, and slide 26's stills function as the lecture's own image of "hyperreal nostalgia for a pre-apocalyptic consumption space".

I qualify the thesis. Not every consumption space is fully hyperreal. Strinati's limits section reminds us that consumers are not passive recipients: "the ability to consume is restricted by economic and cultural inequalities", and Strinati flags "the nature of people's daily lives, popular attitudes towards consumption, the continuity of identities" as questions that postmodern theory tends to ignore (Strinati, 1995: 228–229). Baudrillard's analysis sometimes flattens this variety.

I also note the ethnographic point. Visitors to Disneyland are not duped. They know it is a simulation. The pleasure of the simulation is part of its appeal. Strinati makes the parallel point: "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere" (Strinati, 1995: 228). The simulation is co-produced by knowing participants.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's analysis of Disneyland is the indispensable template for understanding contemporary spaces of consumption from Las Vegas to the hypermarket to the Download Festival to the *Last of Us* abandoned mall. The third order, the model preceding and exceeding the real, is the dominant logic of these spaces. The analysis must be qualified by Strinati's reminders about audience competence and unequal access, but it remains the most powerful single concept the lecture offers for the analysis of consumption.

### Q7: The Gulf War did not take place

I answer this question by arguing that Baudrillard's *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* (1995) is not the absurd literal claim that nothing happened in Iraq in 1991. It is the diagnostic claim that the Gulf War as available to audiences was a hyperreal televisual spectacle whose relation to ground events was systematically indeterminate. I work through the lecture notes' summary of the argument, the iconography of slide 20, and the connections Baudrillard's account forces with his own surrounding theses on simulation, dissimulation and reality TV. I qualify the thesis by acknowledging that real people died, and conclude that Baudrillard's hyperbole captures a structural truth about war as media event.

Baudrillard's argument is reconstructed in the lecture notes as follows. "Gulf war was overwhelming strength (like a pre-programmed war), using US technology and broadcast 'live'. The war was a postmodern act to see if war was possible in the current age. Shrinking of perspective (audience were there) and imagery so not possible to say if it happened or not" (Lecture 16 notes). The audience was "there", embedded by CNN in Baghdad: slide 20 lists Peter Arnett, the "Cleared by Iraqi Censors LIVE" caption, the CNN night-vision green flashes, the German *Krieg am Golf* news, the bomb-sight crosshairs over a target, Douglas Kellner's *The Persian Gulf TV War* as the academic register of the same diagnosis. The perspective shrank to a televisual fragment whose relation to the war on the ground was unverifiable. Baudrillard called this a postmodern act: a test of whether war was still possible in the current age, since war classically required the symmetrical encounter of forces and the production of historical meaning, both of which the asymmetric, pre-programmed Gulf operation lacked.

The provocative title is hyperbole, not literal denial. Baudrillard's point is that the event so thoroughly imploded into its broadcast that there was no longer a referent outside the broadcast to which the broadcast could correspond. The war did not take place in the sense that it took place only as a broadcast. This is the structural logic of slide 19 generalised from reality TV to war: "Real and represented are identical – they implode into one thing, no perceptual distance" (Lecture 16 notes).

I connect the diagnosis to Baudrillard's surrounding theses. Slide 18 catalogues political scandal as hyperreal dissimulation: Nixon's resignation front page, the Prince Andrew–Maxwell–Giuffre photograph, the Mandelson–Starmer image. The Watergate analysis, "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate gives the example of simulating an armed robbery (not possible as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery)" (Lecture 16 notes), applies to war too. To broadcast a war is to conduct it as broadcast; the broadcast is the war's primary mode of existence for everyone outside the immediate combat zone. The 9/11 discussion the lecture flags in slide 35, Baudrillard's "pure event…the revenge of the real", is the exception that proves the rule, an event that briefly broke the flow of hyperreality precisely because its televisual scale exceeded available framing.

This does not mean nothing happened. The proper response to Baudrillard is not to deny the dead but to insist that the gap between televisual representation and ground event is now structural. Investigative journalism, the academic war-studies tradition represented on slide 20 by Kellner's book, and the lecture's own essential reading (Strinati on the limits of postmodernism) are the practices that hold the diagnosis open without surrendering to it.

I qualify the thesis. Baudrillard sometimes flirts with nihilism, the implication that since the war is hyperreal there is nothing to be said or done. Strinati's limits section is the proper corrective: postmodern theory exaggerates "the role of media / commercialism in forming reality" (Lecture 16, slide 38), and "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere" (Strinati, 1995: 228). The Gulf War took place; Baudrillard's hyperbole is true at the level of media phenomenology, not at the level of ontology.

I also note that the diagnosis applies asymmetrically. State actors with broadcasting capacity benefit from hyperreal indeterminacy because they can produce the televisual frame within which the war is consumed. This is consistent with Jameson's reminder that postmodernism is the cultural logic of late capitalism: hyperreal war is a privilege of the side that owns the cameras.

In conclusion, *The Gulf War Did Not Take Place* is not the absurd literal claim that nothing happened but the diagnostic claim that the war as available to audiences was a hyperreal televisual spectacle whose relation to ground events was systematically indeterminate. The diagnosis is structurally linked to Baudrillard's analyses of political scandal (slide 18) and reality TV (slide 19): all three describe the implosion of real and represented. It must be qualified by Strinati's reminders that audiences are still capable of distinguishing media reality from ground reality, but it remains the most powerful single diagnosis the lecture offers of war as media event.

### Q8: Reality TV and the end of the panopticon

I answer this question by arguing that Baudrillard's claim that reality television marks the end of the Foucauldian panopticon is broadly correct: the panoptic gaze of disciplinary modernity has been replaced by an invited gaze of self-display, in which "YOU are the content". I work through Foucault's panopticon (cited on slide 19), Baudrillard's revision, the lecture's own reality-TV imagery (slide 19's *Big Brother* studio screens), the new occupation of "influencer" (slide 37), and conclude with a qualification drawn from Strinati: the diagnosis is powerful but does not exhaust media-audience relations.

Foucault's panopticon describes Bentham's prison design as a model for disciplinary modernity. The inmate is potentially always visible to a central observer who is themselves invisible. The asymmetry of visibility produces self-discipline: the inmate internalises the gaze and behaves as if always watched. Slide 19 reproduces the panopticon image precisely as the disciplinary template Baudrillard is overturning.

Baudrillard's claim, deployed in the lecture, is that the eye of TV is no longer a panoptic gaze. The gaze is invited; the contestants offer themselves for visibility; "YOU are the content" (slide 19). The lecture notes are explicit: "the gaze is invited and is the 'test', 'YOU are the content' the end of the panopticon. Media stages reality" (Lecture 16 notes). This inverts Bentham's design: the observer is no longer central and invisible but distributed and explicitly addressed. The subject does not internalise an asymmetric gaze; the subject performs for a desired gaze.

Reality TV proper confirms the diagnosis. *Big Brother*, foregrounded in slide 19's collage of reality-TV studio screens, is the lecture's chosen example: contestants explicitly volunteer for total visibility, and the show's value is constituted by their performance. The contestants do not pre-exist the show; their personalities are produced by their on-camera self-display. There is no perceptual distance between real and represented; they implode into one thing. The show stages reality, and reality is the show.

The "what about social media now?" prompt at the bottom of slide 19 generalises the diagnosis. The slide invites us to extend the implosion of real and represented from reality TV to the broader media environment. Slide 37 names the structural outcome: media saturation is "exacerbated by…new occupations (influencer)". The influencer is the figure in whom Baudrillard's diagnosis is most complete, the influencer's feed is their life rather than its representation, and the slide's foregrounding of the term registers exactly this.

The hypermarket diagnosis (slide 14) reinforces the point. "Consumers who pass through this space are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption. Yet behaviour is playful and whimsical" (Lecture 16 notes). The shopper and the reality-TV contestant are structurally the same subject: both are surveilled but playful, both produce the content their visibility consumes. The end of the panopticon is not just a reality-TV phenomenon but the general logic of contemporary consumption.

I qualify the thesis with Strinati. The implosion of real and represented "into one thing" is a strong claim, and Strinati's limits section is sceptical: "Most people would still be able to distinguish between the 'reality' created by the media, and that which exists elsewhere. Of course, if reality has really 'imploded' into the media, how would we know it has happened?" (Strinati, 1995: 228). The phenomenology of reality TV is more nuanced than total implosion, audiences enjoy reality TV partly because they retain a critical distance from its constructions, and contestants often complain that the editing distorts their "real" selves. The panopticon may not have ended so much as combined with the invited gaze, producing a more complex regime than either Foucault or Baudrillard, taken alone, could describe.

The dialogical and discourse-theoretical approaches Strinati discusses in his chapter (the use of Foucault's notion of discourse to analyse audiences, the dialogical analysis after Bakhtin/Volosinov) supply the corrective. Audiences read reality TV inside multiple discourses; their reception is not exhausted by the show's offered invitation. This restores some critical agency without overturning the diagnosis.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's claim that reality television marks the end of the Foucauldian panopticon is broadly correct at the level of phenomenology: the panoptic gaze has been replaced by an invited gaze in which subjects offer themselves as content. The "what about social media now?" slide-19 prompt extends the diagnosis from television to the new occupations slide 37 catalogues. But the diagnosis must be qualified by Strinati's reminders about audience competence and by the dialogical-discursive corrective: audiences still distinguish staged reality from lived reality, and the diagnosis describes a tendency rather than a completed displacement.

### Q9: Mark Fisher and Capitalist Realism as an extension of Jameson

I answer this question by arguing that Mark Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* (2009) is the most important extension of Jameson's diagnosis, sharpening the "end of the world easier than end of capitalism" line into a fully developed account of how late capitalism forecloses alternative imagination. I work through Fisher's key concepts, capitalist realism, business ontology, precorporation and hauntology, and apply them to contemporary case studies. I qualify Fisher by noting his tendency to despair and his under-attention to actually existing alternatives.

Fisher's central claim is that capitalism dominates all so that it is "impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it" (Fisher, 2009: 2, author's italics), even post-apocalypse. This is the operationalisation of Jameson's diagnosis: the cultural logic of late capitalism is not just that capitalism produces dominant aesthetic forms but that it forecloses the imagination of alternatives at the level of desire.

A "business ontology" (Fisher, 2009: 17) treats everything as if it were a corporation. Slide 27 paraphrases this precisely: "treats everything as a corporation". Schools, hospitals, friendships, intimate relationships are all reframed in the language of brands, deliverables and stakeholder management. The lecture's image of "Universities as hypermarket" on slide 14 is the Baudrillardian version of the same diagnosis: the institution that should produce knowledge is reframed as a consumption space that screens, tests and surveils its users.

Capitalism has an "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6); it "can incorporate anything, even the apocalypse" (Lecture 16, slide 27). The lecture demonstrates this through the post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76: Burning Springs*, *The Last of Us*): even imagined civilisational collapse is reabsorbed as prestige entertainment, sold back to viewers as content. The apocalypse is precorporated.

"Precorporation" (Fisher, 2009: 9) is Fisher's sharpest concept. Our desires, aspirations and hopes are framed by the logic of capitalism before they enter consciousness: "Before being incorporated into our consciousness they are already framed by the logic of capitalism" (Lecture 16, slide 27, paraphrasing Fisher, 2009: 9). The radical critique that we might have produced is already neutralised at the point of formation. Strinati's catalogue of new "postmodern" occupations (advertising, marketing, design, journalism, television production: Strinati, 1995: 225) is the labour-market correlate of precorporation: an entire occupational class whose work is to produce desires already framed by the logic of consumption.

Derrida's hauntology, deployed by Fisher, captures the cultural phenomenology of this regime (slide 28). Capitalist media is figuratively haunted by ghosts of former utopias and theorisations. The lecture's image is a bust of Marx in a wintry forest, modernist grand narratives surviving as nostalgic residues that we keep mourning. Slide 28's question is precise: "we can't escape modernism / grand narratives?" Hauntology marks the impossibility of either fulfilling or abandoning the modernist promise.

I extend Fisher to four sites foregrounded by the lecture. First, education: the "Universities as hypermarket" slide 14 entry is business ontology in action. Second, media saturation: the slide-37 essential-reading bullet on "new occupations (influencer)" names the human face of precorporation, the labour of having one's desires shaped by the logic of consumption before they enter consciousness. Third, apocalypse media: the post-apocalyptic cycle on slides 24–26 literally enacts Jameson's diagnosis (slide 22) by making post-apocalypse the only available imagined alternative. Fourth, the slide-26 ruined-mall sequence in *The Last of Us*: even the death of the consumption space becomes hyperreal nostalgia for it, the absorbent quality of capitalism made image.

I qualify Fisher in two ways. First, his tendency to despair: *Capitalist Realism* sometimes reads as if alternatives were genuinely impossible, which risks the political quietism it diagnoses. The slide-34 "not guilty" case lists resources for resistance (alternative spaces of critique, polyvocality, irony, the deflation of authoritarian pomposity) that Fisher's totalising diagnosis can flatten. Second, Strinati's limits apply: Fisher inherits the postmodern tendency to exaggerate "the role of media / commercialism in forming reality" (slide 38), and underattends to the persistent role of work, family and material life that Strinati's limits section flags (Strinati, 1995: 227–229).

I also note Fisher's pedagogical power. *Capitalist Realism* is short, accessible and written without postmodern obscurantism. The slide's chosen quotations from pages 2, 6, 9 and 17 are the operational vocabulary the lecture builds its account of contemporary capitalism around. The book's success is itself evidence that the diagnosis is recognised by readers as describing their lived experience.

In conclusion, Fisher's *Capitalist Realism* is the most important extension of Jameson's diagnosis. The concepts, capitalist realism, business ontology, precorporation, hauntology, give Jameson's "end of the world easier than end of capitalism" line operational purchase. The diagnosis must be qualified by the slide-34 "not guilty" resources and by Strinati's limits, but it remains the most powerful single account the lecture offers of how late capitalism forecloses the imagination of alternatives.

### Q10: Pastiche, parody and postmodern culture

I answer this question by arguing that Jameson's distinction between parody and pastiche is one of the lecture's most analytically powerful tools, and that pastiche has indeed replaced parody as the dominant mode of contemporary culture, but that this is not a complete replacement, and certain contemporary works recover critical parody inside pastiche-saturated environments. I work through the distinction, four case studies, and a qualification.

Jameson's distinction is precise. Parody implies a normative original to mock; it carries a critical edge because the parodist has a position from which to satirise the original. Pastiche is blank quotation: the imitation of dead styles without critical purpose, the "wearing of a stylistic mask" in a culture that has lost the conviction that any style is normative. Slide 23 paraphrases the diagnosis directly: postmodern culture "produces an ahistoricism where texts become styles and history is represented through pastiche". Postmodern culture is dominated by pastiche because it has lost confidence in normative originals; everything is already quoted, sampled, remixed.

Strinati's chapter is the lecture's most concentrated catalogue of cinematic pastiche. *Body Heat* (1981) is "parasitic on the cinema's past, recycling…the crime thriller of the 1940s", engaging in "retro-nostalgia" (Strinati, 1995: 218). The *Rocky* (1976, 1979, 1982, 1985, 1990) and *Rambo* (1982, 1985, 1988) sequels "go no further than recycling the recent past, making films which are merely imitations of other films rather than reflections of social reality" (Strinati, 1995: 218). Spielberg's *Indiana Jones* and *Back to the Future* sequences point back to "earlier forms of popular culture such as cartoons, 'B' feature science-fiction films, and the Saturday morning, movie-house, adventure series people of Spielberg's generation would have viewed in their youth" (Strinati, 1995: 217–218). *Who Framed Roger Rabbit?* (1988) is postmodern through its "deliberate use of distinct (cultural and technical) genres: the cartoon strip and the detective story" (Strinati, 1995: 218). The cumulative effect is that history itself becomes a wardrobe of styles available for assembly.

*Blade Runner* (1982), Strinati's most extended example, exemplifies how pastiche corrodes coherent time: its architecture has "lighting characteristic of contemporary skyscrapers but the overall look of ancient temples", its "'street talk' consists of words and phrases taken from a whole range of distinct languages", its 'non-human humans' are "'replicants', almost perfect simulations of human beings", and its genre is "not clear" (Strinati, 1995: 218–219). It is a science-fiction film whose look is not obviously futuristic, a detective story whose voice-over draws on film noir. The result is a film inside which "we appear to be in the past, the present and the future at the same time".

Television confirms the pastiche pattern. Strinati's chosen example, *Miami Vice* (1985–1990), was "heavily reliant upon style and surface", with its producer Michael Mann working to the rule "no earth tones" (Strinati, 1995: 220). Its visual debt to film noir, its designer clothes, its imaginative day and night-time images of Miami, its obtrusive pop and rock soundtrack: each is pastiche borrowed without critical purpose. The cult series *Twin Peaks* (1990–1991) and *Wild Palms* (1993) extend the pattern through surreal collage. The advertising chapter Strinati then offers, in which adverts now "send up or parody advertising itself by citing other adverts, by using references drawn from popular culture and by self-consciously making clear their status as advertisements" (Strinati, 1995: 220–221), is pastiche generalised across the commercial form.

Pop music exemplifies pastiche through sampling and mash-up. Girl Talk, foregrounded on slide 40, "creates music by mashing up existing songs", and the question the slide poses, "is this 'new' music in any sense, or does such a thing even exist?", is the question pastiche poses in general. Strinati's wider catalogue includes "eclectic successions of pop and rock-'n'-roll records, mixing, collage constructions, reggae sound systems, rap, house, hip hop and techno" alongside "the so-called 'art rock' musical innovations and mixing of styles associated with groups and performers such as Talking Heads, Laurie Anderson and the Pet Shop Boys" (Strinati, 1995: 222). Modernist pop music, by contrast, fused influences into something distinctively new (rock-'n'-roll fusing country and rhythm and blues; soul music fusing gospel and blues), where postmodern pop maintains the separate identities of its sources.

Architecture is where Jameson and Strinati both anchor pastiche. Strinati's examples are Philip Johnson's grandfather-clock-shaped AT&T Building in New York, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia in New Orleans, and Richard Rogers's Lloyds building in the City of London (Strinati, 1995: 216–217). The slide-4 image-grid extends this: a building with a collapsed Ionic column on top of a glass office block, a Frank Gehry-style white sculptural building. The result is a built environment in which historical periods are aesthetic options rather than living traditions.

I qualify the thesis in two ways. First, parody is not extinct. Strinati notes that *Miami Vice* "did not so much reject narrative as such, but rather parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre, while abounding with self-conscious references to popular culture. For example, the conventions of the gangster film were often parodied, while one episode was a more or less straightforward remake of the western *High Noon* (1952)" (Strinati, 1995: 220). Pastiche can carry residual parodic charge; the line between blank quotation and critical quotation is porous.

Second, the parody/pastiche distinction is more analytic than empirical. Most contemporary works contain both modes; the question is which dominates. Jameson's distinction works best as a diagnostic tool for the dominant tendency rather than as a binary classification. Strinati's wider limits section reminds us that "popular culture has always used mixture of styles" (Lecture 16, slide 38), so the diagnostic must be careful not to treat pastiche as wholly novel.

In conclusion, Jameson's distinction between parody and pastiche is one of the lecture's most analytically powerful tools. Pastiche has indeed replaced parody as the dominant mode of contemporary culture, from *Body Heat* and *Blade Runner* to the *Rocky* and *Rambo* franchises, from *Miami Vice* and *Twin Peaks* to Girl Talk and the "art rock" of Talking Heads and the Pet Shop Boys, from Philip Johnson's AT&T to Frank Gehry. The replacement is not complete (residual parody persists), and the distinction works best as a diagnostic of dominant tendency, but the broad Jamesonian claim that contemporary culture is dominated by ahistorical pastiche is well supported by the evidence Strinati assembles.

### Q11: Does postmodernism exaggerate the role of media in constituting reality?

I answer this question by arguing that postmodernism does sometimes exaggerate the role of media in constituting reality, and that this is the most defensible criticism of the tradition, but that the exaggeration captures a real structural feature of late modernity that other approaches miss. I work through the charge, defend a qualified version of the postmodern claim, and conclude that the proper response is to retain the media-constitutive thesis while supplementing it with materialist and ethnographic correctives.

The essential reading flags this charge explicitly: postmodernism exaggerates the role of media and commercialism in forming reality and rehashes Frankfurt School claims about the culture industry without acknowledging the debt. The charge is fair in three ways. First, Baudrillard's most extreme formulations, that the Gulf War did not take place, that all of America is hyperreal, risk treating the media environment as the sole reality, denying the existence of any extra-media referent. Second, the postmodern tendency to read everything as text or discourse can flatten the material substrates of social life, labour, food, housing, bodies, weather, into semiotic operations. Third, the methodological focus on media texts means postmodern theory underweights ethnographic study of how audiences actually receive and use media.

I defend a qualified version of the postmodern claim. Media do constitute reality in important senses, even if they do not exhaust it. Three case studies, all anchored in the lecture's own material, confirm the constitutive thesis. First, the hypermarket as analysed in slide 14: consumers passing through "are screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption" and the space generates its own "spatial / temporal distortions like a satellite having gravitational pull" (Lecture 16 notes). The hypermarket is a media-constituted environment whose architecture, signage, surveillance and product display together produce the consumer subject. Second, political scandal as analysed in slide 18: the lecture notes that Watergate "exists as the hyperreal to covers up that Government / capitalism itself is corrupt and a scandal" (Lecture 16 notes). Political reality is constituted in and through media operations of dissimulation. Third, the Gulf War as analysed in slide 20: war as available to audiences was constituted by the broadcast itself, and the "shrinking of perspective (audience were there) and imagery so not possible to say if it happened or not" (Lecture 16 notes).

These cases show that the constitutive thesis is not metaphysical idealism. It is a sociological claim that, under conditions of media saturation, important domains of social reality are constituted in and through media operations. Strinati's emergence-of-postmodernism section makes the same point in its own register: "the world…will consist more and more of media screens and popular cultural images, TVs, VDUs, videos, computers, computer games, personal stereos, adverts, theme parks, shopping malls" (Strinati, 1995: 224).

The proper response to the exaggeration charge is therefore not to abandon the constitutive thesis but to supplement it with the correctives Strinati and the slide-38 essential reading already provide. The slide-38 bullet, "May overcome some of these limits by bringing in other theories in dialogue with post-modernism. For example, post-structuralism (media as discourse / dialogic), cultural populism (culture as understood through anti-elitism, working class cultures)", names the supplements directly. The discourse-theoretical approach treats media as discourse rather than as totalising simulation; the dialogical approach (after Bakhtin/Volosinov, as Strinati discusses) restores audience agency; cultural populism foregrounds working-class uses of media.

I also note that postmodernism shares the exaggeration with rivals, a point the slide-38 essential reading makes explicit: postmodernism "rehashes claims of Frankfurt School". The Frankfurt School treated the culture industry as a totalising apparatus of manipulation, and postmodern theory inherits both that strength and that weakness. Strinati's broader chapter and Lecture 13 on British cultural studies under Stuart Hall correct this with attention to active audiences and oppositional readings.

I qualify further by considering the most extreme version of the charge: that postmodernism's media-constitutive thesis denies the reality of suffering. The charge is sometimes levelled against Baudrillard's Gulf War book. The charge can be answered: Baudrillard's hyperbole captures a media-phenomenological truth without denying that real people died. But the charge is right that postmodern rhetoric sometimes courts political quietism by treating reality as a discursive effect. The corrective is Jameson's materialism and Fisher's diagnosis of capitalist realism, both of which retain the constitutive thesis while reconnecting it to political-economic substrates.

In conclusion, postmodernism does sometimes exaggerate the role of media in constituting reality, and this is the most defensible criticism of the tradition. But the exaggeration captures a real structural feature of late modernity that other approaches miss: the increasing extent to which important domains of social reality are constituted in and through media operations. The proper response is to retain the constitutive thesis while supplementing it with materialist political economy, ethnographic study of audiences and recognition of the persistent importance of non-mediated experience.

### Q12: Use, exchange and sign value

I answer this question by arguing that Baudrillard's introduction of sign value alongside Marx's use value and exchange value is the lecture's most analytically productive single concept, because it captures the dominant logic of contemporary consumer culture in a way that neither classical economics nor classical Marxism can. I work through the three-term distinction, four case studies, and a qualification.

Marx's distinction is precise. Use value is the qualitative satisfaction a commodity provides, a coat keeps me warm, a meal satisfies hunger. Exchange value is the quantitative ratio at which one commodity trades for another in the market, a coat exchanges for twenty meals or for £100. Marx's labour theory of value grounds exchange value in socially necessary labour time. Use value and exchange value are not strictly comparable, they are different dimensions of the commodity.

Baudrillard's third term is sign value: the signification a commodity carries within a coded system of social difference. "The sign object is neither given nor exchanged: it is appropriated, withheld and manipulated by individual subjects as a sign, that is as a coded difference" (Baudrillard, 1994: [VERIFY PAGE?]). The lecture's own master example is the trainer. Slide 11 displays Jordans, Converse, Asics, Nike Cortez as the commodity in the sign-value system; slide 12 places Nike trainers next to portraits of Marx and Baudrillard as the comparative example. A pair of Nike Jordans has use value (running), exchange value (the market price), and sign value (membership in a coded community of sneaker aficionados, demonstration of taste, performance of identity). The sign value is increasingly dominant in late capitalism, with use value sometimes reduced to a pretext.

I extend the diagnosis to other sites foregrounded by the lecture and its essential reading. First, the hypermarket: slide 14 catalogues consumers being "screened and tested, surveilled and noted in terms of their consumption" while behaviour remains "playful / whimsical". The shopper inside the hypermarket consumes signs as much as commodities, and the data trail of consumption is itself a sign within the surveillance system. Second, the virtual handbag: slide 11's third image is a woman in a VR/AR headset shopping a virtual handbag, which is hyperreal sign value in its purest form, a commodity whose use value is zero and whose exchange value depends entirely on the coded difference it confers. Third, Disneyland and Las Vegas: the consumption of branded experience inside themed environments is sign-valued consumption par excellence; the visitor pays for the sign of having been to Disneyland (a postcard, a hat) more than for any "use" of the rides. Fourth, the mediated commodity sequence on slide 13: "Culturally the object exists within the world of other sign objects… the media displays that a miracle may happen… the subject is brought into the world of events. But is kept distant from reality… within a dizzying whirl of reality… but media is the site of the playing out of our desires" (Lecture 16, slide 13). The pink Nike trainers and the woman watching a giant screen on slide 13 stage the mediation of sign value as a sequence: commodity becomes desire becomes mediated experience.

The sign-value concept also illuminates Strinati's wider analysis of consumerism and media saturation. Once a fully functioning system of capitalist production has been established, "the need for consumption begins to grow, and people need to acquire a leisure or consumer ethic in addition to a work ethic" (Strinati, 1995: 224). The "growth of consumer credit, the expansion of agencies such as advertising, marketing, design and public relations, encouraging people to consume" (Strinati, 1995: 224) is precisely the institutional apparatus that produces sign value as the dominant mode of consumption. The new "postmodern" occupations (Strinati, 1995: 225) are those whose work is to circulate signs.

I qualify the thesis in two ways. First, sign value does not replace use and exchange value but overlays them. The Nike trainers still keep one's feet supported; the hypermarket still sells food. The dominance of sign value is a tendency, not an exclusive principle. Strinati's limits section makes the parallel point that "the ability to consume is restricted by economic and cultural inequalities" (Strinati, 1995: 228), which materially anchors any sign system. Second, sign value is not free-floating; it is anchored in real social hierarchies. Strinati notes that the new occupational class "exert an important influence over other people's life-styles and values or ideologies (while expressing their own as well)" and that their "quest for cultural power leads them towards postmodernism and away from the cultures of other classes" (Strinati, 1995: 226). The coded differences that signs encode are themselves the distinctions of occupational class fractions.

I also note the political stakes. The dominance of sign value means that contemporary consumption is increasingly difficult to evaluate by criteria of need. Strinati's limits section flags exactly this difficulty: postmodern theory "fails to recognise how useful the commodities which people buy are for them" (Strinati, 1995: 228). A serious analysis of consumption must hold sign value and use value together.

In conclusion, Baudrillard's introduction of sign value alongside Marx's use value and exchange value is the lecture's most analytically productive single concept for understanding contemporary consumer culture. The dominance of sign value over use and exchange value is the structural feature of late capitalist consumption, anchored by the slide-11/12/13 trainer sequence and extended by the hypermarket diagnosis (slide 14). The concept must be qualified by Strinati's reminders about material inequality and the persistent usefulness of commodities, but it remains the most powerful single tool the lecture offers for the analysis of consumption.

### Q13: Liberation or nihilism?

I answer this question by arguing that the breakdown of grand narratives is neither uniquely liberating nor uniquely nihilist but produces a contested terrain whose political valence depends on what fills the vacuum. I work through both sides via the lecture's own slide-33/34 "guilty/not guilty" debate and conclude with a qualified position: the breakdown is conditionally liberating where it makes space for previously silenced voices, and conditionally nihilist where it dissolves the basis for shared political action.

The liberation case is the "not guilty" case (slide 34). Postmodernism "critiques grand narratives (nationalism / conspiracy)"; it "provides alternative spaces / modalities of critique"; it is "polyvocal and new modalities, alternative spaces of politics"; it emphasises "flow, shifting identities and agencies" and "does not replace one form of authoritarianism with another"; it "allows space for different ideas to develop"; it "deflates pomposity with irony"; and it is "explicitly named by authoritarian governments as leading to identity politics / cultural degeneration". Grand narratives have historically been exclusionary. Lyotard's critique was explicitly aimed at the legitimating stories of modernity, the Enlightenment rationality, Marxism and Christianity Strinati lists (Strinati, 1995: 215) as ideas that "make absolute, universal and all-embracing claims to knowledge and truth". The polyvocality the slide-34 case celebrates is the precondition of voices that the modernist consensus silenced.

The nihilism case is the "guilty" case (slide 33). Postmodernism "denies grand narratives, all truth is relative so all theories of equal merit"; it "denies alternative counter-narratives (such as Marxism) and so opens space for alterative political views / facts"; it "promotes plurality / polyvocal, therefore all voices are equal"; "obtuse language prevents a popular critique from forming"; it is "more about style than substantive arguments"; "postmodernism is nihilist, denies meaning and is destructive"; and there is some evidence that "postmodern art / culture was funded by US Government as an alternative to more radical forms of critical theory / Marxism (some evidence for this but may be a 'left' conspiracy theory)". Slide 32's catalogue of conspiracy theories (Pizzagate, David Icke, the 2024-election-stolen X post, the fake FEMA-death-camps banner) is the public-sphere consequence the "guilty" verdict cites: a polyvocality in which no game has authority and the worst little narratives proliferate.

I work through this through the lecture's own examples. First, the Pizzagate cluster on slide 32: the breakdown of grand narratives is conditionally nihilist here, because conspiracy little narratives substitute for vanished big ones without any shared rule by which to adjudicate. Second, postmodern art and architecture (slide 4, Strinati's discussion of Philip Johnson's AT&T, Charles Moore's Piazza Italia, Richard Rogers's Lloyds, the slide-4 image-grid): the breakdown of the modernist grand narrative is unambiguously liberating, allowing for "highly ornate, elaborately designed, contextualised and brightly coloured buildings; a stress on fictionality and playfulness; and the mixing of styles drawn from different historical periods" (Strinati, 1995: 216). Third, postmodern cinema and television (slide 37, Strinati on *Blade Runner*, *Pulp Fiction*, *Breaking Bad*, *Miami Vice*, *Twin Peaks*): the breakdown allows for non-linearity, multiple narratives, pastiche, ambiguity, none of which is intrinsically nihilist. Fourth, the consumption ecology of slides 11–14: the breakdown produces consumers who are "playful and whimsical" but also "screened and tested, surveilled" (Lecture 16 notes), an ambivalent condition rather than a clear emancipation or nihilism.

This patchwork suggests that the question cannot be answered as a generality. The breakdown of grand narratives is liberating in some domains and nihilist in others. The slide-33/34 framing leaves the question productively open.

I qualify further with Strinati. The dichotomy may itself be misleading because the premise (that grand narratives are in decline) is contestable. Strinati's limits section flags that postmodernism's claim "that metanarratives are in decline" is self-undermining: "what is postmodernism, after all, if it is not another metanarrative?…If it is indeed another metanarrative, how can metanarratives be in decline?" (Strinati, 1995: 228–229). The slide-38 essential-reading bullet "Meta-narratives are not in decline" formalises the same critique. If grand narratives are not in fact in decline, then the question of whether their breakdown is liberating or nihilist may be the wrong question; the operative question is which grand narratives are dominant and to whose benefit.

I also note that the slide-34 case for postmodernism's resources of critique (alternative modalities, polyvocality, irony, the deflation of authoritarian pomposity) supplies the answer to the nihilism charge. The postmodern condition does not preclude truth practices; it relocates them inside specific language games and local communities of inquiry.

In conclusion, the breakdown of grand narratives, to the extent it has occurred, is neither uniquely liberating nor uniquely nihilist. It produces a contested terrain whose political valence depends on what fills the vacuum, with the slide-32 conspiracy cluster as the nihilist symptom and the postmodern aesthetic resources of slides 4 and 37 as the liberating ones. Strinati's reminder that "Meta-narratives are not in decline" qualifies the very premise of the question, and the slide-34 "not guilty" resources supply the response to the nihilism charge.

### Q14: Apply postmodern theory to a current media event

I take as my current media event the slide-31 cluster on fake news and AI-generated imagery, Trump's "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE", the *Washington Post* coverage of Trump's fake *Time* Magazine cover, the Ipsos poll finding that 74% across 29 countries think AI makes fake news easier, the AI-generated ICE image with wrong-uniform and missing-windows annotation, and the AI-generated CCTV-style "evidence" images. I work through Lyotard, Baudrillard and Jameson in turn, draw on slide-35's invitation to think this material through Baudrillard's concept of the "pure event", and conclude with a synthesis.

Lyotard's lens is sharpest on the epistemological dimension. The slide-31 cluster presupposes the absence of any authoritative grand narrative about what counts as evidence. In a media ecology with a shared rule for adjudicating images, fake covers and AI-generated photographs would be quickly identified and dismissed. In the actual postmodern condition, "no single media narrative can claim authority over others" (Lecture 16 notes) and "Media supports / undermines grand-narratives" (slide 8). The Ipsos finding of 74% recognising AI's role in fake news is a survey of audiences who already know they inhabit Lyotard's plural language games. Performativity governs: the image works if it satisfies the audience's constituency, regardless of its provenance.

Lyotard would predict that fact-checking will not by itself eliminate the cluster, because fact-checking operates inside one language game (mainstream professional journalism) while the fakes operate inside others (partisan amplification networks). The two games meet only as a differend; fact-checks are read inside the partisan networks as further evidence of mainstream conspiracy. Trump's "THE LEAKS ARE REAL, THE NEWS IS FAKE" formula is precisely this differend made slogan, the partisan reframing of every mainstream finding as the dishonest output of an opposing game.

Baudrillard's lens is sharpest on the ontological dimension. The AI-generated ICE image and the AI-generated CCTV-style "evidence" on slide 31 are fourth-order simulacra: signs that "bears no relation to any reality whatsoever; pure simulacrum" (slide 15). They confirm Baudrillard's diagnosis that the implosion of real and represented has progressed beyond the third order (hyperreality, where the model precedes and exceeds the real) to the fourth, with the Swedish Fish Oreos figure of slide 16 as the lecture's own image of the fourth order in miniature.

Baudrillard's analysis of dissimulation applies sharply. The lecture notes' formulation, "Baudrillard argues that it's not possible to dissimulate gives the example of simulating an armed robbery (not possible as to simulate would be to conduct an armed robbery)" (Lecture 16 notes), maps directly onto the fake *Time* cover and the fabricated CCTV images. The fake is not a pretence of evidence; the fake is an evidentiary act inside the information ecology that consumes it. The gap between simulating and doing has collapsed.

Slide 35's prompt about the "pure event" sharpens the analysis. Baudrillard called 9/11 a "pure event…the revenge of the real", an event that temporarily broke the flow of hyperreality. The slide-31 cluster is the opposite: not a "pure event" but the steady-state hyperreality that 9/11 briefly interrupted. The lesson of slide 35 is that pure events are rare; most of the time, the media environment is the dissimulation that Trump's fake *Time* cover and the AI ICE image exemplify.

Jameson's lens is sharpest on the political-economic dimension. The slide-31 cluster is the predictable output of late capitalism in its current stage. Slide 22 reminds us that "in this phase of capitalism, capital comes to dominate media and cultural production, and hence the sphere of ideas". The Ipsos poll's 74% finding is a global recognition of this domination: media saturation produces fake-news outputs as routine product, and audiences know it.

Fisher's precorporation applies. The cultural responses to the slide-31 cluster (technical watermarking, media-literacy education, individual scepticism) are framed by capitalist logic before they enter consciousness; structural alternatives are precorporated out of the imagination. The "absorbent and plastic quality" Fisher (2009: 6) ascribes to capitalism is visible in how the fake-news ecology itself becomes content, news about fake news, podcasts about misinformation, reaction videos to viral fakes, all of which sustain rather than disrupt the system.

The synthesis. Lyotard explains why the slide-31 cluster succeeds in the media ecology: there is no authoritative game with which to adjudicate it. Baudrillard explains what the cluster is ontologically: fourth-order simulacra whose simulation is the event. Jameson and Fisher explain why the cluster proliferates: late capitalism's domination of media production produces engagement-maximising fakes as routine output and precorporates the political imagination that could regulate them. The three lenses together produce a full diagnosis that no single lens provides.

The proper response combines all three. Lyotard suggests building local truth practices that build authority through method rather than claim to metanarrative. Baudrillard suggests media-phenomenological literacy: teaching audiences that fourth-order simulacra exist and how to read for them. Jameson and Fisher suggest political-economic intervention: democratic constraints on the cultural production apparatus that late capitalism has built. None is sufficient alone; together they constitute a postmodern response to a postmodern crisis.

In conclusion, the slide-31 fake-news and AI-imagery cluster is a textbook demonstration of Lyotard's epistemological diagnosis, Baudrillard's ontological diagnosis, and Jameson's and Fisher's political-economic diagnosis. Slide 35's "pure event" prompt frames the cluster correctly: this is not the revenge of the real but the steady-state hyperreality the lecture has been describing throughout.

### Q15: Postmodernism as truth of late capitalism, not its critique

I answer this question by arguing that this is Jameson's deepest insight: postmodernism is the truth of late capitalism rather than its critique, because postmodernism describes the cultural logic that late capitalism actually produces. I work through the claim, distinguish strong and weak versions, defend the strong version against Lyotardian objections, and conclude with a qualification about the possibility of critique inside the postmodern condition.

The thesis is that postmodernism, as cultural sensibility, theoretical orientation and aesthetic practice, is not external to late capitalism but is its characteristic self-expression. Postmodern fragmentation, polyvocality, irony, pastiche and incredulity towards grand narratives are the cultural logic produced by global financialised capitalism. Lyotard's diagnosis of language games is the philosophical version of capitalism's actual dissolution of shared frameworks. Baudrillard's hyperreality is the philosophical version of capitalism's actual production of sign value. The theorists are not standing outside the system describing it; they are inside the system articulating it.

I distinguish a strong and a weak version. The weak version is that postmodernism happens to coincide historically with late capitalism, the periodisation matches. The strong version is that postmodernism is structurally produced by late capitalism, its theoretical forms could not have arisen without the political-economic transformations of the 1970s onwards. I defend the strong version. The end of the Bretton Woods system, the deregulation of financial capital, the de-industrialisation of the global North, the rise of service and information economies, the globalisation of supply chains, the financialisation of everyday life, these transformations produced the social conditions in which grand narratives collapse, language games proliferate and sign value displaces use value. Lyotard, Baudrillard and Jameson are theorising the social form of which they are themselves the cultural symptoms.

The Lyotardian objection is that postmodernism contains a critical edge, particularly in its anti-totalising stance toward authoritarian metanarratives, as the slide-34 "not guilty" case maintains. I grant the critical edge but argue that even the critical edge is recuperable by capitalism. Fisher's "absorbent and plastic quality" (Fisher, 2009: 6) explains why: late capitalism "can incorporate anything, even the apocalypse" (Lecture 16, slide 27), so the polyvocal celebration of difference, the ironic deflation of authoritarian pomposity, even the postmodern aesthetic itself, all become marketable identities. Slides 24–26's post-apocalyptic cycle (*The Walking Dead*, *Fallout 76*, *The Last of Us*) is the most vivid illustration: even imagined civilisational critique is sold back as prestige entertainment.

Jameson's strong version of the thesis explains things no rival explains. It explains why postmodern theory was so quickly adopted by the academy in the late twentieth century, the universities themselves were being marketised, and postmodernism was the cultural form that legitimated that marketisation. It explains why postmodern aesthetic forms (pastiche, irony, surface, sampling) became the dominant aesthetics of advertising and entertainment industries, these aesthetics are perfectly adapted to commodity culture. It explains why postmodernism's political effects were so weak relative to its theoretical sweep, the theory had no leverage on the system because it was the system's own self-expression.

I qualify the thesis in two ways. First, the strong version risks reductionism. Not every postmodern work is reducible to its capitalist function; Strinati notes that *Miami Vice* "did not so much reject narrative as such, but rather parodied and stylised the established conventions of the genre" (Strinati, 1995: 220), and the slide-34 "not guilty" case lists resources (irony, polyvocality, alternative modalities of critique) that retain critical purchase. The thesis identifies a dominant tendency, not a complete capture. Second, the thesis must allow for the possibility of critique inside the postmodern condition, or it slides into Fisher's despair. Slide 28's hauntology suggests a way out: capitalist media is haunted by ghosts of former utopias, and the work of mourning those utopias can itself be the seed of new political imagination.

This is the question's deepest provocation. If postmodernism is the truth of late capitalism, can it also be its critique? My answer is conditional. Postmodernism can be critique only when it is supplemented by political-economic analysis (Jameson, Fisher), by Strinati's reminder of the materialist limits of postmodern theory, and by the dialogical and cultural-populist correctives Strinati flags as routes for postmodernism to "overcome some of these limits by bringing in other theories in dialogue with post-modernism" (Lecture 16, slide 38). Postmodernism alone, as theoretical orientation without these supplements, cannot critique what it is.

I also note the meta-irony. To call postmodernism the truth of late capitalism is itself a grand narrative, a claim about the structural relation between cultural form and economic substrate. The claim performs the contradiction it diagnoses. I take this as evidence that the postmodern condition does not preclude theoretical work; it requires that theoretical work be self-aware about its own conditions of possibility. Jameson and Fisher both write inside this self-awareness, which is part of what makes their critique productive.

In conclusion, postmodernism is the truth of late capitalism rather than its external critique. Its theoretical forms, language games, hyperreality, pastiche, capitalist realism, describe the cultural logic that late capitalism actually produces. The strong thesis explains things no rival explains, including the academy's adoption of postmodernism, the dominance of postmodern aesthetics in commodity culture (Strinati's catalogue of cinema, television, advertising and pop music), and the political weakness of postmodernism as a critical force. The thesis must be qualified by recognition of works that retain critical purchase inside the condition (the slide-34 "not guilty" resources, Strinati's residual-parody observation about *Miami Vice*), and by the possibility of critique inside late capitalism that operates through the dialogical and cultural-populist supplementations slide 38 names. The proper response is to take postmodernism seriously as the truth of our condition without surrendering the effort to think and act against it.
