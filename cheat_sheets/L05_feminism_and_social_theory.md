## 3. Key notes from the presentation (with slide-question callouts)

**Slide 1–2: Framing the lecture.** Feminism aims to challenge what is taken as appropriate behaviour/roles for women; "the more people that feminism can 'talk for', the stronger its arguments" — but the lecturer immediately problematises this. The main question for the exam: *can or should feminism attempt to talk for all women?*

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "whether feminism can or should attempt to talk for all women, or not… Or maybe you disagree?" — this is the live question I am being asked to take a position on. I will refuse a simple yes/no and argue that feminism must do both: hold open a strategic collective subject while practising internal critique.

**Slide 3: First-wave feminism.** Post-French Revolution (1789–) liberal/equal-rights feminism; the Enlightenment background; reacts against the exclusion of women from the universality of rights; focuses on the vote and equal pay; key text Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792).

**Slide 4: But why should women want to be like men?**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club? Or should it aim to change the rules? Or to start another club?" — my answer: the rules themselves must change, because joining the club leaves the patriarchal architecture intact (Beauvoir's point about woman-as-other, hooks' point about the master's house).

**Slides 5–7: Beauvoir and the move toward second-wave feminism.** Writing in the 1940s–50s when the "aim" of life for many women was to "marry well"; *The Second Sex* (1949) asks "what is a woman?" (de Beauvoir, 1988 [1953]: 13). She insists there is no essential thing that defines women; rather, woman is a status produced through experience.

**Slide 6: A word on "gender".** Beauvoir uses *genre*; the Latin *genus* indicates type/kind; gender as an arbitrary category for organising the world — important for later post-structuralist moves (Butler).

**Slide 8: Three key points.** No essence of woman; "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman"; woman has the EXPERIENCE of being the "other" of men.

**Slide 9: "Other" and bifurcations.** "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 2010 [1949], *The Second Sex*: 6).

**Slide 10: Women as objects, not subjects.** "The representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men; they describe it from the point of view which is theirs and which they confuse with the absolute truth" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 236). Note the Disney-princess image — a contemporary mass-cultural illustration of male-authored representation.

**Slide 11: Critique of Beauvoir.**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Does de Beauvoir try to 'universalize' her argument? Is her account historically and socially limited (she is a middle class, white, European intellectual)? Can she really describe the way ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" — my answer: no. This is exactly the opening that Black feminism, postcolonial feminism, and post-structuralism rush into.

**Slides 12–15: Second-wave and the personal is political.** Patriarchy, reproduction as labour, exploitation of women's work. The private/public split is historically produced with industrial capitalism: production leaves the home, "you go **out** to work, in factories", domesticity is invented as private and feminised. Women become inconspicuous reproducers of the wage-worker and of future wage-labourers; "Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers" (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). The domestic sphere becomes invisible.

**Slide 16: Three lessons.** Different standpoints/perspectives matter; experiences reveal the operations of power; experience has a duality.

**Slide 17: Arguing for difference.** Feminism before the 1980s is criticised on three counts: it universalises (a shared history of oppression is presumed), it essentialises (being a woman is enough), and it is ahistorical (e.g. it ignores how white women have benefited from colonialism).

**Slides 19–20: Black feminist thought, matrix of domination, intersectionality.** "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Matrix of domination: "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Collins). Gender does not exist on its own; "we can't think gender without thinking 'race'". Crenshaw's intersectionality: different types of discrimination intersect and compound experiences of exclusion.

**Slides 21–22: bell hooks and the home as resistance.** Collins and hooks question the second-wave reading of home as pure repression — for Black women under racial terror the home is also refuge and resistance. The oppositional gaze: "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring… The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115).

**Slides 23–25: Hartsock and feminist materialism.** Women are both insiders (essential to the system's continuation) and outsiders (no power within it); this dual position is epistemologically privileged. "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142).

**Slides 26–27: Post-structuralist feminism and Butler.** Identities are not fixed; "woman" is not a stable category; sex and gender are signifiers, not labels of given objects. Butler: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics… Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). This opens the door to queer theory and to questioning the unified subject.

**Slide 28: Conclusion.** Feminism introduces new concepts (sex–gender, essentialism, the politics of birth as opposed to a male preoccupation with death); it shows how the world could be otherwise; and it leaves us with the standing question: can/should feminism speak for all women?

## 4. Information from the readings

### Harding (ed.), *The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, Intro pp. 4–7 and ch. 8 ("Strong Objectivity")

Harding sets up standpoint theory as a response to a need: "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women" (Harding, *Standpoint Reader*, p. 4). The "implied 'speaker' of scientific… sentences was never women. It was supposed to be humanity in general" — what Haraway calls "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (p. 4).

Harding's diagnosis is structural, not about bad individuals: "The problem was not prejudiced and biased individuals… Rather, it was a different kind of obstacle… The conceptual frameworks themselves promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns" (pp. 4–5). The disciplines were "complicitous with sexist and androcentric agendas" (p. 5).

Politics is therefore not a contaminant of knowledge but a resource: "politics and culture often function as 'prisonhouses' of knowledge… Yet they can and often do also function as 'toolboxes'" (p. 5). The remedy is to "start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities" (p. 6). But this immediately raises the controversial issue Harding identifies in the intro: "How does and should standpoint theory account for and engage with differences between women?" (p. 7) — the same question my exam asks.

In ch. 8, Harding develops **strong objectivity**. The conventional notion is "not rigorous or objectifying enough" (p. 128). Standpoint theory does not commit the **God-trick**, is not ethnocentric (because standpoint theorists "all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge", p. 129), and is not relativist ("It is not equally true… that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history", pp. 131–132). The **subject of knowledge** for standpoint theory is "embodied and visible… multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (p. 134). Strong objectivity demands "strong reflexivity": "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (p. 136). Crucially, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (p. 135) — feminism is not the property of women.

### Adichie, *We Should All Be Feminists* (Vintage Short, 2014; based on TEDxEuston 2012)

Adichie's argument is short, autobiographical, and forceful. She defines feminism using the dictionary: "a person who believes in the social, political, and economic equality of the sexes". She tells the story of being called a feminist at fourteen by her friend Okoloma, "the same tone with which a person would say, 'You're a supporter of terrorism.'" She enumerates the negative baggage: "You hate men, you hate bras, you hate African culture… you don't shave, you're always angry, you don't have a sense of humor".

Her childhood Igbo example (the class monitor): "Then, to my surprise, my teacher said the monitor had to be a boy… If we do something over and over, it becomes normal… If we keep seeing only men as heads of corporations, it starts to seem 'natural' that only men should be heads of corporations." Her Lagos examples — being refused entry to clubs alone, being thanked through her male friend Louis for money she had given a parking attendant, being assumed to be a sex worker in hotel lobbies — work as concrete intersectional evidence of how gender shapes everyday life across class.

Adichie's central reframings:
- **Anger is legitimate.** "Gender as it functions today is a grave injustice. I am angry. We should all be angry."
- **Masculinity damages men.** "Masculinity is a hard, small cage, and we put boys inside this cage… The harder a man feels compelled to be, the weaker his ego is."
- **Femininity should not have to apologise.** "I have chosen to no longer be apologetic for my femininity."
- **Culture is not destiny.** "Culture does not make people. People make culture. If it is true that the full humanity of women is not our culture, then we can and must make it our culture."
- **The case for the *word* "feminist".** "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Adichie also pushes back on the "un-African" charge — feminism is not a Western import; her great-grandmother, who ran away from a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." This is important for the universalising critique: a properly intersectional feminism can find local feminist genealogies without rebranding them as Western.

### Criado-Perez, "The deadly truth about a world built for men" (*Guardian*, 23 Feb 2019; extract from *Invisible Women*)

Criado-Perez documents the **gender data gap** — a "female-shaped 'absent presence'" in design, science, and public policy. Key empirical anchors:
- **The reference man.** Toxicology and occupational safety still rely on a Caucasian male, 25–30, 70kg. "Reference Man… his superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not."
- **Crash-test dummies.** Until 2011 US tests used only a 50th-percentile male; even now, EU regulation does not require an anthropometrically female dummy; the "female" dummy used in some tests is "just a scaled-down male dummy". Result: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured… 17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash.
- **PPE.** Body armour and stab vests sized for male bodies. A British female police officer was stabbed and killed in 1997 after removing her armour to use a hydraulic ram; another had breast-reduction surgery because of the vest. 95% of female emergency-service workers report PPE hampers their work.
- **Office temperature.** A 1960s formula based on the resting metabolic rate of the average male overestimates female metabolism by up to 35%; offices are on average five degrees too cold for women.
- **Phones, voice recognition, Apple Health.** Smartphones at the upper limit of male hand size; Google speech recognition 70% more likely to recognise male speech accurately; Apple Health launched without a period tracker.
- **Toilets.** "50/50 division of floor space has even been formalised in plumbing codes" — but women need more time and trips, so formal equality is substantive inequality.

This article is gold for the standpoint argument: starting from women's bodies and women's daily life generates *more accurate* science (Harding's strong objectivity), and starting from men's bodies as the default is not neutral — it is, in Harding's terms, the God-trick in engineering.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The arc of feminist theory in this lecture is a movement from rights, to experience, to standpoint, to difference, to deconstruction — and the question I am asked is whether this trajectory empties feminism of any usable subject. I argue it does not, provided we read each move as a correction rather than a replacement.

**Wollstonecraft's liberal feminism is necessary but insufficient.** It treats women as already-formed rational subjects who have been wrongly excluded; this leaves intact the very norm against which they are measured. The slide's deliberately provocative question — "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club?" — names the limit of equal-rights feminism. Beauvoir's intervention is precisely to refuse the male norm: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Beauvoir's anti-essentialism — "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" — is the conceptual hinge of the whole module. It makes gender a social process, not a biological destiny, and it grounds the second-wave slogan that the personal is political.

**Patriarchy and the public/private split are not eternal facts but historical products.** Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) historicise the very domesticity that earlier liberal feminism took for granted: with industrial capitalism, "you go **out** to work, in factories", and the home is reinvented as a feminised private. Hartsock's contribution is to translate this materialist insight into an epistemology. Women's dual position — insiders to social reproduction, outsiders to power — generates a privileged vantage point: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The Criado-Perez piece is, in effect, a 21st-century vindication of this claim: starting from women's bodies in nail salons, in cars, in stab vests, in offices, produces better engineering — empirically less partial, more accurate, "strong objectivity" in Harding's sense.

**But standpoint theory is not safe from its own universalising tendencies.** Hill Collins, Crenshaw, and hooks all insist that "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70); the matrix of domination shows that "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Collins). Crenshaw's intersectionality is the conceptual tool here; hooks' oppositional gaze — "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) — names the active, resistant subject that flat universalism misses. Importantly, Collins and hooks also rewrite the second-wave reading of "home": for Black women living under racial terror, home is not only a site of patriarchal repression but also a refuge and an organising base.

**Butler radicalises this further.** "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). This is the queer theoretical move: the unified subject that liberal feminism relied on is itself an effect of power, and any "we" implicates exclusions (of trans women, of intersex people, of non-binary subjects). Performativity replaces essence; the destabilisation of "woman" is not the end of feminism but the condition of a more inclusive politics.

**Where this leaves the exam question.** I am persuaded by Harding's resolution: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). Adichie supplies the practical correlate — the term "feminist" must be retained because dropping it for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded". "Feminism" and "feminisms" are therefore not rivals: they are two sides of one practice. Feminism names the collective political subject under construction; feminisms names the plural standpoints whose internal argument keeps that subject honest.

## 8. Essay answers

### Past-Q 2024 (Q4): Is 'feminisms' a better way to understand women's experiences than 'feminism'?

I argue that the plural and the singular are not rivals: "feminisms" is the better description of the field, but "feminism" remains a necessary political fiction. Dropping the singular concedes too much; refusing the plural concedes too little.

The case for "feminisms" is overwhelming once one looks closely at women's lives. The lecture slides reach this conclusion explicitly: feminism before the 1980s was universalising (it presumed a shared history of oppression), essentialising (it presumed being a woman is enough) and ahistorical (it neglected how white women have benefited from colonialism). Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) names a real structural condition — "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6) — but the lecture immediately asks: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir is a middle-class, white, European intellectual, and what is universalised under "woman" too often turns out to be one specific class location.

Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks make this concrete. "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70), and the matrix of domination shows that gender always operates with race and class. Crenshaw's intersectionality, originally developed to explain why Black women workers fell through both racial-discrimination law and sex-discrimination law, is the conceptual reason we must speak in the plural. hooks' rewriting of the home — "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115) — refuses the second-wave reading that home is uniformly repressive; for Black women under racial terror, home is also refuge.

Concrete examples follow easily. Lean In feminism, which urges women to ask harder for raises and promotions, presupposes a corporate ladder already accessible to a particular class of (largely white, college-educated) women; it has very little to say to the care workers, mainly women of colour, whose unpaid and underpaid labour holds families together — a fact made brutally visible during the pandemic when care work and "essential" work were revealed to overlap. The Roe v Wade reversal in 2022 hits women very differently along racial and economic lines: a wealthy woman in Texas flies to New York; a poor woman, disproportionately Black or Latina, carries an unwanted pregnancy. Iran's "women, life, freedom" movement after the killing of Mahsa Amini is feminist, but it is not the same feminism as the trans-rights debates roiling the UK; menstrual-leave policies in Spain and Japan look feminist in one frame and patronising in another. To force all of these into one "feminism" is to make Beauvoir's mistake again.

And yet I want to resist the conclusion that we should simply drop the singular. The pluralist position has its own pathology: if every standpoint is irreducibly its own, there is no political subject left at all, and feminism dissolves into a thousand identity claims. Judith Butler's caution — "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2) — does not mean abandoning "woman", it means handling the category carefully. Adichie puts the point in its most usable form: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." The Criado-Perez evidence is decisive on this. When crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, voice recognition and Apple Health are designed around the reference man, women of every race, class and sexuality are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash, freeze in the office, and find their phones unsafe to use one-handed at a protest. These are not symbolic harms. They name a structural condition that crosses every "intersection".

Sandra Harding offers the synthesis I find most convincing: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). This is exactly the relationship I want to defend between feminism and feminisms. The plural names the *internal pluralism of the movement*; the singular names the *external political subject* the movement constructs in order to make the kind of claim Adichie makes when she says, simply, "I am a feminist."

So my answer is: feminisms describes the field; feminism names the practice. To pick one without the other is to lose either the descriptive accuracy that standpoint and intersectional theory have so painfully won, or the political traction without which feminist claims have no purchase. The exam question pushes for a choice; I refuse the false choice. We need both terms, and the productive tension between them, to understand women's experiences — experiences that are radically plural in form and yet, as Criado-Perez shows, still recognisably gendered in effect. ### Past-Q 2025 (Q5): Outline and assess the political importance of feminism today.

Feminism remains politically important today because the structural problem it identifies — that women, as a class, are positioned as "other" to a male norm that masquerades as universal — is empirically alive in 2026, even as the form of that othering has shifted. I will outline the political agenda that follows from this and then assess its limits.

The outline first. Liberal first-wave feminism, in Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792), set the equal-rights agenda: vote, education, equal pay. That agenda is unfinished — the gender pay gap persists across OECD economies — but it is no longer sufficient. Simone de Beauvoir's diagnosis remains the live one: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Second-wave feminism translated this into a political programme — "the personal is political" — that connected patriarchy, reproduction-as-labour and the exploitation of women's work, showing that the public/private split was a historical product of industrial capitalism (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). Black feminist thought, intersectionality and post-structuralist feminism refined this further: Collins' matrix of domination, Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze, Butler's destabilisation of "woman".

Now the assessment. Why is this politically important *now*?

First, because the gender data gap is killing women. Caroline Criado-Perez documents that crash-test dummies until 2011 were modelled on a 50th-percentile male, and even now the EU does not require an anthropometrically female dummy. Result: a woman in a comparable car crash is 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die. Police stab vests do not fit women; in 1997 a British officer was stabbed to death after removing her armour to use a hydraulic ram. Heart-attack diagnostic criteria were built on male symptoms, so women's "atypical" presentations are missed in A&E. Office temperatures are five degrees too cold for the female metabolic rate. Apple Health launched without a period tracker. These are not abstract grievances; they are the price of an engineering culture that takes the "reference man" as the human norm. Feminism is the only political movement systematically attending to this.

Second, because reproductive rights are in retreat. The 2022 Dobbs decision reversed Roe v Wade; abortion is now illegal or near-illegal in much of the US South. Sex-work decriminalisation debates, menstrual leave policies, and the regulation of fertility technologies are all live legislative questions in 2026. None of these can be addressed without a vocabulary that names women's specific bodily situation — which is precisely what "feminism", as opposed to "human rights" in general, supplies. Adichie's defence stands: "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Third, because care work is a public crisis. The pandemic made it impossible to ignore that the global economy runs on (mostly women's) unpaid and low-paid care. Nancy Hartsock's point — "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284) — is now a budget question. Care workers, disproportionately women of colour and migrants, hold the social fabric together while being structurally invisible.

Fourth, because of #MeToo and what came after it. The 2017 wave that toppled Weinstein and others was, sociologically, a standpoint moment: it built collective insight out of shared but previously private experience, exactly as Hartsock predicted. #MeToo's limits — its uneven reach into low-wage workplaces, its complicated relationship with due process — are real, but they are arguments for *more* feminism, not for replacing feminism with something blander.

Fifth, because the global picture is intensifying, not relaxing. Iran's "women, life, freedom" movement, after the killing of Mahsa Amini, is a feminist movement under conditions of state violence. Afghan women have been forced back out of public life. India's gang-rape cases continue. These cannot be dealt with through liberal-equality language alone because the situations are not symmetrical; the analytical apparatus of patriarchy, of woman-as-other, and of intersectionality is needed.

I must assess feminism's limits honestly. Lean In corporate feminism has been rightly criticised by Black feminists and labour scholars for marketing class-bound professional advancement as women's liberation. The current trans-rights debates have split older feminists from younger ones in ways that have damaged feminist alliances. Some forms of carceral feminism have collaborated with punitive state power against the very communities they claim to defend. These are real internal pathologies. They are also, as Harding insists, reasons to plurialise the term ("feminisms") while keeping the practice ("feminism").

In sum, the political importance of feminism today is high and rising. It is the only language that simultaneously names the historical specificity of women's subordination, refuses to flatten that subordination into a single experience, and provides the analytic tools — patriarchy, standpoint, intersectionality, the gender data gap — for diagnosing material harms in real institutions. To abandon the word now would be to lose, in Harding's words, the resource through which "a social and political disadvantage can be turned into" a critical insight. We need that resource. (Word count: 748.)

### Past-Q 2023 (Q5): Should sociology use the term "feminism" or "feminisms"?

Sociology should use both terms, deliberately, and should resist the rhetorical pressure to pick one. This is not a fudge; it reflects how sociological knowledge is actually produced — by holding analytic categories open enough to capture a structural pattern while keeping them porous enough to admit internal difference. I will defend this position in three steps: the case for the singular, the case for the plural, and the case for their disciplined coexistence.

The case for the singular ("feminism") rests on the fact that sociology must be able to name structures. Simone de Beauvoir's claim — "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6) — describes a structural pattern of othering that crosses cultures, classes and historical periods even as it takes different concrete forms. Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap is a striking sociological demonstration: in 2026 women are still 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash because the engineering profession has, for half a century, treated the male body as the human default. The thermostat in the office, the stab vest, the smartphone, the voice-recognition system, Apple Health's missing period tracker — these are all the same sociological fact, namely that the "reference man" stands in for humanity. Sociology needs a name for that fact. "Feminism" is that name. To abandon the singular is to give up on the claim that there is a recognisable, describable pattern. Adichie makes the same point politically: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

The case for the plural ("feminisms") is just as strong sociologically. The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. Patricia Hill Collins formulates the Black feminist counter-position: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender does not "exist on its own"; it is produced through race, class, sexuality, and disability simultaneously. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalised this for legal scholarship and then for sociology. bell hooks' rewriting of the home — "There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) — refuses the second-wave assumption that the domestic sphere is uniformly repressive. Judith Butler pushes the move further: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). For Butler the unified category "woman" is itself a production of power, and a sociology that uses the term naively will reproduce exclusions — historically of trans women, of working-class women, of women of colour.

This pluralism is not an academic affectation; it has empirical purchase. Sociologically, "feminism in the singular" cannot explain why the Roe reversal in 2022 hit Black and Latina women in the US South harder than wealthier white women in coastal states. It cannot explain why menstrual-leave policies look emancipatory in some contexts and patronising in others. It cannot explain why the Iranian women-life-freedom movement and the UK trans-rights debate look almost incommensurable. Adichie's own essay is a beautifully empirical demonstration: her Igbo background, her Lagos clubs and hotels, her great-grandmother who refused a forced marriage, all show that feminist consciousness arises in many forms with many vocabularies, and that "Culture does not make people. People make culture."

So far this looks like a straightforward win for "feminisms". But the case for their coexistence is the sociologically interesting one. Sandra Harding gives the cleanest statement: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). What Harding is describing is the way sociology actually works: we generalise *and* we attend to difference, in the same breath. The plural names the internal pluralism of standpoints; the singular names the political subject those standpoints jointly construct.

A purely pluralist sociology would face two problems. First, it would lose the capacity to make structural claims of the Criado-Perez type — claims that hold for women as such, across other axes of difference. Second, it would underestimate the way "feminisms" can serve as a covert essentialism in its own right (e.g. when "Black feminism" is used to mean a single thing rather than a plural tradition in turn). Conversely, a purely singular sociology repeats Beauvoir's mistake of universalising one location.

My recommendation, then, is that sociology should treat "feminism" and "feminisms" as a dialectical pair. We use the singular when we are making a structural claim about patriarchy, the gender data gap, the public/private split, the standpoint of social reproduction. We use the plural when we are attending to the standpoints of Black, working-class, lesbian, trans, Igbo, Iranian and other women — whose differences are not noise around a signal but are themselves the signal. To insist on one term is to lose half the analytic work sociology has to do. (Word count: 748.)

### Past-Q 2022 (Q8): Should women's experience be the basis of feminism?

Women's experience should be a foundational input to feminism, but it cannot be its sole basis without producing precisely the universalising, essentialising and ahistorical errors that Black, post-structuralist and standpoint feminism have spent forty years correcting. The right answer is the one Sandra Harding develops: experience is the *starting point* but not the *foundation* of feminist knowledge.

The case for experience is the strongest part of the feminist tradition. Beauvoir built *The Second Sex* on the claim that the lived experience of being woman — "Women have the EXPERIENCE of being considered the 'other' of men" — had never been theorised. Her famous formulation, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman", makes experience constitutive rather than incidental. The second-wave slogan "the personal is political" extended this: experiences that had been quarantined as private (housework, sexual violence, reproductive control) were revealed to be political because their pattern was systemic. Nancy Hartsock's feminist standpoint translated experience into epistemology: women's "dual social role" as both insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power) generates a critical vantage on patriarchy, just as the proletariat's dual position generated Marx's critique of bourgeois ideology (Hartsock, 1983: 142). Adichie's TEDx talk is a contemporary refresh of this argument: she does not start from theory but from being a fourteen-year-old Igbo girl called a feminist by her friend, from being thanked through her male friend Louis by a Lagos parking attendant, from being assumed to be a sex worker in a hotel lobby. Experience generates the analysis.

The Criado-Perez evidence shows the analytic power of starting from experience. Sandi Toksvig's anthropology professor reframes an antler bone with 28 markings: a male anthropologist sees "man's first attempt at a calendar"; a female anthropologist sees a woman tracking her cycle. The interpretive difference flows from a different bodily experience of time. The same logic explains why Apple Health launched without a period tracker, why office thermostats freeze women, why stab vests get women killed: a male-only experiential base produced systematically defective knowledge. Starting from women's experience would have produced better engineering, better medicine, better policy. This is Harding's strong objectivity: politically engaged knowledge that starts from marginalised standpoints is, empirically, more accurate.

But experience is also dangerous as a *foundation*. Three problems.

First, the universalising problem. The lecture asks of Beauvoir directly: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir's "woman" is implicitly a middle-class, white, European intellectual. Patricia Hill Collins responds by insisting on Black women's specific standpoint: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender is always co-produced with race and class; a feminism that takes one experience as the experience effaces this.

Second, the essentialising problem. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* warns that "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). If we ground feminism in "women's experience", we have to specify whose experience counts as women's — and any specification will exclude. Trans women's experiences? Intersex experiences? Asexual women's experiences? Women without children? Each line we draw reproduces a power-laden boundary.

Third, the empirical problem. Adichie tells of an academic who told her feminism was "un-African". Adichie's response is that her great-grandmother, who fled a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." Experience alone, without an interpretive frame, does not arrive labelled "feminist"; the same experiences are mobilised by anti-feminist as well as feminist analyses (e.g. "but what is a girl doing in a room with four boys?"). Experience requires theory to become knowledge.

Harding's resolution is the most defensible. Standpoint theory holds that "Women's lives and experiences provide the 'grounds' for this knowledge, though these clearly do not provide foundations for knowledge in the conventional philosophical sense. These grounds are the site, the activities, from which scientific questions arise" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 128). Experience is the necessary starting point; reflexive theoretical work — strong objectivity — is what turns it into knowledge. And, Harding insists, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (p. 135). Feminism is not the property of those who have the relevant experiences; it is a theoretical and political practice that *begins* with those experiences but is owed by everyone.

So my answer is: yes, women's experience should be the basis of feminism, in the sense of the starting point; no, it cannot be the foundation, in the sense of an unmediated ground that licenses claims by itself. To collapse the distinction is either to repeat Beauvoir's universalising move (one experience for all women) or to dissolve feminism into a thousand incommensurable testimonies. The discipline of standpoint theory keeps both errors at bay. (Word count: 749.)

### Past-Q 2021 (Q8): Is it still possible to talk of "feminism" as a unified project or do we need to use the term "feminisms"?

It is still possible — and politically necessary — to talk of feminism as a unified project, provided we understand that unity in the right way. The unity is not of content (one experience, one theory, one programme) but of *practice*: a shared commitment to identifying and dismantling patriarchy in its many forms. Within that practice, "feminisms" in the plural names the internal pluralism that keeps the practice honest. I argue, against the implied dichotomy of the question, that the two terms are complementary rather than rival.

The case for "feminisms" is, by 2026, almost the default position in academic sociology, and rightly so. The lecture identifies three failures of feminism before the 1980s: it universalised (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialised (presuming being a woman is enough) and was ahistorical (ignoring, for instance, how white women had benefited from colonialism). Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks gave us the conceptual tools to repair these failures. Collins' matrix of domination ("structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression"), Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze — all insist that "gender" is never produced in isolation from race, class, sexuality, ability. Judith Butler radicalises the move: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). Sociologically, the plural is needed because women's lives are radically different across location: a Black domestic worker in Mississippi, an Igbo writer in Lagos, an Iranian student in Tehran, a Polish nurse in London, a trans woman in São Paulo, a stay-at-home parent in Tokyo. To force these into one "feminism" is to repeat Beauvoir's universalising error — the error that the lecture deliberately puts on the table when it asks whether Beauvoir, a middle-class, white, European intellectual, can really speak for all women.

But the case for the singular "feminism" is also strong, and is sometimes underestimated by the pluralist consensus. Three points.

First, there is a structural pattern that holds across difference. Caroline Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* shows that crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, smartphones, voice-recognition systems and clinical trials are all built around a "reference man", and that women — across race and class — pay the price: 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash; freezing in offices set to a male metabolism; killed in policing accidents because body armour leaves them unprotected. This is the kind of finding that "feminism" in the singular is needed to name. Crenshaw's intersectionality refines the analysis, but it does not erase the pattern.

Second, "feminism" performs political work that "feminisms" cannot. Adichie defends the term directly: "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." When she stands on a TEDx stage and says "I am a feminist", she is doing something the plural cannot do — naming a political subject. The Iranian women-life-freedom movement, the Polish black-protest march against abortion restrictions, the global #MeToo wave: each of these is empowered by being able to speak under one banner without forfeiting internal difference.

Third, Sandra Harding's strong objectivity argument is the philosophical synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). The unified project is the project of starting from marginalised lives; the plural is built into that project, not opposed to it.

Concrete examples illustrate the productive tension. Lean In corporate feminism is a feminism that mostly serves white professional women; Black feminism rightly critiques it; the result is a more accurate analysis of class within feminism, not a fragmenting of feminism into incommensurable parts. The 2022 Roe reversal is a single political event with starkly intersectional effects: a wealthy white woman in Texas flies to a legal state; a poor Black woman carries an unwanted pregnancy. Feminism in the singular names the regulatory regime; feminisms in the plural names the differential impact. Trans rights debates in the UK have split feminists — but the dispute, however bitter, presupposes a shared term within which to argue.

Butler herself, in *Gender Trouble*, is not trying to abolish "feminism" — she is trying to make it more honest. The destabilisation of "woman" is the condition of a more inclusive feminism, not a replacement for feminism.

My answer therefore is: yes, it is still possible to talk of feminism as a unified project, and we should, because the alternative is to lose the analytic and political category that allows us to say things like "women are 47% more likely to die in a comparable car crash". And yes, we also need "feminisms", because without internal pluralism the singular collapses back into Beauvoir's universalism. The unified project is unified in *practice*, not in *experience*; the practice is the joint construction, by many different standpoints, of a feminist politics that none of them could construct alone. (Word count: 748.)

### Mock 1: "The personal is political." Discuss in light of #MeToo and post-Roe America.

I argue that the second-wave slogan the personal is political is more analytically useful in 2026 than at any point since its coinage, and that #MeToo and post-Roe America are textbook demonstrations of its force. The slogan does not claim that all private life is identical with the political; it claims that the boundary between personal and political is itself politically produced — and therefore politically contestable. #MeToo and the Dobbs reversal are both that boundary being actively redrawn.

Recall the provenance. Second-wave consciousness-raising in the late 1960s discovered that experiences each woman had treated as her own — housework, marital sex, harassment, body image, reproductive anxiety — were systemically patterned and therefore political. The lecture's account of the public/private split gives the historical move: industrial capitalism produced "the personal" as a depoliticised feminised domestic sphere (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986), and the slogan repoliticises it. Nancy Hartsock translates this epistemologically: "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), and that structural location generates standpoint epistemology.

Apply the slogan to #MeToo. The 2017 wave that toppled Harvey Weinstein and a cascade of others was a textbook the-personal-is-political moment. What women had treated as private misfortune — the hotel-suite meeting, the late-night call, the unsolicited touch — became visible through collective naming as a system. Tarana Burke had been doing the work with young Black women since 2006; the 2017 wave reached unprecedented scale. The conceptual move is exactly the slogan: my experience is not only mine; it is a pattern; the pattern is political. The structural domain (employment law, NDAs), the disciplinary (HR, casting-couch routines), the hegemonic (the joke about "what she was wearing"), and the interpersonal (everyday touching) all participate — Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination is the right map here.

#MeToo also exposes the slogan's class and racial limits, demanding intersectional revision. The 2017 wave foregrounded mostly white celebrity perpetrators and victims; agricultural workers, hotel housekeepers and domestic workers had organised against the same patterns for decades with far less visibility. Alianza Nacional de Campesinas's open letter to Hollywood made the point: the personal is political, but whose personal counts as politically nameable depends on whose voice the public sphere registers. Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality is what lets us see that the same systemic harm operates differently across race and class.

Apply the slogan to post-Roe America. The 2022 Dobbs decision reversed nearly fifty years of federal abortion protection. The "personal" — what happens in a pregnant body — was repoliticised by the state in a regressive direction. Abortion has always been political; the point is that the boundary has been forcibly redrawn, and that the redrawing falls disproportionately on Black, Latina, low-income and rural women. A wealthy Texan flies to New York; a poor woman in Mississippi carries an unwanted pregnancy. The lecture's slide on the public/private split is the exact framework.

Three objections must be met. First, the slogan is too broad: if everything is political, nothing is. This misreads. The slogan claims that the boundary between personal and political is a political artefact, not that all private life is uniformly political. Recognising this opens specific questions: who benefits from this boundary being drawn here? Whose labour is privatised? Whose violence?

Second, the slogan flattens difference. This was Black feminism's legitimate concern. The reply is that the slogan needs to be read plurally: which personal is whose political, and how do they interact? bell hooks's revision — "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life... There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) — uses the slogan while pluralising it. The home that was Betty Friedan's "comfortable concentration camp" was, for Black families under racial terror, also a wall of protection.

Third, the slogan is dated. Against this I set Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap. Crash-test dummies modelled on the reference man (50th-percentile male, 1.77m, 76kg) leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die in a comparable car crash. Office thermostats run five degrees too cold for the female metabolism. Apple Health launched without a period tracker. Voice recognition was 70% more accurate on male speech. Each looks personal — my cold office, my big phone — and is in fact a political arrangement of who counts as the human default.

Adichie's TEDx talk is a sustained the-personal-is-political demonstration: being thanked through her male friend Louis by a Lagos parking attendant, being assumed a sex worker in a hotel lobby, being told her novel was too "angry" — each private moment becomes a political diagnosis. "Culture does not make people. People make culture", she says, and the slogan is the conceptual lever.

The slogan, properly read through intersectional revision, remains one of feminism's most powerful tools. #MeToo and Dobbs are its latest and clearest vindications. (Word count: 745.)

### Mock 2: Critically assess hooks's argument in *Where We Stand: Class Matters* in light of contemporary class-gender politics.

bell hooks's *Where We Stand: Class Matters* (2000) argues that mainstream US feminism has been class-bound from the start, that "the issues of women in poverty" have been pushed to the margins, and that a feminism which does not put class at the centre reproduces the very class hierarchy it claims to dismantle. I assess this argument as broadly correct, sharper than most contemporary intersectional feminism makes it, and decisively vindicated by post-pandemic and post-Dobbs class-gender politics.

hooks's argument runs as follows. Second-wave feminism was dominated, demographically and imaginatively, by college-educated white women whose central grievance was exclusion from professional and creative work. When these women won the right to "lean in", they did so by displacing care work onto poorer women — disproportionately women of colour and migrants — who became the cleaners, nannies and aides whose labour made middle-class feminism's gains possible. Mainstream feminism then converted this displacement into a narrative of women's progress, leaving the women at the bottom of the care chain conceptually invisible. hooks's argument generalises a strand from her earlier work — *Ain't I a Woman* (1981), *Feminist Theory: From Margin to Center* (1984), *Teaching to Transgress* (1994) — and connects it to Patricia Hill Collins's insistence that gender, race and class be analysed as a matrix.

The contemporary evidence is overwhelming. Take three cases.

Care work and the pandemic. When COVID-19 emptied workplaces in 2020, governments discovered that the economy ran on care labour — childcare, eldercare, hospital cleaning, food production — that had been devalued because it was performed by women, disproportionately women of colour and migrants. Female-headed households absorbed the largest share of the closure burden; care workers were "essential" in rhetoric and underpaid in fact. The lean-in frame had nothing to say about this; hooks had been saying it for thirty years. The structural fact she names — that mainstream feminism's professional gains rest on undervalued care work — was simply made visible by a public-health emergency.

The post-Dobbs landscape. The 2022 reversal of Roe v Wade lands very differently across class and race. A wealthy white woman in Texas books a flight to New York; a poor Black or Latina woman in Mississippi carries an unwanted pregnancy or risks an unsafe self-managed abortion. Reproductive justice — the framework developed by Loretta Ross and SisterSong in 1994 — extends hooks's claim directly: rights without resources are class-bound rights. The class-gender politics of Dobbs is exactly what hooks predicted: a juridical event with sharply differentiated class effects, dismissed by lean-in feminism as a "rights" problem when it is also a redistribution problem.

The gender pay gap. The headline gender pay gap masks much larger gaps between Black and Latina women and white men, and between care-sector workers and the professional cohort hooks calls the "feminism of the boardroom". Sheryl Sandberg's *Lean In* (2013) addresses the smaller gap and treats it as the problem; hooks's frame says the larger gap is the problem. Caroline Criado-Perez's reporting in *Invisible Women* extends the point: even where women have entered the professional public sphere, the engineering (PPE, thermostats, crash-test dummies modelled on the reference man) still assumes a male default, and the cost falls hardest on those who cannot afford to opt out of dangerous work.

The argument also has limits.

First, hooks sometimes writes as if class displaces gender, when the analytic point — shared with Collins's matrix of domination — is that the two are mutually constitutive. Read at her best, hooks does not subordinate gender to class; she refuses to subordinate class to gender. The matrix framing is the cleaner version.

Second, hooks's analysis of US class politics maps imperfectly onto European welfare states, which absorb some care-work invisibility into public provision. Northern European feminism's class story is different from the US story; hooks's text needs translation in those contexts. This is not a refutation but a reminder that the matrix has local specifications.

Third, hooks is sometimes accused of romanticising working-class authenticity. The charge has limited force where her prose lingers on "the politics of plainness", but the substantive sociology is unaffected.

A final point. hooks's argument lands particularly hard on lean-in feminism's claim that women's empowerment can be achieved within existing class structures by women asking harder for raises. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* sits more ambiguously: her dictionary definition is universalising in the lean-in style, but her examples — Lagos clubs, hotels, parking attendants, Igbo extended-family meetings — pull the analysis back toward class and racial specificity.

So my assessment is that hooks's argument is broadly right, decisively vindicated by contemporary class-gender politics, and should be the starting point of any feminism that hopes to make sense of post-pandemic, post-Dobbs material conditions. Class matters; hooks said so before it was obvious. (Word count: 745.)

### Mock 3: What does de Beauvoir mean by "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman", and why does she treat lived experience as central?

I argue that the sentence contains two of the most consequential moves in twentieth-century feminist theory: an anti-essentialist account of gender, and a methodological commitment to lived experience as the ground of philosophical knowledge. Together, they make *The Second Sex* (1949) the hinge between liberal first-wave feminism and the second-wave, standpoint and post-structuralist traditions that follow.

Begin with the sentence. Beauvoir's claim is not that biological sex is irrelevant but that "woman" — the social, psychological, juridical, aesthetic category — is constituted through a process. One becomes a woman through the accumulated weight of socialisation, mythology, institutional positioning and interpersonal recognition. The category is historical and contingent, not natural. This is the conceptual seed of what Judith Butler later theorises as performativity: gender as constituted through repeated acts that present themselves as if they were always already there.

The structural finding that grounds the claim is woman-as-other. "Humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him; she is not regarded as an autonomous being... She is the incidental, the inessential as opposed to the absolute. He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Because man is taken as the universal human, woman becomes the marked term — defined relationally, judged against a norm she does not embody. To become a woman is to be inserted into this asymmetric structure and to learn it as if it were nature.

Why does Beauvoir treat lived experience as central? Three reasons.

First, because the existentialist apparatus she inherits demands it. Human beings are not essences but projects, freedoms exercised within concrete situations. To know what it is to be a woman one must investigate the lived situation, not legislate from outside. *The Second Sex* spends hundreds of pages on biology, psychoanalysis, history, myth and then on women's lived experience — childhood, sexual initiation, marriage, motherhood, old age — because the situation cannot be deduced; it must be described.

Second, because experience is the ground on which the woman-as-other diagnosis becomes available. Women have the experience of being treated as the inessential, and theorising this experience is the unfinished task of philosophy. The second-wave slogan the personal is political, coined two decades later, is already implicit here. Nancy Hartsock's standpoint epistemology — "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284) — extends Beauvoir's move into a full theory of socially situated knowledge.

Third, because the work has to do double duty. It must persuade women that patterns they have been taught to read as personal failings (mediocre marriage, unfulfilling motherhood, unrecognised intellectual ambition) are systemic; and it must persuade philosophy that "women" is a question. Lived experience is the medium for both moves. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* uses the same method: not theoretical first principles but the autobiographical episode — the friend Okoloma calling her a feminist, the Lagos parking attendant thanking her through Louis, the hotel staff assuming her to be a sex worker — to generate the diagnosis.

The anti-essentialism is the most enduring contribution. If becoming a woman is a process, it can be done differently — by raising daughters and sons differently, as Adichie urges, or by destabilising the binary itself, as Butler argues. The same move underwrites trans-feminist and queer-theoretical interventions: if "woman" is constituted rather than given, there is no metaphysical bar to its being constituted otherwise.

The centrality of lived experience must, however, be qualified. Beauvoir is herself a middle-class, white, European intellectual writing in 1949, and the "lived experience" *The Second Sex* describes is in important respects her own. Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks make the corrective: many lived experiences, structured by the matrix of domination across race, class, sexuality and ability. Harding makes the point explicitly: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131).

Concrete examples reinforce why the move matters. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is Beauvoir restated as empirical fact: when the world is engineered around the reference man, women's lived experience — freezing offices, ill-fitting stab vests, smartphones too big for one-handed use — registers the asymmetry materially. Iran's women-life-freedom movement after Mahsa Amini's killing in 2022 has a Beauvoirian grammar: women refusing to be the inessential other of a theocratic public sphere by making their bodies into political ground.

So the sentence means: woman is a process, not an essence; and one must read that process from inside the lives it shapes. That is why Beauvoir puts lived experience at the centre, and why the move still organises feminist thought seventy-five years on. (Word count: 749.)

### Mock 4: Compare Hartsock's feminist standpoint with Hill Collins's matrix of domination.

I argue that Hartsock and Hill Collins are doing related but distinct theoretical work, and that the most defensible contemporary feminist sociology requires both. Hartsock supplies an epistemology — a theory of how knowledge is produced from structurally located lives. Hill Collins supplies an ontology of power — a theory of how oppression is organised across domains and axes. Each completes what the other leaves under-theorised.

Begin with Hartsock. Her 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint" adapts Marx's standpoint move from the proletariat to women. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), and that structural difference — women's location in social reproduction, in care of dependents — generates a critical vantage on social relations that dominant-group thinkers cannot easily reach. Women occupy a dual role: insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power). This duality permits the move Marx makes from the proletariat's location: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The standpoint is achieved through political and theoretical work, not given by anatomy; Harding's later refinement ("female feminists are made, not born") confirms this.

Now Hill Collins. *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) and "Learning from the Outsider Within" (Hill Collins, 1986) develop the matrix of domination as a framework for how power is organised. Collins identifies four interlocking domains: the structural (laws, constitutions, formal institutions), the disciplinary (bureaucratic application of structural rules through schools, prisons, welfare offices, HR), the hegemonic (culture, ideology, common sense), and the interpersonal (face-to-face encounters, everyday microaggressions). Any system of oppression operates through all four domains simultaneously, and the systems mutually reinforce each other through their shared architecture. Collins also insists on a Black feminist standpoint: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70).

The comparison yields four contrasts and three convergences.

First contrast: scope. Hartsock writes about women's structural location in social reproduction as if it were singular. Collins explicitly plurialises: there are many standpoints because there are many positions in the matrix. A Black domestic worker in 1950s Mississippi and a white middle-class housewife of the same decade share some features and differ in others; Hartsock's singular standpoint elides the difference, Collins's matrix makes it central.

Second contrast: object. Hartsock's primary object is knowledge: how do we know what we know, and why does location matter? Collins's primary object is power: how is domination structured, and why does it recur across oppressions?

Third contrast: genealogy. Hartsock works in a Marxist register, extending the proletarian standpoint. Collins works in a Black feminist register, building on Sojourner Truth, Anna Julia Cooper, Audre Lorde and Angela Davis.

Fourth contrast: method. Hartsock theorises top-down from structural location to epistemic consequence. Collins theorises bottom-up from Black women's lived practices — domestic workers, mothers, churchwomen, blues singers — to analytic categories.

The convergences. Both reject the God-trick of disembodied universal knowledge. Both insist location matters for what one can know. Both treat standpoint as achieved rather than given.

Three contemporary examples clarify the complementarity.

#MeToo. ### Mock 5: How does intersectionality help us analyse post-Dobbs America?

I argue that intersectionality is not merely a useful lens for analysing post-Dobbs America but the indispensable one: any account that does not start from the intersecting effects of gender, race, class and geography misdescribes both the harm and the political stakes. Kimberlé Crenshaw's 1989 concept, refined through three decades of legal and sociological work, supplies the apparatus the situation requires.

Recall the concept. Crenshaw introduced intersectionality to explain why Black women workers were falling through the gaps of US discrimination law: their suits were dismissed because what they experienced — being treated worse than white women and than Black men, as Black women specifically — fit neither sex-discrimination nor race-discrimination doctrine taken separately. Intersectionality names the analytic moment at which two or more axes of oppression cross and compound, producing harms not legible from either axis alone. The lecture's summary is precise: "the notion that different types of discrimination intersect and often compound experiences of exclusion and social subjugation." Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination supplies the systemic counterpart.

Now Dobbs. In June 2022 the US Supreme Court overturned Roe v Wade, returning abortion to the states. Within eighteen months, roughly half had imposed bans or near-bans. The headline framing — "women lose abortion rights" — is true at one level and badly misleading at another. The intersectional analysis fills in the rest.

First, the racial intersection. Black women in the US are three to four times more likely to die of pregnancy-related causes than white women, in part because of cumulative medical-system biases and unequal access to high-quality prenatal care. Forced pregnancy compounds this directly: the maternal mortality differential becomes a much larger absolute number of preventable deaths. Black and Latina women are also disproportionately concentrated in Southern states with the most restrictive bans.

Second, the class intersection. A wealthy woman in Texas — of any race — books a flight to New York or California, takes time off, pays for a private clinic. A poor woman in the same state cannot. The cost of inter-state travel, missed work, childcare for existing children, and the legal exposure of self-managed medication abortion fall directly on those without resources.

Third, the geographic intersection. Rural women in trigger-law states face the longest travel distances to legal providers, the thinnest health infrastructure, the most surveillance from neighbours and clinic protesters, and the least legal aid. The Mississippi Delta, the Texas Rio Grande Valley, rural Alabama — these are also disproportionately Black, Latina and poor. Geography compounds class compounds race compounds gender.

Fourth, the intersection with immigration status. Undocumented women in Texas cannot legally cross state lines through internal Border Patrol checkpoints; Dobbs effectively forces them to carry unwanted pregnancies regardless of medical risk. Intersectionality is the only framework that captures this specific harm.

Fifth, the trans intersection. ### Mock 6: Is queer theory a vindication or a refutation of feminist standpoint theory?

I argue, against the disjunction in the question, that queer theory is best read as a *modification* of feminist standpoint theory — neither vindication nor refutation, but a critical extension that exposes a flaw in the original formulation while preserving its core epistemic claim. The most defensible contemporary position, articulated by Sandra Harding, integrates the queer-theoretical correction without abandoning standpoint epistemology.

Begin with the two positions in their pure form.

Feminist standpoint theory, in Hartsock's 1983 formulation, holds that women's structural location — particularly in social reproduction — produces a critical vantage on patriarchy that dominant-group thinkers cannot easily occupy. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), and that difference, theorised reflexively, generates situated knowledge. Standpoint privilege is achieved, not given; Harding's refinement ("female feminists are made, not born") confirms this. The empirical pay-off is the Criado-Perez gender data gap: starting from women's bodies and lives would have produced better engineering, medicine and policy than the engineering from the reference man that has dominated.

Queer theory, in Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) and the literature that follows, destabilises the very category "woman" on which standpoint theory rests. The famous opening: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Gender, on Butler's reading, is performative — constituted through repeated acts that present themselves as if they were always already there. The heterosexual matrix produces "woman" as one of two stable categories, and anything that does not fit (intersex bodies, trans subjectivities, non-binary identities) is rendered unintelligible.

Why might queer theory look like a refutation of standpoint theory? Because if "woman" is unstable, the structural location from which the standpoint is supposed to be generated is itself politically constructed. Hartsock's grounding in social reproduction looks suspect: women are not simply "in" social reproduction; women are constituted as those who belong there. The standpoint is, on this reading, an artefact of the very gender system feminism aims to dismantle.

The refutation reading has force but overstates the case. Three reasons.

First, Hartsock and Harding already insisted the standpoint is achieved, not given. Standpoint privilege does not depend on a metaphysically prior "woman" but on the political and theoretical work that turns a structural location into an analytic vantage. Butler's destabilisation does not undermine this; it deepens it by adding that the location itself must be analysed as constructed.

Second, the empirical pay-off of standpoint theory does not depend on the metaphysical stability of "woman". When Criado-Perez shows that crash-test dummies modelled on the reference man leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured, the category "women" is doing material-statistical work, not metaphysical work. Butler never denied that "woman" is necessary as a category of political analysis; she insisted it be used reflexively. Standpoint theory can — and Harding does — accept the reflexive use.

Third, queer theory's own critical purchase requires something very like a standpoint move. To name the heterosexual matrix as a structure of power requires starting from lives — queer, trans, intersex, non-binary — that the matrix renders unintelligible. Eve Sedgwick's, Gayle Rubin's and later trans-feminist scholars' work depends on attending to the situated knowledge of those whom dominant frames exclude. This is standpoint method in queer-theoretical clothes.

So queer theory is best read as a vindication-through-modification. It vindicates the core claim (knowledge is socially situated; marginalised locations generate critical analytic resources) while modifying the formulation in three ways.

First, the relevant locations are plural. There is not one standpoint of "woman" but many standpoints of differently positioned subjects — including those whose existence the unified category obscures. This is also Hill Collins's correction (the matrix of domination, Black feminist standpoint, "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70)).

Second, the locations are themselves constructed. Standpoint analysis must include analysis of how the location was produced. Harding's strong objectivity — "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" — is the apparatus that handles this.

Third, the standpoint is mobilised strategically. We use "woman" when the structural pattern (the gender data gap, the maternal-mortality gap, the gendered character of Dobbs) needs naming; we destabilise it when its use threatens to exclude.

Concrete examples sharpen the synthesis. The UK trans-rights debate within feminism has been, sociologically, a contest between an unmodified standpoint feminism (treating "woman" as stable) and a queer-modified standpoint feminism (holding the category open). Iran's women-life-freedom movement uses "woman" as a powerful political category in conditions where destabilisation would be a luxury. Adichie uses the category openly while attending to its constructedness ("we must raise our daughters differently. We must also raise our sons differently").

So the answer is neither pure vindication nor pure refutation. The corrected standpoint theory — Harding's strong objectivity, plurialised through Collins, made reflexive through Butler — is stronger than either parent position alone. (Word count: 745.)

### Mock 7: #MeToo and the limits of consent.

I argue that #MeToo's most important sociological contribution is not its individual accusations but its collective demonstration that the liberal model of consent — autonomous individuals contracting freely in private — fails to describe how sexual interaction actually proceeds under structural gender inequality. The movement has been most successful where it has named these structural conditions, and most controversial where it has stretched the language of consent beyond what consent alone can do.

Begin with the liberal model. Mainstream legal and ethical discourse treats consent as a binary act performed by a sovereign individual: yes or no, freely given, freely withdrawn. The model presupposes equal parties, full information, no coercion, and the capacity to refuse without penalty. Where these conditions hold, consent is meaningful. Where they do not, consent becomes a fiction that legitimates outcomes the parties did not in fact authorise. This is the central argument of Carole Pateman's *The Sexual Contract* (1988): the liberal contract tradition has masked, with its language of voluntary agreement, a deeper political ordering in which men have access to women's bodies and labour as a precondition of the contract itself.

#MeToo, from 2017 onward, made the gap between the model and the empirical reality visible at scale. Women who had been "consenting" to hotel-suite meetings, late-night calls and physical contact were testifying that they had done so under conditions that nullified the model's preconditions: structural employment dependence, threats of career destruction, NDAs that pre-emptively privatised any complaint, and the hegemonic framing that converted refusal into "difficult" behaviour. The personal is political: my individual yes was the collective product of an industry's architecture.

The structural reading is exactly Hartsock's standpoint move. Women's location in workplaces, intimate relationships and the public sphere generates analytic resources that powerful men cannot easily access. The collective testimony that produced #MeToo is what Hartsock predicted: standpoint epistemology in action, converting privately held experiences into a public diagnosis. Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination is the architectural reading: the same harassment pattern operates simultaneously through structural (employment law, NDAs), disciplinary (HR procedures, casting-couch routines), hegemonic (the joke about "what she was wearing") and interpersonal (everyday touching) domains.

Now the limits of consent the movement exposed.

The structural limit. When refusing a hotel-suite meeting means losing a career, consent has become a formality. Catharine MacKinnon's argument that workplace sexual harassment is sex discrimination — adopted by US courts from the 1980s — anticipates exactly this. The liberal "she said yes" framing covers a coerced yes that the law's deeper logic does not in fact authorise.

The disciplinary limit. NDAs, "HR procedures" that route complaints back to perpetrators' allies, and confidentiality clauses systematically privatise complaints. The Weinstein machine used NDAs as a routine instrument; consent to silence was the price of any settlement. #MeToo's decisive political move was to break the disciplinary apparatus by going public collectively.

The hegemonic limit. Cultural framings that pre-interpret women's behaviour — "she was flirting", "she went up to the room", "she didn't say no firmly enough" — convert ambiguous social interactions into retrospective consent.

The interpersonal limit. "Stealthing" (non-consensual condom removal), intoxication-blurred encounters, and the asymmetry between male and female reading of social cues show that even within a single bedroom the model's preconditions are routinely unmet.

#MeToo's intersectional revisions are essential. The 2017 wave foregrounded mostly white celebrity victims; Tarana Burke's earlier work with young Black women, and Alianza Nacional de Campesinas's organising for Latina farmworkers, show that the same harassment was rampant in low-wage agricultural, hospitality and domestic work. Crenshaw's intersectionality and Hill Collins's matrix explain why the limits of consent fall harder on women whose immigration status, race or class makes refusal more dangerous.

But the limits of consent have also been the site of #MeToo's controversies.

The due-process tension. Public accusation by collective testimony is epistemically powerful but procedurally outside legal due process. Defenders argued this was necessary because the legal system had systematically failed to convict; critics worried that careers were destroyed without adjudication. Both are true and the trade-off is real.

The carceral-feminism tension. Some streams of post-#MeToo politics have allied with punitive state power — longer sentences, expanded surveillance, mandatory arrest — in ways that have disproportionately harmed the communities Black and queer feminism defend. Angela Davis has long warned against this. The limits of consent should not be addressed by expanding a carceral apparatus that itself commits sexual violence.

The consent-redesign tension. Affirmative-consent standards (the "yes means yes" frame, adopted in California universities) attempted to rebuild consent on more demanding foundations: ongoing, enthusiastic, sober. The reform is partial — it addresses some structural limits but not all — and Pateman's deeper point still stands: the contract form itself may be ill-suited to the task.

So #MeToo's lesson on consent is structural: consent does real political work when its preconditions hold, and legitimates harm when they do not. The movement's task is to keep insisting on the preconditions — structural, disciplinary, hegemonic and interpersonal — and to refuse the liberal sleight-of-hand by which consent in formality covers coercion in fact. The heterosexual matrix produces "woman" as one of two stable categories, and anything that does not fit (intersex bodies, trans subjectivities, non-binary identities) is rendered unintelligible. Butler radicalises: not only is "woman" defined relative to "man", but the very binary that produces both is an effect of power. Beauvoir's anti-essentialism ("one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman") is the seed; Butler's performativity is the full flowering.

Harding's reply is the synthesis: we can hold "woman" as a strategic, plural, historically located term while recognising its constructedness.

The destabilised position has had to do work — defending the inclusion of trans women in women's spaces — that the stable position simply forecloses. "Culture does not make people. People make culture": this is folk-Butler.

So my assessment: very convincing in core, requiring modification at the edges. Butler's argument is best read together with Hill Collins's matrix, Crenshaw's intersectionality and Harding's strong objectivity. Together they deliver a defensible contemporary feminism that none alone can. (Word count: 749.)

Define the target first. "Universalist feminism", for the purposes of this question, is the strand of feminist thinking that takes "woman" as a unified pre-political category whose experiences, interests and oppressions can be theorised in the singular. The strand is detectable in Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792), in much of Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949), in classic second-wave consciousness-raising, in Sheryl Sandberg's lean-in feminism, and in any "human rights for women" framing that imagines a default woman to whom rights are to be extended. The strand has produced enormous wins (suffrage, anti-discrimination law, equal-pay legislation) but it carries an analytic flaw that becomes acute when Black women's lives are taken seriously.

The first failure is descriptive. Hill Collins formulates the alternative starting point: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender does not exist on its own; it is produced through race, class, sexuality and other axes simultaneously, across structural, disciplinary, hegemonic and interpersonal domains. The "woman" of universalist feminism is implicitly white, middle-class, partnered and straight — and Black women's lives do not map onto this default. A 1960s suburban housewife and a Black domestic worker working in that household share certain features and differ in many others; the universalist frame describes the housewife and silently elides the worker. Audre Lorde's "Master's Tools" essay makes the point sharply: the master's house cannot be dismantled with the master's tools, including a feminism whose categories were built around the default woman.

The second failure is juridical and structural. Crenshaw's 1989 paper introduced intersectionality precisely to explain why Black women workers were falling through the cracks of US anti-discrimination law. *DeGraffenreid v General Motors* (1976) is the canonical case: Black women plaintiffs alleged discrimination that was distinctly their own — they were not treated like white women, and not treated like Black men — but the court dismissed the suit because the doctrine permitted only "sex discrimination" or "race discrimination" claims, not their intersection. The juridical apparatus, built on a universalist gender frame, could not register the harm. The same structural fact recurs in employment, in healthcare (Black maternal mortality runs three to four times the white rate in the US, a fact universalist feminism's "women's health" framing cannot explain), in housing, and in policing.

The third failure is political. bell hooks's *Where We Stand: Class Matters* (2000), together with *Ain't I a Woman* (1981) and *Feminist Theory: From Margin to Center* (1984), argues that mainstream feminism has won its gains by displacing care work and economic precarity onto Black, working-class and migrant women, while marketing the gains as women's progress. The senior lean-in executive's career is paid for by the migrant nanny; the gender pay gap that the universalist frame highlights is dwarfed by the gap between Black women and white men that the frame elides. Reproductive justice — the framework Loretta Ross and SisterSong developed in 1994 — extends the structural point: the universalist "right to choose" is, for poor Black women in trigger-law Southern states, a right without resources. Post-Dobbs America has made the failure stark: the same Supreme Court decision falls very differently on the wealthy Texan who flies to New York and the poor Black Mississippian who carries an unwanted pregnancy.

The convergence of the three authors is illuminating. Collins gives the architecture (the matrix), Crenshaw gives the diagnostic (intersectionality at the case level), hooks gives the political indictment (class-bound mainstream feminism). All three reject the universalist frame and all three propose starting from Black women's lives — not as a supplementary perspective but as the methodologically privileged ground for analysing gender, race and class simultaneously.

Concrete examples sharpen the argument.

The Combahee River Collective Statement (1977) anticipates everything: "We realize that the only people who care enough about us to work consistently for our liberation are us." The universalist movements of the day — both mainstream feminism and the male-led Black liberation movement — failed Black women in symmetric ways, each universalising on a different axis.

#MeToo's class limits. The 2017 wave foregrounded white celebrity victims; Tarana Burke's earlier work with young Black women, and Alianza Nacional de Campesinas's organising for Latina farmworkers, had been doing the same political labour without comparable attention.

Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap. Even the most empirically powerful universalist feminist evidence — that engineering from the reference man (50th-percentile male, 1.77m, 76kg) leaves women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash — has a second layer once we add race: Black women face additional medical-system biases. The universalist frame catches half the harm.

Sandra Harding's strong-objectivity directive — "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131) — is the methodological synthesis. Universalist feminism cannot meet this directive without ceasing to be universalist.

So universalist feminism fails Black women descriptively, juridically and politically. The corrective, supplied by Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks together, is a feminism that begins from Black women's lives and treats the matrix of domination as the architectural truth of how power operates. ### Mock 12: Can feminism survive without a universal subject?

I argue that feminism can survive — and must — without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense, while retaining "woman" as a strategic, plural, politically operational category. The question's apparent dichotomy is false: the death of the universal subject of pre-1980s feminism is not the death of feminism but its maturation. Sandra Harding's strong objectivity supplies the synthesis; Hill Collins, Crenshaw, hooks, Butler and Adichie converge on it from different directions.

Begin with what is dead. The universal subject of first-wave and early second-wave feminism — "woman" as a unified pre-political category whose experiences, interests and oppressions could be theorised in the singular — has been progressively dismantled over five decades. Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination shows that gender is always co-produced with race and class. Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality formalises the legal version. bell hooks's *Class Matters* (2000) and *Ain't I a Woman* (1981) press the class and racial point. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) destabilises "woman" as a metaphysical category. The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. The verdict is in: the universal subject in the strong sense is gone.

Does feminism die with it? Three positions.

Position one: pure pluralism. There is no "feminism", only feminisms; the singular is the problem; the future is a thousand local standpoints. The position is almost the default in 2026 academic feminism. Its strength is descriptive accuracy. Its weakness is political: if "woman" is wholly destabilised, the subject of mass mobilisation disappears. Iran's women-life-freedom movement, the post-Dobbs abortion-rights wave, the global #MeToo movement, the Polish black protest against abortion restrictions — each required something that could function as a political "we", and pure pluralism makes the "we" hard to construct.

Position two: tactical universalism. Retain "woman" as a strategic category, knowing it is constructed, and use it where the structural pattern demands. Butler herself, despite often being read as the destroyer of the subject, holds something like this position: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). The sentence does not say abandon the category; it says use it reflexively. Adichie's defence of "feminist" is a more accessible version: dropping the term for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Position three: Harding's synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). The unity is in the *practice* — starting from marginalised lives — rather than in a metaphysically prior subject. The pluralism is built into the practice, not opposed to it. This is the position I defend.

Five reasons feminism survives without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense.

First, the structural patterns persist and require naming. Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap is the cleanest evidence: crash-test dummies modelled on the reference man leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die in a comparable car crash; office thermostats run five degrees too cold for the female metabolism; voice recognition fails on female speech; Apple Health launched without a period tracker. These facts hold for women as such across race and class even while being differentiated within them. "Feminism" in the singular is needed to name the recurring structural pattern.

Second, the political moments require a "we". Iran's women-life-freedom movement, the post-Dobbs mobilisations, the Polish black-protest march, #MeToo: each functioned by mobilising a recognisable political subject who could speak under a shared banner. Pure pluralism cannot generate this; Harding's tactical singular can.

Third, the analytic categories survive without the subject. Patriarchy, the public/private split (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986), the matrix of domination, intersectionality, performativity, the personal is political, the reference man, the oppositional gaze — none of these requires a unified prior subject of feminism. They are tools of analysis, applicable to specific cases.

Fourth, the descriptive plural strengthens rather than threatens the political singular. Hill Collins's "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70) is a Black feminist standpoint; it is also a contribution to feminism in the singular. The local enriches the general; the general gives the local political reach.

Fifth, Butler's own continued use of "feminism" and "woman" demonstrates the position's coherence. The most-cited destabiliser of the unified subject continues to write feminist theory under that name. The performative contradiction is illuminating.

Concrete examples make the synthesis work. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* mobilises "feminist" openly while attending to Igbo specificity, class context, and the constructedness of gender through socialisation. The post-Dobbs reproductive-justice frame uses "women" as a working category while attending to the intersectional differentiation of its impact. Iran's women-life-freedom protests use "woman" against a theocratic public sphere while accommodating internal difference. The UK trans-rights debate is the case where the failure to do this synthesis has been most damaging.

A critic might object that strategic universalism is just universalism in retreat. The reply is that strategic differs from universalism in three respects: it is reflexive (the category is held under interrogation), plural (the same category accommodates different positions), and revisable (the category is open to extension as the politics demands). Universalism in the criticised sense had none of these properties.

So my answer is: yes, feminism can survive — and is in fact flourishing — without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense. The condition of its survival is the synthesis Harding articulates: unity in practice rather than in subject, plurality of standpoints rather than a single voice, and the strategic use of "woman" as a category whose construction is part of feminism's own object. The death of the metaphysical universal subject is the maturation, not the demise, of feminism. (Word count: 748.)
