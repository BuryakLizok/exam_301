## 3. Key notes from the presentation (with slide-question callouts)

**Slide 1–2: Framing the lecture.** Feminism aims to challenge what is taken as appropriate behaviour/roles for women; "the more people that feminism can 'talk for', the stronger its arguments", but the lecturer immediately problematises this. The main question for the exam: *can or should feminism attempt to talk for all women?*

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "whether feminism can or should attempt to talk for all women, or not… Or maybe you disagree?" This is the live question I am being asked to take a position on. I will refuse a simple yes/no and argue that feminism must do both: hold open a strategic collective subject while practising internal critique.

**Slide 3: First-wave feminism.** Post-French Revolution (1789) liberal/equal-rights feminism; the Enlightenment background; reacts against the exclusion of women from the universality of rights; focuses on the vote and equal pay; key text Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792).

**Slide 4: But why should women want to be like men?**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club? Or should it aim to change the rules? Or to start another club?" My answer: the rules themselves must change, because joining the club leaves the patriarchal architecture intact (Beauvoir's point about woman-as-other, hooks' point about the master's house).

**Slides 5–7: Beauvoir and the move toward second-wave feminism.** Writing in the 1940s and 1950s when the "aim" of life for many women was to "marry well"; *The Second Sex* (1949) asks "what is a woman?" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 13). She insists there is no essential thing that defines women; rather, woman is a status produced through experience.

**Slide 6: A word on "gender".** Beauvoir uses *genre*; the Latin *genus* indicates type/kind; gender as an arbitrary category for organising the world, important for later post-structuralist moves (Butler).

**Slide 8: Three key points.** No essence of woman; "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman"; woman has the EXPERIENCE of being the "other" of men.

**Slide 9: "Other" and bifurcations.** "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6).

**Slide 10: Women as objects, not subjects.** "The representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men; they describe it from the point of view which is theirs and which they confuse with the absolute truth" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 236). Note the Disney-princess image, a contemporary mass-cultural illustration of male-authored representation.

**Slide 11: Critique of Beauvoir.**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Does de Beauvoir try to 'universalize' her argument? Is her account historically and socially limited (she is a middle class, white, European intellectual)? Can she really describe the way ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" My answer: no. This is exactly the opening that Black feminism, postcolonial feminism, and post-structuralism rush into.

**Slides 12–15: Second-wave and the personal is political.** Patriarchy, reproduction as labour, exploitation of women's work. The private/public split is historically produced with industrial capitalism: production leaves the home, "you go **out** to work, in factories", domesticity is invented as private and feminised. Women become inconspicuous reproducers of the wage-worker and of future wage-labourers; "Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers" (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). The domestic sphere becomes invisible.

**Slide 16: Three lessons.** Different standpoints/perspectives matter; experiences reveal the operations of power; experience has a duality.

**Slide 17: Arguing for difference.** Feminism before the 1980s is criticised on three counts: it universalises (a shared history of oppression is presumed), it essentialises (being a woman is enough), and it is ahistorical (e.g. it ignores how white women have benefited from colonialism).

**Slides 19–20: Black feminist thought, matrix of domination, intersectionality.** "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Matrix of domination: "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Collins). Gender does not exist on its own; "we can't think gender without thinking 'race'". Crenshaw's intersectionality: different types of discrimination intersect and compound experiences of exclusion.

**Slides 21–22: bell hooks and the home as resistance.** Collins and hooks question the second-wave reading of home as pure repression; for Black women under racial terror the home is also refuge and resistance. The oppositional gaze: "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring… The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115).

**Slides 23–25: Hartsock and feminist materialism.** Women are both insiders (essential to the system's continuation) and outsiders (no power within it); this dual position is epistemologically privileged. "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142).

**Slides 26–27: Post-structuralist feminism and Butler.** Identities are not fixed; "woman" is not a stable category; sex and gender are signifiers, not labels of given objects. Butler: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics… Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). This opens the door to queer theory and to questioning the unified subject.

**Slide 28: Conclusion.** Feminism introduces new concepts (sex/gender, essentialism, the politics of birth as opposed to a male preoccupation with death); it shows how the world could be otherwise; and it leaves us with the standing question: can/should feminism speak for all women?

## 4. Information from the readings

### Harding (ed.), *The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, Intro pp. 4–7 and ch. 8 ("Strong Objectivity")

Harding sets up standpoint theory as a response to a need: "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women" (Harding, 2004: 4). The "implied 'speaker' of scientific… sentences was never women. It was supposed to be humanity in general"; what Haraway calls "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4).

Harding's diagnosis is structural, not about bad individuals: "The problem was not prejudiced and biased individuals… Rather, it was a different kind of obstacle… The conceptual frameworks themselves promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns" (Harding, 2004: 4–5). The disciplines were "complicitous with sexist and androcentric agendas" (Harding, 2004: 5).

Politics is therefore not a contaminant of knowledge but a resource: "politics and culture often function as 'prisonhouses' of knowledge… Yet they can and often do also function as 'toolboxes'" (Harding, 2004: 5). The remedy is to "start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities" (Harding, 2004: 6). But this immediately raises the controversial issue Harding identifies in the intro: "How does and should standpoint theory account for and engage with differences between women?" (Harding, 2004: 7), the same question my exam asks.

In ch. 8, Harding develops **strong objectivity**. The conventional notion is "not rigorous or objectifying enough" (Harding, 2004: 128). Standpoint theory does not commit the **God-trick**, is not ethnocentric (because standpoint theorists "all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129)), and is not relativist ("It is not equally true… that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history" (Harding, 2004: 131–132)). The **subject of knowledge** for standpoint theory is "embodied and visible… multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134). Strong objectivity demands "strong reflexivity": "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Crucially, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135); feminism is not the property of women.

### Adichie, *We Should All Be Feminists* (Vintage Short, 2014; based on TEDxEuston 2012)

Adichie's argument is short, autobiographical, and forceful. She defines feminism using the dictionary: "a person who believes in the social, political, and economic equality of the sexes". She tells the story of being called a feminist at fourteen by her friend Okoloma, "the same tone with which a person would say, 'You're a supporter of terrorism.'" She enumerates the negative baggage: "You hate men, you hate bras, you hate African culture… you don't shave, you're always angry, you don't have a sense of humor".

Her childhood Igbo example (the class monitor): "Then, to my surprise, my teacher said the monitor had to be a boy… If we do something over and over, it becomes normal… If we keep seeing only men as heads of corporations, it starts to seem 'natural' that only men should be heads of corporations." Her Lagos examples (being refused entry to clubs alone, being thanked through her male friend Louis for money she had given a parking attendant, being assumed to be a sex worker in hotel lobbies) work as concrete intersectional evidence of how gender shapes everyday life across class.

Adichie's central reframings:
- **Anger is legitimate.** "Gender as it functions today is a grave injustice. I am angry. We should all be angry."
- **Masculinity damages men.** "Masculinity is a hard, small cage, and we put boys inside this cage… The harder a man feels compelled to be, the weaker his ego is."
- **Femininity should not have to apologise.** "I have chosen to no longer be apologetic for my femininity."
- **Culture is not destiny.** "Culture does not make people. People make culture. If it is true that the full humanity of women is not our culture, then we can and must make it our culture."
- **The case for the *word* "feminist".** "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Adichie also pushes back on the "un-African" charge: feminism is not a Western import; her great-grandmother, who ran away from a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." This is important for the universalising critique: a properly intersectional feminism can find local feminist genealogies without rebranding them as Western.

### Criado-Perez, "The deadly truth about a world built for men" (*Guardian*, 23 Feb 2019; extract from *Invisible Women*)

Criado-Perez documents the **gender data gap**, a "female-shaped 'absent presence'" in design, science, and public policy. Key empirical anchors:
- **The reference man.** Toxicology and occupational safety still rely on a Caucasian male, 25–30, 70kg. "Reference Man… his superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not."
- **Crash-test dummies.** Until 2011 US tests used only a 50th-percentile male; even now, EU regulation does not require an anthropometrically female dummy; the "female" dummy used in some tests is "just a scaled-down male dummy". Result: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured… 17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash.
- **PPE.** Body armour and stab vests sized for male bodies. A British female police officer was stabbed and killed in 1997 after removing her armour to use a hydraulic ram; another had breast-reduction surgery because of the vest. 95% of female emergency-service workers report PPE hampers their work.
- **Office temperature.** A 1960s formula based on the resting metabolic rate of the average male overestimates female metabolism by up to 35%; offices are on average five degrees too cold for women.
- **Phones, voice recognition, Apple Health.** Smartphones at the upper limit of male hand size; Google speech recognition 70% more likely to recognise male speech accurately; Apple Health launched without a period tracker.
- **Toilets.** "50/50 division of floor space has even been formalised in plumbing codes"; but women need more time and trips, so formal equality is substantive inequality.

This article is gold for the standpoint argument: starting from women's bodies and women's daily life generates *more accurate* science (Harding's strong objectivity), and starting from men's bodies as the default is not neutral. It is, in Harding's terms, the God-trick in engineering.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The arc of feminist theory in this lecture is a movement from rights, to experience, to standpoint, to difference, to deconstruction; and the question I am asked is whether this trajectory empties feminism of any usable subject. I argue it does not, provided we read each move as a correction rather than a replacement.

**Wollstonecraft's liberal feminism is necessary but insufficient.** It treats women as already-formed rational subjects who have been wrongly excluded; this leaves intact the very norm against which they are measured. The slide's deliberately provocative question, "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club?", names the limit of equal-rights feminism. Beauvoir's intervention is precisely to refuse the male norm: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Beauvoir's anti-essentialism, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman", is the conceptual hinge of the whole module. It makes gender a social process, not a biological destiny, and it grounds the second-wave slogan that the personal is political.

**Patriarchy and the public/private split are not eternal facts but historical products.** Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) historicise the very domesticity that earlier liberal feminism took for granted: with industrial capitalism, "you go **out** to work, in factories", and the home is reinvented as a feminised private. Hartsock's contribution is to translate this materialist insight into an epistemology. Women's dual position (insiders to social reproduction, outsiders to power) generates a privileged vantage point: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The Criado-Perez piece is, in effect, a 21st-century vindication of this claim: starting from women's bodies in nail salons, in cars, in stab vests, in offices, produces better engineering, empirically less partial, more accurate, "strong objectivity" in Harding's sense.

**But standpoint theory is not safe from its own universalising tendencies.** Hill Collins, Crenshaw, and hooks all insist that "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70); the matrix of domination shows that "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Hill Collins, 1990). Crenshaw's intersectionality is the conceptual tool here; hooks' oppositional gaze, "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), names the active, resistant subject that flat universalism misses. Importantly, Collins and hooks also rewrite the second-wave reading of "home": for Black women living under racial terror, home is not only a site of patriarchal repression but also a refuge and an organising base.

**Butler radicalises this further.** "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). This is the queer theoretical move: the unified subject that liberal feminism relied on is itself an effect of power, and any "we" implicates exclusions (of trans women, of intersex people, of non-binary subjects). Performativity replaces essence; the destabilisation of "woman" is not the end of feminism but the condition of a more inclusive politics.

**Where this leaves the exam question.** I am persuaded by Harding's resolution: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, 2004: 131). Adichie supplies the practical correlate: the term "feminist" must be retained because dropping it for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded". "Feminism" and "feminisms" are therefore not rivals: they are two sides of one practice. Feminism names the collective political subject under construction; feminisms names the plural standpoints whose internal argument keeps that subject honest.

## 8. Essay answers

### Past-Q 2025 (Q5): Outline and assess the political importance of feminism today.

Feminism remains politically important because the structural problem it identifies, that women, as a class, are positioned as "other" to a male norm that masquerades as universal, is empirically alive, even as the form of that othering shifts. I will outline the political agenda that follows from this and then assess its limits.

The outline first. Liberal first-wave feminism, in Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), set the equal-rights agenda: vote, education, equal pay. That agenda is unfinished but it is no longer sufficient. Simone de Beauvoir's diagnosis remains the live one: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Second-wave feminism translated this into a political programme, "the personal is political", that connected patriarchy, reproduction-as-labour and the exploitation of women's work, showing that the public/private split was a historical product of industrial capitalism (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). Black feminist thought, intersectionality and post-structuralist feminism refined this further: Hill Collins' matrix of domination, Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze, Butler's destabilisation of "woman".

Now the assessment.

First, because the gender data gap has lethal effects. Criado-Perez documents that crash-test dummies in US testing relied on a 50th-percentile male until 2011, and EU regulation still does not require an anthropometrically female dummy; the "female" dummy in some tests is "just a scaled-down male dummy". Women are accordingly "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable crash and "17% more likely to die". Police body armour and stab vests do not fit women: in 1997 a British female officer was stabbed and killed after removing her vest to operate a hydraulic ram, and another required breast-reduction surgery because of the vest. Office temperature formulas built in the 1960s around a male resting metabolic rate overestimate female metabolic rate "by as much as 35%", leaving offices "on average five degrees too cold for women". Apple's 2014 health tracker launched without a period tracker; Google speech recognition was "70% more likely to accurately recognise male speech". The Sandi Toksvig antler-bone example, in which a male anthropologist sees "man's first attempt at a calendar" and a female anthropologist sees a woman tracking her cycle, is the conceptual hinge: the "reference man" stands in for humanity, and women pay the price. Feminism is the only political movement systematically attending to this.

Second, because the public/private split keeps reorganising itself. As the lecture's reading of industrial capitalism puts it, "you go out to work, in factories", and the home was reinvented as a feminised private. The same architecture still allocates unpaid reproductive labour to women: Hartsock's "women have been given the responsibility for certain areas of production and reproduction such as childbirth and child-rearing" (Hartsock, 1983: 284) is descriptive, not historical. Adichie's diary of Lagos restaurants and Igbo extended-family meetings ("only the male members of the extended family can attend the meetings where major family decisions are taken… because I am female") confirms that the asymmetry recurs across very different settings.

Third, because women's standpoint generates more accurate knowledge. Harding's "strong objectivity" is the philosophical principle, and Criado-Perez's piece is the engineering proof. Starting from women's bodies and women's daily lives would have produced better cars, safer policing equipment, working heating systems and usable smartphones. Starting from men's bodies as the default is not neutral; it is, in Harding's terms, the "God-trick" in engineering.

Fourth, because feminism supplies a vocabulary other movements cannot. Adichie's defence of the term is exactly this: "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." Her great-grandmother, who "ran away from the house of the man she did not want to marry", "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." This is the case for the *word*: a specific harm requires a specific name.

I must assess feminism's limits honestly. Universalist mainstream feminism has been rightly criticised by Black feminists, including Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks, for taking the experience of white middle-class women as the experience of women in general. The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical, and that "white women have benefited from colonialism". Butler adds a further caution: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). These are reasons to plurialise the term ("feminisms") while keeping the practice ("feminism").

In sum, the political importance of feminism is high. It is the only language that simultaneously names the historical specificity of women's subordination, refuses to flatten that subordination into a single experience, and provides the analytic tools (patriarchy, standpoint, intersectionality, the gender data gap) for diagnosing material harms in real institutions. To abandon the word would be to lose, in Harding's words, the resource through which "a social and political disadvantage can be turned into" a critical insight. We need that resource. (Word count: 720.)

### Past-Q 2024 (Q4): Is 'feminisms' a better way to understand women's experiences than 'feminism'?

I argue that the plural and the singular are not rivals: "feminisms" is the better description of the field, but "feminism" remains a necessary political fiction. Dropping the singular concedes too much; refusing the plural concedes too little.

The case for "feminisms" is overwhelming once one looks closely at women's lives. The lecture slides reach this conclusion explicitly: feminism before the 1980s was universalising (it presumed a shared history of oppression), essentialising (it presumed being a woman is enough) and ahistorical (it neglected how white women have benefited from colonialism). Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) names a real structural condition, "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6), but the lecture immediately asks: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir is a middle-class, white, European intellectual, and what is universalised under "woman" too often turns out to be one specific class location.

Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks make this concrete. "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70), and the matrix of domination shows that gender always operates with race and class. Crenshaw's intersectionality, originally developed to explain why Black women workers fell through both racial-discrimination law and sex-discrimination law, is the conceptual reason we must speak in the plural. hooks' rewriting of the home, "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115), refuses the second-wave reading that home is uniformly repressive; for Black women under racial terror, home is also refuge.

Concrete examples follow easily. Adichie's own essay is a sequence of cases that resist universalisation: the friend Okoloma who first called her a feminist in a Nigerian voice; the Nsukka primary-school class-monitor episode in which a girl with the highest score is passed over because "the monitor had to be a boy"; the Lagos parking attendant who thanked her male friend Louis for money Adichie had given; the Lagos hotels where a woman alone is read as a sex worker; the Igbo extended-family meetings where she "cannot have a formal say" because she is female; her great-grandmother who "ran away from the house of the man she did not want to marry" and "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." Each is recognisably gendered and none collapses into the Beauvoirian "woman" of mid-century French theory. To force these into one "feminism" is to make Beauvoir's mistake again.

And yet I want to resist the conclusion that we should simply drop the singular. The pluralist position has its own pathology: if every standpoint is irreducibly its own, there is no political subject left at all, and feminism dissolves into a thousand identity claims. Judith Butler's caution, "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2), does not mean abandoning "woman", it means handling the category carefully. Adichie puts the point in its most usable form: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." The Criado-Perez evidence is decisive on this. When crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, voice recognition and Apple Health are designed around the reference man, women of every race, class and sexuality are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash, freeze in the office, and find their phones unsafe to use one-handed at a protest. These are not symbolic harms. They name a structural condition that crosses every "intersection".

Sandra Harding offers the synthesis I find most convincing: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, 2004: 131). This is exactly the relationship I want to defend between feminism and feminisms. The plural names the *internal pluralism of the movement*; the singular names the *external political subject* the movement constructs in order to make the kind of claim Adichie makes when she says, simply, "I am a feminist."

So my answer is: feminisms describes the field; feminism names the practice. To pick one without the other is to lose either the descriptive accuracy that standpoint and intersectional theory have so painfully won, or the political traction without which feminist claims have no purchase. The exam question pushes for a choice; I refuse the false choice. We need both terms, and the productive tension between them, to understand women's experiences: experiences that are radically plural in form and yet, as Criado-Perez shows, still recognisably gendered in effect. (Word count: 743.)

### Past-Q 2023 (Q5): Should sociology use the term "feminism" or "feminisms"?

Sociology should use both terms, deliberately, and should resist the rhetorical pressure to pick one. This is not a fudge; it reflects how sociological knowledge is actually produced, by holding analytic categories open enough to capture a structural pattern while keeping them porous enough to admit internal difference. I will defend this position in three steps: the case for the singular, the case for the plural, and the case for their disciplined coexistence.

The case for the singular ("feminism") rests on the fact that sociology must be able to name structures. Simone de Beauvoir's claim, "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6), describes a structural pattern of othering that crosses cultures, classes and historical periods even as it takes different concrete forms. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is a striking sociological demonstration: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable car crash because the engineering profession has, for half a century, treated the male body as the human default. The office thermostat (calibrated to a male resting metabolic rate, overestimating female metabolism "by as much as 35%"), the stab vest (which got a British female police officer killed in 1997), the smartphone (sized to "the limit of men's hand size"), the voice-recognition system (70% more accurate on male speech), Apple Health's missing period tracker: these are all the same sociological fact, namely that the "reference man" stands in for humanity. Sociology needs a name for that fact. "Feminism" is that name. To abandon the singular is to give up on the claim that there is a recognisable, describable pattern. Adichie makes the same point politically: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

The case for the plural ("feminisms") is just as strong sociologically. The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. Patricia Hill Collins formulates the Black feminist counter-position: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender does not "exist on its own"; it is produced through race, class, sexuality, and disability simultaneously. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalised this for legal scholarship and then for sociology. bell hooks' rewriting of the home, "There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), refuses the second-wave assumption that the domestic sphere is uniformly repressive. Judith Butler pushes the move further: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). For Butler the unified category "woman" is itself a production of power, and a sociology that uses the term naively will reproduce exclusions, historically of trans women, of working-class women, of women of colour.

This pluralism is not an academic affectation; it has empirical purchase. Sociologically, "feminism in the singular" cannot explain why Adichie's Igbo great-grandmother, who "refused, protested, spoke up when she felt she was being deprived of land and access because she was female", was a feminist without ever using the word, while a Nigerian academic could later tell Adichie that "feminism was not our culture, that feminism was un-African". It cannot explain why Adichie's brother Kene, "a kind, good-looking, and very masculine young man", can be described by Adichie as "the best feminist I know", while elsewhere in the same essay a male friend warns her that "calling myself a feminist meant that I hated men". Adichie's essay is a sustained empirical demonstration: feminist consciousness arises in many forms with many vocabularies, and "Culture does not make people. People make culture."

So far this looks like a straightforward win for "feminisms". But the case for their coexistence is the sociologically interesting one. Sandra Harding gives the cleanest statement: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, 2004: 131). What Harding is describing is the way sociology actually works: we generalise *and* we attend to difference, in the same breath. The plural names the internal pluralism of standpoints; the singular names the political subject those standpoints jointly construct.

A purely pluralist sociology would face two problems. First, it would lose the capacity to make structural claims of the Criado-Perez type, claims that hold for women as such, across other axes of difference. Second, it would underestimate the way "feminisms" can serve as a covert essentialism in its own right (e.g. when "Black feminism" is used to mean a single thing rather than a plural tradition in turn). Conversely, a purely singular sociology repeats Beauvoir's mistake of universalising one location.

My recommendation, then, is that sociology should treat "feminism" and "feminisms" as a dialectical pair. We use the singular when we are making a structural claim about patriarchy, the gender data gap, the public/private split, the standpoint of social reproduction. We use the plural when we are attending to the standpoints of Black women (Hill Collins), differently positioned women in the matrix of domination, and the various lives Harding gathers when she insists that "one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, 2004: 131). To insist on one term is to lose half the analytic work sociology has to do. (Word count: 740.)

### Past-Q 2022 (Q8): Should women's experience be the basis of feminism?

Women's experience should be a foundational input to feminism, but it cannot be its sole basis without producing precisely the universalising, essentialising and ahistorical errors that Black, post-structuralist and standpoint feminism have spent forty years correcting. The right answer is the one Sandra Harding develops: experience is the *starting point* but not the *foundation* of feminist knowledge.

The case for experience is the strongest part of the feminist tradition. Beauvoir built *The Second Sex* on the claim that the lived experience of being woman, "Women have the EXPERIENCE of being considered the 'other' of men", had never been theorised. Her famous formulation, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman", makes experience constitutive rather than incidental. The second-wave slogan "the personal is political" extended this: experiences that had been quarantined as private (housework, sexual violence, reproductive control) were revealed to be political because their pattern was systemic. Nancy Hartsock's feminist standpoint translated experience into epistemology: women's "dual social role" as both insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power) generates a critical vantage on patriarchy, just as the proletariat's dual position generated Marx's critique of bourgeois ideology (Hartsock, 1983: 142). Adichie's TEDx talk is a contemporary refresh of this argument: she does not start from theory but from being a fourteen-year-old Igbo girl called a feminist by her friend, from being thanked through her male friend Louis by a Lagos parking attendant, from being assumed to be a sex worker in a hotel lobby. Experience generates the analysis.

The Criado-Perez evidence shows the analytic power of starting from experience. Sandi Toksvig's anthropology professor reframes an antler bone with 28 markings: a male anthropologist sees "man's first attempt at a calendar"; a female anthropologist sees a woman tracking her cycle. The interpretive difference flows from a different bodily experience of time. The same logic explains why Apple Health launched without a period tracker, why office thermostats freeze women, why stab vests get women killed: a male-only experiential base produced systematically defective knowledge. Starting from women's experience would have produced better engineering, better medicine, better policy. This is Harding's strong objectivity: politically engaged knowledge that starts from marginalised standpoints is, empirically, more accurate.

But experience is also dangerous as a *foundation*. Three problems.

First, the universalising problem. The lecture asks of Beauvoir directly: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir's "woman" is implicitly a middle-class, white, European intellectual. Patricia Hill Collins responds by insisting on Black women's specific standpoint: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender is always co-produced with race and class; a feminism that takes one experience as the experience effaces this.

Second, the essentialising problem. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* warns that "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). If we ground feminism in "women's experience", we have to specify whose experience counts as women's, and any specification will exclude. Trans women's experiences? Intersex experiences? Asexual women's experiences? Women without children? Each line we draw reproduces a power-laden boundary.

Third, the empirical problem. Adichie tells of an academic who told her feminism was "un-African". Adichie's response is that her great-grandmother, who fled a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." Experience alone, without an interpretive frame, does not arrive labelled "feminist"; the same experiences are mobilised by anti-feminist as well as feminist analyses (e.g. "but what is a girl doing in a room with four boys?"). Experience requires theory to become knowledge.

Harding's resolution is the most defensible. Standpoint theory holds that "Women's lives and experiences provide the 'grounds' for this knowledge, though these clearly do not provide foundations for knowledge in the conventional philosophical sense. These grounds are the site, the activities, from which scientific questions arise" (Harding, 2004: 128). Experience is the necessary starting point; reflexive theoretical work, strong objectivity, is what turns it into knowledge. And, Harding insists, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135). Feminism is not the property of those who have the relevant experiences; it is a theoretical and political practice that *begins* with those experiences but is owed by everyone.

So my answer is: yes, women's experience should be the basis of feminism, in the sense of the starting point; no, it cannot be the foundation, in the sense of an unmediated ground that licenses claims by itself. To collapse the distinction is either to repeat Beauvoir's universalising move (one experience for all women) or to dissolve feminism into a thousand incommensurable testimonies. The discipline of standpoint theory keeps both errors at bay. (Word count: 749.)

### Past-Q 2021 (Q8): Is it still possible to talk of "feminism" as a unified project or do we need to use the term "feminisms"?

It is still possible, and politically necessary, to talk of feminism as a unified project, provided we understand that unity in the right way. The unity is not of content (one experience, one theory, one programme) but of *practice*: a shared commitment to identifying and dismantling patriarchy in its many forms. Within that practice, "feminisms" in the plural names the internal pluralism that keeps the practice honest. I argue, against the implied dichotomy of the question, that the two terms are complementary rather than rival.

The case for "feminisms" is, by now, almost the default position in academic sociology, and rightly so. The lecture identifies three failures of feminism before the 1980s: it universalised (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialised (presuming being a woman is enough) and was ahistorical (ignoring, for instance, how white women had benefited from colonialism). Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks gave us the conceptual tools to repair these failures. Collins' matrix of domination ("structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression"), Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze: all insist that "gender" is never produced in isolation from race, class, sexuality, ability. Judith Butler radicalises the move: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Sociologically, the plural is needed because women's lives are radically different across location, as Adichie demonstrates in her essay: the Igbo great-grandmother who fled a forced marriage; the Lagos primary-school girl denied class-monitor because the role "had to be a boy"; the Lagos woman alone in a hotel read as a sex worker; the woman whose husband's family complain that she has "changed" because she has stopped pretending to enjoy domestic work. To force these into one "feminism" is to repeat Beauvoir's universalising error: the error that the lecture deliberately puts on the table when it asks whether Beauvoir, a middle-class, white, European intellectual, can really speak for all women.

But the case for the singular "feminism" is also strong, and is sometimes underestimated by the pluralist consensus. Three points.

First, there is a structural pattern that holds across difference. Criado-Perez's piece shows that crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, smartphones, voice-recognition systems and consumer technology are all built around a "reference man", and that women (across race and class) pay the price: "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable car crash and "17% more likely to die"; offices "five degrees too cold" because of a 1960s formula calibrated to the male resting metabolic rate; killed in policing accidents because body armour leaves them unprotected, as in the 1997 case Criado-Perez describes. This is the kind of finding that "feminism" in the singular is needed to name. Crenshaw's intersectionality refines the analysis, but it does not erase the pattern.

Second, "feminism" performs political work that "feminisms" cannot. Adichie defends the term directly: "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." When she stands and says "I am a feminist", she is doing something the plural cannot do: naming a political subject. Her concluding line, "All of us, women and men, must do better", presupposes that single political "we".

Third, Sandra Harding's strong objectivity argument is the philosophical synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, 2004: 131). The unified project is the project of starting from marginalised lives; the plural is built into that project, not opposed to it.

Concrete examples illustrate the productive tension. Hill Collins' Black feminist standpoint and hooks' rewriting of the home as a site of refuge rather than pure repression are corrections of mainstream feminist universalism, and the corrections produce a sharper feminism rather than fragmenting it. Adichie's Igbo great-grandmother who "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one" is the same point in autobiographical form: the singular term reaches across difference once we let it.

Butler herself, in *Gender Trouble*, is not trying to abolish "feminism"; she is trying to make it more honest. The destabilisation of "woman" is the condition of a more inclusive feminism, not a replacement for feminism.

My answer therefore is: yes, it is still possible to talk of feminism as a unified project, and we should, because the alternative is to lose the analytic and political category that allows us to say things like "women are 17% more likely to die in a comparable car crash". And yes, we also need "feminisms", because without internal pluralism the singular collapses back into Beauvoir's universalism. The unified project is unified in *practice*, not in *experience*; the practice is the joint construction, by many different standpoints, of a feminist politics that none of them could construct alone. (Word count: 720.)

### Mock 1: "The personal is political." Discuss with reference to Hall and Walby on the public/private split.

I argue that the second-wave slogan "the personal is political" remains one of feminism's most analytically powerful tools. The slogan does not claim that all private life is identical with the political; it claims that the boundary between personal and political is itself politically produced, and therefore politically contestable. Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) historicise that boundary; Hartsock translates the historicisation into epistemology; Adichie and Criado-Perez document its present effects.

Recall the provenance. Second-wave consciousness-raising in the late 1960s discovered that experiences each woman had treated as her own (housework, marital sex, harassment, reproductive anxiety) were systemically patterned and therefore political. The lecture's account of the public/private split gives the historical move: "Public-private split was established with the rise of capitalism… The production of goods shifted from the small-scale domestic level to the large-scale, anonymous level… You go out to work. In factories." The home is reinvented as private and feminised; "Women's role becomes the inconspicuous work of making the work of the (male) wage-worker possible… The domestic sphere becomes invisible" (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). The slogan repoliticises this newly invented private.

Hartsock translates this epistemologically. "Hartsock builds on Marx's work by saying women have been given the responsibility for certain areas of production and reproduction such as childbirth and child-rearing… whereas men have been given simply the role of producing material and financial goods… 'women's lives differ structurally from those of men'" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). The personal (childbirth, childrearing, domestic labour) is precisely what has been politically allocated; calling this allocation "private" is itself the political move that the slogan exposes.

Apply the slogan to Adichie's autobiographical case studies, each a small the-personal-is-political demonstration. Adichie tips a Lagos parking attendant; "he, this man who was happy and grateful, took the money from me, and then looked across at Louis and said, 'Thank you, sah!'" The interpersonal moment is a political diagnosis: Louis is taken as the source of money simply "because Louis is a man". The class-monitor episode at her Nsukka primary school is the same logic in microcosm: Adichie scores highest on the test, but "my teacher said the monitor had to be a boy… If we do something over and over, it becomes normal." Igbo extended-family meetings, from which Adichie is excluded "because I am female", show how the public/private split is reproduced in non-European institutional forms. The waiters who greet her male companion and ignore her, the hotel staff who assume she is a sex worker, the friend who sold her house to avoid intimidating a future husband: each personal moment becomes a political pattern.

Three objections must be met. First, the slogan is too broad: if everything is political, nothing is. This misreads. The slogan claims that the boundary between personal and political is a political artefact, not that all private life is uniformly political. Recognising this opens specific questions: who benefits from this boundary being drawn here? Whose labour is privatised? Whose violence?

Second, the slogan flattens difference. This was Black feminism's legitimate concern. The reply is that the slogan needs to be read plurally: which personal is whose political, and how do they interact? hooks's revision, "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), uses the slogan while pluralising it. Hill Collins and hooks both, the lecture notes, "questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation"; for Black women under racial terror, the home was also refuge.

Third, the slogan is dated. Against this I set Criado-Perez's gender data gap, which is the slogan in 21st-century empirical clothes. Crash-test dummies modelled on the "reference man" leave women "47% more likely to be seriously injured" and "17% more likely to die" in a comparable car crash. Office thermostats run "five degrees too cold for women" because the 1960s formula was calibrated to a male resting metabolic rate. Apple Health launched without a period tracker. Google voice recognition is "70% more likely to accurately recognise male speech". Each looks personal (my cold office, my unrecognised voice command) and is in fact a political arrangement of who counts as the human default. As Criado-Perez puts it, "for one simple reason: good smartphones are designed for male hands".

Butler radicalises the slogan further. "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). The personal-political distinction is not only redrawn; the subject who is supposed to inhabit "the personal" is itself a political production.

The slogan, properly read through intersectional revision, remains one of feminism's most powerful tools. Adichie's parking attendant, Criado-Perez's reference man, Hartsock's standpoint and the lecture's historicisation of the public/private split are its current vindications. (Word count: 730.)

### Mock 2: Critically assess hooks's argument that home can function as a site of resistance.

The lecture notes that "Collins and bell hooks both questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation" and that, for Black women, home can be a refuge as well as a constraint. I assess this argument as broadly correct, sharper than mainstream second-wave feminism's framing, and necessary to any feminism that does not collapse into Beauvoir's universalising mistake.

The standard second-wave reading. The lecture's account of the public/private split, drawing on Hall (1980) and Walby (1986), treats the home as the seat of women's invisibility under capitalism. "Women's role becomes the inconspicuous work of making the work of the (male) wage-worker possible through the provision of sustenance (material and emotional) in the home… The domestic sphere becomes invisible." This is the canonical second-wave story: the home is where women are denied subjecthood, where their labour is unwaged, where they are made the "other" of the wage-earning man. Beauvoir's account of woman-as-other gives the phenomenology; "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6); the home is the architectural form.

hooks's correction. For Black women under racial terror, the home is not only a site of repression; it is also a space of refuge from the violence of the white public sphere. The lecture summarises: Hill Collins and hooks "both questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation". hooks's "oppositional gaze" essay extends the point. "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring, for those hard intense direct looks children would give grown-ups, looks that were seen as confrontational, as gestures of resistance, challenges to authority. The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… Afraid to look, but fascinated by the gaze. There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Resistance, on hooks's account, is forged in the home, in the family, in the practices of looking that the public sphere punishes. The home is the school of the oppositional gaze.

Why this matters analytically. Three points.

First, the second-wave universalisation. Mainstream feminist analysis of the home was implicitly an analysis of the white middle-class suburban home. Hill Collins's matrix of domination shows that the same architectural form (the dwelling, the family) functions differently across racial and class positions. "'Matrix of domination'… 'structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression'" (Hill Collins). The home is one node in that matrix and its meaning varies by position.

Second, the standpoint move. The home, on Hartsock's account, is the site of social reproduction; "women have been given the responsibility for certain areas of production and reproduction such as childbirth and child-rearing" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). This is the place from which the feminist standpoint is generated. hooks complicates the picture by pointing out that the standpoint Hartsock describes is achieved differently depending on whether the home is also a barrier to racial terror. Standpoint privilege is plural.

Third, the intersectional refinement. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalises the point. The home is gendered, racialised and classed at once; it is not the same institution for the Black domestic worker and her white employer; its meaning cannot be theorised in the singular.

Adichie's text adds a non-Black-American case to the picture. Igbo family life is, for Adichie, both the site of her socialisation and the site of her exclusion: "only the male members of the extended family can attend the meetings where major family decisions are taken. So although I am the one who is most interested in these things, I cannot attend the meeting. I cannot have a formal say. Because I am female." The home is repressive in this respect. And yet it is also the site of her formation as a feminist: it is where she learns from her great-grandmother, "from stories I've heard", that "she ran away from the house of the man she did not want to marry and married the man of her choice. She refused, protested, spoke up when she felt she was being deprived of land and access because she was female. She did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." The same home that excludes her from the formal meeting transmits the genealogy of feminist resistance.

The argument has limits. hooks's rewriting of the home does not abolish its repressive function; it adds a resistance function. The two coexist. A feminism that took hooks to mean that the home is simply liberatory would lose Hall's, Walby's and Hartsock's structural critique. A feminism that ignored hooks would repeat the second-wave universalisation. The synthesis is the matrix reading: same architectural form, different functions at different intersections.

Criado-Perez's piece is the contemporary cousin of this argument. The home is where the office thermostat is *not* set to a male resting metabolic rate, and yet the office is where women find themselves "five degrees too cold". The public/private split keeps shifting, and the resistance functions of the home keep needing to be defended.

So hooks is right: the home can be a site of resistance, particularly under conditions where the public sphere is racially hostile. The right reading holds repression and resistance together, in the matrix of domination Hill Collins describes. (Word count: 740.)

### Mock 3: What does de Beauvoir mean by "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman", and why does she treat lived experience as central?

I argue that the sentence contains two of the most consequential moves in twentieth-century feminist theory: an anti-essentialist account of gender, and a methodological commitment to lived experience as the ground of philosophical knowledge. Together, they make *The Second Sex* (1949) the hinge between liberal first-wave feminism and the second-wave, standpoint and post-structuralist traditions that follow.

Begin with the sentence. Beauvoir's claim is not that biological sex is irrelevant but that "woman" (the social, psychological, juridical, aesthetic category) is constituted through a process. One becomes a woman through the accumulated weight of socialisation, mythology, institutional positioning and interpersonal recognition. The category is historical and contingent, not natural. This is the conceptual seed of what Judith Butler later theorises as performativity: gender as constituted through repeated acts that present themselves as if they were always already there.

The structural finding that grounds the claim is woman-as-other. "Humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him; she is not regarded as an autonomous being... She is the incidental, the inessential as opposed to the absolute. He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Because man is taken as the universal human, woman becomes the marked term, defined relationally, judged against a norm she does not embody. To become a woman is to be inserted into this asymmetric structure and to learn it as if it were nature.

Why does Beauvoir treat lived experience as central? Three reasons.

First, because the existentialist apparatus she inherits demands it. Human beings are not essences but projects, freedoms exercised within concrete situations. To know what it is to be a woman one must investigate the lived situation, not legislate from outside. *The Second Sex* spends hundreds of pages on biology, psychoanalysis, history, myth and then on women's lived experience (childhood, sexual initiation, marriage, motherhood, old age) because the situation cannot be deduced; it must be described.

Second, because experience is the ground on which the woman-as-other diagnosis becomes available. Women have the experience of being treated as the inessential, and theorising this experience is the unfinished task of philosophy. The second-wave slogan the personal is political, coined two decades later, is already implicit here. Nancy Hartsock's standpoint epistemology, "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), extends Beauvoir's move into a full theory of socially situated knowledge.

Third, because the work has to do double duty. It must persuade women that patterns they have been taught to read as personal failings (mediocre marriage, unfulfilling motherhood, unrecognised intellectual ambition) are systemic; and it must persuade philosophy that "women" is a question. Lived experience is the medium for both moves. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* uses the same method: not theoretical first principles but the autobiographical episode (the friend Okoloma calling her a feminist, the Lagos parking attendant thanking her through Louis, the hotel staff assuming her to be a sex worker) to generate the diagnosis.

The anti-essentialism is the most enduring contribution. If becoming a woman is a process, it can be done differently: by raising daughters and sons differently, as Adichie urges, or by destabilising the binary itself, as Butler argues. The same move underwrites trans-feminist and queer-theoretical interventions: if "woman" is constituted rather than given, there is no metaphysical bar to its being constituted otherwise.

The centrality of lived experience must, however, be qualified. Beauvoir is herself a middle-class, white, European intellectual writing in 1949, and the "lived experience" *The Second Sex* describes is in important respects her own. Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks make the corrective: many lived experiences, structured by the matrix of domination across race, class, sexuality and ability. Harding makes the point explicitly: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, 2004: 131).

Concrete examples reinforce why the move matters. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is Beauvoir restated as empirical fact: when the world is engineered around the "reference man", women's lived experience (freezing offices, ill-fitting stab vests, smartphones designed to "the limit of men's hand size") registers the asymmetry materially. Adichie's class-monitor episode is Beauvoir in autobiographical miniature: a girl who scores highest on the test is told "the monitor had to be a boy" because the teacher "assumed it was obvious". The girl becomes a woman partly through such episodes; one is not born a "woman who cannot be class monitor", one is made one.

So the sentence means: woman is a process, not an essence; and one must read that process from inside the lives it shapes. That is why Beauvoir puts lived experience at the centre, and why the move still organises feminist thought seventy-five years on. (Word count: 730.)

### Mock 4: Compare Hartsock's feminist standpoint with Hill Collins's matrix of domination.

I argue that Hartsock and Hill Collins are doing related but distinct theoretical work, and that the most defensible contemporary feminist sociology requires both. Hartsock supplies an epistemology: a theory of how knowledge is produced from structurally located lives. Hill Collins supplies an ontology of power: a theory of how oppression is organised across domains and axes. Each completes what the other leaves under-theorised.

Begin with Hartsock. Her 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint" adapts Marx's standpoint move from the proletariat to women. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), and that structural difference (women's location in social reproduction, in care of dependents) generates a critical vantage on social relations that dominant-group thinkers cannot easily reach. Women occupy a dual role: insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power). This duality permits the move Marx makes from the proletariat's location: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The standpoint is achieved through political and theoretical work, not given by anatomy; Harding's later refinement ("female feminists are made, not born") confirms this.

Now Hill Collins. *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) and "Learning from the Outsider Within" (Hill Collins, 1986) develop the matrix of domination as a framework for how power is organised. Collins identifies four interlocking domains: the structural (laws, constitutions, formal institutions), the disciplinary (bureaucratic application of structural rules through schools, prisons, welfare offices, HR), the hegemonic (culture, ideology, common sense), and the interpersonal (face-to-face encounters, everyday microaggressions). Any system of oppression operates through all four domains simultaneously, and the systems mutually reinforce each other through their shared architecture. Collins also insists on a Black feminist standpoint: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70).

The comparison yields four contrasts and three convergences.

First contrast: scope. Hartsock writes about women's structural location in social reproduction as if it were singular. Collins explicitly plurialises: there are many standpoints because there are many positions in the matrix. A Black domestic worker and a white middle-class housewife of the same period share some features and differ in many others; Hartsock's singular standpoint elides the difference, Collins's matrix makes it central.

Second contrast: object. Hartsock's primary object is knowledge: how do we know what we know, and why does location matter? Collins's primary object is power: how is domination structured, and why does it recur across oppressions?

Third contrast: genealogy. Hartsock works in a Marxist register, extending the proletarian standpoint. Collins works in a Black feminist register, grounded in "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70).

Fourth contrast: method. Hartsock theorises top-down from structural location to epistemic consequence. Collins theorises bottom-up from Black women's lived practices to analytic categories.

The convergences. Both reject the "God-trick" (Haraway, in Harding, 2004: 4) of disembodied universal knowledge. Both insist location matters for what one can know. Both treat standpoint as achieved rather than given; Harding's gloss confirms this: standpoint is something to which one is not born but at which one arrives through political and theoretical work.

Three illustrations clarify the complementarity.

Adichie's parking-attendant episode. Hartsock's standpoint explains why women generate the analysis Adichie offers: women occupy the position of the one whose money is taken and whose recognition is rerouted to the nearest man. Collins's matrix explains why the harassment of recognition operates across domains simultaneously: structural (Igbo inheritance and meeting rules), disciplinary (everyday workplace and restaurant routines that read women through male companions), hegemonic (the cultural assumption that "Louis is a man" suffices to redirect thanks), interpersonal (the moment of the gift itself).

Criado-Perez's gender data gap. Hartsock's standpoint explains why an engineering community without women produced systematically defective car safety: crash-test dummies modelled on the "reference man", leaving women "47% more likely to be seriously injured" and "17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash. Collins's matrix explains why the gap is not symmetric across race: Criado-Perez notes that the male face-shape used for dust, hazard and eye masks doesn't fit most women, "as well as a lot of black and minority ethnic men".

The public/private split. Hartsock's standpoint reading of women as both insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power) names the epistemic privilege. Collins's matrix reading of structural, disciplinary, hegemonic and interpersonal domains explains why the split is reproduced across radically different cultural settings, from Hall and Walby's industrial-capitalism case to Adichie's Igbo extended-family meetings.

So which is more useful? Neither alone. For epistemic and methodological questions about knowledge production, Hartsock is sharper. For structural-architectural questions about how power is organised, Collins is sharper. The right contemporary feminist sociology reads them together, as Harding's strong objectivity already implicitly does. The exam question implies a choice; the productive position is the synthesis. ### Mock 5: How does Crenshaw's intersectionality refine Beauvoir's account of woman-as-other?

Beauvoir names the structural asymmetry in which "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6); Crenshaw shows that the asymmetry is not single-axis. The two thinkers, read together, deliver a sharper analytic apparatus than either alone.

Recall Beauvoir. *The Second Sex* (1949) diagnoses the structural condition of "woman": she is "defined and differentiated with reference to man and not he with reference to her; she is the incidental, the inessential as opposed to the absolute. He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Beauvoir's anti-essentialism, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman", makes the othering process social and historical. Her famous methodological move is to centre lived experience: "Women have the EXPERIENCE of being considered the 'other' of men."

The limits of Beauvoir, as the lecture asks directly. "Does de Beauvoir try to 'universalize' her argument/situation? Is her account historically and socially limited (she is a middle class, white, European intellectual)? Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" The answer is no. The "woman" of *The Second Sex* is a middle-class white European; the experience of being othered that Beauvoir describes is one specific experience.

Crenshaw refines. The lecture's summary is precise: intersectionality is "the notion that different types of discrimination intersect and often compound experiences of exclusion and social subjugation." Crenshaw's point is that the othering Beauvoir describes is itself differentiated. A Black woman is not simply "woman" as Beauvoir's other; she is othered by race and by gender simultaneously, and the experience of that compound othering is not equal to the sum of being othered as Black plus being othered as a woman. The compound is its own analytic object.

Hill Collins's matrix of domination is the systemic counterpart. "'Matrix of domination'… 'structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression'… Gender does not exist 'on its own'… It is always related/produced in and through other contexts and power relations… 'Race' (or the idea of race) is equally important, if not more important, than 'gender'… we can't think gender without thinking 'race'" (Hill Collins). The matrix is the architecture; intersectionality is the diagnostic.

How this refines Beauvoir. Four moves.

First, the universalising critique. Beauvoir's woman-as-other is, on Crenshaw's reading, an account of a particular position in the matrix that has been mistakenly generalised. The refinement is to insist that the position be located: which woman, othered by which combination of axes, in which historical setting?

Second, the experience critique. Beauvoir treats lived experience as central, and Hartsock translates this into standpoint epistemology. Crenshaw inherits the experience-centring but plurialises the experiential ground: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The standpoint is achieved from a specific intersectional position.

Third, the home critique. Beauvoir's reading of the home as the site of female othering generalises a particular case. Hill Collins and hooks, the lecture notes, "both questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation". For Black women, the home is also refuge. The intersectional refinement is that "home" is one architectural form whose function varies by position.

Fourth, the colonial critique. The lecture explicitly identifies "how white women have benefited from colonialism" as one of the histories that pre-1980s feminism ignored. Beauvoir's woman-as-other was, in some respects, also a coloniser; intersectionality requires that this be theorised.

hooks's oppositional gaze is the active counterpart. "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring… The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). The othered subject of Beauvoir's analysis is, on hooks's reading, also capable of looking back; the othering is not total.

Adichie's text supplies a non-Black-American case for intersectional refinement. Her Igbo great-grandmother, her Lagos hotel experience, her Nsukka primary-school class-monitor episode, her exclusion from Igbo extended-family meetings: each is a Beauvoirian othering, and each is also intersectional (gender, ethnicity, region, class, age). Beauvoir's "woman" is not pre-political; the othering produces the category. Intersectionality and post-structuralist destabilisation converge here.

So Crenshaw refines Beauvoir by plurialising the othered subject without abandoning the structural diagnosis. The diagnosis stands; the diagnostic apparatus gets sharper. (Word count: 740.)

### Mock 6: Is queer theory a vindication or a refutation of feminist standpoint theory?

I argue, against the disjunction in the question, that queer theory is best read as a *modification* of feminist standpoint theory: neither vindication nor refutation, but a critical extension that exposes a flaw in the original formulation while preserving its core epistemic claim. The most defensible contemporary position, articulated by Sandra Harding, integrates the queer-theoretical correction without abandoning standpoint epistemology.

Begin with the two positions in their pure form.

Feminist standpoint theory, in Hartsock's 1983 formulation, holds that women's structural location, particularly in social reproduction, produces a critical vantage on patriarchy that dominant-group thinkers cannot easily occupy. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284), and that difference, theorised reflexively, generates situated knowledge. Standpoint privilege is achieved, not given; Harding's refinement ("female feminists are made, not born") confirms this. The empirical pay-off is the Criado-Perez gender data gap: starting from women's bodies and lives would have produced better engineering, medicine and policy than the engineering from the reference man that has dominated.

Queer theory, in Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) and the literature that follows, destabilises the very category "woman" on which standpoint theory rests. The famous opening: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Gender, on Butler's reading, is performative: constituted through repeated acts that present themselves as if they were always already there. The heterosexual matrix produces "woman" as one of two stable categories, and anything that does not fit (intersex bodies, trans subjectivities, non-binary identities) is rendered unintelligible.

Why might queer theory look like a refutation of standpoint theory? Because if "woman" is unstable, the structural location from which the standpoint is supposed to be generated is itself politically constructed. Hartsock's grounding in social reproduction looks suspect: women are not simply "in" social reproduction; women are constituted as those who belong there. The standpoint is, on this reading, an artefact of the very gender system feminism aims to dismantle.

The refutation reading has force but overstates the case. Three reasons.

First, Hartsock and Harding already insisted the standpoint is achieved, not given. Standpoint privilege does not depend on a metaphysically prior "woman" but on the political and theoretical work that turns a structural location into an analytic vantage. Butler's destabilisation does not undermine this; it deepens it by adding that the location itself must be analysed as constructed.

Second, the empirical pay-off of standpoint theory does not depend on the metaphysical stability of "woman". When Criado-Perez shows that crash-test dummies modelled on the reference man leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured, the category "women" is doing material-statistical work, not metaphysical work. Butler never denied that "woman" is necessary as a category of political analysis; she insisted it be used reflexively. Standpoint theory can, and Harding does, accept the reflexive use.

Third, queer theory's own critical purchase requires something very like a standpoint move. To name the structures of compulsory heterosexuality as a structure of power requires starting from lives that those structures render unintelligible. This is standpoint method in queer-theoretical clothes, and Butler herself does it: her opening move in *Gender Trouble* depends on attending to the situated knowledge of those whom the unified subject "woman" excludes.

So queer theory is best read as a vindication-through-modification. It vindicates the core claim (knowledge is socially situated; marginalised locations generate critical analytic resources) while modifying the formulation in three ways.

First, the relevant locations are plural. There is not one standpoint of "woman" but many standpoints of differently positioned subjects, including those whose existence the unified category obscures. This is also Hill Collins's correction (the matrix of domination, Black feminist standpoint, "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70)).

Second, the locations are themselves constructed. Standpoint analysis must include analysis of how the location was produced. Harding's strong objectivity, "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge", is the apparatus that handles this.

Third, the standpoint is mobilised strategically. We use "woman" when the structural pattern (the gender data gap, the public/private split, the woman-as-other diagnosis) needs naming; we destabilise it when its use threatens to exclude.

Concrete examples sharpen the synthesis. Adichie uses the category "woman" openly while attending to its constructedness: "we must raise our daughters differently. We must also raise our sons differently." Her account of being told that feminism was "un-African", and her response that her great-grandmother who fled a forced marriage "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one", uses "feminist" as a strategic category that can be applied retrospectively to a person who lacked the term but did the practice. The Criado-Perez evidence is the empirical anchor: the category "women" does material-statistical work (47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash) even as Butler insists it be handled reflexively.

So the answer is neither pure vindication nor pure refutation. The corrected standpoint theory (Harding's strong objectivity, plurialised through Hill Collins, made reflexive through Butler) is stronger than either parent position alone. (Word count: 730.)

### Mock 7: Does Harding's strong objectivity rescue or replace standpoint theory?

I argue that Harding's strong objectivity rescues standpoint theory rather than replacing it. The core standpoint claim, that knowledge is produced from socially located lives and that marginalised locations generate analytically privileged resources, is preserved. What Harding adds is a methodological apparatus that defends the claim against the charges of ethnocentrism, relativism, and unique-abilities-of-the-oppressed essentialism. Standpoint theory comes out of the encounter stronger, not displaced.

Recall the original claim. Hartsock's 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint" theorises women's structural location in social reproduction as the ground of a critical epistemology. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284); women are insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power); this duality generates a privileged vantage. The Marxist analogy is explicit: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142).

The charges against standpoint theory. Three classical objections, all addressed by Harding in chapter 8.

First, the God-trick. Critics suggest that standpoint theory just substitutes one privileged universal viewpoint (women's) for another (the disembodied scientific subject). Harding's reply: "Standpoint knowledge projects do not claim to originate in purportedly universal human problematics; they do not claim to perform the 'God-trick.'… All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, 2004: 128).

Second, ethnocentrism. Critics suggest standpoint theory treats the standpoint-holder's culture as superior. Harding answers in five steps: "standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129); thinkers with "center identities" have endorsed the move; feminist theory criticises "all gender loyalties as capable of producing only partial and distorted results of research"; there are many feminisms each starting from different historically-positioned lives; and the universalising claim is itself the ethnocentric one.

Third, relativism. Harding is uncompromising: "Judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). "It is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history" (Harding, 2004: 131–132). Standpoint theory holds that some social locations are scientifically better starting points; this is a substantive epistemological claim, not relativism.

Harding's positive synthesis: strong objectivity. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge. Thus, strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). The standard "objectivism" Harding criticises is "not rigorous or objectifying enough"; it identifies and eliminates only those values that differ within the existing community of "qualified" researchers, while leaving culturewide assumptions intact. Strong objectivity adds two requirements: that the subject of knowledge be analysed as part of the object of knowledge; and that the community of inquiry be expanded to include those whose lives have been marginalised by the discipline.

The subjects of knowledge in this revised account are "embodied and visible… multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134). The unified universal subject of empiricism is gone; many subjects, plural and located, take its place. Crucially, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation. Men's thought, too, will begin first from women's lives" (Harding, 2004: 135). Feminism is not the property of women.

Why this rescues rather than replaces. Three points.

First, the standpoint claim is preserved. Marginalised locations still generate privileged analytic resources; the lecture's Hartsock summary remains intact. Harding's contribution is to embed this claim in a methodological framework that handles the legitimate objections.

Second, the pluralism is enhanced. Hill Collins's Black feminist standpoint, hooks's oppositional gaze, and Crenshaw's intersectionality are absorbed without difficulty. Harding writes explicitly: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131).

Third, Butler's destabilisation is accommodated. The reflexivity requirement turns the subject of knowledge into part of the object of knowledge; this is exactly Butler's move in *Gender Trouble*. "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Strong objectivity is the methodological form of this critical injunction.

Criado-Perez's piece is the empirical verification. The reference man (50th-percentile male, 1.77m, 76kg) is the unreflexive subject of engineering; strong objectivity demands that the subject be made part of the object, so that the male assumption shows up in the design. Crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, smartphones, voice recognition: each is a case where strong objectivity would have produced empirically more accurate engineering.

So Harding rescues. Standpoint theory was vulnerable to the three classical objections in its raw form; Harding's strong-objectivity reformulation defends it. The lecture's framing, that "different standpoints/perspectives" matter, that "experiences can provide insights into the operations of power", that there is a "duality to experience", is preserved and made operational. The labour (her own professional travel) is invisible; the projected meaning (her sexual function for men) is what registers. Beauvoir's woman-as-other, "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6), is the structural diagnosis; Adichie supplies the lived datum.

Take the waiters who acknowledge Adichie's male companion and ignore her, even when she is paying. Take the Igbo extended-family meetings where Adichie, despite being a successful adult, cannot have a formal say "because I am female". Each episode is a Hartsockian case study: labour is essential, power is not.

Adichie generalises through the analysis. "We teach girls to shrink themselves, to make themselves smaller." Socialisation produces a feminine subject who is essential to the social order's reproduction but trained away from the assertion of power within it. "Culture does not make people. People make culture", she insists: the asymmetry is contingent and contestable, not natural.

The Criado-Perez evidence is the macro counterpart. Women's bodies and labour are essential to the economy (medical reception, education, retail, care, cleaning, food production, all disproportionately female) but the engineering and policy that shape their working conditions are calibrated to the reference man. Crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, smartphones, Apple Health's missing period tracker: women's contribution is taken for granted and their needs are not registered.

Three intersectional refinements are needed.

First, the asymmetry is not the same for all women. Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination shows that race, class and other axes intersect with the gendered asymmetry. The Black domestic worker's labour is essential to the white professional household; the migrant nanny's labour is essential to the destination economy.

Second, the asymmetry is reproduced across very different cultural settings. Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) trace its origin to the rise of industrial capitalism in Europe ("you go out to work. In factories. All this helped establish a new conception of what constituted the public realm"). Adichie's Lagos episodes show the same asymmetry holding in postcolonial Nigerian urban life. The Igbo extended-family meetings from which Adichie is excluded "because I am female" show that even within a culture where Adichie is also a celebrated public figure, the formal channels of family power remain male-only.

Third, Butler's destabilisation must be folded in. The category "woman" through which Hartsock's epigram operates is itself produced by power; "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). The essential labour and the unequal power are both, in part, effects of the category through which "woman" is constituted.

Adichie's defence of the word "feminist" articulates the political stake of keeping Hartsock's epigram in view. The asymmetry is real, recurrent and specific to women as a structural category, even as the category is itself plural and constructed. Adichie's text is the autobiographical proof; Hartsock's epigram is the analytic theorem; the gender data gap is the empirical verification. Together, they are difficult to refute. ### Mock 11: Using the work of at least two authors, explain why universalist feminism fails Black women.

I argue, using Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks as my primary authors, that universalist feminism fails Black women in three connected ways: it misdescribes their experience, mishandles their exclusion from rights regimes, and misidentifies the targets of political action. The corrective is not adding Black voices to an unchanged universalism but starting feminist analysis from a different ground.

Define the target first. "Universalist feminism", for the purposes of this question, is the strand of feminist thinking that takes "woman" as a unified pre-political category whose experiences, interests and oppressions can be theorised in the singular. The strand is detectable in Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), in much of Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949), and in classic second-wave consciousness-raising. The strand has produced wins but it carries an analytic flaw that becomes acute when Black women's lives are taken seriously. The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism failed on three counts: it universalised (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialised (presuming being a woman is enough), and was ahistorical (neglecting, for example, "how white women have benefited from colonialism").

The first failure is descriptive. Hill Collins formulates the alternative starting point: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender does not exist on its own; it is produced through race, class, sexuality and other axes simultaneously, across "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power" (Hill Collins). "Gender does not exist 'on its own'… 'Race' (or the idea of race) is equally important, if not more important, than 'gender'… we can't think gender without thinking 'race'." The "woman" of universalist feminism is implicitly white, middle-class, partnered and straight, and Black women's lives do not map onto this default.

The second failure is structural. Crenshaw's intersectionality, in the lecture's summary, is "the notion that different types of discrimination intersect and often compound experiences of exclusion and social subjugation." The universalist single-axis frame cannot register harms that are produced by the compound. Where the frame imagines a "woman" who is also incidentally Black, or a "Black person" who is also incidentally a woman, the actual subject is a Black woman whose experience is irreducible to either component.

The third failure is political. The lecture summarises hooks's contribution: "Collins and bell hooks both questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation." The universalist reading of the home as the seat of women's oppression had taken the white middle-class suburban home as the case; for Black women living under racial terror, the home was also a site of refuge, family, organisation and resistance. The political programme of mainstream feminism (escape the domestic, enter the public) presupposed the universalist case and did not fit the Black feminist standpoint. hooks's oppositional gaze, "There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), is the political consequence: Black women looking back at the systems that universalist feminism has not adequately analysed.

The convergence of the three authors is illuminating. Hill Collins gives the architecture (the matrix), Crenshaw gives the diagnostic (intersectionality at the level of compound harm), hooks gives the political indictment (the universalist reading of the home is wrong for Black women, and the oppositional gaze is the corrective). All three reject the universalist frame and all three propose starting from Black women's lives, not as a supplementary perspective but as the methodologically privileged ground for analysing gender, race and class simultaneously.

Concrete examples sharpen the argument.

Beauvoir's universalising slide is the canonical case. "Humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6) is a real diagnosis, but the "woman" being defined is a middle-class white European. The lecture asks the question directly: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" The Black feminist answer is no.

Adichie's text gives a non-Black-American case for the same point. Adichie's great-grandmother, who "ran away from the house of the man she did not want to marry", was a feminist without the word; her Igbo extended-family meetings exclude her "because I am female"; the academic who told her feminism was "un-African" used a universalising mainstream frame to deny her standing. Adichie's response, that her brother Kene is "the best feminist I know" and that "All of us, women and men, must do better", refuses both the universalist frame and the un-African dismissal.

Criado-Perez's gender data gap. Even the most empirically powerful universalist feminist evidence has a second layer once we add race: Criado-Perez notes that the male face-shape used for dust, hazard and eye masks doesn't fit most women "as well as a lot of black and minority ethnic men". The universalist frame catches part of the harm; the matrix catches the rest.

Harding's strong-objectivity directive, "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, 2004: 131), is the methodological synthesis. Universalist feminism cannot meet this directive without ceasing to be universalist.

So universalist feminism fails Black women descriptively, structurally and politically. The corrective, supplied by Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks together, is a feminism that begins from Black women's lives and treats the matrix of domination as the architectural truth of how power operates. ### Mock 12: Can feminism survive without a universal subject?

I argue that feminism can survive, and must, without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense, while retaining "woman" as a strategic, plural, politically operational category. The question's apparent dichotomy is false: the death of the universal subject of pre-1980s feminism is not the death of feminism but its maturation. Harding's strong objectivity supplies the synthesis; Hill Collins, Crenshaw, hooks, Butler and Adichie converge on it from different directions.

Begin with what is dead. The universal subject of first-wave and early second-wave feminism, "woman" as a unified pre-political category whose experiences, interests and oppressions could be theorised in the singular, has been progressively dismantled. Hill Collins's matrix of domination shows that gender is always co-produced with race and class ("Gender does not exist 'on its own'… 'Race'… is equally important, if not more important, than 'gender'… we can't think gender without thinking 'race'"). Crenshaw's intersectionality formalises the diagnostic. hooks's oppositional gaze rewrites the home as also a site of resistance. Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) destabilises "woman" as a metaphysical category: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. The verdict is in: the universal subject in the strong sense is gone.

Does feminism die with it? Three positions.

Position one: pure pluralism. There is no "feminism", only feminisms; the singular is the problem; the future is a thousand local standpoints. Its strength is descriptive accuracy. Its weakness is political: if "woman" is wholly destabilised, the subject of any structural diagnosis disappears. Criado-Perez's claim that women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable car crash presupposes a working category "women". Pure pluralism makes such claims hard to articulate.

Position two: tactical universalism. Retain "woman" as a strategic category, knowing it is constructed, and use it where the structural pattern demands. Butler herself, despite often being read as the destroyer of the subject, holds something like this position. The sentence quoted above does not say abandon the category; it says use it reflexively. Adichie's defence of "feminist" is a more accessible version: dropping the term for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Position three: Harding's synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, 2004: 131). The unity is in the *practice* (starting from marginalised lives) rather than in a metaphysically prior subject. The pluralism is built into the practice, not opposed to it. This is the position I defend.

Five reasons feminism survives without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense.

First, the structural patterns persist and require naming. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is the cleanest evidence: crash-test dummies modelled on the "reference man" leave women "47% more likely to be seriously injured" and "17% more likely to die" in a comparable car crash; office thermostats run "five degrees too cold for women" because of a 1960s formula calibrated to a male resting metabolic rate; Google voice recognition is "70% more likely to accurately recognise male speech"; Apple Health launched without a period tracker. These facts hold for women as such across race and class even while being differentiated within them. "Feminism" in the singular is needed to name the recurring structural pattern.

Second, the political "we" can be operational without being metaphysical. Adichie's "I am a feminist", followed by "All of us, women and men, must do better", uses the category as a working tool. The category is enough to do political work without being a unified prior subject.

Third, the analytic categories survive without the subject. Patriarchy, the public/private split (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986), the matrix of domination, intersectionality, performativity, the personal is political, the reference man, the oppositional gaze: none of these requires a unified prior subject of feminism. They are tools of analysis, applicable to specific cases.

Fourth, the descriptive plural strengthens rather than threatens the political singular. Hill Collins's "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70) is a Black feminist standpoint; it is also a contribution to feminism in the singular. The local enriches the general; the general gives the local political reach.

Fifth, Butler's own continued use of "feminism" and "woman" demonstrates the position's coherence. The most-cited destabiliser of the unified subject continues to write feminist theory under that name. The performative contradiction is illuminating.

Concrete examples make the synthesis work. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* mobilises "feminist" openly while attending to Igbo specificity, class context, and the constructedness of gender through socialisation. Her brother Kene is described as "the best feminist I know", a kind, masculine man who illustrates Harding's point that "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135). The great-grandmother who "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one" applies the strategic singular retrospectively, across cultural and historical distance.

A critic might object that strategic universalism is just universalism in retreat. The reply is that strategic differs from universalism in three respects: it is reflexive (the category is held under interrogation), plural (the same category accommodates different positions), and revisable (the category is open to extension as the politics demands). Universalism in the criticised sense had none of these properties.

So my answer is: yes, feminism can survive, and is in fact flourishing, without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense. The condition of its survival is the synthesis Harding articulates: unity in practice rather than in subject, plurality of standpoints rather than a single voice, and the strategic use of "woman" as a category whose construction is part of feminism's own object. The death of the metaphysical universal subject is the maturation, not the demise, of feminism. (Word count: 740.)
