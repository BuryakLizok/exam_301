## 3. Key notes from the presentation (with slide-question callouts)

**Slide 1–2: Framing the lecture.** Feminism aims to challenge what is taken as appropriate behaviour/roles for women; "the more people that feminism can 'talk for', the stronger its arguments" — but the lecturer immediately problematises this. The main question for the exam: *can or should feminism attempt to talk for all women?*

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "whether feminism can or should attempt to talk for all women, or not… Or maybe you disagree?" — this is the live question I am being asked to take a position on. I will refuse a simple yes/no and argue that feminism must do both: hold open a strategic collective subject while practising internal critique.

**Slide 3: First-wave feminism.** Post-French Revolution (1789–) liberal/equal-rights feminism; the Enlightenment background; reacts against the exclusion of women from the universality of rights; focuses on the vote and equal pay; key text Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792).

**Slide 4: But why should women want to be like men?**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club? Or should it aim to change the rules? Or to start another club?" — my answer: the rules themselves must change, because joining the club leaves the patriarchal architecture intact (Beauvoir's point about woman-as-other, hooks' point about the master's house).

**Slides 5–7: Beauvoir and the move toward second-wave feminism.** Writing in the 1940s–50s when the "aim" of life for many women was to "marry well"; *The Second Sex* (1949) asks "what is a woman?" (de Beauvoir, 1988 [1953]: 13). She insists there is no essential thing that defines women; rather, woman is a status produced through experience.

**Slide 6: A word on "gender".** Beauvoir uses *genre*; the Latin *genus* indicates type/kind; gender as an arbitrary category for organising the world — important for later post-structuralist moves (Butler).

**Slide 8: Three key points.** No essence of woman; "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman"; woman has the EXPERIENCE of being the "other" of men.

**Slide 9: "Other" and bifurcations.** "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 2010 [1949], *The Second Sex*: 6).

**Slide 10: Women as objects, not subjects.** "The representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men; they describe it from the point of view which is theirs and which they confuse with the absolute truth" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 236). Note the Disney-princess image — a contemporary mass-cultural illustration of male-authored representation.

**Slide 11: Critique of Beauvoir.**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Does de Beauvoir try to 'universalize' her argument? Is her account historically and socially limited (she is a middle class, white, European intellectual)? Can she really describe the way ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" — my answer: no. This is exactly the opening that Black feminism, postcolonial feminism, and post-structuralism rush into.

**Slides 12–15: Second-wave and the personal is political.** Patriarchy, reproduction as labour, exploitation of women's work. The private/public split is historically produced with industrial capitalism: production leaves the home, "you go **out** to work, in factories", domesticity is invented as private and feminised. Women become inconspicuous reproducers of the wage-worker and of future wage-labourers; "Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers" (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). The domestic sphere becomes invisible.

**Slide 16: Three lessons.** Different standpoints/perspectives matter; experiences reveal the operations of power; experience has a duality.

**Slide 17: Arguing for difference.** Feminism before the 1980s is criticised on three counts: it universalises (a shared history of oppression is presumed), it essentialises (being a woman is enough), and it is ahistorical (e.g. it ignores how white women have benefited from colonialism).

**Slides 19–20: Black feminist thought, matrix of domination, intersectionality.** "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Matrix of domination: "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Collins). Gender does not exist on its own; "we can't think gender without thinking 'race'". Crenshaw's intersectionality: different types of discrimination intersect and compound experiences of exclusion.

**Slides 21–22: bell hooks and the home as resistance.** Collins and hooks question the second-wave reading of home as pure repression — for Black women under racial terror the home is also refuge and resistance. The oppositional gaze: "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring… The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115).

**Slides 23–25: Hartsock and feminist materialism.** Women are both insiders (essential to the system's continuation) and outsiders (no power within it); this dual position is epistemologically privileged. "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142).

**Slides 26–27: Post-structuralist feminism and Butler.** Identities are not fixed; "woman" is not a stable category; sex and gender are signifiers, not labels of given objects. Butler: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics… Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). This opens the door to queer theory and to questioning the unified subject.

**Slide 28: Conclusion.** Feminism introduces new concepts (sex–gender, essentialism, the politics of birth as opposed to a male preoccupation with death); it shows how the world could be otherwise; and it leaves us with the standing question: can/should feminism speak for all women?

## 4. Information from the readings

### Harding (ed.), *The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, Intro pp. 4–7 and ch. 8 ("Strong Objectivity")

Harding sets up standpoint theory as a response to a need: "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women" (Harding, *Standpoint Reader*, p. 4). The "implied 'speaker' of scientific… sentences was never women. It was supposed to be humanity in general" — what Haraway calls "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (p. 4).

Harding's diagnosis is structural, not about bad individuals: "The problem was not prejudiced and biased individuals… Rather, it was a different kind of obstacle… The conceptual frameworks themselves promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns" (pp. 4–5). The disciplines were "complicitous with sexist and androcentric agendas" (p. 5).

Politics is therefore not a contaminant of knowledge but a resource: "politics and culture often function as 'prisonhouses' of knowledge… Yet they can and often do also function as 'toolboxes'" (p. 5). The remedy is to "start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities" (p. 6). But this immediately raises the controversial issue Harding identifies in the intro: "How does and should standpoint theory account for and engage with differences between women?" (p. 7) — the same question my exam asks.

In ch. 8, Harding develops **strong objectivity**. The conventional notion is "not rigorous or objectifying enough" (p. 128). Standpoint theory does not commit the **God-trick**, is not ethnocentric (because standpoint theorists "all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge", p. 129), and is not relativist ("It is not equally true… that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history", pp. 131–132). The **subject of knowledge** for standpoint theory is "embodied and visible… multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (p. 134). Strong objectivity demands "strong reflexivity": "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (p. 136). Crucially, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (p. 135) — feminism is not the property of women.

### Adichie, *We Should All Be Feminists* (Vintage Short, 2014; based on TEDxEuston 2012)

Adichie's argument is short, autobiographical, and forceful. She defines feminism using the dictionary: "a person who believes in the social, political, and economic equality of the sexes". She tells the story of being called a feminist at fourteen by her friend Okoloma, "the same tone with which a person would say, 'You're a supporter of terrorism.'" She enumerates the negative baggage: "You hate men, you hate bras, you hate African culture… you don't shave, you're always angry, you don't have a sense of humor".

Her childhood Igbo example (the class monitor): "Then, to my surprise, my teacher said the monitor had to be a boy… If we do something over and over, it becomes normal… If we keep seeing only men as heads of corporations, it starts to seem 'natural' that only men should be heads of corporations." Her Lagos examples — being refused entry to clubs alone, being thanked through her male friend Louis for money she had given a parking attendant, being assumed to be a sex worker in hotel lobbies — work as concrete intersectional evidence of how gender shapes everyday life across class.

Adichie's central reframings:
- **Anger is legitimate.** "Gender as it functions today is a grave injustice. I am angry. We should all be angry."
- **Masculinity damages men.** "Masculinity is a hard, small cage, and we put boys inside this cage… The harder a man feels compelled to be, the weaker his ego is."
- **Femininity should not have to apologise.** "I have chosen to no longer be apologetic for my femininity."
- **Culture is not destiny.** "Culture does not make people. People make culture. If it is true that the full humanity of women is not our culture, then we can and must make it our culture."
- **The case for the *word* "feminist".** "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Adichie also pushes back on the "un-African" charge — feminism is not a Western import; her great-grandmother, who ran away from a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." This is important for the universalising critique: a properly intersectional feminism can find local feminist genealogies without rebranding them as Western.

### Criado-Perez, "The deadly truth about a world built for men" (*Guardian*, 23 Feb 2019; extract from *Invisible Women*)

Criado-Perez documents the **gender data gap** — a "female-shaped 'absent presence'" in design, science, and public policy. Key empirical anchors:
- **The reference man.** Toxicology and occupational safety still rely on a Caucasian male, 25–30, 70kg. "Reference Man… his superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not."
- **Crash-test dummies.** Until 2011 US tests used only a 50th-percentile male; even now, EU regulation does not require an anthropometrically female dummy; the "female" dummy used in some tests is "just a scaled-down male dummy". Result: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured… 17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash.
- **PPE.** Body armour and stab vests sized for male bodies. A British female police officer was stabbed and killed in 1997 after removing her armour to use a hydraulic ram; another had breast-reduction surgery because of the vest. 95% of female emergency-service workers report PPE hampers their work.
- **Office temperature.** A 1960s formula based on the resting metabolic rate of the average male overestimates female metabolism by up to 35%; offices are on average five degrees too cold for women.
- **Phones, voice recognition, Apple Health.** Smartphones at the upper limit of male hand size; Google speech recognition 70% more likely to recognise male speech accurately; Apple Health launched without a period tracker.
- **Toilets.** "50/50 division of floor space has even been formalised in plumbing codes" — but women need more time and trips, so formal equality is substantive inequality.

This article is gold for the standpoint argument: starting from women's bodies and women's daily life generates *more accurate* science (Harding's strong objectivity), and starting from men's bodies as the default is not neutral — it is, in Harding's terms, the God-trick in engineering.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The arc of feminist theory in this lecture is a movement from rights, to experience, to standpoint, to difference, to deconstruction — and the question I am asked is whether this trajectory empties feminism of any usable subject. I argue it does not, provided we read each move as a correction rather than a replacement.

**Wollstonecraft's liberal feminism is necessary but insufficient.** It treats women as already-formed rational subjects who have been wrongly excluded; this leaves intact the very norm against which they are measured. The slide's deliberately provocative question — "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club?" — names the limit of equal-rights feminism. Beauvoir's intervention is precisely to refuse the male norm: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Beauvoir's anti-essentialism — "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" — is the conceptual hinge of the whole module. It makes gender a social process, not a biological destiny, and it grounds the second-wave slogan that the personal is political.

**Patriarchy and the public/private split are not eternal facts but historical products.** Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) historicise the very domesticity that earlier liberal feminism took for granted: with industrial capitalism, "you go **out** to work, in factories", and the home is reinvented as a feminised private. Hartsock's contribution is to translate this materialist insight into an epistemology. Women's dual position — insiders to social reproduction, outsiders to power — generates a privileged vantage point: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The Criado-Perez piece is, in effect, a 21st-century vindication of this claim: starting from women's bodies in nail salons, in cars, in stab vests, in offices, produces better engineering — empirically less partial, more accurate, "strong objectivity" in Harding's sense.

**But standpoint theory is not safe from its own universalising tendencies.** Hill Collins, Crenshaw, and hooks all insist that "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70); the matrix of domination shows that "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Collins). Crenshaw's intersectionality is the conceptual tool here; hooks' oppositional gaze — "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) — names the active, resistant subject that flat universalism misses. Importantly, Collins and hooks also rewrite the second-wave reading of "home": for Black women living under racial terror, home is not only a site of patriarchal repression but also a refuge and an organising base.

**Butler radicalises this further.** "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). This is the queer theoretical move: the unified subject that liberal feminism relied on is itself an effect of power, and any "we" implicates exclusions (of trans women, of intersex people, of non-binary subjects). Performativity replaces essence; the destabilisation of "woman" is not the end of feminism but the condition of a more inclusive politics.

**Where this leaves the exam question.** I am persuaded by Harding's resolution: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). Adichie supplies the practical correlate — the term "feminist" must be retained because dropping it for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded". "Feminism" and "feminisms" are therefore not rivals: they are two sides of one practice. Feminism names the collective political subject under construction; feminisms names the plural standpoints whose internal argument keeps that subject honest.

## 8. Essay answers

### Past-Q 2024 (Q4): Is 'feminisms' a better way to understand women's experiences than 'feminism'?

I argue that the plural and the singular are not rivals: "feminisms" is the better description of the field, but "feminism" remains a necessary political fiction. Dropping the singular concedes too much; refusing the plural concedes too little.

The case for "feminisms" is overwhelming once one looks closely at women's lives. The lecture slides reach this conclusion explicitly: feminism before the 1980s was universalising (it presumed a shared history of oppression), essentialising (it presumed being a woman is enough) and ahistorical (it neglected how white women have benefited from colonialism). Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) names a real structural condition — "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6) — but the lecture immediately asks: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir is a middle-class, white, European intellectual, and what is universalised under "woman" too often turns out to be one specific class location.

Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks make this concrete. "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70), and the matrix of domination shows that gender always operates with race and class. Crenshaw's intersectionality, originally developed to explain why Black women workers fell through both racial-discrimination law and sex-discrimination law, is the conceptual reason we must speak in the plural. hooks' rewriting of the home — "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115) — refuses the second-wave reading that home is uniformly repressive; for Black women under racial terror, home is also refuge.

Concrete examples follow easily. Lean In feminism, which urges women to ask harder for raises and promotions, presupposes a corporate ladder already accessible to a particular class of (largely white, college-educated) women; it has very little to say to the care workers, mainly women of colour, whose unpaid and underpaid labour holds families together — a fact made brutally visible during the pandemic when care work and "essential" work were revealed to overlap. The Roe v Wade reversal in 2022 hits women very differently along racial and economic lines: a wealthy woman in Texas flies to New York; a poor woman, disproportionately Black or Latina, carries an unwanted pregnancy. Iran's "women, life, freedom" movement after the killing of Mahsa Amini is feminist, but it is not the same feminism as the trans-rights debates roiling the UK; menstrual-leave policies in Spain and Japan look feminist in one frame and patronising in another. To force all of these into one "feminism" is to make Beauvoir's mistake again.

And yet I want to resist the conclusion that we should simply drop the singular. The pluralist position has its own pathology: if every standpoint is irreducibly its own, there is no political subject left at all, and feminism dissolves into a thousand identity claims. Judith Butler's caution — "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2) — does not mean abandoning "woman", it means handling the category carefully. Adichie puts the point in its most usable form: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." The Criado-Perez evidence is decisive on this. When crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, voice recognition and Apple Health are designed around the reference man, women of every race, class and sexuality are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash, freeze in the office, and find their phones unsafe to use one-handed at a protest. These are not symbolic harms. They name a structural condition that crosses every "intersection".

Sandra Harding offers the synthesis I find most convincing: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). This is exactly the relationship I want to defend between feminism and feminisms. The plural names the *internal pluralism of the movement*; the singular names the *external political subject* the movement constructs in order to make the kind of claim Adichie makes when she says, simply, "I am a feminist."

So my answer is: feminisms describes the field; feminism names the practice. To pick one without the other is to lose either the descriptive accuracy that standpoint and intersectional theory have so painfully won, or the political traction without which feminist claims have no purchase. The exam question pushes for a choice; I refuse the false choice. We need both terms, and the productive tension between them, to understand women's experiences — experiences that are radically plural in form and yet, as Criado-Perez shows, still recognisably gendered in effect. ### Past-Q 2025 (Q5): Outline and assess the political importance of feminism today.

Feminism remains politically important today because the structural problem it identifies — that women, as a class, are positioned as "other" to a male norm that masquerades as universal — is empirically alive in 2026, even as the form of that othering has shifted. I will outline the political agenda that follows from this and then assess its limits.

The outline first. Liberal first-wave feminism, in Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792), set the equal-rights agenda: vote, education, equal pay. That agenda is unfinished — the gender pay gap persists across OECD economies — but it is no longer sufficient. Simone de Beauvoir's diagnosis remains the live one: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Second-wave feminism translated this into a political programme — "the personal is political" — that connected patriarchy, reproduction-as-labour and the exploitation of women's work, showing that the public/private split was a historical product of industrial capitalism (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). Black feminist thought, intersectionality and post-structuralist feminism refined this further: Collins' matrix of domination, Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze, Butler's destabilisation of "woman".

Now the assessment. Why is this politically important *now*?

First, because the gender data gap is killing women. Caroline Criado-Perez documents that crash-test dummies until 2011 were modelled on a 50th-percentile male, and even now the EU does not require an anthropometrically female dummy. Result: a woman in a comparable car crash is 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die. Police stab vests do not fit women; in 1997 a British officer was stabbed to death after removing her armour to use a hydraulic ram. Heart-attack diagnostic criteria were built on male symptoms, so women's "atypical" presentations are missed in A&E. Office temperatures are five degrees too cold for the female metabolic rate. Apple Health launched without a period tracker. These are not abstract grievances; they are the price of an engineering culture that takes the "reference man" as the human norm. Feminism is the only political movement systematically attending to this.

Second, because reproductive rights are in retreat. The 2022 Dobbs decision reversed Roe v Wade; abortion is now illegal or near-illegal in much of the US South. Sex-work decriminalisation debates, menstrual leave policies, and the regulation of fertility technologies are all live legislative questions in 2026. None of these can be addressed without a vocabulary that names women's specific bodily situation — which is precisely what "feminism", as opposed to "human rights" in general, supplies. Adichie's defence stands: "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Third, because care work is a public crisis. The pandemic made it impossible to ignore that the global economy runs on (mostly women's) unpaid and low-paid care. Nancy Hartsock's point — "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284) — is now a budget question. Care workers, disproportionately women of colour and migrants, hold the social fabric together while being structurally invisible.

Fourth, because of #MeToo and what came after it. The 2017 wave that toppled Weinstein and others was, sociologically, a standpoint moment: it built collective insight out of shared but previously private experience, exactly as Hartsock predicted. #MeToo's limits — its uneven reach into low-wage workplaces, its complicated relationship with due process — are real, but they are arguments for *more* feminism, not for replacing feminism with something blander.

Fifth, because the global picture is intensifying, not relaxing. Iran's "women, life, freedom" movement, after the killing of Mahsa Amini, is a feminist movement under conditions of state violence. Afghan women have been forced back out of public life. India's gang-rape cases continue. These cannot be dealt with through liberal-equality language alone because the situations are not symmetrical; the analytical apparatus of patriarchy, of woman-as-other, and of intersectionality is needed.

I must assess feminism's limits honestly. Lean In corporate feminism has been rightly criticised by Black feminists and labour scholars for marketing class-bound professional advancement as women's liberation. The current trans-rights debates have split older feminists from younger ones in ways that have damaged feminist alliances. Some forms of carceral feminism have collaborated with punitive state power against the very communities they claim to defend. These are real internal pathologies. They are also, as Harding insists, reasons to plurialise the term ("feminisms") while keeping the practice ("feminism").

In sum, the political importance of feminism today is high and rising. It is the only language that simultaneously names the historical specificity of women's subordination, refuses to flatten that subordination into a single experience, and provides the analytic tools — patriarchy, standpoint, intersectionality, the gender data gap — for diagnosing material harms in real institutions. To abandon the word now would be to lose, in Harding's words, the resource through which "a social and political disadvantage can be turned into" a critical insight. We need that resource. (Word count: 748.)

### Past-Q 2023 (Q5): Should sociology use the term "feminism" or "feminisms"?

Sociology should use both terms, deliberately, and should resist the rhetorical pressure to pick one. This is not a fudge; it reflects how sociological knowledge is actually produced — by holding analytic categories open enough to capture a structural pattern while keeping them porous enough to admit internal difference. I will defend this position in three steps: the case for the singular, the case for the plural, and the case for their disciplined coexistence.

The case for the singular ("feminism") rests on the fact that sociology must be able to name structures. Simone de Beauvoir's claim — "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6) — describes a structural pattern of othering that crosses cultures, classes and historical periods even as it takes different concrete forms. Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap is a striking sociological demonstration: in 2026 women are still 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash because the engineering profession has, for half a century, treated the male body as the human default. The thermostat in the office, the stab vest, the smartphone, the voice-recognition system, Apple Health's missing period tracker — these are all the same sociological fact, namely that the "reference man" stands in for humanity. Sociology needs a name for that fact. "Feminism" is that name. To abandon the singular is to give up on the claim that there is a recognisable, describable pattern. Adichie makes the same point politically: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

The case for the plural ("feminisms") is just as strong sociologically. The lecture is explicit that pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. Patricia Hill Collins formulates the Black feminist counter-position: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender does not "exist on its own"; it is produced through race, class, sexuality, and disability simultaneously. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalised this for legal scholarship and then for sociology. bell hooks' rewriting of the home — "There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) — refuses the second-wave assumption that the domestic sphere is uniformly repressive. Judith Butler pushes the move further: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). For Butler the unified category "woman" is itself a production of power, and a sociology that uses the term naively will reproduce exclusions — historically of trans women, of working-class women, of women of colour.

This pluralism is not an academic affectation; it has empirical purchase. Sociologically, "feminism in the singular" cannot explain why the Roe reversal in 2022 hit Black and Latina women in the US South harder than wealthier white women in coastal states. It cannot explain why menstrual-leave policies look emancipatory in some contexts and patronising in others. It cannot explain why the Iranian women-life-freedom movement and the UK trans-rights debate look almost incommensurable. Adichie's own essay is a beautifully empirical demonstration: her Igbo background, her Lagos clubs and hotels, her great-grandmother who refused a forced marriage, all show that feminist consciousness arises in many forms with many vocabularies, and that "Culture does not make people. People make culture."

So far this looks like a straightforward win for "feminisms". But the case for their coexistence is the sociologically interesting one. Sandra Harding gives the cleanest statement: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). What Harding is describing is the way sociology actually works: we generalise *and* we attend to difference, in the same breath. The plural names the internal pluralism of standpoints; the singular names the political subject those standpoints jointly construct.

A purely pluralist sociology would face two problems. First, it would lose the capacity to make structural claims of the Criado-Perez type — claims that hold for women as such, across other axes of difference. Second, it would underestimate the way "feminisms" can serve as a covert essentialism in its own right (e.g. when "Black feminism" is used to mean a single thing rather than a plural tradition in turn). Conversely, a purely singular sociology repeats Beauvoir's mistake of universalising one location.

My recommendation, then, is that sociology should treat "feminism" and "feminisms" as a dialectical pair. We use the singular when we are making a structural claim about patriarchy, the gender data gap, the public/private split, the standpoint of social reproduction. We use the plural when we are attending to the standpoints of Black, working-class, lesbian, trans, Igbo, Iranian and other women — whose differences are not noise around a signal but are themselves the signal. To insist on one term is to lose half the analytic work sociology has to do. (Word count: 748.)

### Past-Q 2022 (Q8): Should women's experience be the basis of feminism?

Women's experience should be a foundational input to feminism, but it cannot be its sole basis without producing precisely the universalising, essentialising and ahistorical errors that Black, post-structuralist and standpoint feminism have spent forty years correcting. The right answer is the one Sandra Harding develops: experience is the *starting point* but not the *foundation* of feminist knowledge.

The case for experience is the strongest part of the feminist tradition. Beauvoir built *The Second Sex* on the claim that the lived experience of being woman — "Women have the EXPERIENCE of being considered the 'other' of men" — had never been theorised. Her famous formulation, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman", makes experience constitutive rather than incidental. The second-wave slogan "the personal is political" extended this: experiences that had been quarantined as private (housework, sexual violence, reproductive control) were revealed to be political because their pattern was systemic. Nancy Hartsock's feminist standpoint translated experience into epistemology: women's "dual social role" as both insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power) generates a critical vantage on patriarchy, just as the proletariat's dual position generated Marx's critique of bourgeois ideology (Hartsock, 1983: 142). Adichie's TEDx talk is a contemporary refresh of this argument: she does not start from theory but from being a fourteen-year-old Igbo girl called a feminist by her friend, from being thanked through her male friend Louis by a Lagos parking attendant, from being assumed to be a sex worker in a hotel lobby. Experience generates the analysis.

The Criado-Perez evidence shows the analytic power of starting from experience. Sandi Toksvig's anthropology professor reframes an antler bone with 28 markings: a male anthropologist sees "man's first attempt at a calendar"; a female anthropologist sees a woman tracking her cycle. The interpretive difference flows from a different bodily experience of time. The same logic explains why Apple Health launched without a period tracker, why office thermostats freeze women, why stab vests get women killed: a male-only experiential base produced systematically defective knowledge. Starting from women's experience would have produced better engineering, better medicine, better policy. This is Harding's strong objectivity: politically engaged knowledge that starts from marginalised standpoints is, empirically, more accurate.

But experience is also dangerous as a *foundation*. Three problems.

First, the universalising problem. The lecture asks of Beauvoir directly: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir's "woman" is implicitly a middle-class, white, European intellectual. Patricia Hill Collins responds by insisting on Black women's specific standpoint: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows that gender is always co-produced with race and class; a feminism that takes one experience as the experience effaces this.

Second, the essentialising problem. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* warns that "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). If we ground feminism in "women's experience", we have to specify whose experience counts as women's — and any specification will exclude. Trans women's experiences? Intersex experiences? Asexual women's experiences? Women without children? Each line we draw reproduces a power-laden boundary.

Third, the empirical problem. Adichie tells of an academic who told her feminism was "un-African". Adichie's response is that her great-grandmother, who fled a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." Experience alone, without an interpretive frame, does not arrive labelled "feminist"; the same experiences are mobilised by anti-feminist as well as feminist analyses (e.g. "but what is a girl doing in a room with four boys?"). Experience requires theory to become knowledge.

Harding's resolution is the most defensible. Standpoint theory holds that "Women's lives and experiences provide the 'grounds' for this knowledge, though these clearly do not provide foundations for knowledge in the conventional philosophical sense. These grounds are the site, the activities, from which scientific questions arise" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 128). Experience is the necessary starting point; reflexive theoretical work — strong objectivity — is what turns it into knowledge. And, Harding insists, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (p. 135). Feminism is not the property of those who have the relevant experiences; it is a theoretical and political practice that *begins* with those experiences but is owed by everyone.

So my answer is: yes, women's experience should be the basis of feminism, in the sense of the starting point; no, it cannot be the foundation, in the sense of an unmediated ground that licenses claims by itself. To collapse the distinction is either to repeat Beauvoir's universalising move (one experience for all women) or to dissolve feminism into a thousand incommensurable testimonies. The discipline of standpoint theory keeps both errors at bay. (Word count: 749.)

### Past-Q 2021 (Q8): Is it still possible to talk of "feminism" as a unified project or do we need to use the term "feminisms"?

It is still possible — and politically necessary — to talk of feminism as a unified project, provided we understand that unity in the right way. The unity is not of content (one experience, one theory, one programme) but of *practice*: a shared commitment to identifying and dismantling patriarchy in its many forms. Within that practice, "feminisms" in the plural names the internal pluralism that keeps the practice honest. I argue, against the implied dichotomy of the question, that the two terms are complementary rather than rival.

The case for "feminisms" is, by 2026, almost the default position in academic sociology, and rightly so. The lecture identifies three failures of feminism before the 1980s: it universalised (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialised (presuming being a woman is enough) and was ahistorical (ignoring, for instance, how white women had benefited from colonialism). Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and bell hooks gave us the conceptual tools to repair these failures. Collins' matrix of domination ("structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression"), Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze — all insist that "gender" is never produced in isolation from race, class, sexuality, ability. Judith Butler radicalises the move: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, *Gender Trouble*, 1990: 2). Sociologically, the plural is needed because women's lives are radically different across location: a Black domestic worker in Mississippi, an Igbo writer in Lagos, an Iranian student in Tehran, a Polish nurse in London, a trans woman in São Paulo, a stay-at-home parent in Tokyo. To force these into one "feminism" is to repeat Beauvoir's universalising error — the error that the lecture deliberately puts on the table when it asks whether Beauvoir, a middle-class, white, European intellectual, can really speak for all women.

But the case for the singular "feminism" is also strong, and is sometimes underestimated by the pluralist consensus. Three points.

First, there is a structural pattern that holds across difference. Caroline Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* shows that crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, smartphones, voice-recognition systems and clinical trials are all built around a "reference man", and that women — across race and class — pay the price: 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash; freezing in offices set to a male metabolism; killed in policing accidents because body armour leaves them unprotected. This is the kind of finding that "feminism" in the singular is needed to name. Crenshaw's intersectionality refines the analysis, but it does not erase the pattern.

Second, "feminism" performs political work that "feminisms" cannot. Adichie defends the term directly: "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." When she stands on a TEDx stage and says "I am a feminist", she is doing something the plural cannot do — naming a political subject. The Iranian women-life-freedom movement, the Polish black-protest march against abortion restrictions, the global #MeToo wave: each of these is empowered by being able to speak under one banner without forfeiting internal difference.

Third, Sandra Harding's strong objectivity argument is the philosophical synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways by the operative systems of social stratification. The different feminisms inform each other; we can learn from all of them and change our patterns of belief" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 131). The unified project is the project of starting from marginalised lives; the plural is built into that project, not opposed to it.

Concrete examples illustrate the productive tension. Lean In corporate feminism is a feminism that mostly serves white professional women; Black feminism rightly critiques it; the result is a more accurate analysis of class within feminism, not a fragmenting of feminism into incommensurable parts. The 2022 Roe reversal is a single political event with starkly intersectional effects: a wealthy white woman in Texas flies to a legal state; a poor Black woman carries an unwanted pregnancy. Feminism in the singular names the regulatory regime; feminisms in the plural names the differential impact. Trans rights debates in the UK have split feminists — but the dispute, however bitter, presupposes a shared term within which to argue.

Butler herself, in *Gender Trouble*, is not trying to abolish "feminism" — she is trying to make it more honest. The destabilisation of "woman" is the condition of a more inclusive feminism, not a replacement for feminism.

My answer therefore is: yes, it is still possible to talk of feminism as a unified project, and we should, because the alternative is to lose the analytic and political category that allows us to say things like "women are 47% more likely to die in a comparable car crash". And yes, we also need "feminisms", because without internal pluralism the singular collapses back into Beauvoir's universalism. The unified project is unified in *practice*, not in *experience*; the practice is the joint construction, by many different standpoints, of a feminist politics that none of them could construct alone. (Word count: 748.)

### Mock 1: To what extent does Simone de Beauvoir's account of woman-as-other survive intersectional critique?

Beauvoir's account survives, but it survives in modified form. Her structural insight — that woman is constituted as the inessential against a male absolute — remains analytically powerful; her implicit universalism, which presents woman in the singular, does not. A defensible reading of Beauvoir today treats *The Second Sex* as the first move in a longer argument that intersectionality completes rather than refutes.

Begin with Beauvoir's core claim. *The Second Sex* (1949) asks "what is a woman?" and refuses any essentialist answer; one becomes a woman, one is not born one. The structural finding follows: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him; she is not regarded as an autonomous being… She is the incidental, the inessential as opposed to the absolute. He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Two further claims follow. Women are made objects rather than subjects: "The representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men; they describe it from the point of view which is theirs and which they confuse with the absolute truth" (*The Second Sex*: 236). And experience is the missing ground: women have the experience of being treated as other, and theorising this experience is the unfinished task of philosophy. This is an enormous theoretical achievement; it grounds second-wave feminism, the personal-is-political slogan, the standpoint tradition and (via Butler) post-structuralist feminism.

But the lecture is explicit that Beauvoir's account has limits. "Does de Beauvoir try to 'universalize' her argument? Is her account historically and socially limited (she is a middle class, white, European intellectual)? Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" The honest answer is no. Beauvoir's woman-as-other is recognisable to a Parisian intellectual of 1949 but it is not unproblematically applicable to, say, an enslaved African woman in the eighteenth-century Caribbean, for whom the master/slave binary cuts across the man/woman binary; or to a colonised woman, for whom European feminism was itself sometimes an instrument of empire; or to a working-class woman, whose labour was very far from invisible. Patricia Hill Collins's complaint is precisely this: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70), and the matrix of domination shows that gender always operates through race and class. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalises the move.

Concrete examples sharpen the critique. The double-day of paid plus unpaid labour is not equally distributed among women: women of colour and migrant women disproportionately do the care work that allows white professional women to "lean in". Reproductive politics post-Roe falls differently on Black and Latina women in US states than on white women with resources. Beauvoir's woman-as-other does not, by itself, predict these differentials; the matrix of domination does. The Adichie story of being thanked through her male friend Louis by a Lagos parking attendant — "Because Louis is a man" — is recognisably Beauvoirian; but Adichie also explicitly insists that her Igbo cultural location matters, that her great-grandmother was a feminist without using the word, and that "Culture does not make people. People make culture." This is intersectional Beauvoir.

A further pressure comes from Butler. If "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2), then Beauvoir's "woman" is not the pre-given subject of feminist liberation but is itself a production. Trans-feminist scholarship has pushed this further: Beauvoir's account, in its 1949 form, does not have the conceptual resources to address transgender women's experiences of being woman-as-other, nor does it accommodate non-binary subjects.

So in what sense does Beauvoir survive? In two senses.

First, the *structural finding* survives. Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap is Beauvoir restated empirically: when the world is built around the "reference man", woman is literally the second sex of engineering. Crash-test dummies modelled on a 50th-percentile male; stab vests sized for male torsos; office thermostats calibrated to male metabolism; voice-recognition systems trained on male speech — these are 21st-century instances of "the representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men". Intersectional critique adds that the reference man is also Caucasian, but it does not erase the gender finding.

Second, Beauvoir's *method* survives. Starting from the lived experience of being constituted as other is the move Hartsock generalises into standpoint theory and Harding refines into strong objectivity. Hill Collins, hooks and Crenshaw extend the method to multiple standpoints; they do not abandon it.

So to the extent: Beauvoir's woman-as-other survives intersectional critique as a *structural starting point*, not as a *closed description*. The mistake to avoid is reading Beauvoir as the final word; the equal mistake to avoid is reading intersectional critique as a reason to discard Beauvoir. The two are most powerful when read together. (Word count: 750.)

### Mock 2: Critically assess Nancy Hartsock's claim that women occupy an epistemically privileged position.

Hartsock's claim is that women, by virtue of their structural location, can produce more accurate knowledge of the social world than dominant-group thinkers can. I argue that the claim is defensible in a modified form: standpoint privilege is real but it is conditional, achieved rather than given, and plural rather than singular. The strongest version of Hartsock's argument is the one Sandra Harding develops as "strong objectivity"; the weakest is the version that treats biological womanhood as automatically conferring insight.

Hartsock's argument runs as follows. Women occupy a dual role: they are insiders, "crucial to the continuation of the current system" through reproduction and care; and they are outsiders, with "less power within the system". This duality produces a vantage point that dominant-group thinkers cannot easily occupy: "It is because of this dual social role, that women's experiences more accurately reflect the reality of the social world." Hartsock derives this from Marx: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). And: "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284).

The case for Hartsock is empirical as well as philosophical. Caroline Criado-Perez's reporting is, in effect, a vindication. The "reference man" of toxicology and crash-testing produces systematically biased science: women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash; offices are five degrees too cold for the female metabolic rate; stab vests get female police officers killed. A scientific community that had taken women's standpoints seriously from the start would have produced better engineering, better medicine, better policy. Harding generalises the point: "Standpoint theories argue for 'starting off thought' from the lives of marginalised peoples; beginning in those determinate, objective locations in any social order will generate illuminating critical questions that do not arise in thought that begins from dominant group lives" (Harding, ch. 8). This is not relativism — it is the claim that politically engaged starting points produce *more*, not less, accurate knowledge.

Sandi Toksvig's antler-bone anecdote in the Criado-Perez piece captures the logic perfectly. A male anthropologist looks at 28 markings and sees "man's first attempt at a calendar"; a female anthropologist looks at the same markings and asks what a man would need to know in 28-day cycles, and proposes that it is a woman tracking her menstruation. The female standpoint generates the better hypothesis. The lecture's slide on Hartsock makes this concrete: the duality is not metaphorical, it is a position in the division of labour.

But Hartsock's claim faces serious objections.

First, the universalising objection. Hartsock writes as if "women" had a single structural location, but this is precisely what Black feminism and intersectionality refute. Hill Collins's matrix of domination shows that women occupy many different structural locations, intersecting with race and class. A Black domestic worker in 1950s Mississippi and a white middle-class American housewife of the same decade do not share a standpoint; they share certain features and differ in others. Hartsock's claim must therefore be plurialised: there are *standpoints*, not a single feminist standpoint.

Second, the essentialising objection. Critics ask whether being a woman is sufficient to produce feminist insight. Harding answers crisply: no. Standpoint privilege is achieved through political struggle, not given by anatomy. "Female feminists are made, not born" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 135). And Harding goes further: men "must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation"; feminism is not a property of women.

Third, the relativising objection. If different standpoints produce different knowledges, on what grounds can we adjudicate between them? Harding's reply is that this is the wrong question: standpoint theory does not say all standpoints are equally accurate; it says that "some social situations are scientifically better than others as places from which to start off knowledge projects" (Harding, ch. 8). Strong objectivity demands strong reflexivity — examining the social situatedness of the knower as part of the knowledge.

Fourth, the political objection. Some critics worry that standpoint theory privileges suffering: the more oppressed, the more epistemically authoritative. Harding rejects this caricature: standpoint theory privileges *the analytic vantage that marginalised locations afford*, not victimhood as such.

My critical assessment, then, is that Hartsock's core claim is right and important: structural location matters for knowledge, and women's structural location generates analytic resources unavailable to dominant-group thinkers. The Criado-Perez data is hard to read in any other way. But the claim must be modified along three axes. The privilege is *plural* (different women, different standpoints, in the matrix of domination); *achieved* (the standpoint is the product of political work, not the gift of biology); and *epistemic but not exclusive* (men too can contribute feminist knowledge from their specific positions).

In the modified form Harding gives it as strong objectivity, Hartsock's claim survives and indeed flourishes. In the unmodified 1983 form it is vulnerable to exactly the critiques that intersectional and post-structuralist feminism have pressed since. (Word count: 749.)

### Mock 3: Compare Patricia Hill Collins' matrix of domination with Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality. Which is more useful for contemporary feminist sociology?

Hill Collins and Crenshaw are usually grouped together as Black feminist thinkers who insist on multiple, interacting axes of oppression, and rightly so. But the two concepts do different analytic work, and the choice of one over the other depends on what kind of sociological question one is asking. I argue that Crenshaw's intersectionality is more useful as a *diagnostic* tool for specific cases of compounded discrimination, while Collins' matrix of domination is more useful for *systemic* analysis of how power is structured. Contemporary feminist sociology needs both.

Begin with the matrix of domination. Collins defines Black feminist thought as "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix is then her account of how power operates: "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression". Four domains. The *structural* domain refers to laws, institutions, the constitution of the state. The *disciplinary* domain refers to bureaucratic practice — schools, prisons, welfare offices — which apply structural rules through routinised procedures. The *hegemonic* domain refers to culture, ideology, common sense: the stories a society tells itself. The *interpersonal* domain refers to face-to-face encounters and everyday microaggressions. Collins's argument is that any system of oppression — racism, sexism, classism, heterosexism — operates through all four domains simultaneously, and that different systems mutually reinforce each other through these shared domains.

Now intersectionality. Crenshaw introduced the concept in a 1989 legal article to explain why Black women workers were falling through the cracks of US discrimination law: their cases were dismissed because what they experienced (being treated worse than white women *and* than Black men, *as Black women*) did not fit either sex-discrimination or race-discrimination doctrine taken separately. Intersectionality names the moment at which two or more axes of oppression cross and compound. The lecture summarises it well: "the notion that different types of discrimination intersect and often compound experiences of exclusion and social subjugation."

Where the concepts differ. Intersectionality is, in its origin and in much of its use, a *case-level* concept. It is a tool for diagnosing the experiences of specific subjects at the crossroads of specific axes. Concretely: a Black trans sex worker in 2026 sits at the intersection of racism, transphobia, misogyny and whorephobia, and intersectionality is exactly the right lens. Hill Collins's matrix is, by contrast, a *system-level* concept. It is a tool for mapping how power as such — across all systems of oppression — is structured into recurring domains. The matrix does not depend on identifying a particular subject; it is a description of the architecture of oppression.

For contemporary sociology, both are indispensable. Consider three examples.

*#MeToo and the Weinstein case.* Intersectionality is essential to explain why the movement, while triggered by mostly white actresses, was systematically prefigured by Tarana Burke's earlier work with young Black women, and why working-class women in low-wage service jobs were under-served by the wave that toppled celebrity perpetrators. But Hill Collins's matrix is what explains the *recurrence* of the same harassment patterns across structural (employment law), disciplinary (HR), hegemonic (jokes about casting couches) and interpersonal (everyday workplace touching) domains.

*The Roe v Wade reversal in 2022.* Intersectionality is the right lens for explaining the differential impact: Black, Latina, low-income and rural women bear the heaviest burden of abortion bans. But the matrix of domination is what explains why patriarchy, anti-Blackness and class power converge in reproductive politics — the same four domains (state law, disciplinary application by clinics and providers, hegemonic moralisation, interpersonal stigma) operate at once.

*Care work and the pandemic.* Intersectionality names the specific position of migrant women of colour who do disproportionate care work. The matrix names the systemic fact that care work as such has been structurally devalued through the long history of the public/private split that Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) describe.

So which is more useful? My answer depends on the sociological task.

For *advocacy and legal analysis*, intersectionality is sharper. It is action-oriented; it generates testable claims about specific subjects falling between specific doctrines.

For *theory and systemic critique*, the matrix is sharper. It is architectural; it shows how power-structures rhyme across oppressions. It also gives feminist sociology a way to talk to anti-racist and anti-capitalist sociology without collapsing one into the other.

The two are mutually reinforcing rather than rival. Crenshaw's intersectionality without Collins's matrix risks becoming a checklist of identity categories; Collins's matrix without intersectionality risks becoming a high-altitude abstraction. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* implicitly uses both — her examples of being assumed a sex worker in a hotel lobby and ignored by waiters are intersectional case data; her analysis of why "Culture does not make people. People make culture" is matrix-level critique.

The right answer to the exam question is therefore a refusal of the forced choice: contemporary feminist sociology should treat intersectionality and the matrix of domination as complementary instruments. The matrix tells us how power is structured; intersectionality tells us where it falls. (Word count: 750.)

### Mock 4: Discuss, with concrete examples, whether Caroline Criado-Perez's gender data gap vindicates feminist standpoint theory.

Criado-Perez's reporting in *Invisible Women* and the *Guardian* extract is, I argue, an unusually clean empirical vindication of feminist standpoint theory in its Harding/Hartsock formulation. The gender data gap is not a moral claim — it is a list of cases in which starting science and engineering from women's bodies and lives would have produced more accurate, less partial knowledge. Strong objectivity, properly understood, predicts exactly this.

Standpoint theory's core claim, as set out by Hartsock and refined by Harding, is twofold. First, that knowledge is socially situated; "the implied 'speaker' of scientific… sentences was never women. It was supposed to be humanity in general", and Haraway calls this the "God-trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, *Standpoint Reader*, p. 4). Second, that *starting* research from marginalised lives produces less partial knowledge: "Starting off research from women's lives will generate less partial and distorted accounts not only of women's lives but also of men's lives and of the whole social order" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 128).

Criado-Perez's reporting is, methodologically, a sequence of demonstrations of this exact claim. Take her examples one by one.

*Crash-test dummies and car safety.* Until 2011 in the US and still today in EU regulatory testing, the standard crash-test dummy is the 50th-percentile male, 1.77m tall, 76kg, with male muscle-mass proportions and a male spinal column. The "female" dummy in some EU tests is just a scaled-down male dummy. Result: a woman in a comparable car crash is 47% more likely to be seriously injured, 71% more likely to be moderately injured, and 17% more likely to die. Women sit further forward to reach pedals; they have less neck and upper-torso muscle, so are up to three times more vulnerable to whiplash. None of this is mysterious; all of it would have been visible to engineers who had started from women's bodies. The God-trick — "the car as such" — produced systematically defective engineering for half of the population.

*Stab vests and PPE.* A British female police officer was stabbed and killed in 1997 after removing her body armour to use a hydraulic ram, which she could not operate while wearing the vest. Another had breast-reduction surgery because of the health effects of the standard-issue vest. 700 officers in the same force came forward with similar complaints. A 2017 TUC report found that only 5% of women in emergency services said their PPE never hampered their work. Starting design from women's bodies — including breasts, hips, thighs — would have prevented these outcomes. Standpoint theory's claim is empirical here, not normative.

*Office temperature.* The 1960s formula for "ambient comfort" was based on the resting metabolic rate of the average man and overestimates female metabolism by up to 35%; offices are on average five degrees too cold for women. The Dutch study Criado-Perez cites is, methodologically, a standpoint study: starting measurement from female bodies produced a more accurate value for the underlying physiology. Harding's "less partial and distorted accounts not only of women's lives but also of men's lives and of the whole social order" is exactly the right description.

*Smartphones and voice recognition.* Average smartphone size (5.5 inches) is now at the upper limit of male hand size, leaving women — who buy more iPhones than men — physically incapable of safely operating their devices one-handed. The journalist Zeynep Tufekci could not photograph tear-gas use in Gezi Park in 2013 because her phone was too big. Google's voice recognition was 70% more likely to accurately recognise male speech. Volvo and Ford voice-command systems refused to recognise female users. Apple Health launched in 2014 without a period tracker. These are not isolated bugs; they are the predictable output of design teams starting from a male default.

*Medicine.* Reference Man — Caucasian, 25–30, 70kg — was, until recently, the basis of much pharmacological dosing. Heart-attack symptoms in women, which often differ from the "classic" male presentation, are systematically missed in A&E. Pregnant women's seatbelts: 62% of third-trimester pregnant women do not fit the standard belt design that 2004 research recommends them to use.

Now the assessment. Does this vindicate standpoint theory?

In the strong sense, yes. The Criado-Perez cases are not anecdotes; they are evidence that scientific and engineering communities that operate the God-trick produce knowledge that is empirically worse than knowledge produced by communities that start from marginalised standpoints. Harding's exact phrase — "we need not — indeed, must not — choose between 'good politics' and 'good science,' standpoint theorists argued, for the former can produce the latter" (Harding, p. 6) — is, in the crash-test dummy case, literally a description of preventable deaths.

But the vindication has to be qualified along the lines intersectional critique demands. The "reference man" in Criado-Perez is Caucasian as well as male; Black and minority-ethnic men also fail the standard dust-mask fit test, and Asian women fall under both axes. Standpoint theory must be plural (Hill Collins, matrix of domination) to do justice to the evidence. Standpoint privilege is also achieved through political struggle and feminist research, not given by anatomy: Astrid Linder's team is *building* the anthropometrically female crash-test dummy, not simply being one.

The conclusion is that Criado-Perez vindicates standpoint theory in its Harding/Collins form, not in any naive 1970s essentialist form. Starting from women's bodies produces better knowledge. Engineering and medicine should take note. (Word count: 749.)

### Mock 5: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Discuss.

Butler's sentence is one of the most condensed statements of post-structuralist feminism, and reading it carefully reveals exactly what is and is not radical about her project. The sentence does *not* say "abandon the category of women"; it says feminism must understand how that category is itself produced by power. I argue that Butler's move is necessary, sociologically productive, and compatible with — not a replacement for — the standpoint and intersectional traditions.

Begin with what Butler is reacting against. Liberal first-wave feminism took "woman" as a pre-given subject who had been wrongly excluded from rights. Second-wave feminism, including Hartsock's standpoint theory, took "woman" as the bearer of a distinctive structural location whose experiences grounded a critical epistemology. Both moves treat "woman" as if she existed before feminism showed up to defend her. Butler's point is that this is theoretically naive. The category "woman" is itself a product of the very gender system feminism wishes to dismantle. To take the category as given is to entrench the system one is trying to challenge.

The argument runs as follows. Gender, on Butler's reading, is performative: it is constituted through repeated acts that constitute it as if it were always already there. The system of intelligibility that says there are two sexes, mapped onto two genders, mapped onto a heterosexual desire — what Butler calls the heterosexual matrix — produces "woman" as one of two stable categories. Anything that does not fit (intersex bodies, trans subjectivities, non-binary identities, lesbians who refuse the man/woman dyad) is rendered unintelligible or pathological. Feminism that takes "woman" as its given subject reproduces this exclusion. Hence the famous opening move of *Gender Trouble*: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics. Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2).

This is the conceptual foundation of queer theory and of contemporary trans-feminist scholarship. It is also, importantly, *not* the same as saying "feminism cannot use the term woman". Butler herself uses it constantly. The point is to use it reflexively — to know that the category one is mobilising is also a category one's analysis must interrogate.

Why is this radical and necessary? Three reasons.

First, because exclusions matter politically. Pre-Butler feminism repeatedly failed lesbian, working-class, non-white and trans women by assuming a default "woman" who was straight, middle-class, white and cis. Hill Collins (1986: 70) and Crenshaw made the analogous point about race and class; Butler completes the move for gender identity and sexuality. Contemporary trans-rights debates within feminism — including the bitter UK split — turn precisely on whether to take "woman" as a self-evident category. Butler's intervention is the conceptual reason to refuse that move.

Second, because the heterosexual matrix is itself a *structure of power*, not a neutral fact. Beauvoir already saw this: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him" (de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*: 6). Butler radicalises Beauvoir: "woman" is not just defined relative to "man"; the very binary that produces both is an effect of power.

Third, because feminism needs internal critique. Butler's sentence ensures that feminism keeps interrogating its own foundations. Without that, feminism risks reproducing — within itself — the exclusionary moves that the broader patriarchal culture makes.

But Butler's move also faces serious objections, and I do not want to defend a triumphalist reading.

The first objection is political. If "woman" is unstable, what is the subject of feminist politics? Who marches? Who claims rights? Nancy Fraser and others have worried that post-structuralist feminism has emptied out the political subject. Butler's reply — that politics can proceed through provisional, strategic uses of categories — is intellectually satisfying but practically difficult in, say, a campaign for abortion rights or against femicide.

The second objection is epistemological. Standpoint theory needs *some* description of where the standpoint is located. Hartsock locates women in social reproduction. If "woman" is wholly destabilised, this locating move becomes harder. Harding's reply — that we can hold "woman" as a strategic, plural, historically located term while recognising its constructedness — is the synthesis I find persuasive.

The third objection is empirical. The Criado-Perez evidence shows that gender, as a category, has hard material effects: women die in car crashes at 17% higher rates because of male-default engineering. The category "woman" is necessary to name and address that fact, even if the category is itself produced by power. Butler does not deny this; her injunction is that we name it reflexively.

Adichie offers the practical wisdom: she defends the term "feminist" precisely because "human rights" elides the specific exclusion of women. Butler defends the term too, but insists that we keep asking who is being included and who excluded each time we use it.

In sum, Butler's claim is not a destruction of feminism but its self-disciplining. It says: keep using the category, and keep watching what your use of the category does. That is exactly the standard of intellectual honesty that contemporary feminist sociology should aspire to. (Word count: 748.)

### Mock 6: Is bell hooks correct that the home can be a site of resistance as well as oppression? Discuss with examples.

hooks is correct, and her correction of second-wave feminism is one of the most important interventions of late twentieth-century feminist thought. The home is, for many women, a site of both repression and resistance, and which it is depends crucially on race, class and historical location. I defend hooks's claim by setting it against second-wave feminism's account of the home, examining the racial history that hooks foregrounds, and considering contemporary examples that test the claim.

Second-wave feminism, as the lecture summarises it, treated the home as the central site of women's subordination: "Women's role becomes the inconspicuous work of making the work of the (male) wage-worker possible through the provision of sustenance (material and emotional) in the home"; "Domestic sphere becomes invisible. Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers (Hall, 1980, Walby 1986)." Betty Friedan called the suburban home the "comfortable concentration camp" of the educated American housewife. The personal-is-political slogan named housework, marital rape and childrearing as political because they were systematically privatised in this domestic space. This account is not wrong — but it generalises from a specific historical experience (white, middle-class, mid-century American) to all women.

hooks's intervention, drawing on the lived experience of African-American women, is to insist that for women under conditions of racial terror the home has been *also* a refuge and a base of resistance. The 1992 essay on the oppositional gaze gives us the central image: "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring, for those hard intense direct looks children would give grown-ups, looks that were seen as confrontational, as gestures of resistance, challenges to authority. The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). The home is where Black girls learn the oppositional gaze; it is where they learn to talk back, to refuse the imposed meanings of mass-cultural and institutional representation.

The historical case is strong. Black families under slavery and Jim Crow constructed home as the one place where Black personhood was protected from the gaze of white supremacy. Patricia Hill Collins makes the analogous point about Black motherhood as a form of resistance, not only as reproductive labour for capitalism. The slide on the lecture explicitly cites this revision: "Collins and bell hooks both questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation." For working-class and migrant women, similarly, the home is often the only space outside the disciplinary reach of bosses and immigration officers.

Concrete examples sharpen the argument.

The Civil Rights Movement was, in part, organised from kitchens. Septima Clark's citizenship schools — where Black adults learned to read in preparation for voter registration — often met in private homes. The Montgomery Bus Boycott was coordinated from Jo Ann Robinson's mimeograph machine and from Black women's living rooms.

Domestic-worker organising in the early twentieth century, including the National Domestic Workers Alliance more recently, has used the home — both employer's home and worker's home — as a site of political organising. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* offers a related case: her great-grandmother fled an arranged marriage. The home she refused was patriarchal; the home she built with the man of her choice was, for her, a site of self-determination.

Iranian women's resistance during the "women, life, freedom" movement after Mahsa Amini's killing has included, alongside public protests, intensive networks of mutual aid, hijab-burning gatherings and political education that operate through and across homes. In states that criminalise women's public political activity, the home becomes both refuge and conspiracy site.

The Roe-overturned US has seen abortion-pill networks, "menstrual extraction" workshops and rideshare networks operate through homes as sites of resistance to the state's reproductive control.

But hooks's claim must be qualified. The home is also still a site of intense gendered harm. Domestic violence statistics are stark: across most jurisdictions, the home is the most dangerous place for women, and femicide is disproportionately committed by intimate partners. The 2020 pandemic data showed that lockdowns increased intimate-partner violence sharply. The home as resistance for Black women under racial terror is not the same as the home as resistance under intra-household patriarchy. hooks does not deny this; she insists on holding both at once.

The conceptual upshot is that the home, like all sociological objects, is *relational*. What it is depends on what it stands in opposition to. For a Black family standing against white supremacy outside, home is a wall of protection. For an unpaid wife standing against a patriarchal husband inside, home is a cage. The same dwelling can be both, often simultaneously, often for different family members at the same time.

This is why hooks's intervention matters: it forces feminist sociology to drop the universalising "home = oppression" reading inherited from second-wave feminism and replace it with a more careful, intersectional account. Collins's matrix of domination provides exactly the tool: structural, disciplinary, hegemonic and interpersonal domains operate in the home as elsewhere, and women's positions within them vary with race, class and history.

So yes, hooks is correct: the home can be a site of resistance as well as oppression, and a feminist sociology that cannot recognise both is impoverished. (Word count: 749.)

### Mock 7: To what extent has feminism succeeded in moving beyond the liberal "equal rights" project of Wollstonecraft?

Feminism has moved a long way beyond Wollstonecraft in its theory but only partially beyond her in its practice. The conceptual horizon has expanded radically — through Beauvoir, Hartsock, Collins, Crenshaw, hooks and Butler — but the unfinished business of the liberal equal-rights project (vote, equal pay, bodily autonomy, freedom from violence) remains live in 2026. I argue, against a triumphalist reading of post-1970s feminism, that the equal-rights project is a precondition for, not a competitor of, more radical feminist work.

Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792) is an Enlightenment text. Its argument is that women are rational beings and therefore entitled to the same rights as men: education, political participation, equal labour. The slide summarises the agenda: "equal access for women to political life (right to vote; right to representation) and labour (equal work for equal pay)". The intellectual horizon is Enlightenment universalism: the claim that women have been wrongly excluded from a universality of rights that was always supposed to include them.

This project achieved enormous things — universal suffrage, anti-discrimination law, equal-pay legislation, access to higher education and the professions, divorce rights, contraceptive access (in many countries) — but it left in place the very norm against which equality was measured. The lecture's pointed question — "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club? Or should it aim to change the rules? Or to start another club?" — names exactly the limit of Wollstonecraft. Joining the male club leaves the club's architecture intact.

Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) is the first major move beyond Wollstonecraft. Beauvoir's anti-essentialism — "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" — and her diagnosis of woman-as-other reframe the problem. The issue is not just that women have been excluded from rights; it is that the very category "woman" is constituted as the inessential against the male absolute (*The Second Sex*: 6). Equal rights within a system that produces woman-as-other is not enough.

Second-wave feminism extends this. "The personal is political" reveals that the public/private split — itself a historical product of industrial capitalism (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986) — privatises a huge domain of gendered harm (housework, marital rape, reproductive labour) and removes it from political contest. Hartsock's standpoint theory translates the move into epistemology: women's structural location generates critical knowledge. Sandra Harding's strong objectivity formalises it: "We need not — indeed, must not — choose between 'good politics' and 'good science'" (Harding, p. 6).

Black feminism and intersectionality go further again. Hill Collins's matrix of domination and Crenshaw's intersectionality show that the equal-rights project always implicitly assumed a single woman (white, middle-class, straight), and Black, working-class and lesbian women's lives could not be addressed by a generic equal-rights claim. hooks rewrites the home as a possible site of resistance.

Butler closes the loop: the very category "woman" is "produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Equal rights for *whom* is now a serious question.

In theory, then, feminism is far beyond Wollstonecraft. But in practice the liberal agenda remains unfinished, and recent years have shown that even won rights can be lost.

Consider the evidence. The gender pay gap in 2026 persists across the OECD; in the UK it sits in the high single digits, and is far worse for women of colour and disabled women. Women's political representation has improved but remains under 30% in most parliaments globally. Reproductive rights have actively regressed in the US since Dobbs (2022) and in several other jurisdictions. Sex-work decriminalisation debates show no consensus. Menstrual-leave policies are patchy and controversial. Caroline Criado-Perez documents the gender data gap in 2019 and the situation has improved only marginally since: women in 2026 are still 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable car crash because EU crash-test regulations still do not require anthropometrically female dummies. #MeToo exposed systemic workplace harassment that anti-discrimination law had failed to prevent. Iran's women-life-freedom movement is, at its base, an equal-rights movement under conditions of theocratic repression. Afghan women have been forced back out of public life entirely.

The conclusion is that the equal-rights project is necessary but not sufficient. Feminism's theoretical sophistication beyond Wollstonecraft has not made the original equal-rights agenda obsolete; it has shown that agenda's preconditions and its limits. A defensible contemporary feminism does both: it pursues equal rights (the Wollstonecraft agenda) *and* it interrogates the system within which rights are framed (the Beauvoir/Hartsock/Collins/Crenshaw/hooks/Butler agenda).

Adichie's defence of the word "feminist" — that dropping it for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded" — is in this sense a partial defence of Wollstonecraft. We are not done with rights. We are also not done with the deeper structural and conceptual work.

So to the extent: feminism has moved theoretically far beyond Wollstonecraft, but politically the Wollstonecraft agenda is still unfinished, and contemporary feminism is best understood as both completing and exceeding it. (Word count: 750.)

### Mock 8: Compare Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* with Butler's *Gender Trouble* as accounts of what a feminist subject is.

Adichie and Butler offer almost diametrically different accounts of the feminist subject — Adichie's is concrete, autobiographical, definitionally clear; Butler's is theoretical, deconstructive, definitionally suspicious. Both, I argue, are right about different things, and a defensible feminism today must read them together rather than choosing.

Adichie's feminist subject is the woman or man who can say, plainly, "I am a feminist." She offers the dictionary definition unembarrassed: "Feminist: a person who believes in the social, political, and economic equality of the sexes." Her own self-description is performative: "Happy African Feminist Who Does Not Hate Men and Who Likes to Wear Lip Gloss and High Heels for Herself and Not For Men." The subject is concrete: she is a fourteen-year-old Igbo girl arguing with her friend Okoloma, she is a young woman in Lagos being thanked through her male friend Louis by a parking attendant, she is a writer being told her novel is too "angry". The subject is also explicitly inclusive: "My own definition is a feminist is a man or a woman who says, yes, there's a problem with gender as it is today and we must fix it." Adichie's subject is constituted by recognition — by being able to call oneself, and be called, a feminist — and by an ethical commitment to doing better.

Butler's feminist subject is, by contrast, deeply problematised from the start. The famous opening of *Gender Trouble* warns: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics. Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). For Butler, taking "women" as a stable category is not just analytically careless but politically dangerous: every "we" implicates exclusions (of trans women, of intersex subjects, of those who refuse the man/woman binary). The feminist subject must be held under interrogation; it cannot be a given.

The two accounts diverge on at least four dimensions.

*Ontology.* Adichie's subject is a person — a Nigerian woman with a brother and a great-grandmother. Butler's subject is a position in discourse — produced through performative repetitions of the gender norm.

*Method.* Adichie's method is testimony — concrete stories, examples, autobiography. Butler's method is genealogical critique — tracing how categories are produced through power.

*Politics.* Adichie's politics is straightforwardly identifiable: equal pay, freedom from harassment, raising sons and daughters differently. Butler's politics is more elusive — destabilisation, parodic performance, refusal of the heterosexual matrix.

*Audience.* Adichie speaks to a TED audience and to a generation of young Nigerian women and men. Butler speaks to academic philosophy and queer-theoretical scholarship. The accessibility gap is itself a feminist issue.

But the apparent opposition is partly stage-managed. Read carefully, the two thinkers converge more than they diverge.

Adichie is not naive about the category "woman". Her TEDx talk *opens* with the multiplicity of "happy feminist", "African feminist", "feminist who does not hate men", which is structurally a Butlerian point: there is no single, stable feminist subject. Her insistence that "we must raise our daughters differently. We must also raise our sons differently" treats gender as performatively produced through socialisation — which is the same insight Butler theorises philosophically. Adichie's anecdote of the class monitor — "If we do something over and over, it becomes normal" — is a folk statement of performativity.

Butler, conversely, is not arguing for the dissolution of feminism. The point of destabilising "woman" is to make feminism more capacious, not to abandon it. Butler herself continues to write feminist theory throughout her career.

What each does for the other is instructive. Adichie gives Butler what Butler's theoretical apparatus alone cannot provide: a concrete politics, a recognisable subject, a movement someone can join. Without Adichie's voice, *Gender Trouble* risks intellectualising feminism out of mass legibility. Butler gives Adichie what Adichie alone cannot provide: the conceptual vigilance to keep asking who is included and excluded in any feminist "we". Without Butler's vigilance, Adichie's "we should all be feminists" risks reproducing the universalising moves that Black, trans and post-structuralist feminism have spent decades dismantling.

A concrete test case is the UK trans-rights debate. Adichie's stated position, framed in dictionary terms, has been criticised by trans feminists for not adequately defending trans women's standing as women. Butler's apparatus is what one needs to formulate that critique — the category "woman" must be held open. But Butler's apparatus alone cannot do the movement work; for that, the recognition Adichie offers is necessary.

So the comparison yields a partnership rather than a choice. Adichie supplies the *political subject* — the concrete person who can join, organise and self-identify. Butler supplies the *theoretical caution* — the discipline of keeping the category under interrogation. A feminism that has one but not the other is impoverished. A feminism that has both produces movements like Iran's women-life-freedom, which name a political "we" while remaining alive to internal difference.

The exam question asks what a feminist subject is. The honest answer is: it is a project, not a thing — one that Adichie and Butler help us, together, to undertake. (Word count: 749.)

### Mock 9: Discuss, with examples, the claim that the public/private split is a historical product of capitalist modernity rather than a natural feature of human societies.

The claim is empirically and theoretically robust: the modern public/private split as we know it is a product of industrial capitalism, not a universal feature of human social organisation. I defend this with historical evidence, sociological theory, and contemporary examples that show what is at stake in getting the story right.

The lecture is unusually explicit on this point. "Public-private split was established with the rise of capitalism. New relations of production, new employment practices, and the establishment of factories, led to a new mode and sphere of social existence. The production of goods shifted from the small-scale domestic level to the large-scale, anonymous level. 'Work' takes place away from the living-place of those involved. You go **out** to work. In factories. All this helped establish a new conception of what constituted the public realm." And: "The dwelling-place becomes, in distinction to the world of public work, the private world and hence the modern notion of domesticity is created."

The historical evidence is dense. In pre-industrial European economies — peasant households, artisan workshops, merchant households — production and reproduction were largely fused. The household was an economic unit; women's labour (spinning, brewing, dairying, marketing) was visible, productive, and economically central. Industrialisation breaks this unit: the factory removes wage-labour from the home, the home is reinvented as a feminised domestic space, and women's now-unwaged labour in the home becomes invisible as "not work". Stuart Hall (1980) and Sylvia Walby (1986) analyse the corresponding ideological move: "Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers". Friedrich Engels had already gestured at this in *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884). Marxist feminists in the 1970s — Maria Mies, Silvia Federici, Selma James — extended it: housework is unwaged labour necessary to capitalist reproduction.

The theoretical importance is large. If the public/private split is *natural*, then patriarchy is a fact of biology and feminism is at best a redistributive project. If the split is *historical*, then it is contingent, contestable and dismantlable. The second-wave slogan "the personal is political" is a direct response: it claims back as political what capitalist modernity had privatised. Hartsock's standpoint theory builds on this: women's location in the privatised sphere of reproduction is structural, not natural, and generates critical insight precisely because it is structural. Nancy Fraser later writes of the "transnationalisation of the public sphere" and the ways neoliberalism reorganises the split again.

Contemporary examples make the contingency vivid.

*Care work and the pandemic.* When COVID-19 emptied workplaces in 2020, the privatised character of care work was suddenly exposed. Women — disproportionately women of colour and migrants — held the social fabric together through unpaid domestic labour and underpaid frontline care. The crisis forced governments to acknowledge in language they had long avoided that "essential work" was the work that capitalism had privatised. The public/private split was visibly a *policy choice*, not a fact.

*Reproductive politics.* The Dobbs decision (2022) reasserted a particular construction of the private (the home, the family) by removing reproductive decisions from federal protection. The same body, the same decision, was differently positioned by the state at different historical moments. The split is contestable in real time.

*Domestic violence and #MeToo.* For most of the twentieth century, intimate-partner violence was treated by police forces and legal systems as a "private matter". Feminist activism succeeded in moving it into the public-political domain. #MeToo extended the same move to workplace sexual harassment, which had been privatised through non-disclosure agreements and "HR" procedures. Each victory is, structurally, a renegotiation of the public/private boundary.

*Care robots and AI domestic technologies.* Contemporary tech firms are commodifying parts of the private sphere — childcare apps, eldercare robots, smart-home assistants — moving care work back into commercial circulation. This is not a return to pre-industrial fusion of production and reproduction; it is a further reconfiguration of the public/private boundary under platform capitalism.

*Cross-cultural variation.* The "private" sphere of the European nuclear-family household is not the universal form. Many African, South Asian and Indigenous societies organise the relation between household and economy very differently — extended kin networks, communal labour, multi-generational households. Adichie's reference to Igbo extended-family meetings, where she cannot have a formal say "because I am female", makes the point: Igbo culture has its own gendered public/private structures, distinct from but not less patriarchal than European ones. The lesson is that public/private splits are *plural* historical products, not a single one.

The historical-constructivist account does not, however, mean that the split is easy to dismantle. Once the split has been institutionalised in law, planning, labour markets and architecture, it is materially entrenched. The TUC reports on PPE that Criado-Perez cites show that even when women enter the (formerly male) public sphere, the equipment, the office temperature and the safety standards still assume a male public worker and a female private supporter. Joining the (male) club, in the lecture's phrase, is not the same as dismantling the club.

So to the claim: the public/private split is indeed a historical product, not a natural feature, and recognising this is a precondition of feminist political work. Without that recognition, patriarchy looks like nature; with it, patriarchy looks like a contestable arrangement that can be remade. The personal is political because the political — capitalist modernity — invented "the personal" as a place to hide women's labour. (Word count: 750.)

### Mock 10: To what extent does Sandra Harding's notion of "strong objectivity" answer the charge that feminist knowledge is "merely political"?

Harding's strong objectivity is, in my view, the single most important defence of the scientific status of feminist knowledge produced in the late twentieth century. It does answer the "merely political" charge — but it answers it by reframing the question rather than by conceding the terms of the original objection. I will reconstruct Harding's argument, assess it, and argue that the Criado-Perez evidence on the gender data gap empirically vindicates it.

The "merely political" charge runs roughly as follows. Feminist knowledge starts from women's lives and is explicitly politically engaged; therefore it cannot be objective; therefore it falls below the standards of "real" science. Harding's reply, developed in chapter 8 of *The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, is that the very conception of objectivity behind this charge is too weak. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying,' as some have argued, but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*; it is too weak to accomplish even the goals for which it has been designed" (Harding, ch. 8, p. 128).

The argument has several moves.

*The God-trick.* Standard objectivism imagines a knower who speaks "from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, p. 4). Harding, following Haraway, calls this the God-trick. The God-trick is not a higher form of objectivity; it is a delusion. Every knower is socially situated; the only question is whether that situatedness is examined or hidden.

*Cultural background assumptions.* "Conventional conceptions of scientific method enable scientists to be relatively good at eliminating those social interests and values from the results of research that differ within the scientific community… But scientific method provides no rules, procedures, or techniques for even identifying, let alone eliminating, social concerns and interests that are shared by all (or virtually all) of the observers" (Harding, p. 128). Culture-wide assumptions slip through unchallenged. This is the structural reason why women, women of colour and other marginalised groups need to be in the scientific community: not as a quota, but as a methodological resource that catches assumptions a homogeneous community could not catch.

*Strong objectivity = strong reflexivity.* "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, p. 136). The knower is examined as part of the inquiry. This is rigorous, not lax.

*Starting from marginalised lives.* "Starting off research from women's lives will generate less partial and distorted accounts not only of women's lives but also of men's lives and of the whole social order" (Harding, p. 128). This is the empirical bet: politically engaged starting points produce *more* accurate knowledge, not less.

How well does this answer the "merely political" charge?

In one direction, decisively well. The Criado-Perez evidence is hard to read in any other way. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male produced systematically defective car safety; women died at 17% higher rates because of it. Stab vests sized for male torsos got female police officers killed. Office thermostats calibrated to male metabolism made women unproductive (and uncomfortable) for half a century. Voice recognition trained on male speech failed female users. Apple Health launched without a period tracker. In every case, *starting from women's lives* would have produced *more accurate engineering, better medicine, safer policing, better consumer technology*. The "merely political" framing gets the direction of the relationship backwards: politically engaged knowledge is not less rigorous, it is *more* rigorous, because it forces an interrogation of assumptions that a homogeneous community shares.

Harding's argument also dissolves the objection's implicit standard. Standard objectivism imagines that there is a politically neutral position from which "real" science is done. Harding shows there is not: the standard is itself a politically loaded position (that of the dominant group, whose interests are invisible because they are universal). "Commitment to an objectivity defined as maximising social neutrality was not itself socially neutral in its effects" (Harding, p. 5).

But Harding's argument is not without difficulties.

The first is the relativism worry. If all knowledge is socially situated, are we not committed to a relativism in which every standpoint is as good as every other? Harding rejects this: standpoint theory "argues against the idea that all social situations provide equally useful resources for learning about the world" (Harding, ch. 8). Some standpoints are better than others for asking certain questions. The Criado-Perez case shows this: starting from women's bodies generated better engineering; starting from "humans in general" (read: men) did not. This is not relativism; it is the claim that some politically engaged starting points produce empirically better results.

The second difficulty is the question of who counts. Standpoint theory must be plural (Collins's matrix of domination, Crenshaw's intersectionality), and Harding explicitly says so: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, p. 131).

The third is the political reception. "Strong objectivity" still sounds strange to mainstream philosophy of science, and Harding's rhetorical battles continue.

The overall assessment: strong objectivity does answer the "merely political" charge — by showing that the original framing of politics-vs-objectivity is itself a politically loaded standpoint, and by demonstrating empirically that engaged knowledge can be more rigorous than disengaged knowledge. The Criado-Perez evidence is, two decades after Harding's chapter, the strongest possible vindication. "We need not — indeed, must not — choose between 'good politics' and 'good science,' standpoint theorists argued, for the former can produce the latter" (Harding, p. 6) is, in 2026, simply true. ### Mock 11: Is the second-wave slogan "the personal is political" still useful in the era of #MeToo and post-Roe America?

The slogan is more useful than ever, and the period since 2017 has been one continuous demonstration of its analytic and political force. #MeToo and the Dobbs reversal are textbook cases of the second-wave insight that the privatised domains of bodily life, intimate relationships, and reproductive control are politically constituted and politically contestable. I argue that the slogan needs to be read intersectionally, in the way Collins, Crenshaw and hooks revised it, but that this strengthens rather than weakens its usefulness.

Recall the slogan's origin. "The personal is political" emerged from late-1960s and early-1970s consciousness-raising groups, where second-wave feminists identified that experiences they had each taken to be uniquely private — housework, marital sex, control over reproduction, body image, harassment — were systemically patterned, and therefore political. The lecture's account of the public/private split makes this clear: industrial capitalism produced "the personal" as a depoliticised, feminised domestic sphere, and second-wave feminism's task was to repoliticise it. Nancy Hartsock's standpoint theory rendered this epistemologically: women's structural location in social reproduction generates critical knowledge precisely because that location is politically produced.

Now apply the slogan to #MeToo and to post-Roe America.

*#MeToo.* The 2017 wave that toppled Harvey Weinstein and then a cascade of media, entertainment, political and academic figures was, sociologically, a "the personal is political" moment. What women had each treated as their private experience of harassment — the unsolicited touch, the meeting in the hotel suite, the bullying call after work hours — was revealed, through massive collective naming, to be systemic. Tarana Burke had been doing this work with young Black women since 2006, but the 2017 wave reached unprecedented scale. The slogan's logic is exactly what made #MeToo possible: my experience is not (only) mine; it is a pattern; the pattern is political; therefore my experience is political.

Intersectional revisions of the slogan matter here. The 2017 wave initially named mostly white celebrity perpetrators and victims; Tarana Burke and others rightly pointed out that the same pattern, in low-wage service jobs (hotels, agricultural labour, domestic work, sex work), had been operating with greater violence and less visibility for decades. The slogan needs to be read through Collins's matrix of domination: the same structural-disciplinary-hegemonic-interpersonal architecture operates on different bodies, and the architecture is political across all of them.

*Post-Roe America.* The Dobbs decision (2022) reversed nearly fifty years of federal abortion protection. The "personal" — what happens in a pregnant person's body — was repoliticised by the state, this time in a regressive direction. The point is not that abortion was previously "private" and is now "political"; abortion has always been political. The point is that the personal/political boundary has been forcibly redrawn, and that this redrawing falls disproportionately on Black, Latina, low-income and rural women. The lecture's slide on the public/private split is the exact framework: "the dwelling-place becomes, in distinction to the world of public work, the private world… This has implications for the experiences of women."

The pandemic, similarly, was a "the personal is political" moment. Care work — childcare, eldercare, the labour of holding families together — was suddenly visible as the work that sustains the economy. Women, disproportionately women of colour and migrants, were the workers. The same fact had been politically true since the public/private split; the pandemic revealed it.

The slogan's usefulness can be tested against three objections.

The first objection: the slogan is too broad; if everything is political, nothing is. This is a misreading. The slogan does not claim that all aspects of life are political in the same way; it claims that the *boundary* between "personal" and "political" is itself politically produced. Recognising this opens up specific questions: who benefits from this boundary being drawn here? Whose labour is privatised? Whose violence is privatised?

The second objection: the slogan flattens difference. This was the legitimate concern that Black feminism, intersectionality and hooks pressed against second-wave feminism. The reply is that the slogan needs to be read plurally: *which* personal is *whose* political, and how do those interact? Hill Collins's matrix tells us how. hooks's rewriting of the home — "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115) — uses the slogan but pluralises it.

The third objection: the slogan is dated. Against this, I would set the Criado-Perez evidence. The gender data gap is a continuous reminder that what looks like a personal inconvenience (a phone too big for my hand, an office too cold for my metabolism, a car seat too far from the pedals) is in fact a political arrangement of who counts as the human default. The slogan is precisely the conceptual move that reveals this.

The slogan's usefulness in 2026 is high and increasing. Adichie's TEDx talk uses it implicitly throughout: her account of being thanked through her male friend Louis, of being assumed a sex worker in a hotel lobby, of being told her novel was too "angry" — each story converts a personal moment into a political diagnosis. The continuing relevance of the slogan is, in fact, an indictment of how little the underlying conditions have changed.

So yes — the personal is political, still, and now perhaps more visibly than at any time since the 1970s. The slogan, properly read through intersectional revisions, remains one of feminism's most powerful tools. (Word count: 750.)

### Mock 12: Compare Lean In corporate feminism with Black feminist thought. What does the comparison tell us about feminism's class politics?

The comparison is unflattering to Lean In and useful to Black feminism. Sheryl Sandberg's *Lean In* (2013) and the corporate "girlboss" feminism it inspired articulates a feminism for women already inside, or near, the boardroom; Black feminist thought, from Hill Collins to hooks to Crenshaw, articulates a feminism that begins from the women whom the boardroom relies on but does not see. The comparison shows that feminism cannot avoid class politics, and that pretending to do so reproduces class-bound feminism in disguise.

Lean In's central argument is that women hold themselves back in their careers and need to assert themselves more confidently — negotiate harder, take more risks, occupy more space at the table. The book is sociologically not without merit: it documents real workplace dynamics around performance evaluation, salary negotiation, and the "double bind" in which assertive women are penalised. Sandberg's own anecdotes, like Adichie's, capture genuine moments of gendered diminishment. But the analytic frame is narrow. The book addresses women who already have, or could plausibly have, white-collar professional jobs; it treats the workplace as the central site of women's oppression; it locates the problem largely in women's psychology (confidence, ambition, internalised self-doubt). It says little about the women who clean the offices in which the leaning-in is to happen, who care for the children of the women who lean in, who staff the supply chains that make the leaning-in possible.

Black feminist thought begins from a different starting point. "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The starting point is Black women, who in the US have historically been concentrated in domestic service, agricultural labour, low-wage care work and (more recently) the public sector. The matrix of domination shows that race, gender and class are not three additive axes but a single mutually constitutive structure. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalises the legal version of this insight. hooks's work — particularly *Where We Stand: Class Matters* (2000), *Teaching to Transgress* (1994) and the oppositional gaze essay — is unusually explicit on class. hooks argues that mainstream feminism in the US has been class-bound from the start, that "the issues of women in poverty" have been marginalised, and that "the experience of black women… is, by the very nature of their reality, an experience that bring class to the forefront".

The comparison yields a sharp diagnosis. Lean In is feminism designed for the few women who could lean in if obstacles were removed. Black feminism is feminism designed for the many women whose obstacles are the system itself.

Concrete examples make this vivid.

*The care chain.* A senior Lean In executive in New York or London often relies on a domestic worker or nanny — disproportionately a woman of colour, often a migrant — to make her career possible. The Lean In framework reads this as the executive leaning in; Black feminism reads it as the displacement of care work down the racial and class hierarchy. Both readings are true; only one notices the second woman.

*Pay gaps within women.* The gender pay gap is well documented; less well noted is that the gap between Black women and white men in the US is far larger than the gap between white women and white men. Lean In's "women" elides this. Crenshaw's intersectionality and Collins's matrix name it.

*The pandemic and "essential work".* The pandemic exposed how much of the work holding society together — care, cleaning, retail, food production — was done by women, disproportionately women of colour, in jobs that no amount of leaning in would have made safe. The Lean In frame had nothing to say about this; Black feminism had been describing it for half a century.

*#MeToo's class limits.* The 2017 wave largely toppled celebrity perpetrators in white-collar industries. Tarana Burke's earlier work, and subsequent organising by farmworker unions (e.g. Alianza Nacional de Campesinas), showed that the same harassment was rampant in low-wage agricultural and service work, but received little media attention.

*Adichie's Igbo standpoint.* Adichie's TEDx talk is a useful intermediate case. She uses the language of feminism in a way that is closer to Lean In than to Black feminism (the dictionary definition, individual self-identification), but her examples — Lagos clubs, hotels, parking attendants, the Igbo extended family meetings she cannot attend — are intersectional. Adichie shows that you can hold a recognisable "feminism" subject while attending to local and class specificity.

What does the comparison tell us about feminism's class politics?

First, that feminism cannot opt out of class politics. Refusing to engage class produces class-bound feminism by default; Lean In is the case study.

Second, that the standpoint from which feminism starts is not optional. If feminism starts from corporate professional women, it produces Lean In; if it starts from Black domestic workers, it produces Hill Collins. Sandra Harding's directive — "start thought from marginalised lives" — has direct implications here.

Third, that the dichotomy is fixable. Adichie shows it is possible to mobilise a clear, recognisable feminism (with the dictionary definition) without erasing class and racial specificity, provided one explicitly attends to it.

The exam question implies a comparison; the comparison should produce a recommendation. The recommendation is that feminist sociology, in 2026, should treat Black feminist thought, not Lean In, as the more analytically rigorous starting point — because Black feminism captures the structural facts about gender, race and class that Lean In's frame elides. Lean In can be a useful tactical tool within particular workplaces; it cannot be the theory. (Word count: 750.)
