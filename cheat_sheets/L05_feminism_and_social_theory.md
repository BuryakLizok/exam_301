## 3. Key notes from the presentation (with slide-question callouts)

**Slide 1–2: Framing the lecture.** Feminism aims to challenge what is taken as appropriate behaviour/roles for women; "the more people that feminism can 'talk for', the stronger its arguments", but the lecturer immediately problematises this. The main question for the exam: *can or should feminism attempt to talk for all women?*

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "whether feminism can or should attempt to talk for all women, or not… Or maybe you disagree?" This is the live question I am being asked to take a position on. I will refuse a simple yes/no and argue that feminism must do both: hold open a strategic collective subject while practising internal critique.

**Slide 3: First-wave feminism.** Post-French Revolution (1789) liberal/equal-rights feminism; the Enlightenment background; reacts against the exclusion of women from the universality of rights; focuses on the vote and equal pay; key text Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792).

**Slide 4: But why should women want to be like men?**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club? Or should it aim to change the rules? Or to start another club?" My answer: the rules themselves must change, because joining the club leaves the patriarchal architecture intact (Beauvoir's point about woman-as-other, hooks' point about the master's house).

**Slides 5–7: Beauvoir and the move toward second-wave feminism.** Writing in the 1940s and 1950s when the "aim" of life for many women was to "marry well"; *The Second Sex* (1949) asks "what is a woman?" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 13). She insists there is no essential thing that defines women; rather, woman is a status produced through experience.

**Slide 6: A word on "gender".** Beauvoir uses *genre*; the Latin *genus* indicates type/kind; gender as an arbitrary category for organising the world, important for later post-structuralist moves (Butler).

**Slide 8: Three key points.** No essence of woman; "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman"; woman has the EXPERIENCE of being the "other" of men.

**Slide 9: "Other" and bifurcations.** "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6).

**Slide 10: Women as objects, not subjects.** "The representation of the world, like the world itself, is the work of men; they describe it from the point of view which is theirs and which they confuse with the absolute truth" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 236). Note the Disney-princess image, a contemporary mass-cultural illustration of male-authored representation.

**Slide 11: Critique of Beauvoir.**

> 💡 *Slide-question:* "Does de Beauvoir try to 'universalize' her argument? Is her account historically and socially limited (she is a middle class, white, European intellectual)? Can she really describe the way ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" My answer: no. This is exactly the opening that Black feminism, postcolonial feminism, and post-structuralism rush into.

**Slides 12–15: Second-wave and the personal is political.** Patriarchy, reproduction as labour, exploitation of women's work. The private/public split is historically produced with industrial capitalism: production leaves the home, "you go **out** to work, in factories", domesticity is invented as private and feminised. Women become inconspicuous reproducers of the wage-worker and of future wage-labourers; "Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers" (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). The domestic sphere becomes invisible.

**Slide 16: Three lessons.** Different standpoints/perspectives matter; experiences reveal the operations of power; experience has a duality.

**Slide 17: Arguing for difference.** Feminism before the 1980s is criticised on three counts: it universalises (a shared history of oppression is presumed), it essentialises (being a woman is enough), and it is ahistorical (e.g. it ignores how white women have benefited from colonialism).

**Slides 19–20: Black feminist thought, matrix of domination, intersectionality.** "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Matrix of domination: "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Collins). Gender does not exist on its own; "we can't think gender without thinking 'race'". Crenshaw's intersectionality: different types of discrimination intersect and compound experiences of exclusion.

**Slides 21–22: bell hooks and the home as resistance.** Collins and hooks question the second-wave reading of home as pure repression; for Black women under racial terror the home is also refuge and resistance. The oppositional gaze: "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring… The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115).

**Slides 23–25: Hartsock and feminist materialism.** Women are both insiders (essential to the system's continuation) and outsiders (no power within it); this dual position is epistemologically privileged. "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142).

**Slides 26–27: Post-structuralist feminism and Butler.** Identities are not fixed; "woman" is not a stable category; sex and gender are signifiers, not labels of given objects. Butler: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics… Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). This opens the door to queer theory and to questioning the unified subject.

**Slide 28: Conclusion.** Feminism introduces new concepts (sex/gender, essentialism, the politics of birth as opposed to a male preoccupation with death); it shows how the world could be otherwise; and it leaves us with the standing question: can/should feminism speak for all women?

## 4. Information from the readings

### Harding (ed.), *The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, Intro pp. 4–7 and ch. 8 ("Strong Objectivity")

Harding sets up standpoint theory as a response to a need: "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women" (Harding, 2004: 4). The "implied 'speaker' of scientific… sentences was never women. It was supposed to be humanity in general"; what Haraway calls "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4).

Harding's diagnosis is structural, not about bad individuals: "The problem was not prejudiced and biased individuals… Rather, it was a different kind of obstacle… The conceptual frameworks themselves promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns" (Harding, 2004: 4–5). The disciplines were "complicitous with sexist and androcentric agendas" (Harding, 2004: 5).

Politics is therefore not a contaminant of knowledge but a resource: "politics and culture often function as 'prisonhouses' of knowledge… Yet they can and often do also function as 'toolboxes'" (Harding, 2004: 5). The remedy is to "start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities" (Harding, 2004: 6). But this immediately raises the controversial issue Harding identifies in the intro: "How does and should standpoint theory account for and engage with differences between women?" (Harding, 2004: 7), the same question my exam asks.

In ch. 8, Harding develops **strong objectivity**. The conventional notion is "not rigorous or objectifying enough" (Harding, 2004: 128). Standpoint theory does not commit the **God-trick**, is not ethnocentric (because standpoint theorists "all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129)), and is not relativist ("It is not equally true… that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history" (Harding, 2004: 131–132)). The **subject of knowledge** for standpoint theory is "embodied and visible… multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134). Strong objectivity demands "strong reflexivity": "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Crucially, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135); feminism is not the property of women.

### Adichie, *We Should All Be Feminists* (Vintage Short, 2014; based on TEDxEuston 2012)

Adichie's argument is short, autobiographical, and forceful. She defines feminism using the dictionary: "a person who believes in the social, political, and economic equality of the sexes". She tells the story of being called a feminist at fourteen by her friend Okoloma, "the same tone with which a person would say, 'You're a supporter of terrorism.'" She enumerates the negative baggage: "You hate men, you hate bras, you hate African culture… you don't shave, you're always angry, you don't have a sense of humor".

Her childhood Igbo example (the class monitor): "Then, to my surprise, my teacher said the monitor had to be a boy… If we do something over and over, it becomes normal… If we keep seeing only men as heads of corporations, it starts to seem 'natural' that only men should be heads of corporations." Her Lagos examples (being refused entry to clubs alone, being thanked through her male friend Louis for money she had given a parking attendant, being assumed to be a sex worker in hotel lobbies) work as concrete intersectional evidence of how gender shapes everyday life across class.

Adichie's central reframings:
- **Anger is legitimate.** "Gender as it functions today is a grave injustice. I am angry. We should all be angry."
- **Masculinity damages men.** "Masculinity is a hard, small cage, and we put boys inside this cage… The harder a man feels compelled to be, the weaker his ego is."
- **Femininity should not have to apologise.** "I have chosen to no longer be apologetic for my femininity."
- **Culture is not destiny.** "Culture does not make people. People make culture. If it is true that the full humanity of women is not our culture, then we can and must make it our culture."
- **The case for the *word* "feminist".** "to choose to use the vague expression *human rights* is to deny the specific and particular problem of gender. It would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Adichie also pushes back on the "un-African" charge: feminism is not a Western import; her great-grandmother, who ran away from a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." This is important for the universalising critique: a properly intersectional feminism can find local feminist genealogies without rebranding them as Western.

### Criado-Perez, "The deadly truth about a world built for men" (*Guardian*, 23 Feb 2019; extract from *Invisible Women*)

Criado-Perez documents the **gender data gap**, a "female-shaped 'absent presence'" in design, science, and public policy. Key empirical anchors:
- **The reference man.** Toxicology and occupational safety still rely on a Caucasian male, 25–30, 70kg. "Reference Man… his superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not."
- **Crash-test dummies.** Until 2011 US tests used only a 50th-percentile male; even now, EU regulation does not require an anthropometrically female dummy; the "female" dummy used in some tests is "just a scaled-down male dummy". Result: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured… 17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash.
- **PPE.** Body armour and stab vests sized for male bodies. A British female police officer was stabbed and killed in 1997 after removing her armour to use a hydraulic ram; another had breast-reduction surgery because of the vest. 95% of female emergency-service workers report PPE hampers their work.
- **Office temperature.** A 1960s formula based on the resting metabolic rate of the average male overestimates female metabolism by up to 35%; offices are on average five degrees too cold for women.
- **Phones, voice recognition, Apple Health.** Smartphones at the upper limit of male hand size; Google speech recognition 70% more likely to recognise male speech accurately; Apple Health launched without a period tracker.
- **Toilets.** "50/50 division of floor space has even been formalised in plumbing codes"; but women need more time and trips, so formal equality is substantive inequality.

This article is gold for the standpoint argument: starting from women's bodies and women's daily life generates *more accurate* science (Harding's strong objectivity), and starting from men's bodies as the default is not neutral. It is, in Harding's terms, the God-trick in engineering.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The arc of feminist theory in this lecture is a movement from rights, to experience, to standpoint, to difference, to deconstruction; and the question I am asked is whether this trajectory empties feminism of any usable subject. I argue it does not, provided we read each move as a correction rather than a replacement.

**Wollstonecraft's liberal feminism is necessary but insufficient.** It treats women as already-formed rational subjects who have been wrongly excluded; this leaves intact the very norm against which they are measured. The slide's deliberately provocative question, "Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club?", names the limit of equal-rights feminism. Beauvoir's intervention is precisely to refuse the male norm: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Beauvoir's anti-essentialism, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman", is the conceptual hinge of the whole module. It makes gender a social process, not a biological destiny, and it grounds the second-wave slogan that the personal is political.

**Patriarchy and the public/private split are not eternal facts but historical products.** Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) historicise the very domesticity that earlier liberal feminism took for granted: with industrial capitalism, "you go **out** to work, in factories", and the home is reinvented as a feminised private. Hartsock's contribution is to translate this materialist insight into an epistemology. Women's dual position (insiders to social reproduction, outsiders to power) generates a privileged vantage point: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The Criado-Perez piece is, in effect, a 21st-century vindication of this claim: starting from women's bodies in nail salons, in cars, in stab vests, in offices, produces better engineering, empirically less partial, more accurate, "strong objectivity" in Harding's sense.

**But standpoint theory is not safe from its own universalising tendencies.** Hill Collins, Crenshaw, and hooks all insist that "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70); the matrix of domination shows that "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression" (Hill Collins, 1990). Crenshaw's intersectionality is the conceptual tool here; hooks' oppositional gaze, "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), names the active, resistant subject that flat universalism misses. Importantly, Collins and hooks also rewrite the second-wave reading of "home": for Black women living under racial terror, home is not only a site of patriarchal repression but also a refuge and an organising base.

**Butler radicalises this further.** "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). This is the queer theoretical move: the unified subject that liberal feminism relied on is itself an effect of power, and any "we" implicates exclusions (of trans women, of intersex people, of non-binary subjects). Performativity replaces essence; the destabilisation of "woman" is not the end of feminism but the condition of a more inclusive politics.

**Where this leaves the exam question.** I am persuaded by Harding's resolution: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, 2004: 131). Adichie supplies the practical correlate: the term "feminist" must be retained because dropping it for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded". "Feminism" and "feminisms" are therefore not rivals: they are two sides of one practice. Feminism names the collective political subject under construction; feminisms names the plural standpoints whose internal argument keeps that subject honest.

## 8. Essay answers

### Past-Q 2025 (Q5): Outline and assess the political importance of feminism today.

Feminism remains politically important because the structural problem it identifies, that women are positioned as "other" to a male norm masquerading as universal, is empirically alive even as the form of othering shifts. I will outline the political agenda and then assess its limits.

Liberal first-wave feminism, in Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), set the equal-rights agenda: vote, education, equal pay. That agenda is unfinished but no longer sufficient. Beauvoir's diagnosis remains live: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Second-wave feminism translated this into "the personal is political", connecting patriarchy, reproduction-as-labour and the exploitation of women's work, and showing that the public/private split was a historical product of industrial capitalism (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986). Black feminist thought, intersectionality and post-structuralist feminism refined this: Hill Collins' matrix of domination, Crenshaw's intersectionality, hooks' oppositional gaze, Butler's destabilisation of "woman".

The assessment proceeds in four steps.

First, the gender data gap has lethal effects. Criado-Perez documents crash-test dummies based on a 50th-percentile male until 2011, with the "female" dummy "just a scaled-down male dummy"; women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured" and "17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash. A British female police officer was stabbed and killed in 1997 after removing ill-fitting body armour. Office formulas overestimate female metabolic rate "by as much as 35%"; Apple Health launched without a period tracker; Google speech recognition was "70% more likely to accurately recognise male speech". The "reference man" stands in for humanity, and women pay the price.

Second, the public/private split keeps reorganising itself. The home was reinvented as a feminised private with industrial capitalism, and the same architecture still allocates unpaid reproductive labour to women: Hartsock's "women have been given the responsibility for certain areas of production and reproduction such as childbirth and child-rearing" (Hartsock, 1983: 284) is descriptive, not historical. Adichie's Igbo extended-family meetings ("only the male members of the extended family can attend the meetings where major family decisions are taken… because I am female") confirm the asymmetry recurs across very different settings. The cost-of-living crisis sharpens the point: the squeeze on care work, disproportionately performed by women, is structural, not incidental.

Third, women's standpoint generates more accurate knowledge. Harding's "strong objectivity" is the principle; Criado-Perez is the engineering proof. Starting from men's bodies as the default is not neutral; it is, in Harding's terms, the "God-trick" in engineering. The standpoint argument has a Black radical genealogy that strengthens it: Du Bois's "double consciousness", "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), is the form Hartsock's insider/outsider duality and Hill Collins's outsider within both inherit. The epistemic privilege of the marginalised is not a feminist invention but a feminist deepening of an older critical tradition.

Fourth, feminism supplies a vocabulary other movements cannot. #MeToo, Black Lives Matter, post-Roe America and the Iran women-life-freedom protests are intelligible together only through the categories feminism supplies. Adichie's defence of the *word* is exactly this: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

The limits must be assessed honestly. Universalist mainstream feminism (the corporate "Lean In" register) has been rightly criticised by Black and intersectional feminists for taking white middle-class experience as the experience of women in general. Pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical, ignoring "how white women have benefited from colonialism". Butler adds a further caution: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). These are reasons to plurialise the term ("feminisms") while keeping the practice.

In sum, the political importance of feminism is high. It is the only language that simultaneously names the historical specificity of women's subordination, refuses to flatten it into a single experience, and provides the analytic tools (patriarchy, standpoint, intersectionality, the gender data gap) for diagnosing material harms. To abandon the word would be to lose the resource through which social disadvantage becomes critical insight. (Word count: 730.)

### Past-Q 2024 (Q4): Is 'feminisms' a better way to understand women's experiences than 'feminism'?

The plural and singular are not rivals: "feminisms" is the better description of the field, but "feminism" remains a necessary political fiction. Dropping the singular concedes too much; refusing the plural concedes too little.

The case for "feminisms" is overwhelming. Pre-1980s feminism was universalising (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialising (being a woman is enough), and ahistorical (neglecting how white women have benefited from colonialism). Beauvoir names a real structural condition, "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6), but the lecture asks: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir is a middle-class, white, European intellectual, and what is universalised under "woman" too often turns out to be one specific location.

Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks make this concrete. "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70); the matrix of domination shows gender always operates with race and class. Crenshaw's intersectionality is the conceptual reason we must speak in the plural. hooks, "the 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115), refuses the second-wave reading that home is uniformly repressive; for Black women under racial terror, home is also refuge. Mohanty extends the critique geographically: Western feminism produces "the third world woman" as "an effect of discourse… ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), in implicit contrast to its self-image as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies". Singular "feminism" thus risks reproducing the colonial gaze. Black Lives Matter and the Lean In versus Black/intersectional contrast register the same point politically.

Adichie's essay is a sequence of cases that resist universalisation: her friend Okoloma who first called her a feminist; the class-monitor episode where a girl with the highest score is passed over because "the monitor had to be a boy"; the parking attendant who thanked her male friend Louis for money she had given; hotel staff reading a woman alone as a sex worker; Igbo extended-family meetings where she "cannot have a formal say" because she is female; her great-grandmother who "ran away from the house of the man she did not want to marry" and "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." None collapses into Beauvoir's "woman".

And yet I resist simply dropping the singular. The pluralist position has its own pathology: if every standpoint is irreducibly its own, feminism dissolves into identity claims. Butler's caution, "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2), does not mean abandoning "woman" but handling it carefully. Adichie: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." Criado-Perez is decisive. When crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, voice recognition and Apple Health are designed around the reference man, women of every race, class and sexuality are 47% more likely to be seriously injured, freeze in offices, and find their phones unsafe to use one-handed. These are structural harms that cross every intersection. Post-Roe America makes the same point: the rolling back of reproductive control is registered by women as a class, even as it falls unevenly.

Harding's synthesis: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131). The plural names the *internal pluralism*; the singular names the *external political subject* the movement constructs when Adichie says "I am a feminist."

So my answer: feminisms describes the field; feminism names the practice. To pick one without the other is to lose either descriptive accuracy or political traction. The exam pushes for a choice; I refuse it. We need both, and the productive tension between them, to understand women's experiences: radically plural in form yet, as Criado-Perez shows, still recognisably gendered in effect. (Word count: 720.)

### Past-Q 2023 (Q5): Should sociology use the term "feminism" or "feminisms"?

Sociology should use both terms, deliberately, and should resist the pressure to pick one. This is not a fudge; it reflects how sociological knowledge is actually produced: holding categories open enough to capture a structural pattern while keeping them porous enough to admit internal difference. I defend this in three steps: the case for the singular, the case for the plural, and the case for their disciplined coexistence.

The case for the singular rests on sociology's need to name structures. Beauvoir's claim, "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6), describes a pattern of othering that crosses cultures and periods. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is a striking sociological demonstration: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable crash because engineering has treated the male body as default. The office thermostat, the stab vest (which got a British female police officer killed in 1997), the smartphone, Google voice recognition, Apple Health's missing period tracker: these are all the same sociological fact. #MeToo and post-Roe America operate on the same singular. Adichie: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

The case for the plural is just as strong. Pre-1980s feminism was universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. Hill Collins formulates the counter-position: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). The matrix of domination shows gender does not "exist on its own"; it is produced through race, class, sexuality and disability simultaneously. Crenshaw's intersectionality formalised this. hooks's "There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) refuses the second-wave assumption that the domestic sphere is uniformly repressive. Butler pushes further: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Sociology that uses "woman" naively reproduces exclusions, historically of trans women, of working-class women, of women of colour. Current trans-rights debates within feminism turn on whether to treat "woman" as self-evident.

This pluralism has empirical purchase. Singular feminism cannot explain why Adichie's Igbo great-grandmother, who "refused, protested, spoke up when she felt she was being deprived of land and access because she was female", was a feminist without the word, while a Nigerian academic could tell Adichie that "feminism was not our culture, that feminism was un-African". Nor why Adichie's brother Kene, "a kind, good-looking, and very masculine young man", is "the best feminist I know", while a male friend warns her that "calling myself a feminist meant that I hated men". "Culture does not make people. People make culture."

But the case for coexistence is the sociologically interesting one. Harding states it cleanly: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131). This is how sociology actually works: we generalise *and* attend to difference. The plural names the internal pluralism of standpoints; the singular names the political subject those standpoints jointly construct.

A purely pluralist sociology would lose the capacity to make Criado-Perez-type structural claims, and it would underestimate how "feminisms" can serve as covert essentialism in its own right (e.g. when "Black feminism" is taken as a single thing rather than a plural tradition). A purely singular sociology repeats Beauvoir's mistake. The L6 standpoint argument resolves the tension at the level of method: "marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129), and "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). The singular names the practice (start from marginalised lives); the plural names the empirical fact that those lives are many. Sociology operationalises both.

My recommendation: sociology should treat "feminism" and "feminisms" as a dialectical pair. We use the singular when making structural claims about patriarchy, the gender data gap, the public/private split. We use the plural when attending to differently positioned standpoints in the matrix of domination. To insist on one term is to lose half the work. (Word count: 730.)

### Past-Q 2022 (Q8): Should women's experience be the basis of feminism?

Women's experience should be a foundational input to feminism, but it cannot be its sole basis without producing the universalising, essentialising and ahistorical errors Black, post-structuralist and standpoint feminism have spent forty years correcting. Harding's answer holds: experience is the *starting point* but not the *foundation* of feminist knowledge.

The case for experience is the strongest part of the feminist tradition. Beauvoir built *The Second Sex* on women's lived experience of being "the 'other' of men". Her "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" makes experience constitutive rather than incidental. The second-wave slogan "the personal is political" revealed private experiences as political because their pattern was systemic. Hartsock translated experience into epistemology: women's dual position generates a critical vantage, just as the proletariat's dual position generated Marx's critique (Hartsock, 1983: 142). Adichie's TEDx talk starts not from theory but from being thanked through Louis at the parking attendant, from being assumed to be a sex worker in a hotel lobby. #MeToo and the Iran women-life-freedom protests are contemporary versions.

Criado-Perez shows the analytic power. Sandi Toksvig's anthropology professor reframes an antler bone with 28 markings: a male anthropologist sees "man's first attempt at a calendar"; a female anthropologist sees a woman tracking her cycle. The interpretive difference flows from a different bodily experience. The same logic explains why Apple Health launched without a period tracker, why office thermostats freeze women, why stab vests get women killed: a male-only experiential base produced systematically defective knowledge. This is Harding's strong objectivity: politically engaged knowledge starting from marginalised standpoints is, empirically, more accurate.

But experience is dangerous as a *foundation*. Three problems.

First, universalising. The lecture asks of Beauvoir: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" Beauvoir's "woman" is implicitly middle-class, white, European. Hill Collins responds by insisting on Black women's specific standpoint: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Gender is always co-produced with race and class; a feminism taking one experience as *the* experience effaces this.

Second, essentialising. Butler warns: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Grounding feminism in "women's experience" requires specifying whose experience counts as women's, and any specification will exclude. Trans women's experiences? Intersex experiences? Asexual women's? Women without children? Each line reproduces a power-laden boundary. The current trans-rights debates illustrate exactly this.

Third, the empirical problem. Adichie was told feminism was "un-African". Her response is that her great-grandmother, who fled a forced marriage, "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one." Experience without an interpretive frame does not arrive labelled "feminist"; the same experiences are mobilised by anti-feminist analyses too. Experience requires theory to become knowledge.

Harding's resolution is the most defensible. "Women's lives and experiences provide the 'grounds' for this knowledge, though these clearly do not provide foundations for knowledge in the conventional philosophical sense. These grounds are the site, the activities, from which scientific questions arise" (Harding, 2004: 128). Experience is the starting point; strong objectivity turns it into knowledge. And, "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135). Feminism is not the property of those with the relevant experiences; it is a practice that *begins* with those experiences but is owed by everyone. This is precisely the standpoint move L6 generalises beyond feminism: experience grounds rather than founds knowledge, and "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Feminist standpoint theory was the laboratory in which a more general sociology of situated knowledge was developed.

So: yes, women's experience should be the basis of feminism in the sense of the starting point; no, it cannot be the foundation, in the sense of an unmediated ground. To collapse the distinction is to repeat Beauvoir's universalising move or to dissolve feminism into incommensurable testimonies. Standpoint theory keeps both errors at bay. (Word count: 720.)

### Past-Q 2021 (Q8): Is it still possible to talk of "feminism" as a unified project or do we need to use the term "feminisms"?

It is still possible, and politically necessary, to talk of feminism as a unified project, provided we understand the unity rightly. It is not unity of content (one experience, one theory) but of *practice*: a shared commitment to identifying and dismantling patriarchy. Within that practice, "feminisms" names the internal pluralism that keeps it honest. Against the question's implied dichotomy, the two terms are complementary.

The case for "feminisms" is the default position in academic sociology, rightly. Feminism before the 1980s universalised, essentialised, and was ahistorical (ignoring "how white women have benefited from colonialism"). Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks supplied the tools: the matrix of domination ("structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression"), intersectionality, the oppositional gaze. Butler radicalises: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Adichie's lives demonstrate the radical pluralism: the great-grandmother who fled a forced marriage; the class-monitor episode where a girl with the highest score is passed over because the role "had to be a boy"; the Lagos hotel staff who read a woman alone as a sex worker. To force these into one "feminism" is to repeat Beauvoir's error.

But the case for the singular is also strong, sometimes underestimated by the pluralist consensus. Three points.

First, a structural pattern holds across difference. Criado-Perez shows crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats and voice-recognition systems built around a "reference man"; women across race and class pay the price: "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable crash; offices "five degrees too cold" because of a 1960s formula calibrated to the male metabolic rate. Crenshaw refines this; she does not erase it. Post-Roe America is the contemporary echo: harm intelligible only through the singular "women".

Second, "feminism" performs political work the plural cannot. Adichie defends the term: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." Her line "I am a feminist" names a political subject; "All of us, women and men, must do better" presupposes that "we". The #MeToo cultural moment did the same work at scale.

Third, Harding supplies the synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131). The unified project is the project of starting from marginalised lives; the plural is built in, not opposed.

Concrete examples illustrate the productive tension. Hill Collins's Black feminist standpoint and hooks's rewriting of the home as refuge correct universalism and produce a sharper feminism rather than fragmenting it. The Black radical tradition is the deeper lineage here: Du Bois's "double consciousness", "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), is the structural form Hill Collins inherits and pluralises into the matrix. Singular "feminism" can hold this lineage because the lineage is already plural. Adichie's great-grandmother who "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one" is the same point autobiographically: the singular term reaches across difference once we let it. Butler herself, in *Gender Trouble*, is not trying to abolish "feminism" but to make it more honest.

The contrast between Lean In feminism and Black/intersectional feminism is the live political register of this tension. Lean In universalises the experience of professional middle-class women; Black and intersectional feminism plurialises. Both, importantly, remain *feminism*. Social media activism around Black Lives Matter, #MeToo, and reproductive rights operates on the same dual register: the hashtag unifies, the threads pluralise.

So: yes, it is still possible to talk of feminism as a unified project, and we should, because the alternative is to lose the category that allows us to say "women are 17% more likely to die in a comparable car crash". And we also need "feminisms", because without internal pluralism the singular collapses back into Beauvoir's universalism. The unified project is unified in *practice*, not in *experience*; the practice is the joint construction, by many different standpoints, of a feminist politics that none could construct alone. ### Mock 1: Should feminism aim to join the male club, change its rules, or start another club? Discuss with reference to Wollstonecraft and de Beauvoir.

The lecturer's deliberately provocative trichotomy ("Is the aim of feminism simply to join the (male) club? Or should it aim to change the rules? Or to start another club?") is a useful way to plot the trajectory from first-wave liberal feminism to Beauvoir's existential break and onwards. I argue that the rules themselves must change, because joining the club leaves the patriarchal architecture intact, while starting another club risks abandoning the political work feminism alone performs. The defensible position is rule-changing, retaining the strategic singular while plurialising its content.

The "joining" position is Wollstonecraft's. *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), written in the Enlightenment afterglow of the French Revolution, claims for women the rational personhood that liberalism already extended to men. The agenda is vote, education, equal pay, equal access to political life. The strength of this position is that it operates on the universalist register liberalism already grants; the weakness, which Beauvoir later names, is that "universal" rationality was already masculine. Wollstonecraft can demand entry without questioning the architecture of the building. The Lean In framing is the contemporary corporate descendant: lean further into a structure designed against you, and call the result emancipation.

Beauvoir's intervention in *The Second Sex* (1949) refuses the joining frame. "Humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him; she is not regarded as an autonomous being… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Once the club is unmasked as a male universal, joining it means accepting a position pre-marked as derivative. "One is not born but rather one becomes a woman" makes the disqualification socially produced; the response is to alter the social production, not to perform better within it. The L4 work on language makes the same diagnosis structurally: "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15) because the male is the unmarked default. Joining the club leaves the marking in place.

The "rule-changing" position therefore follows from Beauvoir's diagnosis. If woman is constituted through experience as other, then equality requires altering the conditions of constitution, not adjusting the membership list. Hartsock translates this into epistemology: women's dual position as insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power) generates a vantage on the patriarchal architecture itself. "Just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The rules of the club, not its membership, are the object of critique.

Criado-Perez supplies the contemporary engineering proof. The "reference man" is the club's working assumption: a Caucasian male, 25 to 30, 70 kilograms, representing humanity. Joining the club, on these terms, means crash-test dummies modelled on the male body that leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured, stab vests that get a British female police officer killed in 1997, office formulas calibrated to a male resting metabolic rate, smartphones at the upper limit of male hand size. Changing the rules means redesigning the dummies, the vests, the formulas, the phones. Harding's strong objectivity (L6) is the methodological articulation: start research from women's lives, treat the subject of knowledge as part of the object of knowledge (Harding, 2004: 136). #MeToo and post-Roe America are the political analogue: rule changing rather than entry-bargaining.

The "another club" position has force where racial terror has poisoned mainstream institutions. Hill Collins and hooks insist that Black women's standpoint generates a different feminist tradition, and that the home, far from being uniformly repressive, can be a site of refuge and resistance: "There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). But Adichie's defence of the singular word holds: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded". The plural is internal to the singular, not its replacement.

So my answer: change the rules. Wollstonecraft's joining is necessary but insufficient; Beauvoir's diagnosis demands more; the architectural critique that follows, from Hartsock through Hill Collins, Crenshaw, hooks and Butler to Criado-Perez, is the rule-changing programme. Another-club separatism is a tactical response to particular failures, not the general strategy. (Word count: 720.)

### Mock 2: Is the political strength of feminism determined by how many women it can speak for, or by whose voices it begins from?

The opening framing of the lecture sets the question sharply: "the more people that feminism can 'talk for', the stronger its arguments… the more people that feminism can claim to represent, the more powerful its arguments. Or maybe you disagree?" I disagree, and the disagreement is the central methodological lesson of the module. Political strength does not come from the size of the demographic claimed, but from the analytic location chosen as starting point. To start from the wrong location is to lose strength even while gaining numbers.

The numerical view is intuitive. A movement representing more people commands more legitimacy and more leverage. First-wave feminism rallied around suffrage on something like this principle: every woman is a voter denied. Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) extends the universalist Enlightenment subject to women; the more women included, the stronger the claim. #MeToo at its high point operated similarly: the cumulative volume of testimony was its own argument. The cost-of-living crisis presents the same shape: care work disproportionately performed by women is a structural diagnosis that gains traction by counting.

But the numerical view has a built-in flaw, exposed by Black feminism, intersectionality and post-structuralism. Counting requires a category; categories require a default; defaults privilege some lives over others. The lecture is explicit: pre-1980s feminism was universalising (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialising (presuming being a woman is enough) and ahistorical (neglecting "how white women have benefited from colonialism"). Talking for "all women" without specifying the location from which one begins produces a "woman" who is implicitly white, middle-class, partnered, straight, cis. Hill Collins reframes: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Crenshaw's intersectionality is the diagnostic; hooks's oppositional gaze the political consequence. The Lean In versus Black/intersectional contrast is the live political register: a feminism that maximises numerical reach by flattening difference is less strong, not more.

Harding makes the methodological case. "Standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129). The privileged starting point is not the largest demographic but the most marginalised. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136); strong objectivity demands strong reflexivity. The strongest feminism is therefore the one that explicitly names whose lives it begins from, not the one that claims to begin from no one in particular.

Criado-Perez supplies the practical illustration. The reference man is engineering's universal: crash-test dummies until 2011 used a 50th-percentile male, the "female" dummy is "just a scaled-down male dummy", office thermostats are calibrated to male resting metabolic rates, Apple Health launched without a period tracker, Google speech recognition is 70% more likely to recognise male speech. The result is empirically worse engineering: women 47% more likely to be seriously injured, 17% more likely to die. Starting from women's bodies produces more accurate science. The numerical move ("we represent humanity") is exactly what blocks accuracy; the locational move ("we start from women's lives") is what supplies it. This is Harding's strong objectivity in cars, masks, thermostats and phones.

Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* converges. She begins from autobiographical episodes (the Lagos parking attendant thanking Louis, the hotel staff reading a woman alone as a sex worker, the Nsukka class monitor episode where the highest-scoring girl is passed over because the role "had to be a boy"). The political power of these episodes is not statistical; it is analytic, because they are mobilised from specific lives. Her defence of the word: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded". The singular term retains political traction precisely because its plural starting points are named.

So my answer: feminism's political strength is determined by whose voices it begins from, not how many it claims to speak for. Numerical reach without locational specificity reproduces the universalising error pre-1980s feminism is rightly criticised for. The strongest contemporary feminism starts from marginalised lives, plurialises its standpoints, and uses the singular "feminism" as a strategic political category whose interior is reflexively plural. Numbers follow strength; they do not generate it. (Word count: 740.)

### Mock 3: Pre-1980s feminism stands accused of being universalising, essentialising and ahistorical. How decisively do these three charges reshape feminist theory?

The lecture's triplet, that "feminism until 1980s did not pay enough attention to differences between woman" because it was universalising (presuming a shared history of oppression), essentialising (presuming being a woman is enough) and ahistorical (neglecting "how white women have benefited from colonialism"), is the diagnostic that organises everything Black, post-structuralist and standpoint feminism does after 1980. I argue the charges are decisive in reshaping theory but not in dissolving the project: they make pluralism mandatory while leaving the strategic singular intact.

Start with the charge of universalising. Wollstonecraft (1792) and Beauvoir (1949) both rely on a working subject "woman" generalised from their own historical and class position. The lecture asks of Beauvoir specifically: "Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" The honest answer is no. Beauvoir's diagnosis ("humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6)) names a structural pattern but generalises a particular experience. The reshape: Hill Collins specifies "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70); the matrix of domination shows that "structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression". Crenshaw formalises intersectionality. The decisive shift is from one standpoint to many, from a presumed shared history to differentially located histories.

Now essentialising. The charge is that pre-1980s feminism treats "being a woman" as politically sufficient: anatomical or social womanhood automatically grounds feminist claims. Butler delivers the decisive answer: "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Gender is performative; "woman" is constituted through the very acts that present themselves as expressing it. The reshape: the subject of feminism is itself an object of feminist critique. Standpoint theory absorbs this through reflexivity; Harding's "strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'… the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Current trans-rights debates within feminism are the live register: anyone arguing that "woman" is self-evident is operating on the essentialism the post-1980s critique forbade.

The ahistorical charge is the most politically pointed. Gilroy's Black Atlantic shows modernity from the standpoint of those it enslaved. Feminism that absorbs these becomes historically richer; feminism that does not collapses into Lean In universalism.

Method: plural standpoints, intersectional architecture, performative subject, strong reflexivity. These are no longer optional. A strategic "woman" persists, but only as openly constructed and held under interrogation. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is the instance where the strategic singular still does work: crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, the missing period tracker. These harms are recognisably gendered across race and class; intersectionality refines but does not erase them.

So the three charges reshape feminist theory by making the singular strategic, the standpoints plural, and the subject reflexive. The project survives, sharper than before, because the charges were absorbed rather than refused. Harding's synthesis: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131). The charges did not end feminism; they matured it. ### Mock 4: Explain Hartsock's claim that women occupy a "unique social position", and assess what the duality of women's experience contributes to feminist knowledge.

Hartsock's claim that women occupy a unique social position is best read as the epistemological hinge of feminism: women are insiders to the system because their labour is essential to its reproduction, and outsiders because they are denied power within it, and this dual location generates a critical vantage unavailable to dominant-group thinkers. The lecturer summarises the three lessons drawn from first- and second-wave feminism as "the importance of different standpoints/perspectives", how "experiences can provide insights into the operations of power", and how "there can be a duality to experience". Hartsock is the systematic articulation of all three.

Begin with the structural claim. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284). The difference is material: women have historically been allocated the responsibility for social reproduction (childbirth, childrearing, domestic labour, emotional work, care), while men have been assigned material and financial production. The public/private split historicised by Hall (1980) and Walby (1986) is the institutional form: with industrial capitalism, the home is reinvented as feminised private, and "women's role becomes the inconspicuous work of making the work of the (male) wage-worker possible". Women as wasteful consumers, men as productive workers. The asymmetry is what Hartsock means by women's "unique social position": essential, yet powerless.

The duality is the next move. Women are *inside*: their labour underwrites the continuation of the system. The economy, the household, the wage relation all depend on the unwaged or low-waged labour of women. They are *outside*: they have no commensurate power within the system. They are not its directors. This dual location is not an accident of biography; it is the structural condition of women as a class. Hartsock builds on Marx's analogous analysis of the proletariat: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142). The proletariat's dual position (essential to capital, dispossessed by capital) generated critique; women's dual position generates feminist critique.

What does the duality contribute to feminist knowledge? Four contributions.

First, epistemic privilege. The insider/outsider position generates analytic vantage that those purely inside power cannot occupy. Hill Collins's "outsider within" formalises the same insight (Hill Collins, 1986). The L8 lineage runs through Du Bois: "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). The duality is structurally the same; the feminist application is Hartsock's.

Second, the standpoint is achieved, not given. Hartsock insists feminist standpoint requires political and theoretical work; mere anatomy does not deliver insight. This protects the position from biological essentialism and aligns with Butler's later destabilisation: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). The standpoint is a practice, not a property.

Third, the duality grounds the personal-is-political slogan. Hartsock translates consciousness-raising into theory: the patterns women individually experience are systemic precisely because women occupy a shared structural location. Adichie's parking-attendant episode (the gratitude rerouted through her male friend Louis) and her Nsukka class-monitor episode (the highest-scoring girl passed over because "the monitor had to be a boy") are concrete cases of insider essentiality coupled with outsider powerlessness.

Fourth, the duality has empirical payoff. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is the engineering verification: starting from women's lives produces better cars, vests, offices, phones, healthcare. Women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable crash because engineering starts from the reference man. Starting from women's bodies would have produced different dummies, vests and thermostats. Harding's strong objectivity (L6) operationalises Hartsock methodologically: "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136).

Two qualifications. The duality is intersectionally differentiated: Hill Collins's matrix shows the insider/outsider position varies by race, class and sexuality; the Black domestic worker's position is not the white middle-class housewife's. The standpoint is, as Harding insists, plural: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start" (Harding, 2004: 131). And Butler's caution applies: the duality is itself produced by the gender system, not pre-political.

So Hartsock's "unique social position" contributes the structural location, the epistemic principle and the political duality on which contemporary feminist sociology still operates. (Word count: 745.)

### Mock 5: Why does it matter that "gender" is an arbitrary category for organising the world? Discuss with reference to de Beauvoir and Butler.

The lecturer's etymological detour ("De Beauvoir uses the French word 'genre' which is translated into English as 'gender'… These words come from the Latin word 'genus'. Genus indicates a 'type' or 'kind'… Genus, genre, and gender (especially in the grammatical sense) indicates ways of organizing the world and our thoughts about the world according to certain arbitrary categories") is not idle philology. It signals that gender is a sorting device, not a discovery of natural kinds. I argue this matters because once gender is recognised as an organising category rather than a natural fact, both feminism's analytical scope and its political possibilities expand. Beauvoir opens the move; Butler radicalises it.

Begin with Beauvoir. *The Second Sex* (1949) asks "what is a woman?" and answers, scandalously for 1949, that there is no essential answer: "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman". The category "woman" is produced through socialisation, mythology, juridical positioning and interpersonal recognition. The structural finding: "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him; she is not regarded as an autonomous being… He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). The male is taken as the universal human, woman as the marked term. Recognising gender as a sorting device makes this asymmetry visible: humanity is being organised under one of two arbitrary categories, and the category "male" is silently set as the unmarked default. The L4 work on language operationalises the same point: "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15).

Why this matters analytically. First, the world's representation is exposed as partial. ### Mock 10: What does de Beauvoir mean by "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman", and why does she treat lived experience as central?

The sentence contains two of the most consequential moves in twentieth-century feminist theory: an anti-essentialist account of gender, and a methodological commitment to lived experience as the ground of philosophical knowledge. Together they make *The Second Sex* (1949) the hinge between liberal first-wave feminism and the second-wave, standpoint and post-structuralist traditions that follow.

Begin with the sentence. Beauvoir's claim is not that biological sex is irrelevant but that "woman" (the social, psychological, juridical, aesthetic category) is constituted through a process: socialisation, mythology, institutional positioning, interpersonal recognition. The category is historical and contingent, not natural. This is the conceptual seed of what Butler later theorises as performativity: gender as constituted through repeated acts that present themselves as if always already there.

The structural finding grounding the claim is woman-as-other. "Humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him; she is not regarded as an autonomous being... She is the incidental, the inessential as opposed to the absolute. He is the Subject, he is the Absolute; she is the Other" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Because man is taken as the universal human, woman becomes the marked term. To become a woman is to be inserted into this asymmetric structure and to learn it as nature.

Why does Beauvoir treat lived experience as central? Three reasons.

First, the existentialist apparatus demands it. Human beings are not essences but projects, freedoms exercised within concrete situations. To know what it is to be a woman one must investigate the lived situation, not legislate from outside.

Second, experience is the ground on which the woman-as-other diagnosis becomes available. The second-wave slogan "the personal is political" is already implicit, and Hartsock's "women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284) extends Beauvoir into a full theory of situated knowledge.

Third, the work has to do double duty: persuade women that patterns they read as personal failings are systemic, and persuade philosophy that "women" is a question. Adichie's *We Should All Be Feminists* uses the same method: autobiographical episodes (the Lagos parking attendant thanking her through Louis, hotel staff assuming her to be a sex worker) generating the diagnosis.

The anti-essentialism is the most enduring contribution. If becoming a woman is a process, it can be done differently: by raising daughters and sons differently (Adichie's prescription), or by destabilising the binary (Butler). The same move underwrites trans-feminist interventions and the current trans-rights debates: if "woman" is constituted rather than given, there is no metaphysical bar to its being constituted otherwise. Beauvoir's parallel argument in L4 makes the linguistic dimension visible: "a man would never set out to write a book on the peculiar situation of the human male. But if I wish to define myself, I must first of all say: 'I am a woman'" (de Beauvoir, 1988: 15). One is not born a "woman writer"; one becomes one because language pre-marks femininity. Spender's "man-made language" (1980) generalises the point structurally.

The centrality of lived experience must be qualified. Beauvoir is herself a middle-class, white, European intellectual writing in 1949, and the experience she describes is in important respects her own. Hill Collins, Crenshaw and hooks supply the corrective: many lived experiences, structured by the matrix of domination across race, class, sexuality and ability. Harding makes the point explicitly: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways" (Harding, 2004: 131). Black Lives Matter and #MeToo are contemporary instances: shared lived experience becoming structural diagnosis.

Concrete examples reinforce why the move matters. Criado-Perez's gender data gap is Beauvoir restated empirically: when the world is engineered around the "reference man", women's lived experience (freezing offices, ill-fitting stab vests) registers the asymmetry materially. Adichie's class-monitor episode is Beauvoir in autobiographical miniature: a girl who scores highest is told "the monitor had to be a boy". One is not born a "woman who cannot be class monitor"; one is made one.

So the sentence means: woman is a process, not an essence; and one must read that process from inside the lives it shapes. That is why Beauvoir puts lived experience at the centre, and why the move still organises feminist thought seventy-five years on. (Word count: 720.)

Hartsock supplies the epistemic principle, Hill Collins its intersectional architecture, Harding the methodological apparatus that makes both rigorous. (Word count: 730.)

Can she really describe and account for the way in which ALL women have been oppressed all over the world, in every age?" The answer is no. The "woman" of *The Second Sex* is a middle-class white European.

Crenshaw refines. Intersectionality is "the notion that different types of discrimination intersect and often compound experiences of exclusion and social subjugation." The othering Beauvoir describes is itself differentiated. A Black woman is not simply Beauvoir's other; she is othered by race and gender simultaneously, and the compound is its own analytic object. The contrast between Lean In feminism and Black/intersectional feminism is the contemporary live form of this critique.

Hill Collins's matrix of domination is the systemic counterpart. "Structural, disciplinary, hegemonic, and interpersonal domains of power reappear across quite different forms of oppression… Gender does not exist 'on its own'… 'Race'… is equally important, if not more important, than 'gender'… we can't think gender without thinking 'race'" (Hill Collins). The matrix is the architecture; intersectionality is the diagnostic.

How this refines Beauvoir. Four moves.

First, the universalising critique. Beauvoir's woman-as-other is, on Crenshaw's reading, a particular position mistakenly generalised. The refinement: locate the position. Which woman, othered by which combination of axes?

Second, the experience critique. Beauvoir centres lived experience; Hartsock translates this into standpoint epistemology. Crenshaw plurialises the experiential ground: "Black feminist thought consists of ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Black Lives Matter operationalises the same insight.

Third, the home critique. Beauvoir reads the home as the site of female othering by generalising a particular case. Hill Collins and hooks "both questioned second-wave understandings of the home as a site of repression and subjugation". For Black women, the home is also refuge. "Home" is one architectural form whose function varies by position.

Fourth, the colonial critique. The lecture identifies "how white women have benefited from colonialism" as one of the histories pre-1980s feminism ignored. Beauvoir's woman-as-other was, in some respects, also a coloniser; intersectionality theorises this.

hooks's oppositional gaze is the active counterpart. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). The othered subject is also capable of looking back; the othering is not total. The L8 lineage is decisive here: Du Bois's "double consciousness", "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), pre-figures both Beauvoir's woman-as-other and Crenshaw's compound othering. Intersectionality is not simply added to Beauvoir; it grafts the Black radical analysis of doubled vision onto her structural diagnosis.

Adichie supplies a non-Black-American case. Her Lagos hotel experience, her class-monitor episode, her exclusion from Igbo extended-family meetings: each is a Beauvoirian othering and each is also intersectional (gender, ethnicity, region, class, age).

Butler radicalises further. "The category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Beauvoir's "woman" is not pre-political; the othering produces the category.

Criado-Perez confirms the structural diagnosis materially while requiring the intersectional refinement: women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable crash, and the male face-shape used for dust and eye masks fits neither most women nor "a lot of black and minority ethnic men". Same structural pattern, intersectionally differentiated.

So Crenshaw refines Beauvoir by plurialising the othered subject without abandoning the structural diagnosis. The diagnosis stands; the diagnostic apparatus gets sharper. (Word count: 720.)

### Mock 13: Is queer theory a vindication or a refutation of feminist standpoint theory?

Against the disjunction, queer theory is best read as a *modification* of feminist standpoint theory: neither vindication nor refutation, but a critical extension that exposes a flaw in the original formulation while preserving its core epistemic claim. Harding's reformulation integrates the queer-theoretical correction without abandoning standpoint epistemology.

Begin with the two positions.

Standpoint theory, in Hartsock's 1983 formulation, holds that women's structural location, particularly in social reproduction, produces a critical vantage on patriarchy. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284); that difference, theorised reflexively, generates situated knowledge. Standpoint privilege is achieved, not given. The empirical pay-off is the Criado-Perez gender data gap: starting from women's bodies and lives would have produced better engineering, medicine and policy than the engineering from the reference man.

Queer theory, in Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990), destabilises the category "woman". "Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Gender is performative. The heterosexual matrix produces "woman" as one of two stable categories; anything not fitting (intersex bodies, trans subjectivities) is rendered unintelligible. Current trans-rights debates are the live register.

Why might queer theory look like a refutation? Because if "woman" is unstable, the structural location from which the standpoint is supposed to be generated is itself politically constructed. Hartsock's grounding in social reproduction looks suspect: women are not simply "in" social reproduction; women are constituted as those who belong there. The standpoint is, on this reading, an artefact of the very gender system feminism aims to dismantle.

The refutation reading has force but overstates. Three reasons.

First, Hartsock and Harding already insisted the standpoint is achieved. Standpoint privilege does not depend on a metaphysically prior "woman" but on the work that turns a structural location into analytic vantage. Butler's destabilisation deepens this, not undermines it.

Second, the empirical pay-off does not depend on metaphysical stability. When Criado-Perez shows crash-test dummies modelled on the reference man leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured, "women" is doing material-statistical work, not metaphysical work. Butler insists the category be used reflexively, not abandoned. Standpoint theory can, and Harding does, accept the reflexive use.

Third, queer theory's own critical purchase requires something like a standpoint move. To name compulsory heterosexuality as a structure of power requires starting from lives that the structure renders unintelligible. This is standpoint method in queer-theoretical clothes.

So queer theory is best read as a vindication-through-modification. It vindicates the core claim (knowledge is socially situated; marginalised locations generate critical resources) while modifying it in three ways.

First, the relevant locations are plural. Not one standpoint of "woman" but many standpoints of differently positioned subjects. This is also Hill Collins's correction: "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70). Black Lives Matter and Black/intersectional feminism are the live political registers.

Second, the locations are themselves constructed. Standpoint analysis must include analysis of how the location was produced. Harding's strong objectivity, "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge", handles this.

Third, the standpoint is mobilised strategically. We use "woman" when the structural pattern (the gender data gap, the public/private split, the woman-as-other diagnosis) needs naming; we destabilise it when its use threatens to exclude. Post-Roe America illustrates: "women" is needed to name the harm, even as the harm differentiates by race, class and trans status. The L6 epistemic principle holds across the modification: "the subject of knowledge must be considered as part of the object of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Queer theory's contribution is to ensure that the gendered subject of feminist knowledge is itself analysed, not assumed. That is reflexivity, not refutation.

Concrete examples. Adichie uses the category "woman" openly while attending to its constructedness. Her response to being told feminism was "un-African", that her great-grandmother "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one", uses "feminist" as a strategic category applied retrospectively. #MeToo did the same at scale.

So the answer is neither pure vindication nor pure refutation. The corrected standpoint theory (Harding's strong objectivity, plurialised through Hill Collins, made reflexive through Butler) is stronger than either parent position alone. (Word count: 720.)

### Mock 14: Does Harding's strong objectivity rescue or replace standpoint theory?

Harding's strong objectivity rescues standpoint theory rather than replacing it. The core claim, that knowledge is produced from socially located lives and that marginalised locations generate privileged resources, is preserved. Harding adds a methodological apparatus that defends the claim against charges of ethnocentrism, relativism, and unique-abilities-of-the-oppressed essentialism.

Recall Hartsock. "Women's lives differ structurally from those of men" (Hartsock, 1983: 284); women are insiders (essential to social reproduction) and outsiders (denied power); this duality generates a privileged vantage. The Marxist analogy: "just as Marx's understanding of the world from the standpoint of the proletariat enabled him to go beneath bourgeois ideology, so a feminist standpoint can allow us to understand patriarchal institutions and ideologies as perverse inversions of more human social relations" (Hartsock, 1983: 142).

The charges. Three classical objections, all addressed by Harding.

First, the God-trick. Critics suggest standpoint theory just substitutes one privileged viewpoint for another. Harding: "Standpoint knowledge projects do not claim to originate in purportedly universal human problematics; they do not claim to perform the 'God-trick.'… All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, 2004: 128).

Second, ethnocentrism. Critics suggest standpoint theory treats the holder's culture as superior. Harding: "standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129); the universalising claim is itself the ethnocentric one.

Third, relativism. "Judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project" (Harding, 2004: 131). "It is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history" (Harding, 2004: 131–132). Some locations are scientifically better starting points; this is substantive, not relativist.

Harding's positive synthesis. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge. Thus, strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). Standard "objectivism" is "not rigorous or objectifying enough"; it eliminates only values differing within the existing community, leaving culturewide assumptions intact. Strong objectivity makes the subject part of the object, and expands the community of inquiry.

Subjects of knowledge are "embodied and visible… multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134). "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135). Feminism is not the property of women.

Why this rescues. Three points.

First, the standpoint claim is preserved. Marginalised locations still generate privileged resources. Harding embeds the claim in a framework that handles objections.

Second, pluralism is enhanced. Hill Collins's Black feminist standpoint, hooks's oppositional gaze, and Crenshaw's intersectionality are absorbed: "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131). Black Lives Matter and the Lean In / intersectional contrast are political instances.

Third, Butler's destabilisation is accommodated. Reflexivity turns the subject of knowledge into part of the object; this is Butler's move. "The category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Strong objectivity is the methodological form of the critical injunction.

Criado-Perez is the empirical verification. The reference man (a Caucasian male, 25-30, around 70kg) is the unreflexive subject of engineering. Crash-test dummies, stab vests, office thermostats, smartphones, Google voice recognition, Apple Health's missing period tracker: each a case where strong objectivity would have produced more accurate engineering. The cost-of-living crisis is a sociological extension: starting from carers (disproportionately women) generates a more accurate policy diagnosis than starting from the reference worker. The L6 broader standpoint apparatus shows the move is not specific to feminism: Haraway's "situated knowledges" and refusal of "the 'God-trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location" (Harding, 2004: 4) generalise the principle across post-positivist sociology of science.

So Harding rescues. Standpoint theory was vulnerable in its raw form; strong objectivity defends it. The lecture's framing, that "different standpoints/perspectives" matter and that there is a "duality to experience", is preserved and made operational. (Word count: 720.)

### Mock 16: How convincing do you find Butler's argument that "woman" is not a stable category?

Butler's argument is convincing in its core claim (that the category "woman" is produced and restrained by the very structures of power feminism aims to dismantle) but requires reading in a modified form that integrates standpoint and intersectional critique. The destabilisation of "woman" is not an argument against using the category but for using it reflexively.

Recall the argument. *Gender Trouble* (1990) opens: "It is not enough to inquire into how women might become more fully represented in language and politics. Feminist critique ought also to understand how the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). Gender is performative: constituted through repeated acts that present themselves as always already there. The heterosexual matrix produces "woman" as one of two stable categories; anything not fitting (intersex bodies, trans subjectivities, non-binary identities) is rendered unintelligible. Feminism taking "woman" as a self-evident pre-political subject reproduces the system it claims to challenge.

How convincing? Very, on three counts.

First, the politics of exclusion. Pre-Butler feminism repeatedly failed lesbian, working-class, non-white, trans and intersex women by assuming a default "woman" who was straight, middle-class, white and cis. Black feminism had made the analogous point about race and class (Hill Collins's matrix, Crenshaw's intersectionality). Butler completes the move for gender identity and sexuality. The current trans-rights debates within feminism turn precisely on whether "woman" is a self-evident category. Butler's intervention is the conceptual reason to refuse that move.

Second, the deeper logic of Beauvoir. Beauvoir saw that "humanity is male and man defines woman not in herself but as relative to him" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 6). Butler radicalises: the very binary that produces both is an effect of power. Beauvoir's "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" is the seed; Butler's performativity is the full flowering.

Third, the empirical record. First-wave "woman" excluded Black, working-class and colonised women. Second-wave "woman" centred reproductive labour and partly excluded lesbian and child-free women. Butler's argument is the systematic reason why interrogation is permanent.

But the argument faces serious objections.

First, political. If "woman" is unstable, who is the subject of feminist politics? A wholly destabilised category makes the political "we" hard to construct. Adichie's defence of the *word* feminist points the other way: dropping it for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded." #MeToo and post-Roe America are political moments where "women" as a category did indispensable work.

Second, epistemological. Standpoint theory requires some description of where the standpoint is located. Hartsock locates women in social reproduction; if "woman" is wholly destabilised, this becomes harder. Harding's synthesis: hold "woman" as strategic, plural, historically located while recognising its constructedness. Strong objectivity is the apparatus.

Third, empirical. Criado-Perez's gender data gap shows the category has hard material effects: women "17% more likely to die" in a comparable crash because of male-default engineering (the reference man); offices freezing because of a 1960s HVAC formula; Apple Health launching without a period tracker. The category is necessary to name these facts. Butler does not deny this; she insists on reflexive use.

Fourth, intersectional. Hill Collins and Crenshaw had already plurialised "woman" in another idiom; Butler converges with them. The Lean In versus Black/intersectional contrast is the political form.

Concrete examples sharpen the assessment. Adichie handles the tension productively. Her dictionary definition is universalising, but her examples (Igbo extended-family meetings, the parking attendant who thanks Louis, the hotel staff who read a woman alone as a sex worker) implicitly hold the category open. The class-monitor case is performativity in the schoolroom: a girl learns that being "female" is what disqualifies her from the role, and the disqualification is the act through which the gendered subject is produced. The L4 work on language gives the linguistic counterpart: Spender's "man-made language" (1980) shows English structured around male-as-norm so that "female" is "minus male"; the instability Butler theorises is therefore not abstract but embedded in everyday speech. "Culture does not make people. People make culture": Butler at the level of practical pedagogy.

So my assessment: very convincing in core, requiring modification at the edges. Butler is best read with Hill Collins's matrix, Crenshaw's intersectionality and Harding's strong objectivity. Together they deliver a defensible contemporary feminism none alone can. (Word count: 720.)

The universal subject in the strong sense is gone.

Does feminism die with it? Three positions.

One: pure pluralism. No "feminism", only feminisms. Strength: descriptive accuracy. Weakness: political. If "woman" is wholly destabilised, the subject of any structural diagnosis disappears. Criado-Perez's claim that women are "47% more likely to be seriously injured" in a comparable crash presupposes a working category "women".

Two: tactical universalism. Retain "woman" strategically, knowing it is constructed. Butler holds something like this. Adichie's defence: dropping "feminist" for "human rights" "would be a way of pretending that it was not women who have, for centuries, been excluded."

Three: Harding's synthesis. "There is no single, ideal woman's life from which standpoint theories recommend that thought start. Instead, one must turn to all of the lives that are marginalized in different ways… The different feminisms inform each other" (Harding, 2004: 131). The unity is in the *practice*, not in a prior subject. This is the position I defend.

Five reasons feminism survives.

First, structural patterns persist and require naming. Criado-Perez: crash-test dummies leave women "47% more likely to be seriously injured"; office thermostats "five degrees too cold"; Apple Health launched without a period tracker. These facts hold for women as such across race and class.

Second, the political "we" can be operational without being metaphysical. Adichie's "I am a feminist" and "All of us, women and men, must do better" use the category as a tool. #MeToo, Black Lives Matter, post-Roe America and the Iran women-life-freedom protests all use the singular to coordinate plural standpoints.

Third, the analytic categories survive without the subject. Patriarchy, the public/private split (Hall, 1980; Walby, 1986), the matrix of domination, intersectionality, performativity, the personal is political, the reference man, the oppositional gaze: none requires a unified prior subject. The L10 vision material confirms this: Mulvey's male gaze (1975) and hooks's oppositional gaze (hooks, 1992: 116) name structural relations of looking that operate regardless of whether "woman" is metaphysically stable. The structures are there to be analysed; the subject is plural in practice.

Fourth, the descriptive plural strengthens the political singular. Hill Collins's "ideas produced by Black women that clarify a standpoint of and for Black women" (Hill Collins, 1986: 70) is a Black feminist standpoint *and* a contribution to feminism in the singular. The contrast between Lean In feminism and Black/intersectional feminism is the productive form of this dialectic.

Fifth, Butler's own continued use of "feminism" demonstrates the position's coherence. The most-cited destabiliser of the unified subject continues to write feminist theory under that name.

Concrete examples. Adichie mobilises "feminist" openly while attending to Igbo specificity. Her brother Kene is "the best feminist I know", illustrating Harding's point: "Men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135). The great-grandmother who "did not know that word *feminist*. But it doesn't mean she wasn't one" applies the strategic singular retrospectively.

A critic might object that strategic universalism is universalism in retreat. The reply: strategic is reflexive (held under interrogation), plural (accommodating different positions), and revisable. Universalism had none of these.

So: yes, feminism can survive, and is flourishing, without a universal subject in the metaphysical sense. The condition is Harding's synthesis: unity in practice rather than in subject, plurality of standpoints, strategic use of "woman" as a category whose construction is part of feminism's object. The death of the metaphysical universal subject is the maturation of feminism. (Word count: 720.)
