# L12. Culture Industry Reconsidered (Adorno, Frankfurt School, Marx, Benjamin)

---

## 1. Lecture summary

This lecture builds a genealogy of critical theories of culture from Marx through the Frankfurt School to Walter Benjamin. The lecturer is clear that Marx himself did not write systematically about culture (he was a journalist who needed the income, and his references to culture are scattered), but three Marxian texts supply the apparatus that the Frankfurt School later turns on twentieth-century mass entertainment. Early Marx in *The German Ideology* (1846) gives us ruling-class ideology and the means of mental production. The 1859 *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* gives us base and superstructure. Late Marx in *Capital* (1867, 1885, 1894) gives us the critique of political economy, labour power, commodification, commodity fetishism, and the singer-as-wage-labourer who "produces capital directly" when she sings for an entrepreneur. Together these provide the tools for thinking about why music, film and television look the way they do under capitalism: because the class that owns the means of material production also owns the means of mental production, and what looks like culture is in fact a commodity whose real social relations have been inverted into a relation between things, a phantasmagoria. Engels in *Anti-Dühring* (1878) extended the base-and-superstructure framework to culture and media, and Althusser later softened the relation with the qualifying ideas of determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination.

Adorno and Horkheimer, founders (with Felix Weil) of the Institute for Social Research at Frankfurt in 1923, took these Marxian categories into a new historical situation: the failure of revolution in the West, the rise of Nazism and Stalinism, the success of US monopoly capitalism, and the emergence of radio, cinema and the recording industry. The lecturer stresses the school's three signature moves. First, the dialectic of enlightenment: reason that began as critique of myth has become a new myth of total administration, instrumental reason, technocratic control. Second, the culture industry, a phrase deliberately chosen over "mass culture" to refuse the idea that the products arise spontaneously from the people. Third, the diagnostic vocabulary of standardisation, pseudo-individuation and catharsis-that-keeps-them-in-line, illustrated in the slides by Taylor Swift's verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus structure in "Shake It Off," by Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" simulating subversion while reproducing pop's skeleton, and by Oasis's "Cigarettes and Alcohol" offering a working-class catharsis that disarms working-class anger.

Walter Benjamin provides the dialectical counterpoint. In "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) he argues that mass reproduction strips the aura (the here-and-now authority of the unique work tied to ritual and tradition) but in doing so democratises art, replaces cult value with exhibition value, and opens new modes of distracted, collective, critical reception (cinema, the dance hall, photography). Where Adorno saw mechanical reproduction as the engine of conformity, Benjamin saw it as a potentially emancipatory shift in the means of artistic production.

The lecture closes with standard criticisms of the Frankfurt School: it ignores agency, treats audiences as dupes, reads off effects from products, and lacks any empirical theory of reception. The current relevance (the monopolisation of record companies, streaming services, ticket agencies and retailers, the cross-ownership exemplified by Amazon as retailer-and-streamer, the "ordinary person can make it" ideology propagated by the star system, and the commodification even of independent and anti-capitalist music) is left for us to draw.

## 2. Key concepts and named theorists

- **Karl Marx**: early/late distinction; not really a theorist of culture but supplies the categories
- **The German Ideology (1846)**: ruling-class ideas, the means of mental production, camera obscura
- **Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859)**: base and superstructure
- **Capital (1867, 1885, 1894)**: labour power, the singer as productive worker, commodity fetishism
- **Commodity fetishism**: social relations between people appearing as "phantasmagorical" relations between things
- **The means of mental production**: schools, publishers, cinemas, record labels, streaming platforms
- **Camera obscura ideology**: class relations appear upside-down, misrepresented in interests of dominant class
- **Friedrich Engels / Anti-Dühring**: extended base/superstructure to culture and media
- **Louis Althusser**: determination in the last instance, relative autonomy, overdetermination
- **The Frankfurt School / Institute for Social Research**: founded 1923; Geneva 1933; New York 1935; Felix Weil, Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Walter Benjamin, Herbert Marcuse, Erich Fromm, Leo Lowenthal
- **Critical theory**: self-reflexive Marxism incorporating psychoanalysis, dialectic, immanent critique
- **The dialectic of enlightenment**: reason vs myth synthesises into new myth of total administration
- **Instrumental reason**: reason reduced to means-ends calculation, domination of nature and people
- **The totally administered society**: Adorno's diagnosis of late capitalism, fascism, Stalinism, state capitalism
- **The culture industry**: Adorno and Horkheimer's term, replaced "mass culture" to refuse the spontaneity myth
- **Anti-enlightenment / mass deception / fettering consciousness**: the total effect of the industry
- **Standardisation**: verse-chorus-verse, "the same skeleton," genre formulas, the Western
- **Pseudo-individuation** (or pseudo-individuality): illusion of choice / individuality disguising sameness; the necessary correlate of standardisation
- **Catharsis for the masses**: emotional release that "keeps them in line"
- **Pre-digested listening**: "the composition hears for the listener"
- **The star system**: borrowed from individualistic art, propagates the "ordinary person can make it" myth
- **Walter Benjamin**: *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1936)
- **The aura**: "the presence of that which is not present"; here-and-now uniqueness of the ritual work of art
- **Mechanical reproduction**: photography, film, the gramophone; debases aura, democratises art
- **Cult value vs exhibition value**: art for ritual contemplation vs art for public display and circulation
- **Distracted reception**: collective, critical mode of cinema audience, contrasted with absorbed contemplation
- **The subversive truth of art**: art "anticipates the good life," holds open a non-existent alternative
- **Frankfurt School criticisms**: ignores agency, reads off effects from products, no reception theory

## 4. Connections to other lectures

- **Bourdieu (taste, cultural capital):** Bourdieu's habitus and the social construction of taste maps onto pseudo-individuation: the "choice" between sweet and swing that Adorno mocks is exactly what Bourdieu shows as class-coded distinction. Both reject the liberal idea of free aesthetic choice.
- **Stuart Hall, British Cultural Studies (encoding/decoding, hegemony):** Hall's reception theory is the major answer to the Frankfurt School's "no theory of reception" gap. Hall's negotiated and oppositional readings rescue agency from Adorno's "object of calculation," and his Gramscian reading of hegemony as contested terrain provides a less monolithic alternative to the totally administered society.
- **Raymond Williams ("culture is ordinary"):** Williams's insistence that culture is a "whole way of life" lived from below contradicts Adorno's tendency to read culture only as administered from above, while sharing Adorno's resistance to economic determinism.
- **Barthes (structuralism, mythologies):** Barthes' "myth as the depoliticised speech of the bourgeoisie" is the structural-semiotic version of Marx's camera-obscura ideology and of Adorno's "eternal sameness."
- **Foucault (power/knowledge, the disciplinary subject):** Foucault's productive power offers an alternative to the Frankfurt School's still-rather-Marxist account of domination from above; the "totally administered society" is Adorno's name for what Foucault would re-describe as a disciplinary assemblage.
- **Madonna and feminist theory:** Madonna's "Material Girl" (used in the slides as a camera-obscura case) raises the question of whether female pop stars are pure commodity-fetishists or perform a parody that troubles patriarchal norms, a productive tension with Adorno that connects to Butler on gender performativity.
- **Jameson (postmodernism):** Fredric Jameson's "cultural logic of late capitalism" is a direct descendant of Adorno; pastiche, depthlessness, and the collapse of utopian trajectories are postmodern reformulations of standardisation and pseudo-individuation.
- **Paul Gilroy (*The Black Atlantic*):** Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the perspective of enslaved Africans pushes back against Adorno's dismissal of jazz; the racial politics of Adorno's attack on swing is a serious problem.
- **Habermas (public sphere) and Fraser (counterpublics):** Habermas reads the same monopolisation that Adorno calls "culture industry" as the "refeudalisation" of the public sphere by commercial interests; Fraser's subaltern counterpublics suggest a counter-tendency Adorno underplays.

## 5. Why this lecture matters / debates

The culture industry thesis is the foundational sociological account of the mass media. Every later debate about media power, from Williams's "culture is ordinary" to Hall's encoding/decoding to Jameson's late capitalism, argues with it. Three live debates structure how to write about L12.

First, the agency debate. The Frankfurt School is repeatedly accused of "reading off" effects from products, of treating audiences as cultural dupes, and the lecture slides include this as one of the standard criticisms. Stuart Hall and British Cultural Studies rescue active interpretation through encoding/decoding. But Adorno's own claim that the system "incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105) anticipates the reply: even an audience that decodes oppositionally is, day after day, trained on standardised material. The honest essay holds both: agency is real, but so is the cumulative weight of the form.

Second, the high-vs-low art debate. Adorno's defence of "serious" music over popular music (Beethoven, Mozart, Haydn, Brahms against the standardised hit) looks elitist, Eurocentric and frequently racist (his attack on jazz, his sweeping claims about "masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism" in Adorno, 1941: 37). Benjamin offers the corrective: popular forms (cinema, the dance club, photography, the gramophone) can be sites of democratised, critical, distracted reception. The honest essay holds both: Adorno is right that the *industrial* form of culture standardises and reifies, but wrong to read this off from genre or "seriousness." The lecture slide on "music as working-class emancipation" pictures Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" and the Lancaster Music Co-op as examples of popular musical practice that is not industrial in Adorno's sense.

Third, the Marxism debate. Is the culture industry the superstructure determined by the base, or has it become base-like itself? Adorno's late writing pushes toward the latter: the industry "no longer even needs to directly pursue everywhere the profit interests from which it originated. These interests have become objectified in its ideology" (Adorno, 1991: 100). This is post-Marxian: ideology runs on autopilot, cultural production has been folded into the circulation of capital itself. The lecture slide on the monopolisation of record companies, streaming services, ticket agencies and retailers (Kobalt, BMG, Universal, Warner, Sony alongside Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify, Ticketmaster, See Tickets, with Amazon as cross-owner of retail and streaming) gives the contemporary referent.

The takeaway: L12 supplies the vocabulary (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, the culture industry, the aura, base/superstructure, commodity fetishism) that every other media-and-culture lecture will either deploy, modify or reject.

## 8. Full essays

### Essay 1. "The culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above" (Adorno). Discuss with reference to the contemporary monopolisation of recorded music and streaming.

I argue that Adorno's claim that "the culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above" (Adorno, 1991: 98) is intensified, not weakened, by the contemporary structure of recorded music, because the same monopoly concentration that he described in 1947 has now extended across record labels, streaming platforms, ticket agencies and retailers, with cross-owners such as Amazon operating across multiple layers simultaneously. The integration is more total, not less.

Adorno's argument has three steps. First, the masses are not the measure but the ideology of the culture industry: "The customer is not king, as the culture industry would have us believe, not its subject but its object" (Adorno, 1991: 99). The industry presumes a "given and unchangeable" mentality which it then "duplicates, reinforces and strengthens" (Adorno, 1991: 99). Second, cultural commodities are governed by the principle of their realisation as value, not by content: profit motive is "transferred naked onto cultural forms" (Adorno, 1991: 99). Third, what looks like progress, novelty and choice is "the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The integration is from above because the technical and administrative concentration of the means of mental production allows the industry to decide in advance what counts as music, film or news.

The lecture slide on monopolisation gives the contemporary picture. Recorded-music rights are concentrated in Sony, Universal and Warner alongside publishing administrators such as Kobalt and BMG. Streaming distribution is concentrated in Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud. Live performance is concentrated in Ticketmaster and See Tickets. Retail, both physical (HMV) and online, is concentrated, with Amazon notably operating as both retailer and streamer. Adorno's claim that the system is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98) is the slide's organising thesis.

Within this concentrated structure, the standardisation Adorno identified in 1941 continues. The Taylor Swift "Shake It Off" example cited in the slides exemplifies the verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton. Adorno's analysis is exact: "The whole structure of popular music is standardized, even where the attempt is made to circumvent standardization" (Adorno, 1941: 3). Charli XCX's "Von Dutch," also in the slides, performs the work of pseudo-individuation: stylistic edginess, the f-word in chart pop, an image as subversive pop star, while the verse-pre-chorus-chorus structure of conventional pop is reproduced with absolute fidelity. The "supposedly great personalities" Adorno names as the star system (Adorno, 1991: 101) are visible in the slides' "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" gallery.

The lecturer's example of Richard Branson, who set up Virgin Records, is the textbook case of the camera-obscura inversion of ideology. Branson appears wealthy because clever and entrepreneurial; the material basis of his wealth is the exploitation of musicians' labour over their working lives. "If in all ideology men and their circumstances appear upside-down as in a camera obscura, this phenomenon arises just as much from their historical life-process as the inversion of objects on the retina does from their physical life-process" (Marx, 1846). The Branson example is exactly what Marx described: a ruling-class figure whose riches appear as a property of his cleverness rather than as the product of musicians' labour.

The objection from Stuart Hall and British Cultural Studies is that audiences are not passive: they decode actively, sometimes oppositionally, and they appropriate cultural commodities for their own ends. This is right, and Adorno's worst writing on jazz reads off effects from products in the way the standard criticism in the lecture slides alleges. But Adorno's own reply is forceful: "it can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). Even an audience that decodes actively is, day after day, drilled by the standardised form.

There are countervailing tendencies. The slide on "music as working-class emancipation" pictures Rage Against the Machine (commodification within Sony), Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" (anarcho-punk distribution outside the major-label structure) and the Lancaster Music Co-op (worker-owned rehearsal and production space). These are real cracks in the integration-from-above, and Adorno's own theory leaves room for them: he distinguishes the culture industry from culture which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The cracks are real but small relative to the monopolised mainstream.

The deepest update to Adorno is that the integration no longer needs to be conscious. As Adorno himself foresaw, the culture industry's "interests have become objectified in its ideology and have even made themselves independent of the compulsion to sell the cultural commodities which must be swallowed anyway" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The record-label executive does not need to plot against autonomy; the standardised form does it automatically, and "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104).

I conclude that integration from above is the right phrase for what the monopolised music industry does, and Adorno's framework describes the structural concentration shown in the lecture slides quite accurately. The Frankfurt School's pessimism, often dismissed as 1940s elitism, turns out to describe contemporary cross-ownership and standardisation more sharply than its critics have allowed.

### Essay 2. To what extent does Marx's account of the means of mental production in *The German Ideology* anticipate Adorno's culture industry thesis?

I argue that Marx's account of the means of mental production in *The German Ideology* anticipates Adorno's culture industry thesis substantially but not completely: Marx supplies the political-economic skeleton (ownership of cultural production by the ruling class), but Adorno adds three things Marx did not have, namely the industrial form of cultural production, the psychoanalytic theory of the subject who consumes it, and the dialectical reversal whereby enlightenment becomes its opposite.

Marx's famous formulation reads: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, i.e. the class which is the ruling material force of society, is at the same time its ruling intellectual force. The class which has the means of production at its disposal, has control at the same time over the means of mental production" (Marx, 1846). This claim has two halves. The first half (ruling-class ideas) names the *content* of ideology in any epoch (feudal honour codes, bourgeois individualism). The second half (the means of mental production) names the *infrastructure*: who owns the schools, the printing presses, the libraries, the lecture halls. Marx is sketching a sociology of intellectual production that asks not only what the ruling ideas are but how they are reproduced through institutional control of the apparatus of dissemination.

This already anticipates the culture industry in three respects. First, it identifies the asymmetric ownership of cultural production as the engine of ideological domination. The lecture slide on monopolisation shows recorded-music rights concentrated in Sony, Universal and Warner alongside Kobalt and BMG; distribution concentrated in Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud; ticketing concentrated in Ticketmaster and See Tickets; retail concentrated in HMV and Amazon, with Amazon notably operating as both retailer and streamer. This is exactly the concentration of the means of mental production Marx described, now industrially scaled. Second, the formulation makes ideology *material*: it is not just ideas in heads but institutions, buildings, technologies. Third, it builds in the camera-obscura claim: "ideology represents class relations in an illusory form" that "concurs with the interests of the dominant class" (Marx, 1846). The inversion that makes Richard Branson appear rich because clever rather than rich because he exploited the labour of the musicians signed to Virgin is exactly the camera-obscura case the lecturer offers.

Where Marx stops short, Adorno extends. Marx is writing in 1846, before the industrial production of culture. He has the printing press but not the gramophone, the radio, the cinema. The slogan "culture industry" was, Adorno tells us, chosen "in order to exclude from the outset the interpretation agreeable to its advocates: that it is a matter of something like a culture that arises spontaneously from the masses themselves" (Adorno, 1991: 98). The industrial form changes the analysis. Standardisation is no longer just ideological repetition but technical seriality: the verse-chorus-bridge skeleton Adorno identifies in his 1941 essay "On Popular Music" (Adorno, 1941: 3), the Western Adorno cites as exemplary of "the standardization of the thing itself" (Adorno, 1991: 100), the thirty-two-bar chorus and the "characters" (mother songs, home songs, nonsense songs, laments for a lost girl) that Adorno enumerates (Adorno, 1941: 3). Pseudo-individuation, "endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice... on the basis of standardization itself" (Adorno, 1941: 23), is Adorno's name for the gap between Marx's general claim about ruling ideas and the specific way industrial culture markets sameness as difference.

Adorno's second extension is psychoanalytic. Marx's account is sociological: the worker is subject to ruling ideas because she lacks the means of mental production. Adorno, drawing on Freud via Erich Fromm and the Frankfurt School's mass-psychology programme, asks why workers actively desire the culture they are sold. His answer in "On Popular Music" is that "their leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" (Adorno, 1941: 32). Standardised entertainment fits the standardised worker; the rationalised office or factory shift produces the appetite for the thirty-two-bar chorus. Marx has alienation but not yet this loop, in which alienation is consumed as relief from alienation.

Adorno's third extension is the dialectic. Marx is, broadly, a theorist of progress: the productive forces will burst the relations of production, capitalism is its own gravedigger ("Capitalism creates its own gravediggers", as the lecturer puts it). Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1947) reads twentieth-century experience as the inversion of this thesis: reason, science and technical mastery (the enlightenment programme) have synthesised into new forms of domination (Nazism, Stalinism, state capitalism, the culture industry). "The total effect of the culture industry is one of anti-enlightenment... it becomes mass deception and is turned into a means for fettering consciousness" (Adorno and Horkheimer, 1947). This pessimism is genuinely post-Marxian, and it has no precise equivalent in Marx's own writing.

A defender of Marx will reply that the camera-obscura passage and the analysis of commodity fetishism in *Capital*, "a particular social relation between men themselves which in their eyes assumes a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things" (Marx, 1867), already contain the seeds of Adorno's analysis of pseudo-individuation and reification. This is fair. The 2025 UK top-ten chart pictured in the slides (Alex Warren's "Ordinary," Gracie Abrams's "That's So True," Lola Young's "Messy," Chappell Roan's "Pink Pony Club," Billie Eilish's "Birds of a Feather") presents as a list of distinct aesthetic objects whose phantasmagorical thing-relation conceals an enormous social relation of labour: songwriters, producers, mixers, label staff, distribution engineers, marketing teams. This can be read using Marx's tools alone. Adorno's contribution is to sharpen and update, not to invent.

I conclude that the anticipation is substantial. Marx supplies the analytic skeleton (means of mental production, base/superstructure, commodity fetishism); Adorno adds industrial form, psychoanalytic subject, and the dialectical reversal. The honest reading is that one cannot do the Frankfurt School without Marx, and one cannot do contemporary cultural studies without both.

### Essay 3. Critically evaluate the claim that standardisation and pseudo-individuation remain the defining features of twenty-first-century popular music.

I argue that standardisation and pseudo-individuation remain the defining features of twenty-first-century popular music, that the structural concentration documented in the lecture slides has intensified rather than undone the diagnosis, and that the surviving counter-currents pictured in the same slides (Crass, the Lancaster Music Co-op) operate at a scale too small to displace the dominant industrial form.

Adorno's 1941 thesis in "On Popular Music" is uncompromising: "The whole structure of popular music is standardized, even where the attempt is made to circumvent standardization" (Adorno, 1941: 3). The thirty-two-bar chorus, the one-octave-and-a-note range, the rigid sequence of types (mother songs, home songs, nonsense or "novelty" songs, pseudo-nursery rhymes, laments for a lost girl) are the skeleton (Adorno, 1941: 3). Above the skeleton sit pseudo-individuated "effects": breaks, blue chords, dirty notes (Adorno, 1941: 4). "Pseudo-individualization... endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice or open market on the basis of standardization itself" keeps the listener "in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them, or 'pre-digested'" (Adorno, 1941: 23).

Taylor Swift's "Shake It Off" (2014), used in the lecture slides as the textbook case of standardisation, exhibits the canonical structure: Verse-Chorus-Verse-Chorus-Bridge-Chorus. The chorus repeats the title hook with the same trochaic stress on every iteration. The bridge supplies pseudo-individuation: a new lyrical perspective ("My ex-man brought his new girlfriend") and a different rhythmic feel, before the chorus reasserts the skeleton. This is exactly what Adorno predicts: "the hit will lead back to the same familiar experience, and nothing fundamentally novel will be introduced" (Adorno, 1941: 3).

The slides' second example, Charli XCX's "Von Dutch," shows pseudo-individuation in its sharpened contemporary form. Where 1941 jazz disguised standardisation with dirty notes, "Von Dutch" disguises it with attitude: an electroclash style blending 1980s synthpop with contemporary pop, the repeated f-word in chart pop, an image as subversive pop star, a video featuring stylised violence. The slide notes that the track "subverts the conventional pop-song but reproduces its structure." Adorno's own example of pseudo-individuation, sweet versus swing or Benny Goodman versus Guy Lombardo (Adorno, 1941: 26), is the structural analogue: apparently distinctive types whose underlying material is the same.

The structural intensification can be read directly off the slide on monopolisation. Recorded music is concentrated in a small number of majors (Sony, Universal, Warner) and publishing administrators (Kobalt, BMG). Distribution is concentrated in a small number of streaming services (Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal, SoundCloud). Ticketing is concentrated in Ticketmaster and See Tickets. Amazon operates across retail and streaming simultaneously. The same slide on the "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" alongside the YouTube valuation chart (YouTube at 183bn USD, ahead of Netflix at 98bn) shows that platform concentration extends to video too. Adorno's claim that the system is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98) is precisely the structural picture.

The catharsis function Adorno identifies fits the slide's Oasis example. "Cigarettes and Alcohol" (1994) addresses the alienation of work ("Is it worth the aggravation / To find yourself a job when there's nothing worth working for?"), offers the cathartic resolution ("you might as well do the white line"), and disarms the underlying anger by absorbing it into a singable refrain. Adorno's diagnosis is exact: "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43). The class anger that might have powered industrial action is digested by the chorus and discharged on the dance floor.

The "ordinary person can make it" ideology Adorno identifies in the star system (Adorno, 1991: 101) is propagated through the star-making machinery shown in the slides, including the "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" gallery. The lecture's slide on "materialist ideology" makes the operation explicit: musicians are wage-labourers whose labour produces commodities and capital; the ideological supplement is "Record companies make 'stars'" and "You can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money." The ideology survives even as the venues for stardom shift.

The honest objection from Walter Benjamin and from the slide on "music as working-class emancipation" is that mechanical reproduction also enables democratised production. Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand," pictured in the slides, was produced on the band's own anarchist-punk label and distributed outside the major-label system. Rage Against the Machine, also pictured, is the case the lecturer identifies as commodification: anti-capitalist content sold through Sony's commercial machinery. The Lancaster Music Co-op, the third image in the same slide, is a worker-owned space where the means of musical production are collectively held. These are real counter-currents in Adorno's own terms: he distinguishes the culture industry from culture which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100).

The scale answer is decisive. The concentration documented in the monopolisation slide is the structural fact; the worker-owned co-operative is a marginal exception. Adorno's diagnosis, "the customer is not king... not its subject but its object" (Adorno, 1991: 99), describes the dominant industrial form.

I conclude that standardisation and pseudo-individuation are as defining now as they were in 1941, that the lecture slides' own examples ("Shake It Off," "Von Dutch," the monopolisation chart, the Oasis catharsis case) carry the argument, and that the counter-current of independent and co-operative music remains real but marginal.

### Essay 4. "Mechanical reproduction shatters the aura, but the culture industry resurrects it as a foggy mist." Discuss Benjamin and Adorno on the politics of reproducibility.

I argue that Benjamin and Adorno disagree on the political valence of mechanical reproduction not because they disagree about its mechanism but because they disagree about its historical moment: Benjamin writes in 1935 looking at the revolutionary potential of cinema for an emergent working class, Adorno writes after the rise of Nazism and the success of Hollywood looking at how the same techniques were used to manufacture consent. Both diagnoses are correct in their own frame, and the contemporary monopolisation of cultural distribution leans Adornian without refuting Benjamin's hope.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) makes the bold claim that the aura, what the lecture slides describe as the authority of the original work of art tied to "historical ritual and tradition," withers under mass reproduction. Photography, film, the gramophone produce endless copies and dissolve the "presence of that which is not present" (Benjamin, 1935). The slide on Benjamin summarises the consequence: "Society would be freer to organise and control themselves in their reception, i.e. no longer subject to the authority (what he called the aura) of original works of art." The dissolution has two sides. On the loss side, art loses its cult value: the work as object of ritual pilgrimage gives way to the mass-distributed copy. On the gain side, art gains exhibition value: it circulates, it reaches mass audiences, it becomes available for political mobilisation. Crucially, Benjamin reads this gain as democratic. The slide gives his two paradigm examples: "through popular cinema people take the perspective of the camera. Through the consumption of music in clubs, people dance to music." The collective, distracted mode of cinematic and dance-hall reception is, for Benjamin, the new mode of critical engagement, replacing the silent contemplation of bourgeois aesthetics.

Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" is sharp: "Adopting Benjamin's designation of the traditional work of art by the concept of aura, the presence of that which is not present, the culture industry is defined by the fact that it does not strictly counterpose another principle to that of aura, but rather by the fact that it conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist. By this means the culture industry betrays its own ideological abuses" (Adorno, 1991: 102). The culture industry does not destroy aura; it manufactures it. The star system "borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101) is the resurrection of aura around the celebrity. The slides' Madonna "Material Girl" example shows this directly: the song is a mass-reproduced commodity (the recording, the music video, the album sleeve are all copies), and yet the figure of Madonna is wreathed in the foggy-mist aura of an individual personality. The "supposedly great personalities and heart-throbs" Adorno names (Adorno, 1991: 101) are the resurrection of aura under reproduction.

Adorno's "On Popular Music" sharpens the case. He notes that the listener's "moment of recognition is that of effortless sensation" and that the industry must "arouse attention by means of ever-new products, but this attention spells their doom. If no attention is given to the song, it cannot be sold; if attention is paid to it, there is always the possibility that people will no longer accept it" (Adorno, 1941: 33-34). The foggy-mist aura solves this: it gives the mechanically reproduced product enough apparent uniqueness to draw attention while remaining structurally interchangeable. The Charli XCX "Von Dutch" example in the slides is exactly this operation: a manufactured aura of subversion around a structurally conventional pop track.

The disagreement is therefore not about the mechanism but about which moment of the dialectic to emphasise. Benjamin sees the historical opening: the shift in the means of artistic production from ritual to politics. Adorno sees the historical closing: the recapture of that opening by monopoly capital. Adorno is explicit about this recapture: the culture industry is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98). The monopolisation slide (Sony, Universal, Warner, Kobalt, BMG; Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify; Ticketmaster, See Tickets; Amazon as cross-owner) is the institutional referent.

A further wrinkle within Benjamin's own framework: his politics depended on the cinema being a *collective* mode of reception. The slide stresses this: people in the cinema "take the perspective of the camera"; people in the club "dance to music." Bodies are in shared space and rhythm. The atomised consumption of recorded music through headphones erodes this collective condition. Adorno's worry that the industry produces "objects of calculation; an appendage of the machinery" (Adorno, 1991: 99) is the diagnosis of this erosion.

Counter to the Adornian closure, the slide on "music as working-class emancipation" pictures Crass's anarcho-punk distribution outside the major-label system and the Lancaster Music Co-op as a worker-owned space. These are Benjaminian moments within an otherwise Adornian field: the technology of mechanical reproduction (the record, the cassette, the digital file) is used outside monopoly distribution to enable democratised, distracted, collective reception. Benjamin's hope is preserved here at small scale.

The synthesis is that the politics of reproducibility is not fixed by the technology but by the ownership of the means of mental production. The same technology, in monopoly hands, conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist (Adorno's diagnosis); in worker-owned or independent hands, it democratises (Benjamin's hope). Both diagnoses are correct, conditional on ownership.

I conclude that the question's claim is right: the culture industry resurrects aura as a foggy mist around the star and the manufactured product. But Benjamin's hope is not refuted, only conditional on the political-economic question of who owns the means of mental production, which the lecture slides answer with a picture of concentration.

### Essay 5. Is the Frankfurt School's account of mass culture vindicated or refuted by contemporary cross-ownership in the cultural industries?

I argue that the Frankfurt School's account is vindicated rather than refuted by the contemporary structure of cross-ownership documented in the lecture slides, because the same monopoly concentration that Adorno identified in 1947 has extended from broadcasting and Hollywood to span recorded-music rights, streaming distribution, ticketing and retail simultaneously, with single owners operating across multiple layers.

The vindication runs along four lines. First, the structural diagnosis. Adorno argued that the culture industry is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98). The lecture slide on monopolisation makes this concrete. Recorded-music rights are held by Sony, Universal and Warner alongside the publishing administrators Kobalt and BMG. Streaming distribution is held by Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud. Ticketing is held by Ticketmaster and See Tickets. Retail, both bricks-and-mortar (HMV) and online (Amazon), is concentrated. Most strikingly, Amazon operates simultaneously as retailer and streamer, the textbook case of cross-ownership Adorno's framework predicts.

Second, standardisation. Adorno's claim that "what parades as progress in the culture industry, as the incessantly new which it offers up, remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100) describes the verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton of Taylor Swift's "Shake It Off" pictured in the slides, and the standardised types Adorno enumerated in 1941: "mother songs, home songs, nonsense or 'novelty' songs, pseudo-nursery rhymes, laments for a lost girl" (Adorno, 1941: 3). The forms have endured.

Third, pseudo-individuation. Adorno's claim that pseudo-individuation "keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them" (Adorno, 1941: 23) is the lecture's analysis of "Von Dutch": stylised individuality (the f-word, electroclash, an image of subversion) on top of a conventional pop skeleton. The slide caption is exact: "Subverts the conventional pop-song but reproduces its structure."

Fourth, the star system. Adorno's claim that "its ideology above all makes use of the star system, borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101) is illustrated in the slides by the "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" chart, by the "Highest Paid Female Rappers" image, and by the materialist-ideology slide that names "Record companies make 'stars'" and "You can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money" as the central ideological supplements to the labour relation.

Counter-currents are visible in the same slides. The "music as working-class emancipation" slide pictures Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" (anarcho-punk distribution outside the major-label system) and the Lancaster Music Co-op (worker-owned production space). Rage Against the Machine, pictured alongside, is the case the lecturer identifies as commodification: anti-capitalist content sold by a major label. The slide's own caption that "Independent, radical, even anti-capitalist music can be sold as a commodity by capitalists" is Adornian: capture by the form is the rule.

The transformations are real but do not refute Adorno. The locus of decision has partially shifted from named executives to administrative procedures. This makes Adorno's "from above" more impersonal but not less effective. Adorno's late writing anticipates this depersonalisation: the industry's "interests have become objectified in its ideology and have even made themselves independent of the compulsion to sell the cultural commodities which must be swallowed anyway" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The objectification of the profit motive in the form of the cultural commodity is the same operation Adorno described.

The Stuart Hall objection (audiences are active, encode and decode in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways) remains forceful and is registered in the lecture slides among the standard criticisms of the Frankfurt School. But Adorno's reply is the same as ever: "it can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The active audience still listens to standardised material every day.

The Foucauldian alternative, that power is productive rather than merely repressive, complements rather than displaces Adorno: the industry produces a particular kind of consumer ("an appendage of the machinery," Adorno, 1991: 99) rather than merely manipulating a pre-formed one. Adorno's "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104) is consistent with the production of subjects, not merely with their suppression.

I conclude that the structural concentration documented in the slides vindicates rather than refutes the Frankfurt School. The honest critical position is neither dismissal of Adorno as elitist nor uncritical revival; it is to combine his structural diagnosis with the active-audience corrective from British Cultural Studies, while keeping his picture of monopoly concentration as the institutional baseline.

### Essay 6. Discuss the strengths and limits of base-and-superstructure as a model for thinking about contemporary cultural production.

I argue that base-and-superstructure remains an indispensable analytical model for contemporary cultural production but only in the qualified form Engels and Althusser gave it: as a model of determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination, not as a one-way causal arrow. The model's strength is that it forces us to ask who owns the means of cultural production; its limit is that it can read culture as mere reflection if applied crudely.

Marx's formulation in the *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) is foundational: "The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation on which there arises a legal and political superstructure and to which there correspond definite forms of social consciousness" (Marx, 1859). The base is the relations of production (capital and labour, ownership of the means of production); the superstructure is law, politics, culture and "social consciousness." The lecture's base-and-superstructure diagram makes the architecture explicit: economic relations and means of production at the base; education, religion, politics, culture and media as the ideological superstructure; the arrows labelled "shapes" (base to superstructure) and "maintains" (superstructure to base).

The strength of the model is that it makes ownership analytically primary. To understand recorded music, the lecture asks who owns the labels (Sony, Universal, Warner alongside Kobalt and BMG), the streaming platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, SoundCloud), the ticketing operations (Ticketmaster, See Tickets), and the retail layer (HMV, Amazon as cross-owner of both retail and streaming). This is exactly the concentration of the means of mental production Marx described, now industrially scaled.

The materialist-ideology slide gives the contemporary application directly. On the materialist side: "Musicians hold labour power"; "Labour power in exchange for a wage (reproduction)"; "Produce a commodity (music)"; "M – C – C' – M' (circulation)"; "Capitalist invests in fixed capital (studios / production / distribution)." On the ideological side: "Record companies make 'stars'"; "You can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money"; "Music is special"; "Music is precious"; "Music is an industry, technological progress is good for the music industry." The base shapes the superstructure that legitimates it.

The camera-obscura case sharpens the operation. The lecturer's Richard Branson example: Branson appears wealthy and successful because clever and talented; the material basis of his wealth is the exploitation of the labour of musicians signed to Virgin over their working lives. "If in all ideology men and their circumstances appear upside-down as in a camera obscura, this phenomenon arises just as much from their historical life-process as the inversion of objects on the retina does from their physical life-process" (Marx, 1846). Ideology represents class relations in an illusory form that "concurs with the interests of the dominant class."

The limit of the crude reading is that cultural artefacts are not reducible to their economic base. The slide's "music as working-class emancipation" page pictures three different cases that share the same capitalist base but do different cultural work: Rage Against the Machine (commodified anti-capitalism via Sony), Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" (anarcho-punk distribution outside the major label system), and the Lancaster Music Co-op (worker-owned production space). Engels in *Anti-Dühring* (1878) recognised this: the economic is determining only in the last instance; the superstructure has its own dynamics. Althusser systematised this with the qualifying ideas of determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination.

The contemporary cross-ownership of cultural industries adds a complicating layer. Amazon as both retailer and streamer is at once base-level (it owns infrastructure, employs labour, generates surplus) and superstructural (its store-front and recommendation surfaces shape what listeners hear and what films get prioritised). The same monopoly company occupies multiple positions in the base-and-superstructure diagram simultaneously. This is not a refutation of Marx but a vindication: Engels' extension of the base-and-superstructure framework "to include forms of culture, and the media" in *Anti-Dühring* anticipated exactly this scenario.

Stuart Hall pushed further: encoding and decoding are not symmetrical, and audiences make negotiated and oppositional readings that the encoder did not intend. The same monopoly-structured industry that produces standardised pop also produces the conditions for the anti-commercial counter-currents pictured in the emancipation slide. The base does not predict these outcomes; it constrains them. Saying that anti-capitalist music can still be sold as a commodity (the Rage Against the Machine case) is to acknowledge both the determining power of the base and the relative autonomy of the cultural form.

The most honest contemporary use of base-and-superstructure is therefore as a critical heuristic: always ask who owns the means of mental production, but do not assume that ownership predicts content in a one-to-one way. The Rage Against the Machine record and the Crass record share a base; they do different cultural work. The Charli XCX track and the Crass record share a base; they do different cultural work. The base is necessary, not sufficient, for cultural analysis.

I conclude that base-and-superstructure, in its Engelsian-Althusserian qualified form, remains the indispensable starting point for any sociology of contemporary cultural production. Crude versions are vulnerable to the standard objections; refined versions remain analytically essential. The lecturer's slide is right: the base shapes, the superstructure maintains, but the maintaining is itself a labour with its own contradictions.

### Essay 7. "Entertainment is the prolongation of work under late capitalism." Evaluate Adorno and Horkheimer's claim.

I argue that Adorno and Horkheimer's claim that entertainment is the prolongation of work under late capitalism is essentially correct as a description of the structural fit between standardised industrial labour and standardised industrial entertainment, that Adorno's own elaboration in "On Popular Music" gives the mechanism, and that the examples in the lecture slides illustrate the thesis in contemporary form.

The original argument runs: under industrial capitalism, the worker is subject to rationalised, mechanised, alienating labour. In her free time she seeks escape. But the culture industry produces entertainment that is structurally similar to the work she escapes from: standardised, repetitive, requiring no concentration, "pre-digested" (Adorno, 1941: 16). Adorno puts the point sharply: "Popular music is for the masses a perpetual busman's holiday" (Adorno, 1941: 32). The entertainment relaxes by being patterned like the work; it offers no genuine break, only the reproduction of working-day rhythms in a slightly cushioned form.

The mechanism Adorno gives in "On Popular Music" is precise. Distraction "is bound to the present mode of production, to the rationalized and mechanized process of labor to which, directly or indirectly, masses are subject. This mode of production, which engenders fears and anxiety about unemployment, loss of income, war, has its 'nonproductive' correlate in entertainment; that is, relaxation which does not involve the effort of concentration at all" (Adorno, 1941: 30). The form fits the function: "Their spare time serves only to reproduce their working capacity. It is a means instead of an end... They want standardized goods and pseudo-individualization, because their leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" (Adorno, 1941: 32).

The Taylor Swift "Shake It Off" example in the slides shows the structural fit. The song's address ("I stay out too late / Got nothin' in my brain") frames the listener as a worker who needs unwinding; the standardised verse-chorus structure delivers the unwinding in a form that requires no cognitive effort, so that the listener is returned to work on Monday without having experienced anything that might challenge her workplace reality. The Charli XCX "Von Dutch" example does similar work in pseudo-individuated form: its surface stylisation gives the impression of difference while its structural fit with the working-week routine of digestible relaxation is undisturbed.

The catharsis function sharpens the diagnosis. The slides' Oasis example, "Cigarettes and Alcohol" (1994), explicitly addresses the alienation of work ("Is it worth the aggravation / To find yourself a job when there's nothing worth working for?") and supplies a cathartic resolution that defuses the political anger rather than mobilising it. Adorno's commentary is exact: "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43). The class anger Oasis articulates is, by being articulated through the standardised pop form, absorbed.

The star system contributes. Adorno's claim that the industry "propagates supposedly great personalities and operates with heart-throbs" (Adorno, 1991: 101) and the slide's "you can be a star" ideology supply the fantasy that escape from the wage relation is individually available, while the structural concentration documented in the monopolisation slide ensures that almost no one will actually escape. The fantasy is the prolongation of work in another sense: it disciplines the worker into accepting the present arrangement on the promise of personal advancement.

The "what about genuine leisure?" objection has force. People do play sport, garden, read, sing in choirs. Adorno's own writing acknowledges this with his distinction between "culture, in the true sense" which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100), and the culture industry which assimilates rather than protests. The choir and the garden are not the culture industry; the industrial hit is.

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences resignify entertainment for their own purposes. The football match is not just standardised affective release; it is community, identity, embodied collective experience. Adorno's remark in the slide that "sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection" is too strong: football fans do organise, do protest against owners, do build solidarities. The thesis needs qualification: entertainment is more often the prolongation of work than the genuine alternative to it, but the line is not fixed by genre, and the active appropriation of cultural material by audiences (as Hall would emphasise) is real.

I conclude that Adorno and Horkheimer's claim describes the structural fit between alienated labour and standardised entertainment with continuing accuracy. The slides' "Shake It Off," "Von Dutch" and "Cigarettes and Alcohol" examples all illustrate the prolongation: relaxation in a form that re-prepares the worker for Monday rather than enabling her to imagine an alternative. The objections from genuine leisure and active audience reception qualify the thesis but do not refute it.

### Essay 8. Does Walter Benjamin offer a more useful framework than Adorno for analysing the democratising potential of mechanically reproduced art?

I argue that Benjamin offers a more useful framework than Adorno for analysing the *productive* potential of mechanically reproduced art (what producers and audiences can do with cheap recording, cheap printing and direct distribution outside the major-label system) while Adorno remains the more useful framework for analysing the *distributive* layer where monopoly capital concentrates the means of mental production. The honest answer is that the two frameworks describe different layers of the same situation and we need both.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) makes three moves that the lecture slides emphasise. First, mechanical reproduction shatters the aura: the work of art is "disconnected from where the art was made (locale) and its cultural and physical context." Second, this shattering produces "new values / ways of seeing." Third, it democratises: "Society would be freer to organise and control themselves in their reception... no longer subject to the authority (what he called the aura) of original works of art." The two paradigm cases the slide names are cinema (people "take the perspective of the camera") and the club (people "dance to music"). Both are collective, distracted, embodied modes of reception that bourgeois contemplative aesthetics could not accommodate.

The lecturer's slide on the subversive truth of art elaborates the political dimension. Through art "one can conceive a world different from the life dominated by commodity production... Art provided a medium for critical thinking by upholding images of life which contradict the existent... Art anticipates the good life." Mechanical reproduction, by lowering the cost of access and by transferring art from cult contemplation to public exhibition, is the means by which this subversive function reaches the working class.

The lecture's "music as working-class emancipation" slide makes Benjamin's hope concrete. Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" is mechanically reproduced (as a record) but distributed outside the major-label system, sold cheaply, with profits returned to political causes; this is a Benjaminian use of mechanical reproduction. The Lancaster Music Co-op is a worker-owned space where the means of musical production are collectively held; this is Benjaminian at the level of ownership. The slide's accompanying images of the cassette, the mp3 logo, the home recording software and the DJ controller, sitting beside the eighteenth-century court painting, make the historical shift visible: from court patronage to home production.

But Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" remains forceful at the level of distribution. The same monopoly capital that the lecture slide on Sony, Universal, Warner, Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Ticketmaster, See Tickets and HMV depicts is the recapture of Benjamin's democratised production. Adorno's reply is precise: "the culture industry... does not strictly counterpose another principle to that of aura, but rather... conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist" (Adorno, 1991: 102). The star system "borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101) is the manufactured aura around the mass-reproduced personality. The slides' Madonna "Material Girl" example and the "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" gallery both show foggy-mist aura around mechanically reproduced figures.

The cleanest way to combine the two is to distinguish layers. The *production layer* is Benjaminian where it remains outside monopoly capital: home recording, anarcho-punk labels (Crass), worker-owned spaces (the Lancaster Music Co-op). The *distribution layer* is Adornian where monopoly capital prevails: the cross-owned majors and streamers, the concentration of ticketing in Ticketmaster and See Tickets. The Rage Against the Machine case in the lecture slides is the lecturer's point of clearest pedagogical use of this tension: anti-capitalist content (genuinely Benjaminian in intent) distributed by Sony (Adornian capture in practice).

Two further considerations matter. First, Benjamin's optimism depended on the *collective* mode of cinema reception (bodies in shared space, the working class assembling). The atomised consumption of recorded music through headphones erodes this collective condition. Adorno's "the customer is not king, as the culture industry would have us believe, not its subject but its object" (Adorno, 1991: 99) names the diagnosis of this erosion: the audience is an "object of calculation; an appendage of the machinery." Second, Benjamin's analysis has an under-specified theory of ownership. He notices the shift in the means of artistic production but does not develop, as Adorno does and as the monopolisation slide makes vivid, the analysis of monopoly capital's recapture of those means. This is the limit of pure Benjamin: he gives the productive possibilities but not the political-economic constraints. Combining him with Marx, Engels and Adorno gives the constraints.

I conclude that Benjamin is more useful for analysing the productive layer of mechanically reproduced art (cheap tools, democratised production, collective making), Adorno more useful for the distributive layer (concentrated monopoly capital, manufactured aura, the star system). The contemporary critical task is to deploy both. The aura is shattered in Benjamin's sense (the unique object has been replaced by the copiable record) and reconstructed in Adorno's sense (the foggy mist around the manufactured star).

### Essay 9. "Commodity fetishism is the foundational concept of any critical sociology of culture." Discuss.

I argue that commodity fetishism is one of two foundational concepts (alongside the ruling-class control of the means of mental production) for any critical sociology of culture, and that its strength is its ability to explain why social relations between people appear, in capitalist cultures, as relations between things. The lecture slides apply the concept directly to the contemporary chart, and Adorno's reformulation in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" extends it to the form of the cultural commodity itself.

Marx's definition in *Capital Volume 1*, quoted in the lecture slide, is the canonical passage: "The relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation of objects which exists outside them... It is a particular social relation between men themselves which in their eyes assumes a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things... This is what I call fetishism; it attaches itself to the products of labour as soon as they are produced as commodities, and it is therefore inseparable from the production of commodities" (Marx, 1867). Three elements: real social relations exist between producers; these relations are misrecognised as properties of the commodity itself; the misrecognition is inseparable from commodity production. The commodity acquires a phantasmagorical (dreamlike, magical) quality that obscures the labour that produced it.

For cultural analysis the concept is indispensable because culture is, in capitalism, principally produced as commodities. The 2025 top-ten UK chart pictured beside the slide's commodity-fetishism quotation (Alex Warren's "Ordinary," Gracie Abrams's "That's So True," Lola Young's "Messy," Chappell Roan's "Pink Pony Club," Billie Eilish's "Birds of a Feather," Ravyn Lenae's "Love Me Not," Sombr's "back to friends," Gigi Perez's "Sailor Song," Lady Gaga and Bruno Mars's "Die With A Smile," Chappell Roan's "Good Luck, Babe!") appears as a list of self-standing creative achievements. The labour behind each track (songwriters, session musicians, producers, mixers, mastering engineers, music-video crews, marketing teams, distribution staff, retail and streaming workers) is invisible. The track stands as a thing. This is fetishism in Marx's exact sense, and the lecturer's juxtaposition of the chart with Marx's quotation makes the analytical point pictorially.

The lecturer's example of Richard Branson is the textbook teaching case of the camera-obscura inversion that underlies the fetish. Branson appears wealthy because clever, entrepreneurial, risk-taking. The actual material basis of his wealth is, in the lecturer's words, "the exploitation of labour, of artists and musicians who make the music that he sells, over their historical (entire) lives." Fetishism is the operation by which the social relation (Branson capitalises on signed labour) appears as a property of the thing (Branson has a magical talent for business). "This inversion concurs with the interests of the dominant class" (Marx, 1846).

The "Musicians as wage labourers" slide reads the same operation through Marx's *Capital*: "A singer who sings like a bird is an unproductive worker. If she sells her song for money, she is to that extent a wage-labourer or merchant. But if the same singer is engaged by an entrepreneur who makes her sing to make money, then she becomes a productive worker, since she produces capital directly" (Marx, 1867). The fetish is what conceals this wage-labour relation from the listener: the song appears as a thing one can possess (a stream, a download), not as a stage in the circulation M – C – C' – M' that the lecturer's "materialist ideology" slide diagrams.

Adorno's reformulation in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" deepens the fetishism analysis. Where Marx identifies fetishism as a property of the commodity, Adorno identifies its intensification in the form of the cultural commodity: "Cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The 1867 fetishised commodity was a coat or a quantity of linen that obscured the labour of weavers and tailors. The 2026 fetishised cultural commodity is a Charli XCX track or a Taylor Swift hit whose standardised form, manufactured aura and ubiquitous promotion obscure both the labour of production and the standardised character of the product itself. Adorno's claim that "what parades as progress in the culture industry, as the incessantly new which it offers up, remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100) is the form the fetishism takes in industrial culture.

The objection to Marx is that fetishism, taken alone, makes audiences look passive. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding shows that audiences can read against the grain, denaturalise the fetish, recognise the labour. The objection has force: fetishism is a tendency, not a guarantee, and the lecture's "music as working-class emancipation" slide pictures real practices (the anarcho-punk distribution of Crass, the worker-owned Lancaster Music Co-op) that re-foreground labour by changing the conditions of production. But these are minority practices in the structural picture the monopolisation slide gives.

I conclude that commodity fetishism is foundational, alongside the means of mental production, for any critical sociology of culture. Marx supplies the concept; the lecture slide's juxtaposition with the 2025 chart shows its contemporary application; Adorno deepens it into reification of the cultural form itself ("commodities through and through"); Stuart Hall supplements it with reception theory. Without it, cultural analysis loses the central insight that the appearance of culture under capitalism is structurally misleading about its production.

### Essay 10. Compare Adorno's analysis of the star system with the contemporary role of the music industry's "supposedly great personalities."

I argue that the contemporary music industry's parade of "supposedly great personalities," as visualised in the lecture slide's "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" gallery, is the maturation, not the supersession, of the star system Adorno analysed. The functions Adorno identified (manufactured individuality compensating for standardised content, propagation of the ideology that the ordinary person can make it) survive intact; the structural concentration documented in the monopolisation slide tightens rather than loosens the system.

Adorno's analysis runs: "Its ideology above all makes use of the star system, borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation. The more dehumanized its methods of operation and content, the more diligently and successfully the culture industry propagates supposedly great personalities and operates with heart-throbs" (Adorno, 1991: 101). The star system performs two functions. First, it gives the standardised, industrially produced cultural commodity an "individual air"; the aura around the star compensates for the absence of aura in the product. Adorno's claim that "each product affects an individual air; individuality itself serves to reinforce ideology, in so far as the illusion is conjured up that the completely reified and mediated is a sanctuary from immediacy and life" (Adorno, 1991: 101) names the operation. Second, the system propagates the ideology that the ordinary person can make it.

The lecturer's "materialist ideology" slide makes the second function explicit, listing under ideology: "Record companies make 'stars'" and "You can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money." Beside this sits the materialist side: musicians as wage-labourers exchanging labour power for a wage, producing commodities, completing the circulation M – C – C' – M' for capitalists who invest in the fixed capital of studios, production and distribution. The star ideology hides this wage relation behind the figure of the individual personality.

Adorno's own examples in "On Popular Music" sharpen the point. He notes that the listener "is quickly able to distinguish the types of music and even the performing band, this in spite of the fundamental identity of the material and the great similarity of the presentations apart from their emphasized distinguishing trademarks. This labeling technique, as regards type of music and band, is pseudo-individualization" (Adorno, 1941: 26). His paradigm cases were sweet versus swing, Benny Goodman versus Guy Lombardo. The structure is the same in the contemporary chart pictured beside the commodity-fetishism slide: ten different "supposedly great personalities" (Alex Warren, Gracie Abrams, Lola Young, Chappell Roan, Billie Eilish, Ravyn Lenae, Sombr, Gigi Perez, Lady Gaga and Bruno Mars, and Chappell Roan again) presenting as distinct creative identities while the underlying material is industrially standardised.

The "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" gallery on the slide alongside the YouTube valuation chart (YouTube at 183bn USD, ahead of Netflix at 98bn) shows that the star-making machinery extends across video as well as audio, and that the same monopoly concentration that the slide identifies in record labels (Sony, Universal, Warner) extends to the platforms that host star-making. The "Highest Paid Female Rappers" image on the slides is a further instance: the apparent celebration of individual personalities is a function of the same labelling technique Adorno named.

The "ordinary person can make it" claim cuts in both directions. Adorno's diagnosis is that it functions ideologically to legitimate a system that mostly produces precarity: the few visible winners (the Highest Paid Female Rappers, the 25 Biggest Artists) underwrite the many invisible losers (the wage-labouring musicians of the lecturer's slide who never become stars). The structural rate of success is low, the ideological function is high.

The classical Hollywood example Adorno used drew on the studio system: studios owned contracts and manufactured personae. The contemporary structure is functionally identical at the level of the major labels (Sony, Universal, Warner) and the publishing administrators (Kobalt, BMG) shown in the monopolisation slide. The lecture's Richard Branson example is the classical case sharpened: Branson is the star-maker who appears in the camera-obscura as star himself, while the labour of the musicians signed to Virgin disappears from view.

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences are not duped: they identify with stars actively, they form fan communities, they negotiate the meaning of the star's persona. This has force. Fan communities do produce meaning, friendship and solidarity. But the substantial reply is that the structural concentration of star-making in monopoly hands ensures that the meanings the fans produce are produced within a field whose terms have been set by the industry. Adorno's claim that "it can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105) is exactly the structural rejoinder.

I conclude that the contemporary parade of supposedly great musical personalities is the maturation of the star system Adorno analysed. The functions are the same, the ideological supplements are the same, and the structural concentration is more, not less, intense. Adorno's framework predicts the contemporary star economy with surprising accuracy.

### Essay 11. Is the Frankfurt School's pessimism about popular culture an indispensable corrective to celebratory accounts, or a paternalistic dead end?

I argue that the Frankfurt School's pessimism is an indispensable corrective to celebratory accounts of popular culture, but that some of its specific moves (especially Adorno's writing on jazz) are paternalistic and racially blinkered in ways that have to be acknowledged and corrected. The honest position is to retain the structural critique (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, commodity fetishism, integration from above) while abandoning the assumption that "serious" European art music is the implicit measure of cultural value.

The celebratory account of popular culture, associated with the British Cultural Studies tradition (Williams, Hoggart, Hall) covered later in the course, runs: audiences are active, popular culture is meaningful, fans co-create, culture is ordinary, and the dominant text is open to negotiated and oppositional reading. This account has real evidence behind it. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model shows audiences read negotiated and oppositional readings. The Birmingham School tradition is rich, empirically grounded and politically generous.

But the celebratory account, taken alone, has three blind spots that the Frankfurt School corrects. First, it tends to read off active reception from the *existence* of active reception, ignoring the structural conditions under which most reception is patterned and distracted. Adorno's claim that popular music is "for the masses a perpetual busman's holiday" (Adorno, 1941: 32) is, structurally, more often true than the celebratory account allows. The modal experience of listening, as Adorno describes it, is "general inattention which is only interrupted by sudden flashes of recognition" (Adorno, 1941: 36).

Second, the celebratory account tends to treat the market as a neutral medium of choice. The Frankfurt School insists on the political economy: who owns the means of mental production, who profits, who labours, who decides what gets produced. The current concentration of cultural production documented in the lecture's monopolisation slide (Sony, Universal, Warner alongside Kobalt and BMG; Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify; Ticketmaster, See Tickets; Amazon as cross-owner of retail and streaming) is invisible if you only look at audience meanings. Adorno's "cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100) is the corrective.

Third, the celebratory account tends to underestimate the standardisation Adorno identified. The lecture slides' Taylor Swift "Shake It Off" example shows the persistence of the verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton; the "Von Dutch" example shows pseudo-individuation operating as Adorno predicted; the Oasis "Cigarettes and Alcohol" example shows catharsis defusing class anger. None of this contradicts active audience reception, but all of it constrains the conditions under which reception happens.

The paternalism objection is real. Adorno's writing on jazz, including the sweeping claim about "masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism" (Adorno, 1941: 37), treats jazz as a degraded form of serious music, misses its African-American roots, misses its improvisational sophistication, misses the cultural-political work it did for Black Americans. The standard of value implicit in his writing is European concert music (his examples in "On Popular Music" are Beethoven's Seventh Symphony, the Appassionata, the Fifth Symphony, Haydn, Brahms, Mozart). This is a major intellectual failure, not just a stylistic preference. Paul Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* (introduced earlier in the course) reads modernity from the perspective of enslaved Africans and refuses exactly the Eurocentric measure Adorno relied on.

The deeper paternalism is the assumption that ordinary people are dupes who need critical theory to see what is happening to them. This is the standard Birmingham School objection and it has force. People are often more sceptical of their own consumption than Adorno gave them credit for. Adorno himself describes this well: "If it guarantees them even the most fleeting gratification they desire a deception which is nonetheless transparent to them" (Adorno, 1991: 103). The audience is half-aware of being marketed to; the question is whether half-awareness is enough to break the loop. Adorno's view is that it is not, and the lecture slides' standard criticisms (Frankfurt School "ignore agency and resistance," "fail to do justice to what is new and distinctive about the development of the media") are the standard reply.

The most productive use of the Frankfurt School is therefore selective. Retain the structural diagnosis (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, integration from above, commodity fetishism, the dialectic of enlightenment). Drop the implicit European-art-music measure of value. Combine with Stuart Hall's reception theory and the British Cultural Studies "culture is ordinary" insight to get a full picture. Add Foucauldian productive power and Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from African perspectives to fill the gaps Adorno could not see.

I conclude that the Frankfurt School's pessimism is an indispensable corrective, not a paternalistic dead end, provided we abandon its Eurocentric implicit measure of value and combine it with reception theory and contemporary political-economic analysis. The honest critical position is neither dismissal nor revival; it is selective inheritance.

### Essay 12. How does the concept of pseudo-individuation help us understand the manufactured pop song and the manufactured star?

I argue that pseudo-individuation is the single most useful Adornian concept for analysing the manufactured pop song and the manufactured star, because both produce intense impressions of individuality, talent and authenticity while being structurally standardised down to the form level. The lecture slides' choice of Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" as the textbook case of pseudo-individuation, together with the materialist-ideology slide's account of the star system, supplies the material for the analysis.

Adorno's definition in "On Popular Music" is precise: "Pseudo-individualization... endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice or open market on the basis of standardization itself. Standardization of song hits keeps the customers in line by doing their listening for them, as it were. Pseudo-individualization, for its part, keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them, or 'pre-digested'" (Adorno, 1941: 23). The two elements: standardisation does the structural work; pseudo-individuation disguises it as choice. The 1941 example, given by Adorno himself, was sweet versus swing, Benny Goodman versus Guy Lombardo: apparently distinctive performing identities whose underlying material is the same (Adorno, 1941: 26).

The manufactured pop song is the unit on which pseudo-individuation operates most cleanly. The lecturer's "Shake It Off" slide makes the standardised skeleton visible: Verse-Chorus-Verse-Chorus-Bridge-Chorus, with the chorus repeating the title hook on every iteration. Adorno's diagnosis applies exactly: "the harmonic cornerstones of each hit, the beginning and the end of each part, must beat out the standard scheme. This scheme emphasizes the most primitive harmonic facts no matter what has harmonically intervened. Complications have no consequences. This inexorable device guarantees that regardless of what aberrations occur, the hit will lead back to the same familiar experience, and nothing fundamentally novel will be introduced" (Adorno, 1941: 3).

The "Von Dutch" slide shows pseudo-individuation in its sharpened form. The bullet points on the slide name the operation directly: an electroclash style blending 1980s synthpop with contemporary pop; "edgy and alternative in terms of repeated f-word in chart pop-song, image as subversive pop star, video featuring stylised violence"; and most explicitly, "subverts the conventional pop-song but reproduces its structure." This is pseudo-individuation in the lecture's own terms: a halo of free choice, alternative attitude and stylistic distinctiveness layered over a structurally conventional pop track.

Adorno's analysis of the "individual air" applies to the manufactured star in the same way. "Each product affects an individual air; individuality itself serves to reinforce ideology, in so far as the illusion is conjured up that the completely reified and mediated is a sanctuary from immediacy and life" (Adorno, 1991: 101). The materialist-ideology slide pairs this with the lecturer's own diagnosis: under "Ideology" we find "Record companies make 'stars'" and "You can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money." The star is the human face of the manufactured commodity, and the personality is the form pseudo-individuation takes at the level of the producer rather than the song.

The slide's Madonna "Material Girl" example illustrates the operation at scale. Madonna is a mechanically reproduced commodity: the recording is a copy, the music video is broadcast, the album sleeve is printed by the thousand. And yet the figure of Madonna is presented as an irreducible individual personality. The fetishised commodity (the recording, the persona) is presented as the unmediated self. Adorno's phrase about the "supposedly great personalities and heart-throbs" (Adorno, 1991: 101) the industry propagates names the operation, and the lyrics of "Material Girl" (the boy with the cold hard cash is always Mister Right) make the commodity logic of pseudo-individuated stardom textually explicit.

The catharsis function complements pseudo-individuation. The slides' Oasis "Cigarettes and Alcohol" example shows the same operation at the level of working-class anger: the track gives the listener a distinctively rebellious persona to identify with while channelling the underlying anger into a singable refrain. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line" (Adorno, 1941: 43). The pseudo-individuated rebellion of the manufactured rock band defuses the political force of the alienation it articulates.

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences are not duped: fans identify actively with stars, decode their performances in negotiated and oppositional ways, and build communities around shared identification. This has force, and the lecture slides include reception theory among the standard criticisms of the Frankfurt School. But Adorno's reply is that even active identification operates within a field whose terms have been set by the industry, and that "the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The active fan still consumes pseudo-individuated material every day.

I conclude that pseudo-individuation is the indispensable analytical tool for the manufactured pop song and the manufactured star, that it explains the gap between experienced individuality and structural sameness, and that the lecture slides' "Von Dutch" and "Material Girl" examples make the operation visible at both the song level and the star level. Adorno was right about the form.

### Essay 13. "Conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno). Discuss.

I argue that Adorno's claim that "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104) is the strongest single sentence in his cultural writing, that it describes a tendency rather than an absolute, and that the contemporary cross-ownership of cultural industries documented in the lecture slides supplies the structural conditions under which the tendency operates.

Adorno's full passage runs: "The categorical imperative of the culture industry no longer has anything in common with freedom. It proclaims: you shall conform, without instruction as to what; conform to that which exists anyway, and to that which everyone thinks anyway as a reflex of its power and omnipresence. The power of the culture industry's ideology is such that conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104). Two things to notice. First, the imperative is contentless: not "conform to fascism" or "conform to consumerism" but "conform to that which exists anyway." Second, the mechanism is the reflex of power and omnipresence; sheer ubiquity does the work, not argument.

The mechanism Adorno describes is the cumulative effect of a standardised cultural environment. He elaborates: "it can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The lecture slide on monopolisation, which depicts the concentration of recorded music, streaming, ticketing and retail in a small number of firms (Sony, Universal, Warner, Kobalt, BMG; Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify; Ticketmaster, See Tickets; HMV, Amazon as cross-owner), names the structural condition under which the same formulas are drilled.

The standardised form supplies the formulas. The lecture's "Shake It Off" example shows the verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton; the "Von Dutch" example shows pseudo-individuation; the Oasis catharsis example shows the working-day rhythm reproduced in leisure. Each track is a "steady drop"; their cumulative effect is the hollowing of the stone. Adorno's diagnosis in "On Popular Music" of the listener's mode of reception, "general inattention which is only interrupted by sudden flashes of recognition" (Adorno, 1941: 36), names the form in which conformity replaces consciousness at the level of listening.

The slide statements from the lecturer's class activity sharpen the application. "The recognition of brand names has taken the place of choice" makes the point at the level of the consumer: the brand is the contentless imperative; recognition is the reflex. "Sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection" makes the point at the level of public spectacle: the cathartic identification with the team replaces consciousness of the conditions under which one watches. "Anyone who is incapable of talking in the prescribed fashion is threatened in his very existence" makes the point at the level of language itself: the obligation to conform extends to the categories in which one thinks. Each statement is a different facet of the same diagnosis.

The mechanism Adorno gives in "On Popular Music" is the structural fit between alienated work and patterned leisure. "Their leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" (Adorno, 1941: 32). The worker, returned from rationalised labour, encounters a leisure that is structurally similar: pre-digested, standardised, requiring no concentration. The cumulative effect is that the categories of thought she brings to leisure are the same as the categories that organise her work, and the consciousness that might criticise either has nowhere to stand outside both.

The standard objection from the lecture slides' criticisms of the Frankfurt School is that "audience studies suggest that the reception and appropriation of cultural products is a complex social process which involves a dialectical relationship between agency and structure" (lecture slide). Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model gives the form. Adorno's reply, which the slides also record, is that he acknowledges "their deep unconscious mistrust, the last residue of the difference between art and empirical reality" (Adorno, 1991: 105), but holds that this residue is inadequate to break the loop because the structural drilling continues.

Foucault's productive-power objection sharpens but does not refute Adorno. The lecture's monopolisation slide and the materialist-ideology slide read culture not just as repression of an antecedent consciousness but as the production of a particular kind of consumer: "Musicians hold labour power... Produce a commodity (music)... Capitalist invests in fixed capital (studios / production / distribution)" on the material side, "Record companies make 'stars'... You can be a star... Music is special... Music is precious... Music is an industry" on the ideological side. The cultural subject produced is conscious in particular ways (of brands, of stars, of music as industry) that align with conformity to the system that produced her. Adorno's diagnosis is preserved: critical, autonomous, deliberative consciousness has been displaced, even if a different kind of consciousness has been produced in its place.

The counter-current is real but small. The "music as working-class emancipation" slide (Crass, the Lancaster Music Co-op) shows that an alternative form of cultural production, one that does not drill the same formulas because it is structured differently at the level of ownership, is possible. Adorno's own theory allows for this: he distinguishes the culture industry from culture which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The protest is real where it is structurally enabled; conformity prevails where it is not.

I conclude that Adorno's claim describes a tendency that the lecture slides' monopolised cultural industries continue to instantiate, that the standardised form of the cultural commodity (visible in "Shake It Off," "Von Dutch," "Cigarettes and Alcohol") is the mechanism, and that the counter-currents pictured in the emancipation slide preserve a space for consciousness without refuting the diagnosis of the dominant tendency. The corrective politics is exactly Adorno's: take the culture industry "seriously critically" and refuse to cower before its monopolistic character (Adorno, 1991: 102).

### Essay 14. Can independent, radical or anti-capitalist art exist within the culture industry? Discuss with reference to the examples in the lecture slides.

I argue that independent, radical or anti-capitalist art can exist within the culture industry but always in compromised form, because the industry commodifies what it captures, and that the lecture's "music as working-class emancipation" slide offers three positions on this question (Rage Against the Machine, Crass, Lancaster Music Co-op) that map the available options precisely.

Adorno's strongest version of the claim is that the culture industry is total: "cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100). On this reading, no art produced within the industry is innocent of its commodity logic. The radical content is contained by the standardised form; the political message is digested as entertainment; the cathartic release defuses the political energy. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43).

The lecture slide on commodification gives the textbook within-the-industry case: Rage Against the Machine, signed to a major label, releasing anti-capitalist material through capitalist distribution channels. The slide's own caption is exact: "Independent, radical, even anti-capitalist music can be sold as a commodity by capitalists." The contradiction is real and Adorno would have anticipated it. The band's anti-capitalist commitment is genuine; the form of its distribution is exactly what Adorno predicted, with the major label realising the surplus value and the listener consuming the message as a rock song before returning to work. Adorno's claim that "the entire practice of the culture industry transfers the profit motive naked onto cultural forms" (Adorno, 1991: 99) names the operation.

The pure-resistance position is represented in the same slide by Crass, the British anarcho-punk collective whose "Feeding of the Five Thousand" the slide pictures. Crass ran their own anarcho-punk label, sold records cheaply, refused commercial entanglements where possible, and distributed through political and gig networks. They built an explicitly anti-capitalist musical culture outside the major-label structure. This is the position Adorno's framework allows for: a popular culture that genuinely arises from below and that maintains "rebellious resistance inherent within it as long as social control was not yet total" (Adorno, 1991: 99). The cost of this position is relative reach: Crass never approached the audience of a major-label band. The trade-off is real, and Adorno's framework predicts it.

The structural alternative is represented by the Lancaster Music Co-op, also in the same slide. The co-op is not a label or a band but a worker-owned space where the means of musical production (rehearsal rooms, equipment, recording facilities, music education, gig promotion) are collectively held rather than rented out by capitalists. This is the most thoroughly anti-Adornian situation possible within the lecture's material: the means of mental production, which the slide on monopolisation shows concentrated in a small number of corporate hands, are here held collectively. Marx's framework predicts that this is the only way out: the workers must hold their own means of production.

The lecturer's "materialist oppression and emancipation" panel beside the commodification examples gives the analytical frame. The materialist side is structural: "Between equal rights, force decides" (Marx, *Capital Volume 1*) and "The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggle" (Marx and Engels, 1848). The emancipatory side is provisional but real: "Capitalism creates own gravediggers / Capitalist crisis / Worker organisation." The Crass case is an instance of worker organisation in cultural production; the Co-op is an instance of an even deeper organisation, at the level of fixed capital.

Benjamin's framework offers a complementary reading. The cassette and mp3 imagery beside the Subversive Truth of Art slide makes the point: mechanical reproduction lowers the cost of cultural production and creates the technical conditions for democratised art. Crass's records and the Co-op's recordings are Benjaminian uses of mechanical reproduction: technology of reproduction in non-monopoly hands enables the working-class organisation of cultural production. "Through art one can conceive a world different from the life dominated by commodity production... Art anticipates the good life" (the slide's Benjaminian formulation).

The objection from Adorno's pessimism is that the cracks are too small to matter. Crass's reach is limited; the Co-op's productive scale is small. The structural concentration documented in the monopolisation slide (Sony, Universal, Warner, Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Ticketmaster, Amazon as cross-owner) is the dominant fact, and the alternatives operate in its margins.

The Stuart Hall objection is that the boundary between industry and resistance is itself negotiated. Rage Against the Machine's commodified anti-capitalism may still function as a politicising experience for listeners; the major-label commodity may still be read oppositionally; the energy may transfer to organising work outside the consumption. Adorno's reply is the standard one: the structural drilling of standardised forms hollows the stone, even where individual moments of resistance are real.

The honest synthesis: independent, radical and anti-capitalist art can exist within the culture industry (the Rage Against the Machine case) but always at the cost of commodification; can exist outside it (the Crass case) at the cost of reach; and can exist at the structural level (the Lancaster Music Co-op case) where the means of mental production are collectively held. The three positions on the lecture's emancipation slide are the three available stances.

I conclude that the question's "can" is the right modal: it can, but only with awareness of the cost. Adorno's diagnosis remains the analytical baseline; Marx's framework supplies the structural exit (collective ownership of the means of mental production); Benjamin supplies the technical possibility (mechanical reproduction in non-monopoly hands).

### Essay 15. Critically assess the claim that the Frankfurt School "reads off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves."

I argue that the criticism that the Frankfurt School "reads off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves" (lecture slide on criticisms of the Frankfurt School) is partly right and partly wrong: partly right because Adorno often does infer audience effects from formal analysis, partly wrong because Adorno himself anticipated the objection and gave a structural reply that the standard criticism does not fully meet.

The criticism, as listed in the lecture slide on criticisms of the Frankfurt School, has multiple parts: the school "ignore agency and resistance"; they "fail to do justice to what is new and distinctive about the development of the media and mass communications and how these encourage democracy"; they "fail to develop a satisfactory theory accounting for the circulation and reception of symbolic forms"; they "read off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves"; their emphasis on standardisation and reification "fail to take into consideration the everyday practices of popular culture and media organizations"; there is "still no clear evidence that the reception and consumption of the products of the culture industry impel individuals to adhere to any social order"; and "audience studies suggest that the reception and appropriation of cultural products is a complex social process which involves a dialectical relationship between agency and structure." The criticism is dense, well-organised, and has real force.

The criticism has its strongest application to Adorno's writing on jazz. In "On Popular Music" (1941) he makes sweeping claims about the psychic effects of jazz on listeners ("masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism," Adorno, 1941: 37) based largely on formal analysis of the music. He treats jazz as a degraded mass form whose listeners are reduced to obedience by rhythmic conformity. He does not engage seriously with how Black American audiences actually heard or used jazz, with its African-American cultural roots, or with the political work it did. The result is racially blinkered and analytically weak, exactly the failure the standard criticism identifies.

Adorno's own response to the criticism, in "Culture Industry Reconsidered," is twofold. First, he acknowledges the empirical gap: "It is true that thorough research has not, for the time being, produced an airtight case proving the regressive effects of particular products of the culture industry. No doubt an imaginatively designed experiment could achieve this more successfully than the powerful financial interests concerned would find comfortable" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The gap, he suggests, is partly produced by the political economy of research: those who would fund reception studies are themselves part of the industry being studied.

Second, he gives a structural reply: "It can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). Even without empirical proof of any single product's effect, the cumulative exposure to standardised forms is itself a reasonable basis for inference about long-term effects. This is not exactly "reading off effects from products"; it is reading off effects from the *system* of products, which is a different and more defensible analytical move.

The lecture slides' own examples support Adorno's second reply. "Shake It Off" alone proves little about its listeners; but the Verse-Chorus-Verse-Chorus-Bridge-Chorus skeleton drilled into thousands of comparable hits, day after day, year after year, is more than the sum of its instances. "Von Dutch" alone proves little; but pseudo-individuation drilled across the entire chart, with the slide's 2025 UK top-ten showing the same operation at scale, supports an inference about the form of life on offer. "Cigarettes and Alcohol" alone proves little; but catharsis-that-keeps-them-in-line, drilled across the genre, supports an inference about the social function. The structural drilling is the basis Adorno relied on, not any individual product.

The lecturer's own slide on the monopolisation of record companies, streaming services, ticket agencies and retailers gives further support to the structural reading. When the means of mental production are concentrated as the slide depicts (Sony, Universal, Warner; Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify; Ticketmaster, See Tickets; Amazon as cross-owner of retail and streaming), the consumer's available menu is structurally constrained. Adorno's inference from the menu to the consumer is, given that constraint, more defensible than the standard criticism allows.

The Stuart Hall reply remains correct on the individual level: audiences decode in dominant, negotiated and oppositional ways. The Birmingham School's encoding/decoding model gives the framework for this. But the variation in decoding occurs around a mean that is itself shaped by the encoded text and its mass distribution. The Frankfurt move (analyse the product and the system) and the Birmingham move (study the decoding) are complementary, not opposed. The lecturer's framing of the criticisms makes this clear: the dialectical relationship between agency and structure is precisely what audience studies suggest, and Adorno's structural side of that dialectic is not refuted by the agency side.

The deepest defence of the Frankfurt move is that it is a *normative* analysis, not just a predictive one. Adorno is asking what the cultural product is doing, what kind of subject it presupposes and produces, what kind of life it makes available. These questions can be answered by formal analysis even if individual reception varies, because the question is about the form of life the product offers, not the statistical distribution of receptions. The form of life offered by the standardised hit is different from that offered by Beethoven's Seventh Symphony (Adorno's own comparative example in "On Popular Music"); analysing the works tells us something true about that difference regardless of what individual listeners do with them.

The standard objection's strongest version is therefore not refuted but qualified. The Frankfurt School should not be the only frame; empirical reception studies (the Stuart Hall tradition) are also necessary. But the standardised form of the industrial cultural product, drilled at scale through monopoly distribution, supports a structural inference about its effects that the criticism does not undo. The slides' own juxtaposition of monopolisation, standardisation and pseudo-individuation makes this case visually.

I conclude that the criticism is partly right (Adorno's writing on jazz commits the fallacy in racially blinkered ways and the empirical gap is real) and partly wrong (Adorno's structural reply is not addressed by the standard criticism, and the lecture's own monopolisation and standardisation examples support the structural reading). The honest position is to combine Frankfurt structural critique with Stuart Hall's reception theory; the dialectical relationship between agency and structure, which the lecture slide names as the audience-studies position, is exactly the right synthesis.
