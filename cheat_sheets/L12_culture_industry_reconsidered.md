# L12 — Culture Industry Reconsidered (Adorno, Frankfurt School, Marx, Benjamin)

---

## 1. Lecture summary

This lecture builds a genealogy of critical theories of culture from Marx through the Frankfurt School to Walter Benjamin. The lecturer is clear that Marx himself did not write systematically about culture — he was a journalist who needed the income, and his references to culture are scattered — but three Marxian texts supply the apparatus that the Frankfurt School later turns on twentieth-century mass entertainment. Early Marx in *The German Ideology* (1846) gives us ruling-class ideology and the means of mental production. The 1859 *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* gives us base and superstructure. Late Marx in *Capital* (1867, 1885, 1894) gives us the critique of political economy, labour power, commodification, commodity fetishism, and the singer-as-wage-labourer who "produces capital directly" when she sings for an entrepreneur. Together these provide the tools for thinking about why music, film and television look the way they do under capitalism: because the class that owns the means of material production also owns the means of mental production, and what looks like culture is in fact a commodity whose real social relations have been inverted into a relation between things — a phantasmagoria.

Adorno and Horkheimer, founders (with Felix Weil) of the Institute for Social Research at Frankfurt in 1923, took these Marxian categories into a new historical situation: the failure of revolution in the West, the rise of Nazism and Stalinism, the success of US monopoly capitalism, and the emergence of radio, cinema and the recording industry. The lecturer stresses the school's three signature moves. First, the dialectic of enlightenment — reason that began as critique of myth has become a new myth of total administration, instrumental reason, technocratic control. Second, the culture industry — a phrase deliberately chosen over "mass culture" to refuse the idea that the products arise spontaneously from the people. Third, the diagnostic vocabulary of standardisation, pseudo-individuation and catharsis-that-keeps-them-in-line, illustrated in the slides by Taylor Swift's verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus structure in "Shake It Off," by Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" simulating subversion while reproducing pop's skeleton, and by Oasis's "Cigarettes and Alcohol" offering a working-class catharsis that disarms working-class anger.

Walter Benjamin provides the dialectical counterpoint. In "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) he argues that mass reproduction strips the aura — the here-and-now authority of the unique work tied to ritual and tradition — but in doing so democratises art, replaces cult value with exhibition value, and opens new modes of distracted, collective, critical reception (cinema, the dance hall, photography). Where Adorno saw mechanical reproduction as the engine of conformity, Benjamin saw it as a potentially emancipatory shift in the means of artistic production.

The lecture closes with standard criticisms of the Frankfurt School: it ignores agency, treats audiences as dupes, reads off effects from products, and lacks any empirical theory of reception. The current relevance — monopolised streaming, the algorithm, the "you can be a star" ideology of X Factor and reality TV, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, the K-pop manufacturing pipeline, AI-generated music — is left for us to draw.

## 2. Key concepts and named theorists

- **Karl Marx** — early/late distinction; not really a theorist of culture but supplies the categories
- **The German Ideology (1846)** — ruling-class ideas, the means of mental production, camera obscura
- **Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859)** — base and superstructure
- **Capital (1867, 1885, 1894)** — labour power, the singer as productive worker, commodity fetishism
- **Commodity fetishism** — social relations between people appearing as "phantasmagorical" relations between things
- **The means of mental production** — schools, publishers, cinemas, record labels, streaming platforms
- **Camera obscura ideology** — class relations appear upside-down, misrepresented in interests of dominant class
- **Friedrich Engels / Anti-Dühring** — extended base/superstructure to culture and media
- **Louis Althusser** — determination in the last instance, relative autonomy, overdetermination
- **The Frankfurt School / Institute for Social Research** — founded 1923; Geneva 1933; New York 1935; Felix Weil, Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Walter Benjamin, Herbert Marcuse, Erich Fromm, Leo Lowenthal
- **Critical theory** — self-reflexive Marxism incorporating psychoanalysis, dialectic, immanent critique
- **The dialectic of enlightenment** — reason vs myth synthesises into new myth of total administration
- **Instrumental reason** — reason reduced to means-ends calculation, domination of nature and people
- **The totally administered society** — Adorno's diagnosis of late capitalism, fascism, Stalinism, state capitalism
- **The culture industry** — Adorno and Horkheimer's term, replaced "mass culture" to refuse the spontaneity myth
- **Anti-enlightenment / mass deception / fettering consciousness** — the total effect of the industry
- **Standardisation** — verse-chorus-verse, "the same skeleton," genre formulas, the Western
- **Pseudo-individuation** (or pseudo-individuality) — illusion of choice / individuality disguising sameness; the necessary correlate of standardisation
- **Catharsis for the masses** — emotional release that "keeps them in line"
- **Pre-digested listening** — "the composition hears for the listener"
- **The star system** — borrowed from individualistic art, propagates the "ordinary person can make it" myth
- **Walter Benjamin** — *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935)
- **The aura** — "the presence of that which is not present"; here-and-now uniqueness of the ritual work of art
- **Mechanical reproduction** — photography, film, the gramophone; debases aura, democratises art
- **Cult value vs exhibition value** — art for ritual contemplation vs art for public display and circulation
- **Distracted reception** — collective, critical mode of cinema audience, contrasted with absorbed contemplation
- **The subversive truth of art** — art "anticipates the good life," holds open a non-existent alternative
- **Frankfurt School criticisms** — ignores agency, reads off effects from products, no reception theory

## 4. Connections to other lectures

- **L11 (Bourdieu, taste, cultural capital):** Bourdieu's habitus and the social construction of taste maps onto pseudo-individuation — the "choice" between sweet and swing, indie and pop, that Adorno mocks is exactly what Bourdieu shows as class-coded distinction. Both reject the liberal idea of free aesthetic choice.
- **L13/L14 (Hall, encoding/decoding, audience studies):** Stuart Hall and the Birmingham School's reception theory is the major answer to the Frankfurt School's "no theory of reception" gap. Hall's negotiated and oppositional readings rescue agency from Adorno's "object of calculation."
- **L15 (Barthes, structuralism, mythologies):** Barthes' "myth as the depoliticised speech of the bourgeoisie" is the structural-semiotic version of Marx's camera-obscura ideology. Past-Qs on Barthes (2022 Q5, 2021 Q5) sit alongside the L12 material conceptually.
- **L16 (Foucault, power/knowledge):** Foucault's productive power and discourse offer an alternative to the Frankfurt School's still-rather-Marxist account of domination from above; the "totally administered society" is Adorno's name for what Foucault would re-describe as disciplinary and biopolitical assemblage.
- **L17/L18 (Gender, feminism, performativity):** Madonna's "Material Girl" (used in the slides) raises the question of whether female pop stars are pure commodity fetishists or, à la Judith Butler, perform a parody that destabilises norms — a productive tension with Adorno.
- **L19 (Postmodernism / Jameson):** Fredric Jameson's "cultural logic of late capitalism" is a direct descendant of Adorno; pastiche, depthlessness, and nostalgia mode are postmodern reformulations of standardisation and pseudo-individuation.
- **L20 (Race, postcolonial):** Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding and Paul Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* both push back against Adorno's elitist dismissal of jazz; the racial politics of his attack on swing is a serious problem.
- **L8/L9 (Weber, rationalisation, the iron cage):** Adorno's instrumental reason is a direct heir to Weberian rationalisation; the totally administered society is Weber's iron cage radicalised by Marx and psychoanalysis.

## 5. Why this lecture matters / debates

The culture industry thesis is the foundational sociological account of the mass media. Every later debate about media power — from McLuhan to Williams to Hall to Jameson to contemporary platform studies — argues with it. Three live debates structure how to write about L12.

First, the agency debate. The Frankfurt School is repeatedly accused of "reading off" effects from products, of treating audiences as cultural dupes. The Birmingham School (Hall, Hebdige, Morley) and audience-studies tradition rescue active interpretation. But contemporary platform capitalism — Spotify algorithms, recommender systems, TikTok feeds — arguably restores the Frankfurt School's pessimism: when the means of mental production are not just owned by the bourgeoisie but coded into machine learning systems that train consumers' attention in real time, "pseudo-individualisation" reads less like nostalgia and more like a prophecy. The algorithmic playlist is the most refined pseudo-individuating device ever built.

Second, the high-vs-low art debate. Adorno's defence of "serious" music (Beethoven, Schoenberg) over popular music looks elitist, Eurocentric and frequently racist (his attack on jazz). Benjamin offers the corrective: popular forms can be sites of democratised, critical, distracted reception. The honest essay holds both: Adorno is right that the *industrial* form of culture standardises and reifies, but wrong to read this off from genre or "seriousness." Bandcamp, mutual-aid music co-ops, free-improv scenes, the Lancaster Music Co-op pictured in the slides — these are popular but not industrial in Adorno's sense.

Third, the Marxism debate. Is the culture industry the superstructure determined by the base, or has it become base-like itself? Adorno's late writing pushes toward the latter: the industry "no longer even needs to directly pursue everywhere the profit interests from which it originated. These interests have become objectified in its ideology" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100). This is post-Marxian — ideology runs on autopilot, cultural production has been folded into the circulation of capital itself. Streaming-era cultural economy (Spotify pays musicians fractions of a cent per stream while Bandcamp pays directly, Netflix produces "content" rather than films, TikTok monetises affect) seems to vindicate the diagnosis.

The takeaway: L12 supplies the vocabulary — standardisation, pseudo-individuation, the culture industry, the aura, base/superstructure, commodity fetishism — that every other media-and-culture lecture will either deploy, modify or reject.

## 8. Full essays

### Essay 1 — "The culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above" (Adorno). Discuss with reference to contemporary streaming platforms.

I argue that Adorno's claim that "the culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 98) [VERIFY p.?] is more, not less, accurate in the streaming era, because the algorithmic recommender has made integration-from-above into a real-time, machine-learned process invisible to the consumer it constructs. Where 1947 Adorno had to argue that monopoly broadcasters and Hollywood studios fabricated demand, the streaming platform fabricates demand continuously, individually, and unfalsifiably.

Adorno's argument has three steps. First, the masses are not the measure but the ideology of the culture industry: "The customer is not king, as the culture industry would have us believe, not its subject but its object" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 99). The industry presumes a "given and unchangeable" mentality which it then duplicates, reinforces and strengthens. Second, cultural commodities are governed by the principle of their realisation as value, not by content: profit motive is "transferred naked onto cultural forms" (p. 99). Third, what looks like progress, novelty and choice is "the disguise for an eternal sameness" (p. 100). The integration is from above because the technical and administrative concentration of the means of mental production allows the industry to decide in advance what counts as music, film or news.

Spotify is the textbook case. Of roughly one hundred million tracks, the median song is streamed almost never; discovery is mediated by editorial playlists ("RapCaviar," "Today's Top Hits") and by the algorithmic Discover Weekly and Daily Mix. Playlist placement is the new radio plug — and Spotify itself, via its Marquee and Discovery Mode tools, sells artists the right to be promoted in exchange for accepting a lower royalty. This is integration-from-above with a sliding scale: pay-to-play wearing the costume of personalisation. The user experiences Discover Weekly as uncanny intimacy; what they hear is in fact a weighted output of a collaborative-filtering model trained on millions of users with similar listening histories, with a stable of label-promoted seeds spliced in. Adorno's phrase the composition hears for the listener (Adorno, "On Popular Music," [16]) becomes literal: the algorithm hears for the listener.

Netflix exhibits the same logic at the production layer. The recommender data drives commissioning decisions: shows are greenlit because models predict engagement minutes for given audience clusters. The result is what critics have called "Netflix sameness" — the muted colour-grade, the algorithm-friendly cold open, the same true-crime template iterated across countries (Korean, Mexican, Polish variants of the same docuseries skeleton). What parades as progress, in Adorno's words, "remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (p. 100). The Marvel Cinematic Universe pushes this to franchise logic: every "individual" film slots into a pre-determined narrative architecture; novelty is purely combinatorial.

The objection from Stuart Hall and the Birmingham School is that audiences are not passive — they decode actively, sometimes oppositionally, and they appropriate cultural commodities for their own ends. This is right, and Adorno's worst writing on jazz reads off effects from products in the way the standard criticism alleges. But the algorithmic platform partly neutralises the Hall objection, because it personalises the encoding to the individual decoder. When my feed is built around what the system predicts I will engage with, my "oppositional" reading becomes another training signal. The platform absorbs resistance as data. This is the deepest sense in which Adorno's "from above" is intensified, not weakened.

There are countervailing tendencies. Bandcamp pays artists directly, gives them control over pricing and distribution, and supports a folk-cultural commons that the major-label-streaming complex does not capture. The Lancaster Music Co-op, shown in the slides, is a worker-owned space where the means of musical production are collectively held rather than rented out. These are real cracks in the integration-from-above, and Adorno's own theory leaves room for them: he distinguishes the culture industry from popular culture that genuinely arises from below. But the cracks are tiny compared with the scale of the platforms. Spotify has around six hundred million users; Bandcamp's monthly active base is a fraction of that.

The deepest update to Adorno is that the integration no longer needs to be conscious. As Adorno himself foresaw, the culture industry's "interests have become objectified in its ideology and have even made themselves independent of the compulsion to sell the cultural commodities which must be swallowed anyway" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100). The Spotify product manager does not need to plot against autonomy; the engagement metric does it automatically, and "conformity has replaced consciousness" (p. 104) at the level of the engineering team as much as the user.

I conclude that integration from above is the right phrase for what streaming platforms do, and the algorithmic recommender is the most refined integration-from-above mechanism the culture industry has yet developed. The Frankfurt School's pessimism, often dismissed as 1940s elitism, turns out to describe 2025 quite accurately.

### Essay 2 — To what extent does Marx's account of the means of mental production in *The German Ideology* anticipate Adorno's culture industry thesis?

I argue that Marx's account of the means of mental production in *The German Ideology* anticipates Adorno's culture industry thesis substantially but not completely: Marx supplies the political-economic skeleton (ownership of cultural production by the ruling class), but Adorno adds three things Marx did not have — the industrial form of cultural production, the psychoanalytic theory of the subject who consumes it, and the dialectical reversal whereby enlightenment becomes its opposite.

Marx's famous formulation reads: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, i.e. the class which is the ruling material force of society, is at the same time its ruling intellectual force. The class which has the means of production at its disposal, has control at the same time over the means of mental production" (Marx, *The German Ideology*, [VERIFY p.?]). This claim has two halves. The first half — ruling-class ideas — names the *content* of ideology in any epoch (feudal honour codes, bourgeois individualism). The second half — the means of mental production — names the *infrastructure*: who owns the schools, the printing presses, the libraries, the lecture halls. Marx is sketching a sociology of intellectual production that asks not only what the ruling ideas are but how they are reproduced through institutional control of the apparatus of dissemination.

This already anticipates the culture industry in three respects. First, it identifies the asymmetric ownership of cultural production as the engine of ideological domination. Sony, Universal and Warner own roughly seventy per cent of recorded-music rights; Spotify, Amazon Music, Apple Music and YouTube own the distribution layer; Live Nation/Ticketmaster owns ticketing. This is exactly the concentration of the means of mental production Marx described, now industrially scaled. Second, the formulation makes ideology *material*: it is not just ideas in heads but institutions, buildings, technologies. Third, it builds in the camera-obscura claim — "ideology represents class relations in an illusory form" that "concurs with the interests of the dominant class" (Marx, *The German Ideology*, [VERIFY p.?]). The inversion that makes Richard Branson appear rich because clever rather than rich because he exploited musicians is the same inversion Adorno will name the star system.

Where Marx stops short, Adorno extends. Marx is writing in 1846, before the industrial production of culture. He has the printing press but not the gramophone, the radio, the cinema. The slogan "culture industry" was, Adorno tells us, chosen "in order to exclude from the outset the interpretation agreeable to its advocates: that it is a matter of something like a culture that arises spontaneously from the masses themselves" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 98). The industrial form changes the analysis. Standardisation is no longer just ideological repetition but technical seriality: the verse-chorus-bridge skeleton Adorno identifies in his 1941 essay "On Popular Music" [3], the Western Adorno cites as exemplary of "the standardization of the thing itself" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100), the franchise template of the Marvel Cinematic Universe. Pseudo-individuation — "endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice... on the basis of standardization itself" ("On Popular Music," [23]) — is Adorno's name for the gap between Marx's general claim about ruling ideas and the specific way industrial culture markets sameness as difference.

Adorno's second extension is psychoanalytic. Marx's account is sociological: the worker is subject to ruling ideas because she lacks the means of mental production. Adorno, drawing on Freud via Erich Fromm and the Frankfurt School's mass-psychology programme, asks why workers actively desire the culture they are sold. His answer in "On Popular Music" is that "leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" [32]. Standardised entertainment fits the standardised worker; the eight-hour shift on the line produces the appetite for thirty-two-bar choruses. Marx has alienation but not yet this loop, in which alienation is consumed as relief from alienation.

Adorno's third extension is the dialectic. Marx is, broadly, a theorist of progress: the productive forces will burst the relations of production, capitalism is its own gravedigger. Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1947) reads twentieth-century experience as the inversion of this thesis: reason, science and technical mastery — the enlightenment programme — have synthesised into new forms of domination (Nazism, Stalinism, the culture industry). "The total effect of the culture industry is one of anti-enlightenment... it becomes mass deception and is turned into a means for fettering consciousness" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, [VERIFY p.?]). This pessimism is genuinely post-Marxian, and it has no precise equivalent in Marx's own writing.

A defender of Marx will reply that the camera-obscura passage and the analysis of commodity fetishism in *Capital* — "a particular social relation between men themselves which in their eyes assumes a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things" (Marx, *Capital* Vol 1, [VERIFY p.?]) — already contain the seeds of Adorno's analysis of pseudo-individuation and reification. This is fair. The Spotify recommender as commodity fetishism, the K-pop manufactured group as star-system fetish, the AI-generated Suno track as the ultimate phantasmagoria — these can be analysed using purely Marxian tools. Adorno's contribution is to sharpen and update, not to invent.

I conclude that the anticipation is substantial. Marx supplies the analytic skeleton (means of mental production, base/superstructure, commodity fetishism); Adorno adds industrial form, psychoanalytic subject, and the dialectical reversal. The honest reading is that one cannot do the Frankfurt School without Marx, and one cannot do contemporary cultural studies without both.

### Essay 3 — Critically evaluate the claim that standardisation and pseudo-individuation remain the defining features of twenty-first-century popular music.

I argue that standardisation and pseudo-individuation are not only still defining features of twenty-first-century popular music but have been intensified by the platform economy, AI tools, and the algorithmic playlist — though Adorno's framework needs three updates: the unit of standardisation has shrunk from the song to the hook, pseudo-individuation has migrated from sound to identity, and resistance has become harder to locate.

Adorno's 1941 thesis in "On Popular Music" is uncompromising: "The whole structure of popular music is standardized, even where the attempt is made to circumvent standardization" [3]. The thirty-two-bar chorus, the one-octave-plus-a-note range, the rigid sequence of types (mother song, novelty song, lost-girl lament) — these are the skeleton. Above the skeleton sit pseudo-individuated "effects": dirty notes, blue chords, the break, the bridge. "Pseudo-individualization... endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice or open market on the basis of standardization itself" keeps the listener "in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them, or 'pre-digested'" [23].

The 2010s and 2020s look like the most standardised period in pop history. Major-label A&R departments work from genre templates; songwriting camps assemble tracks for K-pop, country and pop simultaneously using shared chord progressions (the I-V-vi-IV "axis of awesome," the four-chord loop). Taylor Swift's "Shake It Off" (2014), invoked in the lecture slides, exhibits the canonical structure: Verse-Chorus-Verse-Chorus-Bridge-Chorus. The chorus repeats the title hook with the same trochaic stress on every iteration. The bridge supplies pseudo-individuation: a new key area, a syncopated breakdown, before the chorus reasserts the skeleton. This is exactly what Adorno predicts.

The 2020s shift is that the unit of standardisation has shrunk. Streaming changes the economics: artists are paid per play, and a play registers after roughly thirty seconds. The hook must therefore arrive in the first fifteen seconds. Songs have become shorter (the average Billboard Top Ten track has lost about a minute since 2000), intros have largely disappeared, and the second verse is often truncated or replaced by a chorus repeat. TikTok intensifies this: the unit is now the fifteen-second clip that loops over a dance trend. Songs are written backwards from the hook — Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" being the breakout case. Adorno's skeleton has become a skeleton of skeletons: not just the song but the loop within the song is standardised.

Pseudo-individuation has correspondingly migrated. Where 1941 jazz disguised standardisation with dirty notes, 2020s pop disguises it with identity. The "subversive pop star" persona — Charli XCX's "Von Dutch," cited in the slides — uses the f-word, stylised violence and an electroclash aesthetic to signal alterity while reproducing the verse-pre-chorus-chorus structure with absolute fidelity. K-pop groups like BTS and BLACKPINK are manufactured by Big Hit Entertainment and YG Entertainment respectively, with each member assigned a role (rapper, vocalist, visual), styled, choreographed and marketed to maximise the impression of distinctive personalities while the songwriting and production templates are shared across the industry. The "ordinary person can make it" ideology Adorno identified in the star system is amplified through reality TV: X Factor, Britain's Got Talent and The Voice perform the producer-as-fairy-godmother script every Saturday night while concealing the standard contractual arrangements that strip winners of rights to their image.

Three further developments push past 1941 Adorno. First, AI music generators — Suno, Udio — automate the production of pseudo-individuated tracks from text prompts. A prompt for "melancholy synthwave with Phoenix-style vocals" returns a track that satisfies the genre conventions without a human writer. This is standardisation as software. Second, "playlist culture" — the rise of the curated, mood-tagged playlist (sad girl, lo-fi beats to study to, gym pump) — completes the integration: songs are written to fit playlists, playlists are populated by Spotify editorial and algorithm, the listener selects mood rather than artist. Third, Cambridge Analytica showed that the data infrastructure underlying recommender systems can be repurposed for political manipulation — the means of mental production are now the means of mental targeting.

The honest objection is that there are real counter-currents. Bandcamp's Friday model (a day where Bandcamp waives fees) supports a folk-cultural commons of small-press releases. The album as form persists in jazz, classical and parts of metal and indie. Crass, pictured in the slides ("Feeding of the Five Thousand"), points to a long history of anti-commercial music — Rage Against the Machine, despite being commodified by Sony, did fund prisoner-defence campaigns. The lecture slide's "music as working-class emancipation" alternative is real. The question is scale.

The scale answer is decisive. By 2025, three major labels and four streaming platforms control the overwhelming bulk of recorded music distribution. The "long tail" promised by 2000s digital optimism has narrowed: the top one per cent of artists capture roughly ninety per cent of streams. Adorno's diagnosis — "the customer is not king... not its subject but its object" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 99) — describes the 2025 listener with chilling accuracy.

I conclude that standardisation and pseudo-individuation are stronger now than they were in 1941, that the framework needs updating (the unit has shrunk, pseudo-individuation has migrated to identity, the algorithm has automated both), and that the counter-current of folk-cultural and worker-owned music remains real but marginal.

### Essay 4 — "Mechanical reproduction shatters the aura — but the culture industry resurrects it as a foggy mist." Discuss Benjamin and Adorno on the politics of reproducibility.

I argue that Benjamin and Adorno disagree on the political valence of mechanical reproduction not because they disagree about its mechanism but because they disagree about its historical moment: Benjamin writes in 1935 looking at the revolutionary potential of cinema for an emergent working class, Adorno writes in 1947 looking at how Hollywood and Hitler used the same techniques to manufacture consent. Both are right within their frame, and the contemporary digital condition combines features of both diagnoses.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) makes the bold claim that the aura — the unique, here-and-now authority of the singular work tied to ritual and tradition — withers under mass reproduction. Photography and film, by producing endless identical copies, dissolve the "presence of that which is not present" [VERIFY p.?]. This dissolution has two sides. On the loss side, art loses its cult value: the Mona Lisa in the Louvre as object of pilgrimage gives way to the Mona Lisa on coffee mugs. On the gain side, art gains exhibition value: it circulates, it reaches mass audiences, it becomes available for political mobilisation. Crucially, Benjamin reads this gain as democratic. The medieval altarpiece existed for the priest and the donor; the cinema exists for the workers of Berlin and Moscow. The collective, distracted mode of cinematic reception is, for Benjamin, the new mode of critical engagement — not the silent contemplation of bourgeois aesthetics but the rowdy, talking, smoking, dancing audience of the popular theatre.

Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" is sharp: "Adopting Benjamin's designation of the traditional work of art by the concept of aura, the presence of that which is not present, the culture industry is defined by the fact that it does not strictly counterpose another principle to that of aura, but rather by the fact that it conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist. By this means the culture industry betrays its own ideological abuses" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 102). The culture industry does not destroy aura; it manufactures it. The star system "borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (p. 101) is the resurrection of aura around the celebrity. Marilyn Monroe, Elvis Presley, Michael Jackson, Beyoncé, Taylor Swift — these figures are mechanically reproduced (every image of Marilyn is a copy) and yet wreathed in a foggy mist of authenticity, personality and uniqueness. The Eras Tour film, simultaneously released in cinemas worldwide, is the apotheosis of this: a mechanically reproduced spectacle of mechanically reproducible aura.

The disagreement is therefore not about the mechanism but about which moment of the dialectic to emphasise. Benjamin sees the historical opening: the shift in the means of artistic production from ritual to politics. Adorno sees the historical closing: the recapture of that opening by monopoly capital. Both are correct historically — Soviet montage cinema (Eisenstein, Vertov) really did mobilise the revolutionary potential Benjamin identified; Nazi propaganda film (Riefenstahl) and Hollywood really did weaponise the same techniques.

The contemporary digital condition vindicates both. On the Benjamin side, the cost of production has collapsed: anyone with a smartphone can shoot, edit and distribute video; Bandcamp lets bedroom producers reach global audiences; TikTok produces a continuous folk-cultural feed of dance trends, comedy bits and political commentary that is more genuinely democratised than anything in 1935. The "means of mental production" have, in technical terms, been partially democratised — exactly the shift Benjamin hoped for. Wikipedia, free software, Creative Commons, the open-access movement: these are Benjaminian. On the Adorno side, the platforms that host this democratisation are owned by a handful of corporations whose business model is to manufacture and harvest attention. The TikTok algorithm decides which folk-cultural artefact goes viral; YouTube's recommender herds users into ideological filter bubbles; Instagram's filter culture manufactures the foggy mist of influencer aura around bodies, products and lifestyles. The Substack model is interestingly hybrid: ostensibly exhibition value (writers reach readers directly, bypassing publishers) but trading on cult value (paid subscriptions to "your favourite writer" reproduce the aura).

A further wrinkle: Benjamin's politics depended on the cinema being a *collective* mode of reception. The dance hall, the political rally, the cinema in 1935 brought bodies into shared space and rhythm. The digital condition fragments this. Streaming makes the cinema audience into a million individual living rooms; the Spotify algorithm makes the dance hall into a million pairs of earbuds. The collective Benjamin counted on for the revolutionary potential of reproducible art has been disaggregated. This is a deep problem for Benjamin's optimism and a partial vindication of Adorno's pessimism.

The synthesis I offer is that the politics of reproducibility is not fixed by the technology but by the ownership structure of the platform and the political organisation of the audience. Mechanical reproduction in worker-owned platforms (Bandcamp Friday, music co-ops like the Lancaster Music Co-op pictured in the slides, Mastodon, Pixelfed) tends Benjaminian. Mechanical reproduction in monopoly platforms tends Adornian. The same technology — a video file, a digital recording — does opposite political work depending on who owns the means of mental production.

I conclude that the statement in the question is right: the culture industry resurrects aura as a foggy mist. But Benjamin's hope is not refuted, only conditional on a politics of platform ownership that 2025 has not yet achieved.

### Essay 5 — Is the Frankfurt School's account of mass culture vindicated, refuted, or transformed by the rise of the algorithm?

I argue that the algorithm both vindicates and transforms the Frankfurt School's account. It vindicates the core diagnoses — standardisation, pseudo-individuation, the integration of consumers from above, the mass deception thesis — while transforming the mechanism: where 1947 culture industry worked through monopoly broadcasting and the studio system, the algorithm works through machine learning trained on user behaviour. This is not refutation; it is a more refined version of what Adorno predicted.

The vindication runs along four lines. First, standardisation. The Spotify recommender, the TikTok For You feed, the Netflix homepage, the YouTube suggested-videos sidebar all operate by clustering users and content into latent categories and recommending within-cluster items. The architecture is technically a standardising one: the more data, the tighter the clusters, the more predictable the recommendations. Genre conventions tighten over time because tracks that fit the cluster get streamed, get more data, become more recommendable. Adorno's claim that "what parades as progress in the culture industry, as the incessantly new which it offers up, remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100) describes the genre-template loop of the recommender almost perfectly.

Second, pseudo-individuation. The algorithm is the most refined pseudo-individuating device ever built. Each user's Discover Weekly playlist is technically unique — it is composed for that user from a personalised model — and yet the inputs are drawn from a small pool of label-promoted, engagement-tested tracks. The illusion of choice, the halo of free selection, is preserved while the actual menu is narrow. Adorno's phrase "pseudo-individualization... keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them" ("On Popular Music," [23]) reads as a literal description of Discover Weekly.

Third, integration from above. Adorno's "the customer is not king... not its subject but its object" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 99) names exactly what the platform business model does: the user is not the customer but the product, harvested for data which is sold to advertisers (in YouTube's case) or used to train the recommender (in Spotify's). Surveillance capitalism, in Shoshana Zuboff's term, is the Frankfurt School updated for the data economy.

Fourth, the mass deception thesis. Adorno claimed that "the total effect of the culture industry is one of anti-enlightenment... it impedes the development of autonomous, independent individuals who judge and decide consciously for themselves" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, [VERIFY p.?]). The 2010s gave us Cambridge Analytica, Facebook's role in election manipulation, YouTube's radicalisation pipelines, and the documented mental-health effects of Instagram on teenage girls. These are not Adornian as such but they are Adorno's worst-case scenario realised at platform scale.

The transformations are equally important. First, the unit of standardisation has shifted from the song or film to the engagement metric. The platform does not need every song to be standardised in the same way; it needs every song to be optimised for retention. The skeletons are more diverse than Adorno could have imagined (lo-fi hip-hop, hyperpop, drill, ambient, ASMR-pop) but each skeleton is engagement-tested.

Second, the locus of decision has shifted from human executives to machine-learned models. The studio mogul who personally chose what to greenlight has been replaced by the recommender team optimising for hours-watched. This makes Adorno's "from above" more impersonal — there may be no individual who could change the outcome — and harder to attribute. The recommender is closer to Marx's "law of value" than to a conscious ruling class. Conformity has replaced consciousness at the platform layer too.

Third, the politics of resistance has changed. The Birmingham School's answer to Adorno — that audiences read oppositionally, that subcultures recycle commodities into resistance — assumed a relatively stable cultural text. The algorithmic feed personalises in real time, absorbing oppositional readings as more training data. The platform learns from resistance. This makes Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model harder to apply: there is no stable encoding, and decoding is itself encoded.

Fourth, the means of mental production have democratised at the production layer while concentrating at the distribution layer. Anyone can make a song; the question is who will hear it. Bandcamp, Mastodon and the Fediverse are real alternatives; they remain small. The Lancaster Music Co-op exists; Spotify dwarfs it. The dialectic of digital culture is exactly this tension between democratised production and concentrated distribution.

The Birmingham objection (audiences are active, agency matters) still has force, but it is a weaker force than it was in 1980. When the platform personalises to my biases, my "active reading" feeds the model. The Foucauldian objection (power is productive, not just repressive) is more useful: the algorithm produces subjects, it does not merely manipulate pre-formed ones. This is consistent with Adorno's late writing — "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 104) — but adds a productive-power dimension Adorno did not have.

I conclude that the algorithm vindicates the Frankfurt School more than it refutes them, and transforms the framework in ways Adorno would have recognised: industrial culture has industrialised further. The honest critical position is neither dismissal of Adorno as elitist nor uncritical revival; it is updating his framework with the analytics of platform capitalism and the political-economic analysis of data infrastructure.

### Essay 6 — Discuss the strengths and limits of base-and-superstructure as a model for thinking about contemporary cultural production.

I argue that base-and-superstructure remains an indispensable analytical model for contemporary cultural production but only in the qualified form Engels, Althusser and Stuart Hall gave it: as a model of determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination, not as a one-way causal arrow. The model's strength is that it forces us to ask who owns the means of cultural production; its limit is that it can read culture as mere reflection if applied crudely.

Marx's formulation in the *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) is foundational: "The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation on which there arises a legal and political superstructure and to which there correspond definite forms of social consciousness" (Marx, *Contribution*, [VERIFY p.?]). The base is the relations of production (capital and labour, ownership of the means of production); the superstructure is law, politics, culture and "social consciousness." The base shapes, the superstructure maintains, in the lecture slide's diagram.

The strength of the model is that it makes ownership analytically primary. To understand Hollywood, ask who owns the studios (Disney, Universal, Warner Bros, Sony, Paramount). To understand recorded music, ask who owns the labels (the same three majors plus indie distribution layers) and the streaming platforms (Spotify, Apple, Amazon, YouTube). To understand news, ask who owns the publications (Murdoch, the Mail, GB News's backers). The Disney purchase of Lucasfilm in 2012, of Marvel in 2009, of Fox in 2019 — these are pure base-level events with massive superstructural consequences. Star Wars stops being George Lucas's imaginative property and becomes a franchise template optimised for theme-park crossover. Marvel becomes the Marvel Cinematic Universe with twenty-plus interlocking films. The base-driven nature of these cultural shifts is undeniable.

The same applies to music. The slide on monopoly — Kobalt, BMG, Universal, Warner, Sony plus Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify plus Ticketmaster and See Tickets — shows the means of mental production concentrated in a small number of corporate hands. The fact that Spotify's CEO Daniel Ek invests in arms manufacturing while paying musicians fractions of a cent per stream is not a coincidence; it is a base-level pattern (capital accumulates, labour does not) expressing itself across cultural and military sectors simultaneously.

The limit of the crude reading is that cultural artefacts are not reducible to their economic base. A Beethoven string quartet, a Public Enemy track, a Crass record and a Charli XCX single all share the same capitalist base but do radically different cultural work. Engels recognised this in *Anti-Dühring* and in his correspondence: the economic is determining only in the last instance; the superstructure has its own dynamics. Louis Althusser systematised this in his concepts of relative autonomy and overdetermination: ideological state apparatuses (schools, the church, the media, the family) do reproduce capitalist relations but they also have their own contradictions, their own histories, their own forms of resistance.

Stuart Hall pushed further: encoding and decoding are not symmetrical, and audiences make negotiated and oppositional readings that the encoder did not intend. The same Adorno-shaped culture industry that produces standardised pop also produces the conditions for punk's anti-commercial revolt, hip-hop's working-class voice, riot grrrl's feminist intervention. The base does not predict these outcomes; it constrains them. Saying that anti-capitalist music can still be sold as a commodity (Rage Against the Machine signed to Sony, Crass distributing via the conventional industry) is to acknowledge both the determining power of the base and the relative autonomy of the cultural form.

The contemporary update is platform capitalism. Here base-and-superstructure has to absorb a new layer: the data layer. Spotify is at once a base entity (it owns infrastructure, employs labour, generates surplus) and a superstructural one (it shapes taste, mediates artist-audience relations, produces cultural categories like the playlist mood). The data harvested from listening shapes what gets produced (artists write for playlist placement), which means the superstructure feeds back into the base in measurable ways. Cambridge Analytica showed that the same loop operates politically: data on cultural consumption shapes political targeting which shapes electoral outcomes which shape policy which shapes the economic base. The neat 1859 diagram does not have a place for this feedback.

The most honest contemporary use of base-and-superstructure is therefore as a critical heuristic: always ask who owns the means of mental production, but do not assume that ownership predicts content in a one-to-one way. The Marvel film and the Crass record share a base; they do different cultural work. The Spotify track and the Bandcamp track share a base; they do different cultural work. The base is necessary, not sufficient, for cultural analysis.

I conclude that base-and-superstructure, in its Althusserian-Hallian qualified form, remains the indispensable starting point for any sociology of contemporary cultural production. Crude versions are vulnerable to the standard objections; refined versions remain analytically essential. The lecturer's slide is right: the base shapes, the superstructure maintains, but the maintaining is itself a labour with its own contradictions.

### Essay 7 — "Entertainment is the prolongation of work under late capitalism." Evaluate Adorno and Horkheimer's claim using contemporary examples.

I argue that Adorno and Horkheimer's claim that entertainment is the prolongation of work under late capitalism is more accurate now than it was in 1944, because platform capitalism has dissolved the boundary between work and leisure to a degree they could not have anticipated, and the entertainment-as-recovery model has been replaced by entertainment-as-second-shift.

The original argument in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* runs: under industrial capitalism, the worker is subject to rationalised, mechanised, alienating labour. In her "free time" she seeks escape. But the culture industry produces entertainment that is structurally similar to the work she escapes from: standardised, repetitive, requiring no concentration, "pre-digested" (Adorno, "On Popular Music," [16]). Adorno puts it sharply in "On Popular Music": "Popular music is for the masses a perpetual busman's holiday" [32]. The entertainment relaxes by being patterned like the work; it offers no genuine break, only the reproduction of working-day rhythms in a slightly cushioned form. This is the prolongation thesis.

Three contemporary phenomena vindicate the thesis. First, scrolling. The TikTok or Instagram or Twitter feed is a leisure activity that mimics, formally, the structure of attention-labour: rapid, fragmented, interruptible, optimised for engagement. The smartphone-using leisure-time worker is doing, in her downtime, exactly what the call-centre worker is doing on shift (responding to prompts, processing information, performing affective availability). The leisure is the work, performed for no wage. Second, gamification. Step counters, language-learning apps, fitness trackers, productivity apps — these turn leisure activities into measurable, scoreable, optimisable tasks. The Duolingo streak is a self-imposed shift. Third, content-watching as a recovery practice that produces no real recovery. Empirical psychology shows that passive scroll-and-watch leisure correlates with lower mood and higher anxiety than active, embodied or social leisure; the entertainment fails as recovery because it is structurally too similar to the work.

The platform economy adds two further mechanisms. First, the "content creator" is the figure who has fully dissolved work and leisure: she is at work whenever she is on her phone, and she is on her phone for most of her waking hours. Her leisure is her productive labour and vice versa. This is Adorno's diagnosis literalised. Second, the gig economy applies the same logic to the supposedly recovering wage worker: the Uber driver, the Deliveroo rider, the Amazon Mechanical Turk worker can never fully clock off because the next ride/order/task is one app-notification away. Work has metastasised into all hours; "leisure" exists only as the time between gigs.

Reality television deserves separate treatment because it is the genre that most cleanly stages the prolongation thesis. The Apprentice, X Factor, Britain's Got Talent, Love Island — these formats turn aspects of life that should be outside the wage relation (talent, charm, romance) into competitive labour. The contestants work for the entertainment; the audience watches them work; the audience's watching is itself harvested as engagement data. The fantasy on offer is that "ordinary people can make it" — a star-system ideology Adorno identified as central to the culture industry (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 101) — but the mechanism is that everyone is now potentially on the talent show, performing for the camera that is always on.

The FYRE festival is the apex case. It was sold as luxury experiential leisure (pseudo-individuated escape from work) but delivered as a debacle of unmet promises. The marketing apparatus — Instagram influencers shooting orange-square promotional content — was the labour that produced the leisure-fantasy that produced the actual labour (festival-goers stranded on a Bahamian island sleeping in disaster-relief tents). The entertainment was the prolongation of work for the influencers who marketed it; the consumption of the entertainment was a deception that catastrophically failed to deliver even the prescribed gratification.

The "what about genuine leisure?" objection has force. People do play sport, garden, read books, walk dogs, have sex, sing in choirs, cook dinners. Not all entertainment is the prolongation of work; some of it is the genuine non-instrumental enjoyment Aristotle called *scholē*. But this is precisely Adorno's distinction between culture in the true sense — which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100) — and the culture industry, which assimilates and integrates. The choir and the dog walk are not the culture industry. The Netflix binge and the TikTok scroll are.

A further objection from Stuart Hall's tradition is that audiences resignify entertainment for their own purposes. The football match is not just standardised affective release; it is community, identity, embodied collective experience. Adorno's own remark in the slide that "sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection" is, frankly, weak. Football fans are not simply duped; they organise, they protest (against owners, against Super League proposals), they build solidarities. The thesis needs qualification: entertainment is more often the prolongation of work than the genuine alternative to it, but the line is not fixed by genre.

I conclude that Adorno and Horkheimer's claim describes the platform-capitalism present even more accurately than the 1944 industrial-capitalism present they wrote it for. The dissolution of the work-leisure boundary, the gamification of recovery, the gig-economy abolition of clocking off, the influencer-as-permanent-content-creator, and the reality-show conversion of life into competitive labour all vindicate the prolongation thesis. The objections from genuine leisure and active audience reception qualify it but do not refute it.

### Essay 8 — Does Walter Benjamin offer a more useful framework than Adorno for analysing the democratising potential of digital media?

I argue that Benjamin offers a more useful framework than Adorno for analysing the *productive* potential of digital media — what users and small producers do with cheap cameras, free software and direct distribution — while Adorno remains the more useful framework for analysing the *distributive* layer where platforms concentrate power. The honest answer is that the two frameworks describe different layers of the same digital condition and we need both.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) makes three moves that are extraordinarily useful for the digital condition. First, mechanical reproduction shatters the aura — the "presence of that which is not present," the here-and-now uniqueness tied to ritual and tradition. Second, this shattering is politically ambivalent: it can be co-opted by fascism (aestheticisation of politics) or mobilised by the left (politicisation of aesthetics). Third, the technological shift opens new modes of collective, distracted, critical reception — cinema audiences, dance halls, the photographic exhibition — that bourgeois contemplative aesthetics could not accommodate. "Society would be freer to organise and control themselves in their reception" (lecture slide).

The digital condition is Benjamin's prediction realised. The cost of producing high-quality video, audio, image and text has collapsed: a smartphone records 4K video; a laptop runs professional-grade audio software; Canva makes graphic design accessible to non-designers; ChatGPT, for better or worse, writes prose. The cost of distribution has also collapsed: anyone can post to YouTube, Bandcamp, Substack, Mastodon, Pixelfed, the open web. The means of mental production at the production layer have been democratised to a degree that 1935 Benjamin could not have imagined. The bedroom producer can release globally; the citizen journalist can document police brutality and broadcast it within minutes; the regional language preservation activist can publish a Wikipedia in her endangered language; the radical pamphleteer can reach more readers via a free Substack than the 1930s Communist Party paper reached.

Benjamin's exhibition value displaces cult value here exactly as predicted. The Mona Lisa is now mostly seen as a JPEG; the album is now mostly heard as a stream; the news is now mostly read as a feed. The auratic, unique, originating object has been replaced by the endlessly copiable file. And, as Benjamin hoped, this creates new political possibilities: meme culture as collective political speech, the Black Lives Matter cell-phone footage that no studio would have produced, the Arab Spring's social-media coordination, the open-source software commons.

But Adorno's response remains forceful at the *distributive* layer. The democratised production tools are hosted on platforms owned by a handful of corporations whose business model is to harvest attention. As Adorno put it, "the culture industry... conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 102). The influencer's Instagram aesthetic — the curated grid, the soft-focus authenticity, the personal-brand "vulnerability" — is the foggy-mist aura reconstructed around the mass-reproduced self. The TikTok algorithm is the integration-from-above that Benjamin's framework does not anticipate. Cambridge Analytica is the aestheticisation of politics Benjamin warned about, returned in data-targeted form. The Spotify Discover Weekly playlist is the manufactured personalisation Adorno called pseudo-individuation.

The cleanest way to combine the two is to distinguish layers. The *production layer* of digital culture is Benjaminian: cheap tools, democratised access, collective and distracted modes of creation and reception. The *distribution layer* is Adornian: concentrated, algorithmic, profit-extracting, integrating-from-above. The *interface* between them is where the political battle is fought. Net neutrality, platform regulation, antitrust action against Big Tech, the development of public-service or co-operatively owned platforms (Bandcamp at small scale, the BBC at national scale, Mastodon at federated scale), the right to repair and to data portability — these are all attempts to keep the Benjaminian production layer from being captured by the Adornian distribution layer.

Two further considerations matter. First, Benjamin's optimism depended on the *collective* mode of cinema reception — bodies in shared space, the working class assembling. Digital media has partially fragmented this: each user has her own feed, her own earbuds, her own filter bubble. The collective conditions for the political-aesthetic mobilisation Benjamin hoped for have been disaggregated. The 2010s saw both the Arab Spring (Benjaminian collective mobilisation through digital tools) and the 2016 election interference (Adornian manufactured consent through targeted personalisation). The digital sword cuts both ways.

Second, Benjamin's analysis has an under-specified theory of ownership. He notices the shift in the means of artistic production but does not develop, as Adorno does, the analysis of monopoly capital's recapture of those means. This is the limit of pure Benjamin: he gives us the productive possibilities but not the political-economic constraints. Combining him with Marx and Adorno gives us the constraints.

I conclude that Benjamin is more useful for analysing the productive layer of digital media (cheap tools, democratised production, collective making), Adorno more useful for the distributive layer (platforms, algorithms, monopoly capital). The contemporary critical task is to deploy both. The aura is shattered in Benjamin's sense (the unique object has been replaced by the copiable file) and reconstructed in Adorno's sense (the foggy mist around the platform-mediated celebrity). The political question is which layer politics can transform.

### Essay 9 — "Commodity fetishism is the foundational concept of any critical sociology of culture." Discuss.

I argue that commodity fetishism is one of two foundational concepts (alongside the ruling-class control of the means of mental production) for any critical sociology of culture, and that its strength is its ability to explain why social relations between people appear, in capitalist cultures, as relations between things. The streaming economy, the influencer economy, AI-generated content and the platform commodity are all best analysed through fetishism, and the concept's main limit is that it needs to be supplemented with theories of subjectivity (Frankfurt psychoanalysis) and reception (Hall) to do full cultural-analytic work.

Marx's definition in *Capital Volume 1* is the canonical passage: "The relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation of objects which exists outside them... It is a particular social relation between men themselves which in their eyes assumes a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things... This is what I call fetishism; it attaches itself to the products of labour as soon as they are produced as commodities" (Marx, *Capital* Vol 1, 1867, [VERIFY p.?]). Three elements: real social relations exist between producers; these relations are misrecognised as properties of the commodity itself; the misrecognition is inseparable from commodity production. The commodity acquires a phantasmagorical (dreamlike, magical) quality that obscures the labour that produced it.

For cultural analysis the concept is indispensable because culture is, in capitalism, principally produced as commodities. The 2025 top-ten chart pictured in the lecture slide — Alex Warren's "Ordinary," Gracie Abrams's "That's So True," Lola Young's "Messy," Chappell Roan's "Pink Pony Club" — appears as a list of self-standing creative achievements with their own auratic properties (Chappell Roan is "authentic"; the song is "good"). The labour behind each track — songwriters at writing camps, session musicians, producers, mixers, mastering engineers, music-video crews, social-media marketing teams, distribution logistics, the streaming-platform engineers and content moderators — is invisible. The track stands as a thing. This is fetishism in Marx's exact sense.

The lecturer's example of Richard Branson is the textbook teaching case. Branson appears wealthy because clever, because entrepreneurial, because risk-taking. The actual material basis of his wealth is the exploitation of musicians who signed early Virgin contracts giving the label most of the rights and most of the revenue. Fetishism is the operation by which the social relation (Branson capitalises on signed labour) appears as a property of the thing (Branson has a magical talent for business). The same operation gives Spotify's Daniel Ek his net worth, Disney's Bob Iger his bonuses, and Netflix's content slate its veneer of creative achievement.

The streaming economy displays fetishism with peculiar clarity. When I press play on Spotify, what I experience is "the song" — a self-standing aesthetic object. What is actually happening is an enormously complex social relation: a server farm somewhere consumes electricity to deliver an audio file; a recommender system trained on millions of users surfaces this track; a label collects most of the per-stream revenue; the songwriter and performer collect fractions of a cent; the data of my listening is harvested and fed back into the model. None of this appears in the listening experience. The phantasmagorical relation between things — me and the song — is the surface; the social relation between humans is concealed.

The influencer economy is fetishism in concentrated form. The influencer's apparent qualities (style, taste, authenticity) are presented as self-standing properties of the individual. The labour behind these qualities — the photographer, the editor, the stylist, the brand-partnership manager, the camera-operator partner, the algorithmic infrastructure that delivers eyeballs — is invisible. The influencer is the commodity-fetish made human: a phantasmagorical relation between her and her followers that conceals the social relations of platform capitalism.

AI-generated content (Suno, Udio for music; Midjourney for images; ChatGPT for prose) is fetishism reaching new heights. The "AI" appears to produce. What it actually does is recombine the labour of millions of human producers whose work was scraped to train the model. The musicians whose recordings trained Suno, the artists whose images trained Midjourney, the writers whose prose trained ChatGPT — these are the unpaid producers. The "AI-generated track" is the most thoroughly fetishised commodity yet: a relation between things (user, model, output) that conceals a vast social relation of unrecognised labour.

The Frankfurt School extends the analysis through the concept of reification (Lukács, then Adorno): not only the commodity but the social relation itself becomes thing-like. The Spotify mood-playlist makes my emotional state ("sad girl autumn") into a thing I select; the dating app makes intimate connection into a swipe; the news algorithm makes political opinion into a position to be A/B tested. The whole field of social experience is reified into selectable, comparable, exchangeable units. Adorno's "cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100) is the cultural-theoretic deepening of Marx's fetishism.

The objection to Marx is that fetishism, taken alone, makes audiences look passive. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding shows that audiences can read against the grain, denaturalise the fetish, recognise the labour. The objection has force: fetishism is a tendency, not a guarantee, and there are real practices (unionising in the music industry, the BAFTA campaign for streamer transparency, Bandcamp Friday, the Lancaster Music Co-op) that de-fetishise by re-foregrounding labour. But these are minority practices; the dominant cultural experience under platform capitalism is fetishistic in Marx's exact sense.

I conclude that commodity fetishism is foundational, alongside the means of mental production, for any critical sociology of culture. The Frankfurt School deepens it into reification; the Birmingham School supplements it with reception theory; contemporary platform critique applies it to algorithms and AI. Without it, cultural analysis loses the central insight that the appearance of culture under capitalism is structurally misleading about its production.

### Essay 10 — Compare Adorno's analysis of the star system with the contemporary phenomenon of the "content creator" and the influencer economy.

I argue that the content creator and the influencer are the contemporary culmination of the star system Adorno analysed: industrial in production, individualistic in marketing, ideological in the message that "the ordinary person can make it," and now democratised in the sense that the threshold of entry has dropped while the political-economic structure of concentration has tightened. The result is a more pervasive, more participatory and in some ways more reactionary version of the system Adorno diagnosed.

Adorno's analysis runs: "Its ideology above all makes use of the star system, borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation. The more dehumanized its methods of operation and content, the more diligently and successfully the culture industry propagates supposedly great personalities and operates with heart-throbs" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 101). The star system performs two functions. First, it gives the standardised, industrially produced cultural commodity an "individual air" — the aura around the star compensates for the absence of aura in the product. Second, it propagates the ideology that the ordinary person can make it: anyone can be a star, with talent and luck, which means the system that mostly produces precarity and exploitation appears as a meritocracy that anyone can win.

The classical Hollywood star system Adorno analysed had narrow gates. The studios — MGM, Warner, RKO, Fox, Paramount — owned the contracts, controlled the publicity machine, manufactured the personae. To be a star you had to be selected, signed, styled and promoted by the studio. The numerical scarcity of stars was structural: a star was a star precisely because most people were not.

The 1990s and 2000s reality-TV wave widened the gates and intensified the ideology. X Factor, Britain's Got Talent, Pop Idol, American Idol: the format dramatises the "ordinary person can make it" myth by parading thousands of hopefuls, winnowing them through judges, and crowning a winner who is, briefly, marketable. The winners' contracts are notoriously bad; most are dropped within a year; the actual material conditions of the music industry remain unchanged. But the format successfully reproduces the ideology: anyone could be next.

The 2010s YouTube wave dropped the gates further. The "YouTuber" — PewDiePie, MrBeast, Logan Paul — is the figure that exemplifies the new star system. They were not signed by a studio; they made their own content, optimised for the platform, accumulated subscribers, monetised through ads and brand deals. The platform replaces the studio as gatekeeper, and the gatekeeping is algorithmic rather than executive. The lecture slide's chart — "25 biggest music artists on YouTube 2025" alongside the YouTube valuation chart ($183bn, ahead of Netflix at $98bn) — captures this shift: YouTube is the new star-making machinery, larger than any 1940s studio.

The 2020s TikTok and Instagram wave dropped the gates to the smartphone. The influencer is the figure of this stage. She does not need a record contract or even a YouTube channel; a Reels account and a few viral hits can build a follower base that monetises through brand partnerships, affiliate links and her own product lines. The "ordinary person can make it" ideology reaches its peak here: literally anyone with a phone can become a content creator. And literally anyone does — there are estimated to be over fifty million people who consider themselves creators worldwide, of whom perhaps two million earn meaningful income.

Adorno's diagnosis applies to all three waves but with intensifications. First, "individual air" is now performed by the creator herself. The studio publicist of 1947 manufactured personae; the influencer of 2025 manufactures her own persona, with the algorithm as silent collaborator. This is more participatory and, in a sense, more authentic-feeling — and therefore more ideologically powerful. The fetishised commodity (the personal brand) is presented as the unmediated self. Second, the "ordinary person can make it" ideology is now empirically verifiable in a few cases (MrBeast really did go from nothing to billionaire) which gives the lottery ticket more plausibility. The structural rate of success is still low — most creators earn less than minimum wage — but the visibility of the winners is much higher.

The contemporary case studies sharpen the point. The K-pop manufactured group (BTS, BLACKPINK, NewJeans) is the classical studio system updated: trainees signed at twelve or thirteen, drilled for years in singing, dancing, language and image, debuted in groups designed by A&R committees. This is the most thoroughly Adornian production process imaginable, and it produces some of the most globally successful cultural commodities of the 2020s. The MCU's "supposedly great personalities" — the actors locked into multi-film contracts, the directors hired and fired by the franchise — are stars in the industrial sense, manufactured to fit pre-existing roles. FYRE festival, marketed by orange-square posts from a coordinated influencer push, is the influencer star system applied to event commerce, with predictable disaster.

There is one development Adorno did not fully anticipate: the participatory layer. Fans of K-pop groups, MCU films and creators are not just consumers; they participate in marketing through fan accounts, edits, theory videos, stan culture. The labour of audience devotion is itself harvested by the system. This is more than Adorno's "object of calculation" — it is a productive deepening of fan labour into industry labour. Mark Fisher and Henry Jenkins have written about this with different valences (Jenkins celebratory, Fisher critical).

The substantial objection from active-audience theory is that fans are not duped, that participation builds community, that the influencer-follower relation can be a genuine social good. This has some force — fan communities really do produce meaning, friendship and solidarity. But the substantial reply is that the system that hosts these meanings extracts most of the economic value from them and is structurally extractive of the labour that produces them.

I conclude that the content creator and the influencer are the maturation, not the supersession, of the star system Adorno analysed. The gates have dropped; the ideology has spread; the industrial concentration has tightened. Adorno's framework, slightly updated for the platform layer, predicts the influencer economy with surprising accuracy.

### Essay 11 — Is the Frankfurt School's pessimism about popular culture an indispensable corrective to celebratory accounts, or a paternalistic dead end?

I argue that the Frankfurt School's pessimism is an indispensable corrective to celebratory accounts of popular culture, but that some of its specific moves (especially Adorno's writing on jazz) are paternalistic and racially blinkered in ways that have to be acknowledged and corrected. The honest position is to retain the structural critique (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, commodity fetishism, integration from above) while abandoning the assumption that "serious" European art music is the implicit measure of cultural value.

The celebratory account of popular culture, dominant in much 1990s-2000s cultural studies and a great deal of contemporary platform-discourse, runs: audiences are active, popular culture is meaningful, subcultures resist, fans co-create, the long tail democratises, digital media empowers, anyone-can-make-it is broadly true. This account has real evidence behind it. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding shows audiences read negotiated and oppositional readings. Dick Hebdige's *Subculture* shows working-class kids reassembled commodities into resistance (punk, mod, skinhead). Henry Jenkins's *Textual Poachers* shows fan communities producing meaning. The Birmingham School tradition is rich, empirically grounded and politically generous.

But the celebratory account, taken alone, has three blind spots that the Frankfurt School corrects. First, it tends to read off active reception from the *existence* of active reception, ignoring the structural conditions under which most reception is passive, distracted and integrated. Adorno's claim that "popular music is for the masses a perpetual busman's holiday" (Adorno, "On Popular Music," [32]) is, empirically, more often true than the celebratory account allows. Most listening is background, most viewing is half-attended, most scrolling is automatic. The active-audience evidence is mostly drawn from articulate subcultures; the modal experience is closer to Adorno's.

Second, the celebratory account tends to treat the market as a neutral medium of choice. The Frankfurt School insists on the political economy: who owns the means of mental production, who profits, who labours, who decides what gets produced. The current concentration of cultural production in a few corporate hands (three major labels, three streamers, six studios, four social-media platforms) is invisible if you only look at audience meanings. Adorno's "cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100) is the corrective.

Third, the celebratory account tends to be politically optimistic about market democratisation. Cambridge Analytica, the YouTube radicalisation pipeline, the Instagram body-image research, the documented harms of algorithmic amplification — these are the empirical vindication of Frankfurt pessimism over Hall optimism. The celebratory account has trouble explaining why the platforms that supposedly democratise have produced such corrosive political and psychological outcomes.

The paternalism objection is, however, real. Adorno's writing on jazz is the clearest case. He treated jazz as a degraded form of "serious" music, missed its African-American roots, missed its improvisational sophistication, missed the cultural-political work it did for Black Americans. The standard of value implicit in his writing was European concert music (Beethoven, Schoenberg). This is a major intellectual failure, not just a stylistic preference. Paul Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic*, Amiri Baraka's *Blues People*, Robin D. G. Kelley's writing on jazz, and the entire tradition of Black music studies show how disabling this blind spot was.

The deeper paternalism is the implicit assumption that ordinary people are dupes who need critical theory to see what is happening to them. This is the standard Birmingham School objection and it has force. People are often more sceptical of their own consumption than Adorno gave them credit for; the meta-awareness of being marketed to is now widespread (the "I know it's manipulation but I enjoy it anyway" stance Adorno actually describes well in "Culture Industry Reconsidered": "If it guarantees them even the most fleeting gratification they desire a deception which is nonetheless transparent to them" (p. 103)). But Adorno's own response — that the transparency of the deception does not free people from it — remains forceful.

The most productive contemporary use of the Frankfurt School is therefore selective. Retain the structural diagnosis (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, integration from above, commodity fetishism, the dialectic of enlightenment). Drop the implicit European-art-music measure of value. Combine with Birmingham reception theory to get a full picture. Add Foucauldian productive power and Gilroy's diasporic frame to fill the gaps Adorno could not see. This is roughly what contemporary critical cultural studies (Mark Fisher, Jodi Dean, Wendy Chun, Jonathan Beller) actually does.

The 2025 evidence on which the case rests includes: continued concentration of cultural ownership; algorithmic curation of cultural consumption; the documented mental-health effects of social-media platforms; the rise of AI-generated content that further reduces the labour visible in cultural commodities; the gig-economy precaritisation of cultural workers; the platform extraction of fan labour. All of this is more readable in Frankfurt terms than in pure Birmingham terms.

I conclude that the Frankfurt School's pessimism is an indispensable corrective, not a paternalistic dead end, provided we abandon its Eurocentric implicit measure of value and combine it with reception theory and contemporary political-economic analysis. The honest critical position is neither dismissal nor revival; it is selective inheritance.

### Essay 12 — How does the concept of pseudo-individuation help us understand reality television and the manufactured pop group?

I argue that pseudo-individuation is the single most useful Adornian concept for analysing reality TV and the manufactured pop group, because both formats produce intense impressions of individual personality, talent and authenticity while being structurally standardised down to the format level. The concept's strength is that it names the gap between the experience of difference and the reality of sameness; its limit is that it underestimates the genuine fan-labour and identification dynamics that build around these formats.

Adorno's definition in "On Popular Music" is precise: "Pseudo-individualization... endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice or open market on the basis of standardization itself. Standardization of song hits keeps the customers in line by doing their listening for them, as it were. Pseudo-individualization, for its part, keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them" [23]. The two elements: standardisation does the structural work; pseudo-individuation disguises it as choice. The 1941 example was sweet vs swing, Benny Goodman vs Guy Lombardo — apparently distinctive but fundamentally interchangeable.

Reality TV is the format that has industrialised pseudo-individuation at the level of personality. The format is rigidly standardised: the audition rounds, the boot camp, the live shows, the public vote, the dramatic exit. Pop Idol, American Idol, X Factor, The Voice, Britain's Got Talent share this skeleton across over a hundred national franchises. Within the skeleton, the contestants are presented as intensely individual: the back-story (the single mother, the bullied teenager, the bereaved widower), the personal style, the choice of song, the "journey." The format produces, week after week, the impression of irreducible individuality. The reality is that the contestants are pre-selected to fit narrative archetypes (the underdog, the diva, the joker, the dark horse), styled to fit camera-ready templates, coached on song choices that maximise vote draw, and contractually bound to the format's parent company. The winner emerges as a brand-new "individual" who is in fact a continuation of the format's eternal sameness.

The Adornian touch is sharpened by the explicit star-system ideology the format propagates. "Anyone can make it" is the explicit text of the format: ordinary people stepping in front of the judges, the dream of stardom, the Cinderella moment of the public vote. The actual material conditions of post-show life — drop contracts, debt to the management company, brief stardom followed by obscurity — are invisible during the run. The format does Adorno's work for him: the star system "propagates supposedly great personalities" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 101) while the standardised industrial process behind them is hidden.

The manufactured pop group sharpens this further. K-pop is the textbook case. Trainees are recruited (often at twelve or thirteen) and signed to multi-year contracts at companies like HYBE (BTS), YG (BLACKPINK), SM (NCT), JYP. They are drilled in singing, dancing, languages, image management, social-media performance. Groups are debuted with members assigned narrowly defined roles (rapper, lead vocalist, dancer, visual, maknae). The songs are written by international songwriting camps drawing on global pop conventions. The choreography is intensively rehearsed. The "personality" of each member is produced through carefully scripted variety-show appearances, fan-meet content, social-media interactions.

The pseudo-individuation is, structurally, total. Each member of BTS appears intensely individual (V the artistic one, Jimin the elegant dancer, Suga the introspective rapper, RM the philosophical leader). Fans (ARMY) identify intensely with their bias, the chosen member; they participate in elaborate fan economies, mass-streaming campaigns, charity drives. The strength of identification appears to refute Adorno's framework — these are not duped consumers but engaged co-producers. But the structural sameness is, on inspection, undeniable: HYBE's other groups (TXT, ENHYPEN, NewJeans, Le Sserafim) repeat the same five-to-seven-member template, the same training pipeline, the same role distribution, the same fan-engagement model. The "individuation" is real at the level of subjective fan experience and pseudo at the level of industrial production.

Three updates to Adorno are warranted. First, the participatory dimension is more developed than 1941 Adorno anticipated. K-pop fans engage in massive labour — mass streaming, voting in awards, organising charity drives, defending their group against critics — that 1940s pop fans did not. This labour is genuinely productive (it builds community, friendship, political coalitions) and genuinely extractive (the company captures most of the economic value). It is both/and, not either/or.

Second, the global circulation of these formats reveals a cultural-imperial dimension Adorno's American-centred analysis missed. The format is sold from country to country (X Factor has franchises in dozens of nations; The Apprentice has Trump's version and many others), and the K-pop industrial model is now being exported (HYBE acquired Ithaca Holdings, Korean training methods are being studied in the US). The standardisation is now global, the pseudo-individuation is now glocal (local adaptations of global templates).

Third, the affective intensity of fan identification is genuinely different from passive consumption. ARMY's mass-mobilisation for the K-pop fandom flooding of racist hashtags during the 2020 BLM protests, or the financial mobilisation around BTS comebacks, shows that pseudo-individuated culture can be the substrate of real collective action. This is closer to Benjamin's distracted-collective reception than to Adorno's atomised consumption.

I conclude that pseudo-individuation is the indispensable analytical tool for reality TV and the manufactured pop group, that it explains the gap between experienced individuality and structural sameness, and that it needs updating for the participatory and global dimensions of contemporary pop. Adorno was right; he just was not right about everything.

### Essay 13 — "Conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno). Discuss with reference to social media and platform capitalism.

I argue that Adorno's claim that "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 104) is the most prescient single sentence in his cultural writing for the social-media age, because the platform economy has engineered conformity into the design layer in ways that pre-empt rather than override conscious reflection. The claim is best read as descriptive of a tendency that the platforms have radicalised, not as a complete dismissal of consciousness as such.

Adorno's full sentence runs: "The categorical imperative of the culture industry no longer has anything in common with freedom. It proclaims: you shall conform, without instruction as to what; conform to that which exists anyway, and to that which everyone thinks anyway as a reflex of its power and omnipresence. The power of the culture industry's ideology is such that conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 104). Two things to notice. First, the imperative is contentless — not "conform to fascism" or "conform to consumerism" but "conform to that which exists anyway." Second, the mechanism is the reflex of power and omnipresence — sheer ubiquity does the work, not argument.

Social media is the most omnipresent cultural form in history. The average user spends two-and-a-half hours a day on social platforms; for teenagers and young adults the figure exceeds four hours. The platforms are designed by behavioural-engineering teams to maximise engagement; the engagement metrics reward content that triggers fast emotional response (outrage, vindication, schadenfreude, envy, anxiety, sexual interest, parasocial identification). The result is a continuous stream of conformity pressure that operates at the pre-conscious level: the like button, the scroll velocity, the algorithmic feed, the FYP curation, the notification ping. None of this requires consciousness; all of it shapes behaviour.

Three mechanisms instantiate Adorno's claim. First, normative cascades. When a user sees that a particular take, aesthetic or political position is well-liked within her feed, she is pre-emptively cued to agree, to like, to share. Decades of conformity research (Asch, Sherif, the contemporary Goldberg and Stein work on social-media cascades) show that visible majority opinion produces conformity even when the individual privately disagrees. Platforms make majority opinion visible in real time. Adorno's "that which everyone thinks anyway" is now empirically rendered as the like count.

Second, algorithmic personalisation produces filter bubbles that reduce the user's exposure to disconfirming views. The TikTok For You page, after a few minutes of training, presents content optimised for the user's revealed preferences. Within the bubble, dissent disappears; the conformity to the bubble is invisible because the bubble feels like the world. This is exactly Adorno's "as a reflex of its power and omnipresence" — the omnipresent feed produces conformity by being all there is to see.

Third, the design layer makes conscious reflection effortful. Every consumer-experience design choice on a social platform is optimised to reduce friction, to make the next action automatic. The infinite scroll, the autoplay, the swipe-to-dismiss, the one-tap reaction — these all remove the moment of pause where consciousness could intervene. The platform is literally engineered to produce conformity without consciousness.

Cambridge Analytica is the political application. The firm bought Facebook data, modelled personality profiles, targeted political ads tuned to the receiver's psychological vulnerabilities. Voters were nudged toward Brexit and Trump not through argument but through emotional priming below the threshold of reflective consideration. This is Adorno's claim weaponised: conformity to a political outcome produced without engaging consciousness. The 2016-2018 reckoning showed that the platforms' design did not just facilitate this — it made it possible at scale.

The Instagram body-image research (the leaked internal studies showing teenage girls feeling worse about their bodies after using the app) is another instance. The platform's image-rich, comparison-inviting design produces conformity to a thin-and-pretty norm; the conformity registers as anxiety, depression, eating disorders; the consciousness of being shaped is partial and after-the-fact. The young woman knows abstractly that influencers are filtered and curated; she still feels worse about her own body. Adorno's "conformity has replaced consciousness" describes this exactly.

The standard objection is that consciousness is not extinct: the meta-awareness of being manipulated is widespread, critical journalism about platforms is plentiful, regulatory pushback (GDPR, the EU Digital Services Act, the US push for TikTok divestment) has been substantial. People do reflect. They do switch platforms, take breaks, delete accounts. Adorno did acknowledge this — he wrote of "their deep unconscious mistrust, the last residue of the difference between art and empirical reality" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 105) — but he thought it inadequate to break the loop. The contemporary evidence is mixed: collective action against platforms exists but is small relative to the scale of the systems.

A further objection comes from Foucault: power is productive, not just repressive. The platform does not just replace consciousness with conformity; it produces a particular kind of self-aware, self-presenting, self-monitoring subject. This is more than Adorno's claim, but compatible with it: the platform-produced subject is conscious in a particular way (conscious of self-image, conscious of metrics, conscious of one's brand) that aligns with conformity to platform norms. Conformity has replaced consciousness in the sense Adorno meant — that critical, autonomous, deliberative consciousness has been displaced — but produced a different kind of consciousness in its place.

The Substack-style intellectual ecosystem and the Mastodon-style decentralised platforms are partial counter-examples: they have user designs that slow the feed, encourage longer-form attention, reduce algorithmic personalisation. They have not, so far, scaled to challenge the dominant platforms. The political question is whether they can.

I conclude that Adorno's claim is the most prescient single sentence in his cultural writing for the platform age. The platforms have engineered conformity into the design layer; consciousness has not been extinguished but has been displaced from the centre of cultural decision-making. The corrective politics is exactly Adorno's: take the culture industry "seriously critically," refuse to cower before its monopolistic character (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 102), build platforms that reinstate the friction in which consciousness can operate.

### Essay 14 — Can independent, radical or anti-capitalist art exist within the culture industry? Discuss with concrete examples.

I argue that independent, radical or anti-capitalist art can exist within the culture industry but always in compromised form, because the industry commodifies what it captures and the only fully uncompromised art exists in spaces that have at least partially exited industrial circulation. The honest answer to the question is "yes, but it costs something" — and the cost is exactly what Adorno identified: the absorption of opposition into the form of the commodity.

Adorno's strongest version of the claim is that the culture industry is total: "cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 100). On this reading, no art produced within the industry is innocent of its commodity logic. The radical content is contained by the standardised form; the political message is digested as entertainment; the cathartic release defuses the political energy. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, "On Popular Music," [43]).

The lecture slide on commodification gives the textbook case: Rage Against the Machine, signed to Sony, releasing anti-capitalist anthems through a major label. The contradiction is real and Adorno would have ridiculed it. The band's commitment is genuine: they have funded prisoner-defence campaigns, donated royalties to political causes, used their platform to speak against US foreign policy. The form is exactly what Adorno predicted: anti-capitalist message delivered through capitalist distribution channels, profits accruing to Sony shareholders, anger commodified into rock anthems that fans can scream along to before going back to work on Monday. "Killing in the Name" reached number one in the UK Christmas charts in 2009 as a protest against X Factor's chart domination — itself a beautifully Adornian moment where anti-culture-industry protest is staged through a culture-industry mechanism.

The pure-resistance position would be: refuse the industry entirely, distribute through anarchist networks, sell only at cost, accept obscurity. Crass, the British anarcho-punk collective whose "Feeding of the Five Thousand" appears in the slides, came closer to this. They ran their own label (Crass Records), sold records at low prices, refused mainstream press, sent profits to political causes. Their distribution was through anarchist bookshops and gig networks. They built an explicitly anti-capitalist musical culture. The cost was relative obscurity: Crass never reached Rage Against the Machine's audience, and Crass's politics travelled less far. The trade-off is real: full ideological purity buys limited cultural reach.

Bandcamp offers an interesting middle path. The platform allows artists to sell directly to fans, set their own prices, control their own catalogue, and pay no recoupment to a label. On Bandcamp Fridays (the day each month when Bandcamp waives its share of revenue) artists keep almost all of the income from a sale. This is more like a folk-cultural commons than an industry: lots of small producers, direct artist-fan relations, no concentration of A&R power. The Lancaster Music Co-op shown in the slides is the analogous institution at the physical-space level: worker-owned rehearsal space, music education, gig-promotion that returns income to musicians rather than to investors.

These spaces are real but not infinite. Bandcamp was acquired by Epic Games in 2022 and then by Songtradr in 2023 — its independence is conditional on a market position that could change. Music co-ops depend on volunteer labour, grants, and supportive landlords. They exist in the cracks. The question is whether the cracks add up to a counter-culture or merely a niche.

Three further cases illuminate the question. First, hip-hop. The genre emerged from working-class Black communities in the South Bronx as a folk-cultural form (block parties, DJ battles, MC competitions, breakdance circles). Within a decade it was a major industrial genre with Sugar Hill, Def Jam, and ultimately every major label. The journey from Public Enemy's "Fight the Power" to Drake's brand-deal-soaked output is the journey Adorno predicted: the folk-cultural radical form becomes industrially captured. But the genre has not been fully absorbed: Kendrick Lamar's *To Pimp a Butterfly* (2015) is on a major label and is genuinely anti-racist, anti-capitalist art; the underground hip-hop scene (Backwoodz Studioz, R.A.P. Ferreira, billy woods) operates outside major-label structures with serious political content.

Second, riot grrrl. The 1990s feminist punk movement built a parallel infrastructure (zines, independent labels, DIY tours, fanzine networks) explicitly to resist the male-dominated industry. Some of its energy was absorbed by the mainstream (the Lilith Fair commercialisation, Sleater-Kinney's eventual major-label success); much of it remained outside, building a feminist musical commons that fed later movements.

Third, the internet's free-software and open-content commons (Linux, Wikipedia, Creative Commons, Mastodon, the Internet Archive) are the most successful sustained alternative to the culture industry yet built. They produce enormously valuable cultural infrastructure without commodification. Adorno did not anticipate this; Benjamin's optimism is partially vindicated here.

The honest synthesis: independent, radical and anti-capitalist art can exist within the culture industry but always at a cost (compromise with the commodity form, the risk of catharsis-without-resistance), and the strongest forms exist in spaces that have at least partially exited the industry (Crass-style autonomy, Bandcamp-direct sales, free-software commons, music co-ops). The political work is to defend and extend the partially-exited spaces while engaging critically with the commodified ones.

I conclude that the question's "can" is the right modal: it can, but only with awareness of the cost. The pure-resistance position trades reach for purity; the within-industry position trades purity for reach; the partially-exited middle path is the most productive but politically precarious. Adorno's diagnosis remains the analytical baseline; the politics has to invent its way out.

### Essay 15 — Critically assess the claim that the Frankfurt School "reads off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves."

I argue that the criticism that the Frankfurt School "reads off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves" is half-right and half-wrong: half-right because Adorno especially often does infer audience effects from formal analysis without empirical reception studies, half-wrong because contemporary platform-capitalism research has shown that formal analysis of products is actually a reliable predictor of effects when the products are mass-produced and the audience is exposed at scale. The most defensible position is to retain Frankfurt formal critique while supplementing it with empirical reception evidence.

The criticism, as articulated in the standard lecture slide and in critics from Stuart Hall to John Fiske to David Morley, is that the Frankfurt School commits an analytic fallacy: it analyses the cultural product (the song, the film, the radio programme), identifies its standardised features, and then infers from these features the consciousness of the audience. The audience is treated as a passive recipient of the product's content. No empirical research on actual audiences is done. The argument is theory-heavy and evidence-light.

The criticism has real force when applied to Adorno's writing on jazz. In "On Jazz" (1936, under pseudonym) and parts of "On Popular Music" (1941) Adorno makes sweeping claims about the psychic effects of jazz on listeners ("masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism" ["On Popular Music," [37]) based almost entirely on formal analysis of the music, with no engagement with how Black American audiences actually heard or used jazz, with no understanding of its African-American cultural roots, and with no empirical study of listener response. The result is racially blinkered, paternalistic, and analytically weak.

The criticism applies less forcefully to other Frankfurt work. *The Authoritarian Personality* (Adorno et al., 1950) is an enormous empirical study of how fascist tendencies correlate with personality structure, using interviews, the F-scale, and survey methods. Leo Lowenthal did empirical content analysis of magazine biographies showing the shift from "idols of production" (industrialists, scientists) to "idols of consumption" (entertainers, athletes) — a finding that anticipates contemporary celebrity studies. Erich Fromm's work combined Freudian theory with empirical social-psychological research. The Frankfurt School is not uniformly armchair theorising.

Adorno's response to the criticism, anticipated in "Culture Industry Reconsidered," is twofold. First: "It is true that thorough research has not, for the time being, produced an airtight case proving the regressive effects of particular products of the culture industry. No doubt an imaginatively designed experiment could achieve this more successfully than the powerful financial interests concerned would find comfortable" (Adorno, *The Culture Industry*, p. 105). He acknowledges the empirical gap and notes that the gap is partly produced by the political economy of research (who funds reception studies). Second: "It can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (p. 105). Even without empirical proof of any single product's effect, the cumulative exposure to standardised forms is itself a reasonable basis for inference about long-term effects.

Contemporary research has partially vindicated Adorno on this second point. Three lines of empirical work support reading-off-from-product analysis when the product is mass-distributed. First, social-psychology research on mere-exposure effects shows that repeated exposure to standardised stimuli produces measurable preference shifts. The pop song repeated three hundred times on commercial radio is heard differently from the song heard once. Adorno's "incessantly drills the same formulas" is empirically supported.

Second, the cultivation theory research from George Gerbner onwards has shown that heavy television viewers come to perceive the world as more dangerous, more violent and more conformist than light viewers do — the world-as-television effect. The product's formal features (genre conventions of crime drama, news pacing, advertising rhythms) reliably predict viewer-belief shifts. Reading off effects from products is empirically defensible when the exposure is heavy.

Third, contemporary platform-research has shown that algorithmic recommender systems do shape user behaviour in measurable ways: the radicalisation pipelines documented in YouTube research, the body-image effects of Instagram, the political-polarisation effects of Facebook. The formal features of the platform (the algorithm, the engagement metric, the infinite scroll) reliably predict effects at population scale. Adorno's analytical move — analyse the product, predict the effects — is increasingly the move that empirical platform researchers themselves make, because the alternative (interview individual users) underestimates the systemic, design-level shaping.

The Birmingham School's encoding/decoding model remains correct that individual decoders make varied readings. Some audience members read oppositionally, some negotiated, some preferred. But the variation is around a mean that is itself shaped by the encoded text and its mass-distribution. The Frankfurt move (predict from the encoding) and the Birmingham move (study the decoding) are complementary, not opposed.

The deepest defence of the Frankfurt move is that it is a *normative* analysis, not just a predictive one. Adorno is asking what the cultural product is doing, what kind of subject it presupposes and produces, what kind of life it makes available. These questions can be answered by formal analysis even if individual reception varies, because the question is about the form of life the product offers, not the statistical distribution of receptions. The form of life offered by the Marvel Cinematic Universe is different from that offered by Tarkovsky's *Stalker*; analysing the films tells us something true about that difference regardless of what individual viewers do with them.

The standard objection's strongest version is therefore not refuted but qualified. The Frankfurt School should not be done as the only thing; empirical reception studies are also necessary. But the empirical evidence of the last fifty years has largely confirmed the Frankfurt diagnoses (mere-exposure effects, cultivation effects, platform-radicalisation effects), not undermined them. The reading-off-from-products move is more analytically defensible now than the standard 1980s Birmingham critique allowed.

I conclude that the criticism is partly right (Adorno's writing on jazz especially commits the fallacy in racially blinkered ways), partly wrong (other Frankfurt work is more empirical than the criticism implies, and the empirical record has supported the diagnoses), and best answered by combining Frankfurt formal critique with Birmingham reception study and contemporary platform empirics. The Frankfurt School's pessimism is more defensible in 2025 than in 1985, and the critics' confidence that audiences are reliably active needs updating in light of algorithmic personalisation, mere-exposure effects, and the documented harms of platform design.
