# L12. Culture Industry Reconsidered (Adorno, Frankfurt School, Marx, Benjamin)

---

## 1. Lecture summary

This lecture builds a genealogy of critical theories of culture from Marx through the Frankfurt School to Walter Benjamin. The lecturer is clear that Marx himself did not write systematically about culture (he was a journalist who needed the income, and his references to culture are scattered), but three Marxian texts supply the apparatus that the Frankfurt School later turns on twentieth-century mass entertainment. Early Marx in *The German Ideology* (1846) gives us ruling-class ideology and the means of mental production. The 1859 *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* gives us base and superstructure. Late Marx in *Capital* (1867, 1885, 1894) gives us the critique of political economy, labour power, commodification, commodity fetishism, and the singer-as-wage-labourer who "produces capital directly" when she sings for an entrepreneur. Together these provide the tools for thinking about why music, film and television look the way they do under capitalism: because the class that owns the means of material production also owns the means of mental production, and what looks like culture is in fact a commodity whose real social relations have been inverted into a relation between things, a phantasmagoria. Engels in *Anti-Dühring* (1878) extended the base-and-superstructure framework to culture and media, and Althusser later softened the relation with the qualifying ideas of determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination.

Adorno and Horkheimer, founders (with Felix Weil) of the Institute for Social Research at Frankfurt in 1923, took these Marxian categories into a new historical situation: the failure of revolution in the West, the rise of Nazism and Stalinism, the success of US monopoly capitalism, and the emergence of radio, cinema and the recording industry. The lecturer stresses the school's three signature moves. First, the dialectic of enlightenment: reason that began as critique of myth has become a new myth of total administration, instrumental reason, technocratic control. Second, the culture industry, a phrase deliberately chosen over "mass culture" to refuse the idea that the products arise spontaneously from the people. Third, the diagnostic vocabulary of standardisation, pseudo-individuation and catharsis-that-keeps-them-in-line, illustrated in the slides by Taylor Swift's verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus structure in "Shake It Off," by Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" simulating subversion while reproducing pop's skeleton, and by Oasis's "Cigarettes and Alcohol" offering a working-class catharsis that disarms working-class anger.

Walter Benjamin provides the dialectical counterpoint. In "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) he argues that mass reproduction strips the aura (the here-and-now authority of the unique work tied to ritual and tradition) but in doing so democratises art, replaces cult value with exhibition value, and opens new modes of distracted, collective, critical reception (cinema, the dance hall, photography). Where Adorno saw mechanical reproduction as the engine of conformity, Benjamin saw it as a potentially emancipatory shift in the means of artistic production.

The lecture closes with standard criticisms of the Frankfurt School: it ignores agency, treats audiences as dupes, reads off effects from products, and lacks any empirical theory of reception. The current relevance (the monopolisation of record companies, streaming services, ticket agencies and retailers, the cross-ownership exemplified by Amazon as retailer-and-streamer, the "ordinary person can make it" ideology propagated by the star system, and the commodification even of independent and anti-capitalist music) is left for us to draw.

## 2. Key concepts and named theorists

- **Karl Marx**: early/late distinction; not really a theorist of culture but supplies the categories
- **The German Ideology (1846)**: ruling-class ideas, the means of mental production, camera obscura
- **Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859)**: base and superstructure
- **Capital (1867, 1885, 1894)**: labour power, the singer as productive worker, commodity fetishism
- **Commodity fetishism**: social relations between people appearing as "phantasmagorical" relations between things
- **The means of mental production**: schools, publishers, cinemas, record labels, streaming platforms
- **Camera obscura ideology**: class relations appear upside-down, misrepresented in interests of dominant class
- **Friedrich Engels / Anti-Dühring**: extended base/superstructure to culture and media
- **Louis Althusser**: determination in the last instance, relative autonomy, overdetermination
- **The Frankfurt School / Institute for Social Research**: founded 1923; Geneva 1933; New York 1935; Felix Weil, Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Walter Benjamin, Herbert Marcuse, Erich Fromm, Leo Lowenthal
- **Critical theory**: self-reflexive Marxism incorporating psychoanalysis, dialectic, immanent critique
- **The dialectic of enlightenment**: reason vs myth synthesises into new myth of total administration
- **Instrumental reason**: reason reduced to means-ends calculation, domination of nature and people
- **The totally administered society**: Adorno's diagnosis of late capitalism, fascism, Stalinism, state capitalism
- **The culture industry**: Adorno and Horkheimer's term, replaced "mass culture" to refuse the spontaneity myth
- **Anti-enlightenment / mass deception / fettering consciousness**: the total effect of the industry
- **Standardisation**: verse-chorus-verse, "the same skeleton," genre formulas, the Western
- **Pseudo-individuation** (or pseudo-individuality): illusion of choice / individuality disguising sameness; the necessary correlate of standardisation
- **Catharsis for the masses**: emotional release that "keeps them in line"
- **Pre-digested listening**: "the composition hears for the listener"
- **The star system**: borrowed from individualistic art, propagates the "ordinary person can make it" myth
- **Walter Benjamin**: *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1936)
- **The aura**: "the presence of that which is not present"; here-and-now uniqueness of the ritual work of art
- **Mechanical reproduction**: photography, film, the gramophone; debases aura, democratises art
- **Cult value vs exhibition value**: art for ritual contemplation vs art for public display and circulation
- **Distracted reception**: collective, critical mode of cinema audience, contrasted with absorbed contemplation
- **The subversive truth of art**: art "anticipates the good life," holds open a non-existent alternative
- **Frankfurt School criticisms**: ignores agency, reads off effects from products, no reception theory

## 4. Connections to other lectures

- **Bourdieu (taste, cultural capital):** Bourdieu's habitus and the social construction of taste maps onto pseudo-individuation: the "choice" between sweet and swing that Adorno mocks is exactly what Bourdieu shows as class-coded distinction. Both reject the liberal idea of free aesthetic choice.
- **Stuart Hall, British Cultural Studies (encoding/decoding, hegemony):** Hall's reception theory is the major answer to the Frankfurt School's "no theory of reception" gap. Hall's negotiated and oppositional readings rescue agency from Adorno's "object of calculation," and his Gramscian reading of hegemony as contested terrain provides a less monolithic alternative to the totally administered society.
- **Raymond Williams ("culture is ordinary"):** Williams's insistence that culture is a "whole way of life" lived from below contradicts Adorno's tendency to read culture only as administered from above, while sharing Adorno's resistance to economic determinism.
- **Barthes (structuralism, mythologies):** Barthes' "myth as the depoliticised speech of the bourgeoisie" is the structural-semiotic version of Marx's camera-obscura ideology and of Adorno's "eternal sameness."
- **Foucault (power/knowledge, the disciplinary subject):** Foucault's productive power offers an alternative to the Frankfurt School's still-rather-Marxist account of domination from above; the "totally administered society" is Adorno's name for what Foucault would re-describe as a disciplinary assemblage.
- **Madonna and feminist theory:** Madonna's "Material Girl" (used in the slides as a camera-obscura case) raises the question of whether female pop stars are pure commodity-fetishists or perform a parody that troubles patriarchal norms, a productive tension with Adorno that connects to Butler on gender performativity.
- **Jameson (postmodernism):** Fredric Jameson's "cultural logic of late capitalism" is a direct descendant of Adorno; pastiche, depthlessness, and the collapse of utopian trajectories are postmodern reformulations of standardisation and pseudo-individuation.
- **Paul Gilroy (*The Black Atlantic*):** Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the perspective of enslaved Africans pushes back against Adorno's dismissal of jazz; the racial politics of Adorno's attack on swing is a serious problem.
- **Habermas (public sphere) and Fraser (counterpublics):** Habermas reads the same monopolisation that Adorno calls "culture industry" as the "refeudalisation" of the public sphere by commercial interests; Fraser's subaltern counterpublics suggest a counter-tendency Adorno underplays.

## 5. Why this lecture matters / debates

The culture industry thesis is the foundational sociological account of the mass media. Every later debate about media power, from Williams's "culture is ordinary" to Hall's encoding/decoding to Jameson's late capitalism, argues with it. Three live debates structure how to write about L12.

First, the agency debate. The Frankfurt School is repeatedly accused of "reading off" effects from products, of treating audiences as cultural dupes, and the lecture slides include this as one of the standard criticisms. Stuart Hall and British Cultural Studies rescue active interpretation through encoding/decoding. But Adorno's own claim that the system "incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105) anticipates the reply: even an audience that decodes oppositionally is, day after day, trained on standardised material. The honest essay holds both: agency is real, but so is the cumulative weight of the form.

Second, the high-vs-low art debate. Adorno's defence of "serious" music over popular music (Beethoven, Mozart, Haydn, Brahms against the standardised hit) looks elitist, Eurocentric and frequently racist (his attack on jazz, his sweeping claims about "masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism" in Adorno, 1941: 37). Benjamin offers the corrective: popular forms (cinema, the dance club, photography, the gramophone) can be sites of democratised, critical, distracted reception. The honest essay holds both: Adorno is right that the *industrial* form of culture standardises and reifies, but wrong to read this off from genre or "seriousness." The lecture slide on "music as working-class emancipation" pictures Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" and the Lancaster Music Co-op as examples of popular musical practice that is not industrial in Adorno's sense.

Third, the Marxism debate. Is the culture industry the superstructure determined by the base, or has it become base-like itself? Adorno's late writing pushes toward the latter: the industry "no longer even needs to directly pursue everywhere the profit interests from which it originated. These interests have become objectified in its ideology" (Adorno, 1991: 100). This is post-Marxian: ideology runs on autopilot, cultural production has been folded into the circulation of capital itself. The lecture slide on the monopolisation of record companies, streaming services, ticket agencies and retailers (Kobalt, BMG, Universal, Warner, Sony alongside Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify, Ticketmaster, See Tickets, with Amazon as cross-owner of retail and streaming) gives the contemporary referent.

The takeaway: L12 supplies the vocabulary (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, the culture industry, the aura, base/superstructure, commodity fetishism) that every other media-and-culture lecture will either deploy, modify or reject.

### M1. "The class which has the means of production at its disposal, has control at the same time over the means of mental production" (Marx, 1846). With reference to the contemporary control of the means of mental production and the camera-obscura inversion (Branson and Virgin Records), discuss the monopolisation and cross-ownership of music capital today.

I argue that the pairing of Marx's 1846 formulation with the contemporary structure of monopolised music capital and the Branson camera-obscura case maps the operation of ruling-class control of the means of mental production with continuing accuracy: ownership of recorded music, streaming, ticketing and retail is concentrated in a small number of cross-owning firms, and the ideology this concentration produces inverts the social relation of exploitation into a relation between cleverness and reward.

Marx's claim in *The German Ideology* (1846) reads: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, i.e. the class which is the ruling material force of society, is at the same time its ruling intellectual force. The class which has the means of production at its disposal, has control at the same time over the means of mental production, so that thereby, generally speaking, the ideas of those who lack the means of mental production are subject to it" (Marx, 1846). The claim operates in two registers: epochally (slavery to feudalism to capitalism, with capitalists as the current ruling class) and infrastructurally (the means of mental production, which I take to include schools, books, libraries and cultural forms, are how working-class mentality is produced). Ideology is therefore both content and infrastructure.

The contemporary picture extends Marx's structural diagnosis with unsettling fidelity. The monopolisation of record companies, streaming services, ticket agencies and retailers, together with cross-ownership of firms such as Amazon, which operates as retailer and online streamer simultaneously, enables the control of mental production by capitalists. Recorded-music rights now sit in Sony, Universal and Warner, with Kobalt and BMG as second-tier; streaming distribution in Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music and Spotify; ticketing in Ticketmaster and See Tickets; retail in HMV and Amazon. Amazon's cross-ownership across retail and streaming is the textbook case of single-firm control across multiple layers of the cultural circuit.

The camera-obscura passage gives the ideological supplement. "If in all ideology men and their circumstances appear upside-down as in a camera obscura, this phenomenon arises just as much from their historical life-process as the inversion of objects on the retina does from their physical life-process" (Marx, 1846). Ideology represents class relations in an illusory form that concurs with the interests of the dominant class. Branson is the worked example: a ruling-class individual such as Richard Branson, who set up Virgin Records, appears rich and successful because clever and talented, whereas the material basis of this success is the exploitation of labour, of artists and musicians who make the music that he sells, over their entire working lives. Branson presents in the press as entrepreneurial genius; the camera-obscura inversion conceals the actual circulation, in which musicians' labour power, traded for wages over working lives, produces the surplus that capitalises the Virgin brand.

Adorno's culture industry thesis is the natural extension. "The total effect of the culture industry is one of anti-enlightenment... [it] becomes mass deception and is turned into a means for fettering consciousness" (Adorno and Horkheimer, 1947). Adorno's "Culture Industry Reconsidered" makes the connection to concentration explicit: the industry is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98). The 1846 framework predicts what the 1947 framework analyses: when the means of mental production are concentrated, the cultural commodities those means produce will be standardised in the interests of the dominant class.

Gramsci (1971) and Hall (1980) complicate the picture without overturning it: ruling-class control of mental production secures *hegemony*, leadership through ideological persuasion as well as economic ownership, and audiences decode actively. But the field on which they decode is determined by who owns the labels, the streamers, the ticketers and the retailers. Habermas (1989) reads the same monopolisation as the "refeudalisation" of the public sphere by commercial cross-owners, so the Frankfurt diagnosis and the Habermasian critique converge on contemporary cultural capital.

I conclude that Marx's 1846 formulation, with its camera-obscura supplement, predicts both the structural concentration of contemporary music capital and the entrepreneurial-genius ideology that conceals the exploitation behind it. The Branson case is the contemporary illustration of an inversion 1846 names with surprising prescience.

### M2. "A singer who sings like a bird is an unproductive worker... But if the same singer is engaged by an entrepreneur who makes her sing to make money, then she becomes a productive worker, since she produces capital directly" (Marx, *Capital Volume 1*). Discuss the relationship between musicians as wage labourers and the ideological supplements that conceal this wage relation in the contemporary music industry.

I argue that the relation Marx's *Capital* establishes between the material conditions of musical labour and the ideological forms that conceal them is the right starting point for any sociology of music: musicians are wage labourers whose labour power produces capital, while the ideology accompanying their labour presents music as a sphere of stardom, talent and special value. The two sides reproduce each other.

Marx's *Capital Volume 1* (1867) makes the singer a textbook case of productive labour. "A singer who sings like a bird is an unproductive worker. If she sells her song for money, she is to that extent a wage-labourer or merchant. But if the same singer is engaged by an entrepreneur who makes her sing to make money, then she becomes a productive worker, since she produces capital directly" (Marx, 1867). The criterion is structural, not aesthetic: productivity is defined by the production of surplus value for a capitalist, not by the worth of what is produced. The contemporary signed artist, whose labour power produces tracks owned by a major label, is precisely Marx's productive worker.

The materialist side of the analysis runs as follows. Musicians hold labour power; they exchange it for a wage (reproduction); they produce a commodity (music); the commodity enters circulation M – C – C' – M'; capitalists invest in fixed capital (studios, production, distribution). This is the *Capital* analysis applied to recorded music. The capitalist's investment in fixed capital is exactly what the monopolisation picture shows: Sony, Universal and Warner own the labels; Spotify, Apple Music and Amazon Music own the streaming infrastructure; Ticketmaster owns the ticketing. The wage-labour relation between artist and label sits inside this structure.

The ideological side names the supplement. Record companies make "stars"; you can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money; music is special; music is precious; music is an industry, and technological progress is good for it. Each ideological claim performs a function. The star myth conceals the wage relation by presenting the few visible winners as proof that musical labour pays. The "music is special" and "music is precious" claims obscure the commodity logic by treating music as a transcendental value rather than as one commodity among others in M – C – C' – M' circulation. The "technological progress is good for the industry" claim reframes platform consolidation, which historically reduces artist royalties, as universal benefit.

Marx's commodity-fetishism passage gives the analytical underpinning. "The relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation of objects which exists outside them... It is a particular social relation between men themselves which in their eyes assumes a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things" (Marx, 1867). The streamed track presents to the listener as a discrete aesthetic thing whose social relation of production (songwriter, producers, mixers, label staff, marketing teams, platform engineers, retail workers) is invisible. The 2025 UK top-ten chart makes this visible at a glance: each track is a unit, its labour-relation a phantasmagoria.

Adorno extends Marx's framework: "Cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The pop song is a commodity from the form up. "Its ideology above all makes use of the star system, borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101). The "you can be a star" claim is exactly this borrowed ideology.

The Branson case (Marx, 1846) shows materialism and ideology in dialectical tension. The materialist account: musicians' labour power, traded over working lives, produces the surplus that capitalises Virgin. The ideological inversion: Branson is rich because clever. The inversion is generated by, and conceals, the labour relation.

Williams (1958) and Hall (1980) supply partial correctives: musicians create within forms of life lived from below, and audiences negotiate meanings actively. But the dominant industrial form, monopoly-distributed and wage-organised, sets the field.

I conclude that the materialism and ideology of the music industry are dialectically paired. Marx's singer-quote names the material relation; the ideological supplement (stars, specialness, technological optimism) conceals it; commodity fetishism is the form the concealment takes. The honest sociology of music holds both sides simultaneously.

### M3. Evaluate Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* as a diagnosis of total control of the population under late capitalism.

I argue that Adorno and Horkheimer's diagnosis of the dialectic of enlightenment, understood as reason becoming a new myth of total administration, is essentially correct as an account of why instrumental rationality coexists with mass deception under late capitalism, and that the culture industry is its central institutional expression.

The dialectic itself has a clean three-step structure: thesis (argument), antithesis (counterargument), synthesis (new, more developed understanding). Applied to enlightenment, the thesis is reason and science, the antithesis is myth, and the synthesis is control, domination and rationalisation leading to a new myth in which hierarchy and control operate through science and technology. The Enlightenment's emancipatory project, freeing thought from religious authority through rational critique, completes itself by producing a new authority, technocratic and total. The political implication follows: total control of the population in fascism, Stalinism, state capitalism and the culture industry.

The intellectual context matters. The Frankfurt School, founded at Frankfurt in 1923, moved to Geneva in 1933 and to New York in 1935 in flight from Nazism. Critical theory took shape there: an analysis of dominant ideology as ruling-class ideology and of cultural hegemony as the total and unified structure of ruling ideas. *Dialectic of Enlightenment* was written after Auschwitz and during Stalinism, with Hollywood as its immediate cultural environment. The argument is that all three (fascism, Stalinism, US monopoly culture) share a structure: rationalised systems of total administration that present themselves as enlightened freedom.

The mechanism is instrumental reason. Adorno and Horkheimer compare enlightenment to a dictatorship in which the man of science knows things so that he can control them. Reason reduced to means-ends calculation produces domination of nature and of people. The cultural form of this domination is the culture industry: media impresses standardised behaviour and standardised consumption on the individual as rational, while the only freedom on offer is the freedom of the industrialists and monopolists. Standardisation is the rational form of mass cultural production; the consumer's "freedom" is to choose among pre-standardised products.

The diagnosis is sharpened in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* itself: "The total effect of the culture industry is one of anti-enlightenment... [it] becomes mass deception and is turned into a means for fettering consciousness. It impedes the development of autonomous, independent individuals who judge and decide consciously for themselves" (Adorno and Horkheimer, 1947). The Enlightenment promised autonomous, judging individuals; the industry delivers their opposite. This is not a betrayal but a completion: the same instrumental rationality that drove Enlightenment science drives the culture industry's optimisation of audience response. The deepest claim is that Enlightenment, having mastered itself and assumed its own power, breaks through its own limits into something worse. Reason produces its own reversal.

The contemporary illustrations are direct. Netflix's recommender, Spotify's playlist optimisation and the TikTok algorithm are instrumental rationality applied to cultural production: rationalised systems for maximising engagement that produce standardised consumption while presenting as personalised choice. AI-generated music and ChatGPT-assisted production extend the logic: rationalised tools for cultural manufacture whose outputs are typically captured by the same monopoly distribution. *Black Mirror*'s satire registers the popular awareness that something has reversed.

Habermas (1989) extends the diagnosis: the "refeudalisation" of the public sphere by commercial-administrative organisations is the dialectic operating in the communicative dimension. Williams (1958) supplies a corrective: the dialectic captures the industrial form but not all the lived practices through which "culture is ordinary" continues to be made and remade in choirs, libraries and worker co-operatives, with worker-owned spaces such as the Lancaster Music Co-op as a concrete example.

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences decode in negotiated and oppositional ways. The point has force but does not refute the dialectic. "Steady drops hollow the stone... the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation" (Adorno, 1991: 105). Active reception happens within a field whose terms are set by rationalised production.

I conclude that the dialectic of enlightenment is the correct frame for understanding why late-capitalist culture combines technocratic sophistication with anti-enlightenment effects. The same instrumental rationality that built scientific modernity built the culture industry. Reason becomes a new myth of hierarchy and control through science and technology, and the culture industry is its everyday face.

### M4. With reference to Adorno's claims that the recognition of brand names has taken the place of choice and that films "knock out" their audiences, discuss the displacement of art by business in the culture industry.

I argue that the compressed formulation "culture no longer has to present itself as art, it is business" names the most important structural transformation in the culture industry: the dropping of the aesthetic alibi. Where culture once required the appearance of art to legitimate itself, the industry now operates openly as commerce, with brand recognition and physiological impact replacing aesthetic judgement.

Adorno's "Culture Industry Reconsidered" supplies the analytical core: "The entire practice of the culture industry transfers the profit motive naked onto cultural forms. Ever since these cultural forms first began to earn a living for their creators as commodities in the market-place they had already had something of this quality. But then they sought profit only indirectly, over and above their autonomous essence. New on the part of the culture industry is the direct and undisguised primacy of a precisely and thoroughly calculated efficacy in its most typical products" (Adorno, 1991: 99). The shift is from indirect profit, mediated by an autonomous aesthetic, to direct and undisguised primacy of calculated profit. Culture no longer has to present itself as art; it is business.

The brand-name claim names the consumer-facing form. Adorno's claim that the recognition of brand names has taken the place of choice reframes the supposed sovereignty of the consumer as a reflex of marketing. The consumer does not weigh aesthetic alternatives; she recognises a Spotify-curated playlist, a Marvel/MCU release, a K-pop debut, a Netflix front-page tile, a Disney franchise extension. The "choice" is between brands; the brand is the contentless prompt to consume. "What parades as progress in the culture industry, as the incessantly new which it offers up, remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100). New brands appear, but their structural sameness underwrites the recognition logic.

The "knock out" claim names the physical mechanism. Adorno's claim that films "knock out" their audiences anchors the production-of-effects in bodily impact. The cultural product is engineered to produce attention, affect and engagement irrespective of the audience's reflective judgement. *X Factor* and *Britain's Got Talent* operate identically: contestants and judges are structured to deliver engineered emotional peaks. Media impresses standardised behaviour and standardised consumption on the individual as rational. TikTok's optimisation toward predictable hooks at platform scale is the contemporary intensification.

The displacement of art by business has three consequences.

First, the disappearance of the autonomous moment. Adorno preserves a normative distinction: "Culture, in the true sense... always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100). True culture protested; the industrial form accommodates. What is lost is the subversive truth of art: the capacity to anticipate the good life by holding open an image of life that contradicts the existing one.

Second, the integration of consumer into commodity. "The culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above... The seriousness of high art is destroyed in speculation about its efficacy; the seriousness of the lower perishes with the civilizational constraints imposed on the rebellious resistance inherent within it" (Adorno, 1991: 98-99). Both lose their distinct functions; the difference flattens into a single commercial logic.

Third, the contentless imperative. "The categorical imperative of the culture industry no longer has anything in common with freedom. It proclaims: you shall conform, without instruction as to what; conform to that which exists anyway" (Adorno, 1991: 104). The brand is the instruction; recognition is the conformity. Habermas (1989) reads the same transformation as the refeudalisation of the public sphere.

Stuart Hall's reception theory is the standard objection: audiences decode brands negotiably, parody them, build counter-identities. The corrective is real but partial. Audiences negotiate within a brand-structured environment whose alternatives are themselves brand-structured. Anarcho-punk projects such as Crass show what non-brand culture looks like; their small scale relative to monopoly distribution names the limit of the alternative.

I conclude that the formulation is exact. Culture in the industrial form no longer presents itself as art; it is business. Brand recognition replaces aesthetic choice; engineered effect replaces reflective response; the autonomous moment of art survives in marginal counter-currents. Adorno's diagnosis remains the analytical baseline.

### M5. To what extent do Engels's extension of base-and-superstructure to "forms of culture, and the media" in *Anti-Dühring* (1878), and Althusser's qualifying concepts of determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination, rescue Marx's 1859 model from the charge of crude economic determinism?

I argue that Engels's *Anti-Dühring* (1878) and Althusser's three qualifying concepts substantially rescue Marx's 1859 base-and-superstructure model from the determinist objection, but at the cost of softening the model's predictive bite: the rescued framework is more defensible but less forceful, and it remains essential as an analytical heuristic rather than as a causal law.

Marx's 1859 formulation in the *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* reads: "The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation on which there arises a legal and political superstructure and to which there correspond definite forms of social consciousness" (Marx, 1859). The picture is dialectical: an economic base of means of production, economic relations, commodities, bourgeoisie and proletariat shapes, and is in turn maintained by, an ideological superstructure of education, religion, politics, media and culture. The base shapes; the superstructure maintains.

The crude reading takes the arrow as a one-way causal law: the base produces the superstructure mechanically; cultural artefacts merely reflect their economic conditions. This is the version Williams (1980) attacks as "vulgar Marxism" and Hall (1980) replaces with a properly Gramscian account of hegemony. Read crudely, the model cannot explain why two musical works with the same capitalist base do different cultural work, why Rage Against the Machine's anti-capitalist content shares an industrial form with Taylor Swift's pop, or why worker-owned cultural spaces such as the Lancaster Music Co-op exist at all in a Sony-Universal-Warner field.

Engels's *Anti-Dühring* (1878), written as a response to Eugen Dühring's claim to provide a scientific socialism, performs the first rescue. Engels extends the model to include forms of culture and the media as arising from the economic base. Two moves matter. First, the superstructure is widened beyond Marx's "legal and political" to include culture and media, opening the framework to industrial cultural production. Second, Engels insists that the determination is dialectical: the superstructure reacts back on the base. The base is determining "in the last instance," not at every instant.

Althusser systematises this rescue with three concepts. He introduces determination in the last instance (not immediate, only determined over a long period of history), relative autonomy (some independence from the base) and overdetermination (many different economic and historical events leading jointly to outcomes). Each concept does specific work.

"Determination in the last instance" displaces causation from immediate event to historical aggregate. Capital's pressure on cultural production is determining only at the level of the field over time. Rage Against the Machine's anti-capitalist content is permitted at the level of any particular release; the field's general tendency to commodify radicalism is the long-run determination.

"Relative autonomy" names the superstructure's own dynamics. Cultural forms have histories, rules and aesthetic traditions not reducible to capital. The verse-chorus-bridge skeleton of "Shake It Off" cannot be derived from Sony's balance sheet, but cannot be understood outside the industrial conditions of mass production either. The autonomy is real, but bounded.

"Overdetermination" names the multiplicity of conditions producing any cultural outcome: economic structure, aesthetic tradition, technological possibility, political moment, marketing strategy, audience expectation. Crude determinism reads off one cause; overdetermination acknowledges many.

The rescued framework survives Hall's hegemony objection, Williams's "culture is ordinary" objection, and Foucault's productive-power objection. The "music as working-class emancipation" tradition (Rage Against the Machine, Crass, the Lancaster Music Co-op) is exactly what it explains: three cultural products of the same capitalist base, overdetermined by different relations, with relative autonomy in varying degrees.

The cost is loss of predictive bite. If the base determines only in the last instance, the model becomes hard to falsify. The reply is that the framework is a structural heuristic asking the right question (who owns the means of mental production) without claiming to deduce every cultural fact from the answer.

I conclude that Engels and Althusser substantially rescue Marx's 1859 model, but at the cost of softening it from causal law to structural heuristic. The honest contemporary use is exactly that: ask who owns the means of cultural production (Sony, Universal, Warner, Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Ticketmaster, Amazon as cross-owner), recognise relative autonomy and overdetermination at the level of particular works, and analyse the field as base-shaped without expecting one-to-one prediction. The rescued model is indispensable; the crude version was never defensible.

### M6. Discuss the democratising and emancipatory potential of mechanically reproduced art with reference to Benjamin's analysis of cinema and the dance club.

I argue that Benjamin offers the strongest case for the democratising potential of mechanical reproduction: when the means of mental production are not monopolised, mechanically reproduced art democratises access, opens new collective modes of reception, and holds open an image of life different from the existing one. The democratisation of the means of production of art is what is at stake.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) makes three moves. First, mechanical reproduction breaks the aura: the work is disconnected from where the art was made (its locale) and from its cultural and physical context. The unique original loses its monopoly on authenticity. Second, the consequence is the decline of cult value (ritual contemplation of the unique original) and the rise of exhibition value (mass circulation, political availability). High art was consumed for its cult status and its value in an exhibition; mechanical reproduction voids those values and creates new ways of seeing. Third, new modes of reception become possible: through popular cinema people take the perspective of the camera, and through the consumption of music in clubs people dance together to music.

The cinema and the club are Benjamin's paradigm cases because both refuse the bourgeois contemplative mode. Cinema is collective: bodies share space; reception is distracted, comparative and politically available. The club is collective and embodied: bodies share rhythm and space, music is participated in rather than contemplated. Both displace the cult mode of the museum painting and the concert hall, opening democratised forms of cultural participation.

The political consequence is sharp. Mechanical reproduction undermines authentic art and the system of patronage; it aids the promotion of truth and of critical-rational experience; it gives life to that which is normally suppressed. Through art one can conceive a world different from the life dominated by commodity production. Art provides a medium for critical thinking by upholding images of life that contradict the existing one. Art anticipates the good life. The eighteenth-century court painting marks the patronage system Benjamin's revolution displaces; the cassette, the mp3, home-recording software and the DJ controller mark the technical means that have democratised production.

The pay-off is direct. Now individuals can experience art in different ways, can think critically about the world and can consider a different life: the means of production of art are democratised. Critical thinking and political imagination, on this view, are not separate from mechanical reproduction; the technology of the copy is the technical condition under which critical thinking can reach the working class at scale.

The working-class emancipation tradition makes the case concrete. Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" was produced and distributed outside the major-label system, sold cheaply, with profits routed to political causes: a Benjaminian use of mechanical reproduction. The Lancaster Music Co-op is collective ownership of the fixed capital Benjamin's politics presupposes (rehearsal rooms, recording equipment, distribution networks). These are not exceptions to Benjamin's analysis but its institutional realisations: mechanical reproduction held outside monopoly capital democratises in exactly the way Benjamin predicted.

Williams (1958) supplements the case from below: culture is "ordinary," a "whole way of life" lived through institutions ordinary people build for themselves. The bridge to Williams's "culture is ordinary" claim sharpens the Benjaminian thesis: art anticipates the good life when produced and circulated in conditions structurally different from monopoly capital.

Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" is the standard objection. The industry "conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist" (Adorno, 1991: 102). Mechanical reproduction in monopoly hands manufactures new aura around the star and the brand. Madonna's "Material Girl," the most-streamed YouTube artists and K-pop manufactured groups are foggy-mist aura around mass-reproduced figures. The technology is not democratic by itself; ownership decides.

The synthesis is layered. At the production layer, mechanical reproduction democratises wherever the means are not monopolised: home recording, anarcho-punk labels, worker-owned spaces. At the distribution layer, monopoly capital recaptures. Benjamin's hope is conditional on the ownership question.

I conclude that Benjamin's argument makes the strongest case for the democratising politics of mechanical reproduction. Art anticipates the good life when the means of its mechanical reproduction are held outside monopoly capital; under monopoly, the aura returns as a foggy mist. The political task that follows is not technological but institutional: democratise the means of mental production.

### M7. Discuss the spectacle-and-sameness logic of the culture industry with reference to Adorno's claims that "the culture business comes to resemble contests... and finally sport" and that "sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection."

I argue that Adorno's claims about sport, contest and ritual capture the spectacle dimension of the culture industry, that differentiation-as-sameness names the structural logic underneath, and that contemporary sport spectacle, reality TV and platform competition all extend the diagnosis. The industry produces variation as an ideology while drilling sameness as its substance.

Adorno's "Culture Industry Reconsidered" gives the sport claim precisely. "The culture business comes to resemble contests... and finally sport." "Sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection" (Adorno, 1991). The first reframes culture as competitive spectacle; the second renames sport itself as ritualised acceptance of the existing hierarchy. Together they name a continuous transformation: cultural goods become competitive contests, contests become sport, sport becomes ritual, ritual celebrates the order under which the spectators live.

The structural logic underneath is differentiation-as-sameness: something is provided for everyone, but differences are hammered home and propagated. Two operations work together. "Something for everyone" promises individuated provision. "Differences are hammered home and propagated" makes the differentiation an ideology in itself, asserted with intensity to compensate for the underlying sameness of the standardised form. This is pseudo-individuation at the level of the system rather than the individual track. Adorno's formulation in "On Popular Music" is the canonical text: pseudo-individuation "keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them" (Adorno, 1941: 23).

The contemporary illustrations are direct. *X Factor*, *Britain's Got Talent* and *The Voice* enact culture-as-contest at platform scale: the format is competitive, the affective payoff is dramatic, the substantive musical production is industrially standardised, and the chief output is the manufactured star. The star system propagates the ideology that the ordinary person can make it: contest reproduces the promise that individual elevation through performance is available, while structural concentration ensures that almost no contestant escapes precarity.

The football match illustrates the sport-as-ritual claim. Fans experience matches as solidarity and identity; the corrective from Hall (1980) registers this active reception. But the structural format remains spectacle: ritualised confrontation resolving in temporary catharsis, leaving the larger social order undisturbed. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43).

The "differences are hammered home" operation works through brand-differentiation across products with structurally identical functions. The biggest contemporary YouTube artists are presented as distinct personae; underlying labour conditions and standardised promotional cycles are continuous. K-pop trains distinct group personalities sharing the same corporate apparatus. Marvel/MCU franchise extension makes character difference an ideology while the template is rigorously continuous. Streaming-platform competition (Spotify versus Apple Music versus Amazon Music) hammers home brand difference while the catalogue overlap is extensive.

Adorno's claim that anyone incapable of talking in the prescribed fashion is threatened in his very existence (Adorno, 1991) names the cost of refusing the contest. Habermas (1989) reads the same transformation as the refeudalisation of the public sphere: where the bourgeois public sphere imagined rational-critical debate, the refeudalised public sphere stages competitive performances of allegiance.

A Williamsian alternative remains visible. Anarcho-punk projects such as Crass and worker-owned spaces such as the Lancaster Music Co-op refuse the contest-and-sameness logic. Williams (1958) supplies the wider corrective: culture as "a whole way of life" includes choirs, brass bands and library reading groups that evade the contest form. The corrective is real but limited in scale.

The Stuart Hall objection is that contests can be subverted. Reality-TV contestants critique their own format; football fans organise against owners; gameplay sometimes generates oppositional meaning. The point has force. Adorno's reply, that "steady drops hollow the stone... the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation" (Adorno, 1991: 105), holds at the structural level: subversion happens within a field whose terms have been set by the spectacle.

I conclude that Adorno's claims about contest, sport and ritual name the spectacle dimension of the culture industry with continuing accuracy. The "something for everyone, differences hammered home" logic identifies the deeper operation: pseudo-individuated provision drilled as ideological difference over structurally standardised production. The honest analysis treats this as the dominant industrial form, retains Hall's reception-theoretic supplement, and notes the marginal but real Williamsian alternatives.

### M8. "The culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above" (Adorno). Discuss with reference to the contemporary monopolisation of recorded music and streaming.

I argue that Adorno's claim that "the culture industry intentionally integrates its consumers from above" (Adorno, 1991: 98) is intensified, not weakened, by the contemporary structure of recorded music, because the same monopoly concentration he described in 1947 has now extended across record labels, streaming platforms, ticket agencies and retailers, with cross-owners such as Amazon operating across multiple layers simultaneously.

Adorno's argument has three steps. First, the masses are not the measure but the ideology of the culture industry: "The customer is not king, as the culture industry would have us believe, not its subject but its object" (Adorno, 1991: 99). The industry presumes a mentality which it then "duplicates, reinforces and strengthens" (Adorno, 1991: 99). Second, cultural commodities are governed by realisation as value, not content: the profit motive is "transferred naked onto cultural forms" (Adorno, 1991: 99). Third, what looks like novelty and choice is "the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100). Integration is from above because concentration of the means of mental production lets the industry decide in advance what counts as music, film or news.

The contemporary structural picture is clear. Recorded-music rights are held by Sony, Universal and Warner. Streaming sits in Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud. Live performance is held by Ticketmaster and See Tickets. Retail (HMV, Amazon) is concentrated, with Amazon operating as both retailer and streamer. Adorno's claim that the system is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98) is the organising thesis.

Within this structure, standardisation continues. Taylor Swift's "Shake It Off" exemplifies the verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton: "The whole structure of popular music is standardized, even where the attempt is made to circumvent standardization" (Adorno, 1941: 3). Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" performs pseudo-individuation: stylistic edginess, the f-word in chart pop, an image as subversive pop star, while the conventional pop structure is reproduced with fidelity. The "supposedly great personalities" (Adorno, 1991: 101) are visible across the most-streamed YouTube and Spotify charts, and TikTok's reward of predictable hooks intensifies the logic.

Richard Branson, who founded Virgin Records, is the textbook camera-obscura case. He appears wealthy because clever; the material basis is the exploitation of musicians' labour over their working lives. "If in all ideology men and their circumstances appear upside-down as in a camera obscura, this phenomenon arises just as much from their historical life-process as the inversion of objects on the retina does from their physical life-process" (Marx, 1846).

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences are not passive: they decode actively, sometimes oppositionally. Adorno's writing on jazz does sometimes read off effects from products. But the reply is forceful: "it can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). Even an audience that decodes actively is daily drilled by the standardised form.

There are countervailing tendencies. Rage Against the Machine (commodification within Sony), Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" (anarcho-punk distribution outside the major-label structure) and the Lancaster Music Co-op (a worker-owned production space) all show working-class musical emancipation in different forms. Adorno's own theory leaves room for these: he distinguishes the culture industry from culture which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The cracks are real but small relative to the monopolised mainstream.

The deepest update is that integration no longer needs to be conscious. As Adorno foresaw, the industry's "interests have become objectified in its ideology" and have "made themselves independent of the compulsion to sell the cultural commodities which must be swallowed anyway" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The executive does not need to plot against autonomy; the standardised form does it automatically. Jameson's reading of postmodernism as "the cultural logic of late capitalism" (Jameson, 1991) is the natural extension here: where Adorno saw integration through standardised hits, Jameson sees pastiche and the foreclosure of utopian trajectories as the same operation generalised, the Marvel/MCU franchise being the cinematic analogue of integration as a routine of corporate planning.

I conclude that integration from above is the right phrase for the monopolised music industry, and Adorno's framework describes the structural concentration of the contemporary cultural industries quite accurately. The Frankfurt School's pessimism, often dismissed as 1940s elitism, describes contemporary cross-ownership and standardisation more sharply than its critics have allowed.

### M9. To what extent does Marx's account of the means of mental production in *The German Ideology* anticipate Adorno's culture industry thesis?

I argue that Marx's account of the means of mental production in *The German Ideology* (1846) anticipates Adorno's culture industry thesis substantially but not completely: Marx supplies the political-economic skeleton (ruling-class ownership of cultural production), but Adorno adds three things Marx did not have, namely the industrial form of cultural production, the psychoanalytic theory of the consumer, and the dialectical reversal whereby enlightenment becomes its opposite.

Marx's formulation reads: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas... The class which has the means of production at its disposal, has control at the same time over the means of mental production" (Marx, 1846). The first half names ideology's content in any epoch (feudal honour, bourgeois individualism). The second names the infrastructure: who owns schools, presses, libraries, lecture halls. Marx asks not only what the ruling ideas are but how they are reproduced through institutional control of dissemination.

This anticipates the culture industry in three respects. First, it identifies asymmetric ownership as the engine of ideological domination. Contemporary concentration makes the point: recorded-music rights in Sony, Universal and Warner; streaming in Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud; ticketing in Ticketmaster and See Tickets; Amazon as retail/streaming cross-owner. This is Marx's concentration industrially scaled. Second, it makes ideology material: institutions, buildings, technologies. Third, it builds in the camera-obscura claim: ideology represents class relations in an illusory form that concurs with the interests of the dominant class (Marx, 1846). The Branson-Virgin case illustrates this directly.

Where Marx stops short, Adorno extends. Marx writes before the industrial production of culture: he has the printing press but not the gramophone, radio or cinema. The phrase "culture industry" was chosen "in order to exclude from the outset the interpretation agreeable to its advocates: that it is a matter of something like a culture that arises spontaneously from the masses themselves" (Adorno, 1991: 98). The industrial form changes the analysis. Standardisation is technical seriality: the verse-chorus-bridge skeleton (Adorno, 1941: 3), the Western as exemplary of "the standardization of the thing itself" (Adorno, 1991: 100), the thirty-two-bar chorus and the "characters" (mother songs, home songs, nonsense songs, laments for a lost girl) Adorno enumerates (Adorno, 1941: 3). Pseudo-individuation, "endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice... on the basis of standardization itself" (Adorno, 1941: 23), is Adorno's name for the gap between Marx's general claim and the specific way industrial culture markets sameness as difference. Netflix's recommender, K-pop's manufactured groups and the TikTok hook all show the same operation.

Adorno's second extension is psychoanalytic. Marx is sociological: the worker is subject to ruling ideas because she lacks the means of mental production. Adorno, drawing on Freud via Fromm, asks why workers actively desire the culture they are sold. His answer in "On Popular Music" is that "their leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" (Adorno, 1941: 32). Marx has alienation but not this loop, in which alienation is consumed as relief from alienation.

Adorno's third extension is the dialectic. Marx is broadly a theorist of progress: capitalism is its own gravedigger. Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1947) reads twentieth-century experience as the inversion of this thesis: reason and technical mastery synthesised into new forms of domination. "The total effect of the culture industry is one of anti-enlightenment... it becomes mass deception and is turned into a means for fettering consciousness" (Adorno and Horkheimer, 1947). This pessimism is post-Marxian.

A defender of Marx will reply that the camera-obscura passage and commodity fetishism in *Capital* ("a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things," Marx, 1867) already contain the seeds of Adorno's reification analysis. The 2025 UK top-ten chart presents as a list of distinct aesthetic objects whose phantasmagorical thing-relation conceals labour: songwriters, producers, mixers, label staff, distribution engineers, marketing teams. A different rejoinder is Gramscian: Hall (1980) and Gramsci (1971) argue that the relation between owners of mental production and audiences is hegemonic rather than determinative, a negotiated leadership rather than a downward broadcast, which both fulfils and complicates the Marx-to-Adorno trajectory by making ruling ideas contested terrain.

I conclude that the anticipation is substantial. Marx supplies the skeleton (means of mental production, base/superstructure, commodity fetishism); Adorno adds industrial form, psychoanalytic subject and dialectical reversal. One cannot do the Frankfurt School without Marx, nor contemporary cultural studies without both.

### M10. Critically evaluate the claim that standardisation and pseudo-individuation remain the defining features of twenty-first-century popular music.

I argue that standardisation and pseudo-individuation remain the defining features of twenty-first-century popular music, that contemporary structural concentration has intensified rather than undone the diagnosis, and that surviving counter-currents (Crass, the Lancaster Music Co-op) operate at a scale too small to displace the dominant industrial form.

Adorno's 1941 thesis is uncompromising: "The whole structure of popular music is standardized, even where the attempt is made to circumvent standardization" (Adorno, 1941: 3). The thirty-two-bar chorus and the rigid sequence of types (mother songs, home songs, novelty songs, laments for a lost girl) form the skeleton. Above sit pseudo-individuated "effects": breaks, blue chords, dirty notes (Adorno, 1941: 4). F. R. Leavis (1930) reaches a structurally similar verdict from the right, fearing the "small minority" displaced by mass commercial culture; the worth of Adorno's reframing is that he locates standardisation in capital rather than in moral decline, making it diagnosable rather than mourned.

Taylor Swift's "Shake It Off" (2014) exhibits the canonical structure: Verse-Chorus-Verse-Chorus-Bridge-Chorus. The chorus repeats the title hook with the same trochaic stress on every iteration. The bridge supplies pseudo-individuation: a new lyrical perspective ("My ex-man brought his new girlfriend") and a different rhythmic feel, before the chorus reasserts the skeleton. This is exactly what Adorno predicts: "the hit will lead back to the same familiar experience, and nothing fundamentally novel will be introduced" (Adorno, 1941: 3). The TikTok algorithm, which rewards quick-hook predictability, drills the same skeleton at platform scale.

Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" shows pseudo-individuation in sharpened form. Where 1941 jazz disguised standardisation with dirty notes, "Von Dutch" disguises it with attitude: electroclash style, the repeated f-word in chart pop, an image as subversive pop star, stylised violence in the video. The track subverts the conventional pop-song surface but reproduces its structure. Adorno's example, Benny Goodman versus Guy Lombardo (Adorno, 1941: 26), is the analogue. *X Factor* and *Britain's Got Talent* perform the equivalent operation at the level of the star.

Structural intensification is visible across contemporary cultural capital. Recorded music is held by Sony, Universal and Warner. Streaming sits in Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud. Ticketing is in Ticketmaster and See Tickets. Amazon operates across retail and streaming. The valuation of YouTube and the lists of its most-streamed artists show that platform concentration extends to video. Adorno's claim that the system is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98) is precisely this picture.

The catharsis function fits Oasis's "Cigarettes and Alcohol" (1994), which addresses the alienation of work ("Is it worth the aggravation / To find yourself a job when there's nothing worth working for?"), offers cathartic resolution, and disarms the underlying anger by absorbing it into a singable refrain. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43). Class anger that might have powered industrial action is digested by the chorus.

The "ordinary person can make it" ideology (Adorno, 1991: 101) is propagated through influencer culture and the parade of most-streamed YouTube artists. The materialist-ideology pairing makes the operation explicit: musicians are wage-labourers; the supplement is that record companies make "stars" and that you can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money. K-pop and BTS show the machinery at global scale.

The honest objection from Benjamin is that mechanical reproduction also enables democratised production. Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" was produced on the band's own anarcho-punk label and distributed outside the major-label system. Rage Against the Machine is the standard commodification case: anti-capitalist content sold through Sony. The Lancaster Music Co-op is a worker-owned space where the means of musical production are collectively held. These are real counter-currents in Adorno's own terms: he distinguishes the culture industry from culture which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100).

The scale answer is decisive. The structural concentration of contemporary music capital is the dominant fact; the co-operative is a marginal exception. Adorno's diagnosis, "the customer is not king... not its subject but its object" (Adorno, 1991: 99), describes the dominant form.

I conclude that standardisation and pseudo-individuation are as defining now as in 1941, that the chart's own examples carry the argument, and that the counter-current of independent and co-operative music remains real but marginal.

### M11. "Mechanical reproduction shatters the aura, but the culture industry resurrects it as a foggy mist." Discuss Benjamin and Adorno on the politics of reproducibility.

I argue that Benjamin and Adorno disagree on the political valence of mechanical reproduction not because they disagree about the mechanism but because they disagree about its historical moment: Benjamin writes in 1935 looking at the revolutionary potential of cinema for an emergent working class; Adorno writes after Nazism and the success of Hollywood, looking at how the same techniques were used to manufacture consent. Contemporary monopolisation of distribution leans Adornian without refuting Benjamin's hope.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) claims that the aura, the authority of the original work tied to historical ritual and tradition, withers under mass reproduction. Photography, film and the gramophone produce endless copies and dissolve the presence of that which is not present (Benjamin, 1935). The consequence is political: society becomes freer to organise and control itself in its reception, no longer subject to the authority of original works of art. Art loses cult value (ritual pilgrimage) and gains exhibition value (circulation, mass audiences, political availability). Benjamin reads this gain as democratic. His paradigm cases are cinema (people take the perspective of the camera) and the dance club (people dance together to music): collective, distracted modes replacing bourgeois contemplation.

Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" is sharp: "Adopting Benjamin's designation of the traditional work of art by the concept of aura, the presence of that which is not present, the culture industry is defined by the fact that it does not strictly counterpose another principle to that of aura, but rather by the fact that it conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist" (Adorno, 1991: 102). The industry does not destroy aura; it manufactures it. The star system "borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101) resurrects aura around the celebrity. Madonna's "Material Girl" shows this: the song is a mass-reproduced commodity (recording, video, sleeve are copies), yet Madonna is wreathed in the foggy-mist aura of an individual personality. Disney's Star Wars rollouts, YouTube influencer brands and the K-pop/BTS apparatus operate identically.

Adorno's "On Popular Music" sharpens the case: the listener's "moment of recognition is that of effortless sensation," and the industry must "arouse attention by means of ever-new products, but this attention spells their doom" (Adorno, 1941: 33-34). The foggy-mist aura gives the reproduced product apparent uniqueness while it remains structurally interchangeable. Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" is the operation: a manufactured aura of subversion around a conventional pop track.

The disagreement is therefore about which moment of the dialectic to emphasise. Benjamin sees the historical opening: art shifting from ritual to politics. Adorno sees the closing: monopoly capital's recapture. The industry is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98). Contemporary cross-ownership (Sony, Universal, Warner; Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music; Ticketmaster; Amazon as cross-owner) is the institutional referent.

A wrinkle within Benjamin: his politics depended on cinema being a *collective* mode of reception. Bodies share space and rhythm. The atomised consumption of streamed music through headphones, and the algorithmic individuation of feeds (Spotify, Netflix, YouTube), erodes this collective condition. Jameson (1991) reads the same erosion as postmodernism's pastiche-driven collapse of utopian imagination: the foggy-mist aura of the manufactured star is what survives when the depth-models that gave Benjamin's distracted reception its political traction have been hollowed out, which is to say that Adorno's diagnosis hardens in the postmodern phase rather than weakens.

Counter to the Adornian closure stand Crass's anarcho-punk distribution outside the major-label system and the Lancaster Music Co-op as a worker-owned space. These are Benjaminian moments within an Adornian field: mechanical reproduction (the record, the cassette, the digital file) used outside monopoly distribution to enable democratised, collective reception. AI-generated music and ChatGPT-assisted production add a new ambivalent layer: democratised tools whose outputs are typically captured by the same monopoly distribution.

The synthesis is that the politics of reproducibility is not fixed by the technology but by the ownership of the means of mental production. The same technology, in monopoly hands, conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist (Adorno); in worker-owned or independent hands, it democratises (Benjamin).

I conclude that the question's claim is right: the culture industry resurrects aura as a foggy mist around the star and the manufactured product. But Benjamin's hope is not refuted, only conditional on the political-economic question of who owns the means of mental production, which contemporary concentration answers brutally.

### M12. Is the Frankfurt School's account of mass culture vindicated or refuted by contemporary cross-ownership in the cultural industries?

I argue that the Frankfurt School's account is vindicated rather than refuted by contemporary cross-ownership, because the same monopoly concentration Adorno identified in 1947 has extended from broadcasting and Hollywood to span recorded-music rights, streaming distribution, ticketing and retail simultaneously, with single owners operating across multiple layers.

The vindication runs along four lines. First, the structural diagnosis. The industry is "made possible by contemporary technical capabilities as well as by economic and administrative concentration" (Adorno, 1991: 98). The contemporary picture is concrete. Recorded-music rights sit in Sony, Universal and Warner. Streaming sits in Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music, Tidal and SoundCloud. Ticketing sits in Ticketmaster and See Tickets. Retail (HMV, Amazon) is concentrated. Amazon operates simultaneously as retailer and streamer, the textbook cross-ownership case Adorno predicted. Netflix and YouTube show the same pattern in video.

Second, standardisation. "What parades as progress in the culture industry, as the incessantly new which it offers up, remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100) describes the verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton of "Shake It Off" and the standardised types Adorno enumerated: "mother songs, home songs, nonsense or 'novelty' songs, pseudo-nursery rhymes, laments for a lost girl" (Adorno, 1941: 3). The Marvel/MCU formula and reality-TV formats from *X Factor* to *Britain's Got Talent* show the same templating.

Third, pseudo-individuation. Pseudo-individuation "keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them" (Adorno, 1941: 23) is exactly what "Von Dutch" performs: stylised individuality on top of a conventional pop skeleton. The track subverts the conventional pop-song surface but reproduces its structure. TikTok virality fits the same shape.

Fourth, the star system. "Its ideology above all makes use of the star system, borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101) is illustrated by the most-streamed YouTube and Spotify charts and by the ideology that record companies make stars and that you can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money. Influencer culture extends the operation to non-musical platforms.

Counter-currents are visible. Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" (anarcho-punk outside the major-label system) and the Lancaster Music Co-op (worker-owned production) operate against the grain. Rage Against the Machine is the commodification case: anti-capitalist content sold by a major. Independent, radical, even anti-capitalist music can be sold as a commodity by capitalists; capture is the rule.

Transformations are real but do not refute Adorno. The locus of decision has shifted from named executives to administrative procedures and algorithms. This makes "from above" more impersonal but not less effective. Adorno anticipated this depersonalisation: the industry's "interests have become objectified in its ideology and have even made themselves independent of the compulsion to sell the cultural commodities which must be swallowed anyway" (Adorno, 1991: 100). Habermas (1989) reads the same monopolisation as the "refeudalisation" of the public sphere: large commercial organisations strike compromises with the state while staging plebiscitary "openness" toward audiences. The Frankfurt critique extends naturally through Habermas, so contemporary cross-ownership of streaming, ticketing and retail fulfils both diagnoses simultaneously, rather than belonging to either alone.

The Stuart Hall objection (audiences are active, decode in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways) is the standard corrective from British Cultural Studies. But Adorno's reply stands: "it can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The active audience still listens to standardised material every day.

The Foucauldian alternative, that power is productive rather than repressive, complements Adorno: the industry produces a particular kind of consumer ("an appendage of the machinery," Adorno, 1991: 99) rather than manipulating a pre-formed one. *Black Mirror*'s satire of platform subjectivity reads as a contemporary illustration of "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104).

I conclude that contemporary structural concentration vindicates rather than refutes the Frankfurt School. The honest critical position is neither dismissal of Adorno as elitist nor uncritical revival; it is to combine his structural diagnosis with the active-audience corrective from British Cultural Studies, while keeping monopoly concentration as the institutional baseline.

### M13. Discuss the strengths and limits of base-and-superstructure as a model for thinking about contemporary cultural production.

I argue that base-and-superstructure remains an indispensable analytical model for contemporary cultural production but only in the qualified form Engels and Althusser gave it: as determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination, not a one-way causal arrow. The strength is that it forces us to ask who owns the means of cultural production; the limit is that it can read culture as mere reflection if applied crudely.

Marx's formulation in the *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) is foundational: "The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation on which there arises a legal and political superstructure and to which there correspond definite forms of social consciousness" (Marx, 1859). The base is the relations of production; the superstructure is law, politics, culture and "social consciousness." The picture is dialectical: economic relations at the base shape an ideological superstructure of education, religion, politics, culture and media, and the superstructure in turn maintains the base.

The strength is that it makes ownership analytically primary. To understand recorded music, one asks who owns the labels (Sony, Universal, Warner), the streaming platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, SoundCloud), the ticketing (Ticketmaster, See Tickets) and the retail layer (HMV, Amazon as cross-owner). This is the concentration of the means of mental production Marx described, industrially scaled.

The materialist-ideology pairing gives the contemporary application. On the materialist side: musicians hold labour power, exchange it for a wage, produce a commodity, complete circulation M – C – C' – M', while capitalists invest in fixed capital (studios, production, distribution). On the ideological side: record companies make "stars"; you can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money; music is special; music is an industry and technological progress is good for it. The base shapes the superstructure that legitimates it.

The camera-obscura case sharpens the operation. Branson appears wealthy because clever; the material basis of his wealth is the exploitation of musicians' labour. "If in all ideology men and their circumstances appear upside-down as in a camera obscura, this phenomenon arises just as much from their historical life-process as the inversion of objects on the retina does from their physical life-process" (Marx, 1846). Ideology represents class relations in an illusory form that concurs with the interests of the dominant class.

The limit of the crude reading is that cultural artefacts are not reducible to their economic base. Three cases share a capitalist base but do different cultural work: Rage Against the Machine (commodified anti-capitalism via Sony), Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" (anarcho-punk distribution outside the major-label system) and the Lancaster Music Co-op (worker-owned production space). Engels in *Anti-Dühring* (1878) recognised this: the economic is determining only in the last instance; the superstructure has its own dynamics. Althusser systematised this with determination in the last instance, relative autonomy and overdetermination.

Contemporary cross-ownership adds a complicating layer. Amazon as both retailer and streamer is at once base-level (infrastructure, labour, surplus) and superstructural (recommendation surfaces shape what listeners hear). The same monopoly occupies multiple positions in the diagram. This is not a refutation but a vindication: Engels' extension of base-and-superstructure to forms of culture and the media in *Anti-Dühring* anticipated this. TikTok and YouTube recommenders are further cases: cultural superstructure folded into the base of platform capital.

Hall (1980) and Gramsci (1971) push further: the superstructure is the terrain of hegemony, where dominant meanings are continually contested through negotiated and oppositional decoding. This refines the base-superstructure model into something more analytically useful for cultural production, where the same monopoly-structured industry that produces standardised pop also produces conditions for anti-commercial counter-currents. The base constrains rather than predicts: Rage Against the Machine acknowledges both the determining power of the base and the relative autonomy of the cultural form.

The most honest contemporary use of base-and-superstructure is therefore as a critical heuristic: always ask who owns the means of mental production, but do not assume ownership predicts content one-to-one. Rage Against the Machine and Crass share a base; they do different cultural work. The base is necessary, not sufficient, for cultural analysis.

I conclude that base-and-superstructure, in its Engelsian-Althusserian qualified form, remains the indispensable starting point for any sociology of contemporary cultural production. Crude versions are vulnerable to the standard objections; refined versions remain essential. The base shapes, the superstructure maintains, but the maintaining is itself a labour with its own contradictions.

### M14. "Entertainment is the prolongation of work under late capitalism." Evaluate Adorno and Horkheimer's claim.

I argue that Adorno and Horkheimer's claim that entertainment is the prolongation of work under late capitalism is essentially correct as a description of the structural fit between standardised labour and standardised entertainment, that Adorno's "On Popular Music" gives the mechanism, and that contemporary examples illustrate the thesis in unsettling form.

The argument runs: under industrial capitalism, the worker is subject to rationalised, alienating labour. In her free time she seeks escape. But the culture industry produces entertainment structurally similar to the work she escapes from: standardised, repetitive, requiring no concentration, "pre-digested" (Adorno, 1941: 16). "Popular music is for the masses a perpetual busman's holiday" (Adorno, 1941: 32). Entertainment relaxes by being patterned like the work; it offers no break, only the reproduction of working-day rhythms in cushioned form.

The mechanism is precise. Distraction "is bound to the present mode of production, to the rationalized and mechanized process of labor to which, directly or indirectly, masses are subject. This mode of production, which engenders fears and anxiety about unemployment, loss of income, war, has its 'nonproductive' correlate in entertainment; that is, relaxation which does not involve the effort of concentration at all" (Adorno, 1941: 30). "Their leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" (Adorno, 1941: 32). Streaming binge-watching on Netflix, doom-scrolling TikTok and YouTube tabs in the background all fit the form.

"Shake It Off" shows the structural fit. The song's address ("I stay out too late / Got nothin' in my brain") frames the listener as a worker who needs unwinding; the standardised verse-chorus structure delivers it in a form requiring no cognitive effort, so the listener returns to work on Monday without anything that might challenge her workplace reality. Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" does similar work in pseudo-individuated form: surface stylisation gives the impression of difference while structural fit with the working-week routine is undisturbed.

The catharsis function sharpens the diagnosis. Oasis's "Cigarettes and Alcohol" (1994) addresses the alienation of work ("Is it worth the aggravation / To find yourself a job when there's nothing worth working for?") and supplies a cathartic resolution that defuses political anger. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43). Class anger articulated through the standardised pop form is absorbed. Reality TV functions identically: ritualised affect that re-prepares the viewer for Monday.

The star system contributes. The industry "propagates supposedly great personalities and operates with heart-throbs" (Adorno, 1991: 101) and the "you can be a star" ideology supplies the fantasy that escape from the wage relation is individually available, while structural concentration ensures almost no one will escape. Influencer culture is the contemporary face: the dream of monetised personality disciplines the worker into accepting present arrangements on the promise of advancement.

The "what about genuine leisure?" objection has force. People play sport, garden, read, sing in choirs. Adorno acknowledges this distinction: "culture, in the true sense" "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100), while the culture industry assimilates. Williams (1958) sharpens the corrective: culture is "ordinary," a "whole way of life" lived from below in choirs, libraries and adult education, not only the industrial hit drilled from above. Williams's insistence keeps Adorno's prolongation-of-work thesis from collapsing into total pessimism, while accepting that the industrial form does dominate.

Stuart Hall's objection is that audiences resignify entertainment. The football match is community, identity, collective experience. Adorno's claim that sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection (Adorno, 1991) is too strong: fans organise, protest against owners, build solidarities. The thesis needs qualification: entertainment is more often the prolongation of work than its genuine alternative, but the line is not fixed by genre, and active audience appropriation is real.

I conclude that Adorno and Horkheimer's claim describes the structural fit between alienated labour and standardised entertainment with continuing accuracy. "Shake It Off," "Von Dutch" and "Cigarettes and Alcohol" all illustrate the prolongation: relaxation that re-prepares the worker for Monday rather than enabling her to imagine an alternative. The objections from genuine leisure and active reception qualify the thesis but do not refute it.

### M15. Does Walter Benjamin offer a more useful framework than Adorno for analysing the democratising potential of mechanically reproduced art?

I argue that Benjamin offers a more useful framework than Adorno for analysing the *productive* potential of mechanically reproduced art (what producers and audiences can do with cheap recording and direct distribution outside the major-label system) while Adorno remains more useful for the *distributive* layer where monopoly capital concentrates the means of mental production. The two frameworks describe different layers of the same situation, and we need both.

Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935) makes three moves. First, mechanical reproduction shatters the aura: the work is disconnected from the locale where it was made and from its cultural and physical context. Second, this produces new values and new ways of seeing. Third, it democratises: society becomes freer to organise and control itself in reception, no longer subject to the authority of original works of art. The paradigm cases are cinema (people take the perspective of the camera) and the club (people dance together to music): collective, distracted, embodied modes of reception that bourgeois contemplative aesthetics could not accommodate.

The political pay-off is clear. Through art one can conceive a world different from the life dominated by commodity production: art anticipates the good life. Mechanical reproduction, by lowering cost and transferring art from cult contemplation to public exhibition, is the means by which this subversive function reaches the working class.

The working-class emancipation tradition makes Benjamin's hope concrete. Crass's "Feeding of the Five Thousand" is mechanically reproduced but distributed outside the major-label system, sold cheaply, with profits to political causes: a Benjaminian use of reproduction. The Lancaster Music Co-op is worker-owned ownership of the means of musical production. The cassette, the mp3, home recording software and the DJ controller, set against the eighteenth-century court painting, show the historical shift from court patronage to home production.

But Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" remains forceful at the level of distribution. The monopoly capital running contemporary cultural circulation (Sony, Universal, Warner, Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Ticketmaster, See Tickets, HMV) is the recapture of Benjamin's democratised production. "The culture industry... does not strictly counterpose another principle to that of aura, but rather... conserves the decaying aura as a foggy mist" (Adorno, 1991: 102). The star system "borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation" (Adorno, 1991: 101) manufactures aura around the mass-reproduced personality. Madonna's "Material Girl," the parade of the most-streamed YouTube artists, K-pop/BTS and the influencer economy all show foggy-mist aura around mechanically reproduced figures.

The cleanest synthesis distinguishes layers. The *production layer* is Benjaminian where it remains outside monopoly capital: home recording, anarcho-punk labels, worker-owned spaces, AI-generated music and ChatGPT-assisted writing in independent hands. The *distribution layer* is Adornian where monopoly capital prevails: cross-owned majors and streamers, the concentration of ticketing. Rage Against the Machine is the clearest illustration of the tension: anti-capitalist content (Benjaminian in intent) distributed by Sony (Adornian capture in practice).

Two further considerations matter. First, Benjamin's optimism depended on the *collective* mode of cinema reception (bodies in shared space). The atomised consumption of music through headphones, and the individuated feed on Spotify and YouTube, erode this collective condition. Adorno's "the customer is not king... not its subject but its object" (Adorno, 1991: 99) diagnoses the erosion: the audience is an "object of calculation; an appendage of the machinery." Second, Benjamin's analysis has an under-specified theory of ownership; he sees the shift in artistic production but does not develop, as Adorno does, the analysis of monopoly capital's recapture. Williams (1958) supplies the bridge from below that both miss: culture as "a whole way of life," in which ordinary people make and remake meaning rather than only receive, an active corrective to Adornian closure without abandoning Benjamin's politics of access.

I conclude that Benjamin is more useful for the productive layer (cheap tools, democratised production, collective making), Adorno more useful for the distributive layer (concentrated capital, manufactured aura, the star system). The aura is shattered in Benjamin's sense (the unique object replaced by the copiable record) and reconstructed in Adorno's sense (the foggy mist around the manufactured star). The contemporary critical task is to deploy both.

### M16. "Commodity fetishism is the foundational concept of any critical sociology of culture." Discuss.

I argue that commodity fetishism is one of two foundational concepts (alongside ruling-class control of the means of mental production) for any critical sociology of culture, and that its strength is its ability to explain why social relations between people appear, in capitalist cultures, as relations between things. The contemporary chart shows the concept in operation, and Adorno's reformulation extends it to the form of the cultural commodity itself.

Marx's definition in *Capital Volume 1* is the canonical passage: "The relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation of objects which exists outside them... It is a particular social relation between men themselves which in their eyes assumes a phantasmagorical form of a relation between things... This is what I call fetishism; it attaches itself to the products of labour as soon as they are produced as commodities" (Marx, 1867). Three elements: real social relations exist between producers; they are misrecognised as properties of the commodity; the misrecognition is inseparable from commodity production. The commodity acquires a phantasmagorical quality that obscures the labour that produced it.

For cultural analysis the concept is indispensable because culture under capitalism is principally produced as commodities. Any UK top-ten chart appears as a list of self-standing creative achievements. The labour behind each track (songwriters, session musicians, producers, mixers, mastering engineers, music-video crews, marketing teams, distribution staff, retail and streaming workers) is invisible. The track stands as a thing. The Netflix or Marvel/MCU release operates identically: a film appears as a discrete object whose vast production labour is invisible behind a brand.

The Branson case is the textbook camera-obscura inversion. He appears wealthy because clever; the actual basis is the exploitation of labour, of artists and musicians who make the music he sells, over their entire working lives. Fetishism is the operation by which the social relation (Branson capitalises on signed labour) appears as a property of the thing (a magical talent for business). This inversion concurs with the interests of the dominant class (Marx, 1846).

Read through *Capital*, the musician's position becomes legible: "A singer who sings like a bird is an unproductive worker. If she sells her song for money, she is to that extent a wage-labourer or merchant. But if the same singer is engaged by an entrepreneur who makes her sing to make money, then she becomes a productive worker, since she produces capital directly" (Marx, 1867). The fetish conceals this wage-labour relation: the song appears as a thing one can possess (a stream, a download), not as a stage in the circulation M – C – C' – M'.

Adorno's reformulation deepens the analysis. Where Marx identifies fetishism as a property of the commodity, Adorno identifies its intensification in the cultural commodity: "Cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The 1867 fetishised commodity was a coat that obscured the labour of weavers and tailors. The contemporary cultural commodity is a Charli XCX track or a Taylor Swift hit whose standardised form, manufactured aura and ubiquitous promotion obscure both the labour of production and the standardised character of the product. "What parades as progress in the culture industry, as the incessantly new which it offers up, remains the disguise for an eternal sameness" (Adorno, 1991: 100) is the form fetishism takes in industrial culture. K-pop and the BTS franchise show the operation at corporate scale.

The objection is that fetishism, taken alone, makes audiences look passive. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding shows that audiences can read against the grain, denaturalise the fetish and recognise the labour. Arnold (1869) and Q. D. Leavis (1932) supply the right-wing variant of the same worry about commercial concealment, but locate it in cultural decline rather than capital. The strength of Marx's concept over the Leavisite tradition is precisely that it analyses the mechanism (commodity production) rather than mourning its symptoms, and so remains diagnostic where their account is elegiac.

I conclude that commodity fetishism is foundational, alongside the means of mental production, for any critical sociology of culture. Marx supplies the concept; the contemporary chart is its everyday application; Adorno deepens it into reification of the cultural form; Hall supplements with reception theory. Without it, cultural analysis loses the insight that culture under capitalism is structurally misleading about its production.

### M17. Compare Adorno's analysis of the star system with the contemporary role of the music industry's "supposedly great personalities."

I argue that the contemporary music industry's parade of "supposedly great personalities," visible across the most-streamed YouTube and Spotify charts, is the maturation, not the supersession, of the star system Adorno analysed. The functions Adorno identified (manufactured individuality compensating for standardised content, propagation of the "ordinary person can make it" ideology) survive intact; structural concentration tightens rather than loosens the system.

Adorno's analysis runs: "Its ideology above all makes use of the star system, borrowed from individualistic art and its commercial exploitation. The more dehumanized its methods of operation and content, the more diligently and successfully the culture industry propagates supposedly great personalities and operates with heart-throbs" (Adorno, 1991: 101). The star system performs two functions. First, it gives the industrially produced commodity an "individual air"; the aura around the star compensates for the absence of aura in the product: "each product affects an individual air; individuality itself serves to reinforce ideology, in so far as the illusion is conjured up that the completely reified and mediated is a sanctuary from immediacy and life" (Adorno, 1991: 101). Second, the system propagates the ideology that the ordinary person can make it.

The materialist and ideological sides of the analysis make the second function explicit. The ideology runs: record companies make "stars"; you can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money. Beside this sits the materialist side: musicians as wage-labourers exchanging labour power for a wage, producing commodities, completing M – C – C' – M' for capitalists who invest in studios, production and distribution. Star ideology hides this wage relation behind the individual personality.

Adorno's "On Popular Music" sharpens the point. He notes that the listener "is quickly able to distinguish the types of music and even the performing band, this in spite of the fundamental identity of the material and the great similarity of the presentations apart from their emphasized distinguishing trademarks. This labeling technique, as regards type of music and band, is pseudo-individualization" (Adorno, 1941: 26). His paradigm cases were sweet versus swing, Benny Goodman versus Guy Lombardo. The contemporary chart shows the same structure: distinct creative identities presented while the underlying material is industrially standardised.

The most-streamed YouTube artists and YouTube's own platform-level valuation show star-making machinery extending from audio to video. Monopoly concentration in record labels (Sony, Universal, Warner) extends to the platforms that host star-making. K-pop/BTS with their corporate training systems, and the Disney/Star Wars franchise apparatus, show the operation in further forms. Fisher (2009) names the affective register of this maturation: capitalist realism, the inability to imagine any life outside the star/influencer feed, the post-political horizon in which "you can be a star" is the only ambition the system is willing to legitimate.

The "ordinary person can make it" claim cuts in both directions. The ideological function is to legitimate a system that mostly produces precarity: the few visible winners underwrite the many invisible losers (the wage-labouring musicians who never become stars). The structural rate of success is low, the ideological function is high. The cost-of-living pressure on working artists makes the gap sharper.

The classical Hollywood example drew on the studio system: studios owned contracts and manufactured personae. The contemporary structure is functionally identical at the level of the major labels (Sony, Universal, Warner). The Branson example is the classical case sharpened: Branson is the star-maker who appears in the camera-obscura as star himself, while the labour of the musicians signed to Virgin disappears from view.

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences are not duped: they identify with stars actively, form fan communities, negotiate meaning. Fan communities do produce meaning, friendship and solidarity. The reply is that the structural concentration of star-making in monopoly hands ensures that the meanings fans produce are produced within a field whose terms have been set by the industry. "It can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105) is the structural rejoinder.

I conclude that the contemporary parade of supposedly great musical personalities is the maturation of the star system Adorno analysed. The functions are the same, the ideological supplements are the same, and the structural concentration is more intense. Adorno's framework predicts the contemporary star economy with surprising accuracy.

### M18. Is the Frankfurt School's pessimism about popular culture an indispensable corrective to celebratory accounts, or a paternalistic dead end?

I argue that the Frankfurt School's pessimism is an indispensable corrective to celebratory accounts of popular culture, but that some of its specific moves (especially Adorno on jazz) are paternalistic and racially blinkered in ways that must be corrected. The honest position is to retain the structural critique (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, commodity fetishism, integration from above) while abandoning the assumption that "serious" European art music is the implicit measure of value.

The celebratory account, associated with British Cultural Studies (Williams, Hoggart, Hall), runs: audiences are active, popular culture is meaningful, fans co-create, culture is ordinary, and dominant texts are open to negotiated and oppositional reading. The Birmingham tradition is empirically grounded and politically generous. Adorno's pessimism shares a worry with Arnold (1869) and F. R. Leavis (1930), who fear that commercial culture flattens the "minority" of discerning judgement; but Adorno locates the problem in capital, not in the masses themselves, and so escapes the paternalism his Leavisite ancestors fall into.

But the celebratory account has three blind spots the Frankfurt School corrects. First, it reads off active reception from the *existence* of active reception, ignoring the structural conditions under which most reception is patterned and distracted. Popular music is "for the masses a perpetual busman's holiday" (Adorno, 1941: 32). The modal experience of listening is "general inattention which is only interrupted by sudden flashes of recognition" (Adorno, 1941: 36). Streaming background listening, Netflix while scrolling, and YouTube as ambient soundtrack fit this profile.

Second, the celebratory account treats the market as a neutral medium of choice. The Frankfurt School insists on the political economy: who owns the means of mental production. The current concentration (Sony, Universal, Warner; Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify; Ticketmaster, See Tickets; Amazon as cross-owner) is invisible if you only look at audience meanings. "Cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100) is the corrective.

Third, the celebratory account underestimates standardisation. "Shake It Off" shows the verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton; "Von Dutch" shows pseudo-individuation; "Cigarettes and Alcohol" shows catharsis defusing class anger. The Marvel/MCU release cycle and reality-TV formats from *X Factor* to *Britain's Got Talent* show the same templating. None of this contradicts active reception, but all of it constrains the conditions under which reception happens.

The paternalism objection is real. Adorno's writing on jazz, including the sweeping claim about "masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism" (Adorno, 1941: 37), treats jazz as a degraded form, misses its African-American roots, misses its improvisational sophistication and the political work it did for Black Americans. The implicit standard is European concert music (Beethoven's Seventh, the Appassionata, the Fifth Symphony, Haydn, Brahms, Mozart). This is a major intellectual failure. Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* reads modernity from the perspective of enslaved Africans and refuses the Eurocentric measure.

The deeper paternalism is the assumption that ordinary people are dupes who need critical theory to see what is happening to them. People are often more sceptical of their own consumption than Adorno gave them credit for. Adorno himself describes this: "If it guarantees them even the most fleeting gratification they desire a deception which is nonetheless transparent to them" (Adorno, 1991: 103). The audience is half-aware of being marketed to; the question is whether half-awareness is enough to break the loop. *Black Mirror*'s satirical reflexivity is a popular site where consumers articulate exactly this half-awareness; Adorno's view is that it is insufficient because the structural drilling continues.

The most productive use of the Frankfurt School is selective. Retain the structural diagnosis (standardisation, pseudo-individuation, integration from above, commodity fetishism, the dialectic of enlightenment). Drop the European-art-music measure. Combine with Hall's reception theory and the "culture is ordinary" insight. Add Foucauldian productive power and Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity to fill the gaps Adorno could not see. Contemporary phenomena (Netflix's recommender, the TikTok algorithm, Marvel/MCU release planning, influencer culture, AI-generated music and ChatGPT outputs entering the chart) are exactly the cases that need the combined frame: structurally drilled forms decoded by audiences who are half-aware but daily exposed.

I conclude that the Frankfurt School's pessimism is an indispensable corrective, not a paternalistic dead end, provided we abandon its Eurocentric implicit measure of value and combine it with reception theory and contemporary political-economic analysis. The honest position is selective inheritance.

### M19. How does the concept of pseudo-individuation help us understand the manufactured pop song and the manufactured star?

I argue that pseudo-individuation is the single most useful Adornian concept for analysing the manufactured pop song and the manufactured star, because both produce intense impressions of individuality and authenticity while being structurally standardised down to the form level. Charli XCX's "Von Dutch" is the textbook contemporary case, and the materialist-ideology pairing on the star system supplies the analytical frame.

Adorno's definition is precise: "Pseudo-individualization... endowing cultural mass production with the halo of free choice or open market on the basis of standardization itself. Standardization of song hits keeps the customers in line by doing their listening for them, as it were. Pseudo-individualization, for its part, keeps them in line by making them forget that what they listen to is already listened to for them, or 'pre-digested'" (Adorno, 1941: 23). Two elements: standardisation does the structural work; pseudo-individuation disguises it as choice. Adorno's 1941 example was sweet versus swing, Benny Goodman versus Guy Lombardo: apparently distinctive identities whose underlying material is the same (Adorno, 1941: 26).

The manufactured pop song is the cleanest unit. "Shake It Off" makes the standardised skeleton visible: Verse-Chorus-Verse-Chorus-Bridge-Chorus, with the chorus repeating the title hook on every iteration. Adorno's diagnosis applies exactly: "the harmonic cornerstones of each hit, the beginning and the end of each part, must beat out the standard scheme... This inexorable device guarantees that regardless of what aberrations occur, the hit will lead back to the same familiar experience, and nothing fundamentally novel will be introduced" (Adorno, 1941: 3). TikTok virality, which rewards predictable hooks at platform scale, intensifies the same operation.

"Von Dutch" shows pseudo-individuation in sharpened form. The operation runs through electroclash blending 1980s synthpop with contemporary pop; an edgy and alternative surface marked by the repeated f-word in chart pop, an image as subversive pop star, a video featuring stylised violence; and, most explicitly, a track that subverts the conventional pop-song surface but reproduces its structure. This is pseudo-individuation textbook: a halo of alternative attitude over a structurally conventional pop track.

The "individual air" applies to the manufactured star. "Each product affects an individual air; individuality itself serves to reinforce ideology, in so far as the illusion is conjured up that the completely reified and mediated is a sanctuary from immediacy and life" (Adorno, 1991: 101). The ideology runs: record companies make "stars"; you can be a star by being a musician and make lots of money. The star is the human face of the manufactured commodity; the personality is pseudo-individuation at the producer level. K-pop/BTS training systems, Disney's franchise actor pipelines and influencer brand-personas all show the operation.

Madonna's "Material Girl" illustrates the operation at scale. Madonna is a mechanically reproduced commodity: recording, video and sleeve are copies. Yet the figure is presented as an irreducible individual. The fetishised commodity is presented as the unmediated self. The lyrics (the boy with the cold hard cash is always Mister Right) make the commodity logic of pseudo-individuated stardom textually explicit.

The catharsis function complements pseudo-individuation. Oasis's "Cigarettes and Alcohol" gives the listener a distinctively rebellious persona while channelling underlying anger into a singable refrain. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line" (Adorno, 1941: 43). Habermas (1989) reads the same operation at the level of communication: the commercial public sphere stages "plebiscitary support" through a display of openness that is itself pseudo-individuated, so pseudo-individuation in pop and refeudalisation of the public are two faces of one process. Reality-TV from *X Factor* to *Britain's Got Talent* operates identically.

The Stuart Hall objection is that audiences are not duped: fans identify with stars actively, decode in negotiated and oppositional ways, build communities around shared identification. This has force. But Adorno's reply is that active identification operates within a field whose terms have been set by the industry, and "the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The active fan still consumes pseudo-individuated material every day.

I conclude that pseudo-individuation is the indispensable analytical tool for the manufactured pop song and the manufactured star, that it explains the gap between experienced individuality and structural sameness, and that "Von Dutch" and "Material Girl" make the operation visible at both song and star levels. The form proves Adorno right.

### M20. "Conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno). Discuss.

I argue that Adorno's claim that "conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104) is the strongest single sentence in his cultural writing, that it describes a tendency rather than an absolute, and that the contemporary cross-ownership of cultural industries supplies the structural conditions under which the tendency operates.

Adorno's full passage runs: "The categorical imperative of the culture industry no longer has anything in common with freedom. It proclaims: you shall conform, without instruction as to what; conform to that which exists anyway, and to that which everyone thinks anyway as a reflex of its power and omnipresence. The power of the culture industry's ideology is such that conformity has replaced consciousness" (Adorno, 1991: 104). Two things to notice. First, the imperative is contentless: not "conform to fascism" but "conform to that which exists anyway." Second, the mechanism is the reflex of power and omnipresence; ubiquity does the work, not argument.

The mechanism is the cumulative effect of a standardised cultural environment. "It can be assumed without hesitation that steady drops hollow the stone, especially since the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). Contemporary cross-ownership (Sony, Universal, Warner; Apple Music, Spotify, Amazon Music; Ticketmaster; Amazon as cross-owner) names the structural condition. TikTok's optimisation toward predictable engagement extends the logic.

The standardised form supplies the formulas. "Shake It Off" shows the verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton; "Von Dutch" shows pseudo-individuation; "Cigarettes and Alcohol" shows the working-day rhythm reproduced in leisure. Each track is a "steady drop"; their cumulative effect is the hollowing of the stone. The listener's mode of reception is "general inattention which is only interrupted by sudden flashes of recognition" (Adorno, 1941: 36).

Three of Adorno's sharpest formulations apply at different levels. The recognition of brand names has taken the place of choice: at consumer level, the brand is the contentless imperative; recognition is the reflex. Sport is not play but ritual in which the subjected celebrate their subjection: at spectacle level. Anyone incapable of talking in the prescribed fashion is threatened in his very existence: at the level of language. Each is a facet of the same diagnosis.

The structural fit between alienated work and patterned leisure runs the mechanism. "Their leisure is an escape from work and at the same time is molded after those psychological attitudes to which their workaday world exclusively habituates them" (Adorno, 1941: 32). The worker encounters a leisure pre-digested, standardised, requiring no concentration. Consciousness that might criticise has nowhere to stand outside both. Fisher (2009) names this enclosure capitalist realism: the inability to imagine alternatives is exactly the form Adorno's contentless imperative ("conform to that which exists anyway") takes once the workplace and the playlist share a logic.

The standard objection is that audience studies suggest reception and appropriation of cultural products is a complex social process involving a dialectical relationship between agency and structure. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model gives the form. Adorno's reply acknowledges the audience's "deep unconscious mistrust, the last residue of the difference between art and empirical reality" (Adorno, 1991: 105), but holds that this residue is inadequate to break the loop because the structural drilling continues.

Foucault's productive-power objection sharpens but does not refute Adorno. Culture under capitalism produces a particular kind of consumer: musicians appear as labour power producing commodities, on the material side; on the ideological side, record companies make "stars" and you can be a star. The subject produced is conscious in ways (brands, stars, music as industry) that align with conformity. Critical, autonomous consciousness has been displaced.

The counter-current is real but small. Crass and the Lancaster Music Co-op show an alternative form of cultural production, structured differently at the level of ownership. Adorno allows for this: he distinguishes the culture industry from culture which "always simultaneously raised a protest against the petrified relations under which they lived" (Adorno, 1991: 100). The protest is real where structurally enabled; conformity prevails where it is not.

I conclude that Adorno's claim describes a tendency the monopolised cultural industries continue to instantiate, that the standardised form ("Shake It Off," "Von Dutch," "Cigarettes and Alcohol") is the mechanism, and that working-class counter-currents (Crass, the Lancaster Music Co-op) preserve a space for consciousness without refuting the dominant diagnosis. The corrective politics is exactly Adorno's: take the culture industry "seriously critically" and refuse to cower before its monopolistic character (Adorno, 1991: 102).

### M21. Can independent, radical or anti-capitalist art exist within the culture industry? Discuss with examples.

I argue that independent, radical or anti-capitalist art can exist within the culture industry but always in compromised form, because the industry commodifies what it captures, and that three positions (Rage Against the Machine, Crass, Lancaster Music Co-op) map the available options precisely.

Adorno's strongest version is that the industry is total: "cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (Adorno, 1991: 100). On this reading, no art produced within the industry is innocent of its commodity logic. The radical content is contained by the standardised form; the political message is digested as entertainment; the cathartic release defuses the political energy. "It is catharsis for the masses, but catharsis which keeps them all the more firmly in line. One who weeps does not resist any more than one who marches" (Adorno, 1941: 43).

The commodification position is exemplified by Rage Against the Machine, signed to a major label and releasing anti-capitalist material through capitalist channels. Independent, radical, even anti-capitalist music can be sold as a commodity by capitalists. The band's anti-capitalist commitment is genuine; the form of distribution is what Adorno predicted, with the major realising the surplus value and the listener consuming the message as a rock song before returning to work. "The entire practice of the culture industry transfers the profit motive naked onto cultural forms" (Adorno, 1991: 99) names the operation.

The pure-resistance position is Crass, whose "Feeding of the Five Thousand" is the paradigm case. Crass ran their own anarcho-punk label, sold cheaply, refused commercial entanglements where possible, and distributed through political and gig networks. Adorno's framework allows for this: a culture maintaining "rebellious resistance inherent within it as long as social control was not yet total" (Adorno, 1991: 99). The cost is reach.

The structural alternative is the Lancaster Music Co-op: a worker-owned space where rehearsal rooms, equipment, recording facilities and gig promotion are collectively held. The means of mental production, concentrated elsewhere in corporate hands, are here held collectively. Williams (1958) gives the theoretical home for the Co-op: culture is "ordinary," made and remade in the institutions ordinary people build for themselves, not only consumed from above. Marx's framework predicts this as the only way out: workers must hold their own means of production.

The analytical frame is materialist oppression and emancipation. On the structural side stand Marx's claim that "between equal rights, force decides" (Marx, 1867) and the famous opening of the *Manifesto*, that "the history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggle" (Marx and Engels, 1848). On the emancipatory side stand capitalism's tendency to create its own gravediggers, the recurrence of capitalist crisis, and worker organisation. Crass is worker organisation in cultural production; the Co-op is organisation at the level of fixed capital.

Benjamin offers a complementary reading. Mechanical reproduction (cassette, mp3, home recording, the DJ controller) lowers cost and creates conditions for democratised art. Crass's records and the Co-op's recordings are Benjaminian uses of reproduction outside monopoly hands. Art anticipates the good life. AI-generated music is an ambivalent new case: democratised tools in principle, captured by monopoly platforms in practice.

The objection from Adorno's pessimism is that the cracks are too small. Crass's reach is limited; the Co-op's scale is small. The structural concentration (Sony, Universal, Warner, Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Ticketmaster, Amazon as cross-owner) is dominant, and alternatives operate in its margins. The cost-of-living squeeze on artists narrows the margin further.

Stuart Hall's objection is that the boundary between industry and resistance is negotiated. Rage Against the Machine's commodified anti-capitalism may still politicise listeners; the major-label commodity may be read oppositionally; energy may transfer to organising outside consumption. Adorno's reply is that structural drilling of standardised forms hollows the stone even where individual moments of resistance are real.

The honest synthesis: anti-capitalist art can exist within the industry (Rage Against the Machine) at the cost of commodification; can exist outside it (Crass) at the cost of reach; and can exist at the structural level (Lancaster Music Co-op) where the means of mental production are collectively held. These three positions are the three available stances.

I conclude that the question's "can" is the right modal: it can, but only with awareness of the cost. Adorno's diagnosis remains the analytical baseline; Marx supplies the structural exit (collective ownership of the means of mental production); Benjamin supplies the technical possibility (mechanical reproduction in non-monopoly hands).

### M22. Critically assess the claim that the Frankfurt School "reads off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves."

I argue that the criticism that the Frankfurt School "reads off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves" is partly right and partly wrong: partly right because Adorno often does infer audience effects from formal analysis, partly wrong because Adorno himself anticipated the objection and gave a structural reply the standard criticism does not fully meet.

The criticism has multiple parts. The school is said to ignore agency and resistance; to fail to do justice to what is new and distinctive about the development of the media; to fail to develop a satisfactory theory accounting for the circulation and reception of symbolic forms; to read off the consequences of cultural products from the products themselves; and to neglect the audience-studies finding that reception and appropriation are complex social processes involving a dialectical relationship between agency and structure. It has real force.

It applies most strongly to Adorno on jazz. In "On Popular Music" (1941) he makes sweeping claims about psychic effects ("masochistic adjustment to authoritarian collectivism," Adorno, 1941: 37) based on formal analysis. He treats jazz as a degraded mass form whose listeners are reduced to obedience by rhythmic conformity. He does not engage seriously with how Black American audiences actually heard or used jazz, its African-American roots, or the political work it did. The result is racially blinkered and analytically weak.

Adorno's response in "Culture Industry Reconsidered" is twofold. First, he acknowledges the empirical gap: "It is true that thorough research has not, for the time being, produced an airtight case proving the regressive effects of particular products of the culture industry. No doubt an imaginatively designed experiment could achieve this more successfully than the powerful financial interests concerned would find comfortable" (Adorno, 1991: 105). The gap is partly produced by the political economy of research.

Second, he gives a structural reply: "steady drops hollow the stone... the system of the culture industry that surrounds the masses tolerates hardly any deviation and incessantly drills the same formulas on behaviour" (Adorno, 1991: 105). Cumulative exposure to standardised forms is a reasonable basis for inference. This is not "reading off effects from products" but reading off effects from the *system* of products, a more defensible move.

The contemporary examples support the structural reply. "Shake It Off" alone proves little; the verse-chorus-bridge-chorus skeleton drilled into thousands of hits is more than the sum of its instances. "Von Dutch" alone proves little; pseudo-individuation drilled across the chart supports an inference about the form of life on offer. "Cigarettes and Alcohol" alone proves little; catharsis-that-keeps-them-in-line drilled across the genre supports an inference about social function. TikTok's algorithm intensifies the structural drilling.

When the means of mental production are concentrated (Sony, Universal, Warner; Apple Music, Tidal, SoundCloud, Amazon Music, Spotify; Ticketmaster, See Tickets; Amazon as cross-owner), the consumer's menu is structurally constrained. Adorno's inference from menu to consumer is, given the constraint, more defensible than the criticism allows.

The Hall (1980) and Gramsci (1971) reply remains correct on the individual level: hegemony is contested terrain, and audiences decode in dominant, negotiated and oppositional ways. But variation occurs around a mean shaped by the encoded text and its mass distribution, so the Frankfurt move (analyse the product and the system) and the hegemony move (study the decoding) are complementary, not opposed.

The deepest defence is that the Frankfurt move is *normative*, not just predictive. Adorno asks what the cultural product is doing, what kind of subject it presupposes, what kind of life it makes available. These questions can be answered by formal analysis even if reception varies, because the question concerns the form of life on offer. The form offered by the standardised hit is different from Beethoven's Seventh (Adorno's comparative example); analysing the works tells us something true regardless of what individual listeners do.

The criticism's strongest version is therefore not refuted but qualified. The Frankfurt School should not be the only frame; empirical reception studies are also necessary. But the standardised form of the industrial commodity, drilled at scale through monopoly distribution, supports a structural inference the criticism does not undo.

I conclude that the criticism is partly right (Adorno on jazz commits the fallacy in racially blinkered ways and the empirical gap is real) and partly wrong (Adorno's structural reply is not addressed by the standard criticism, and contemporary monopolisation and standardisation support the structural reading). The honest position is to combine Frankfurt structural critique with Stuart Hall's reception theory.
