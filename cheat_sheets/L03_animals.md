## 2. Key concepts and thinkers

- **Human exceptionalism**: the doctrine that humans alone possess intention, morality, symbolism, consciousness, and so stand outside natural law (slide on "Human Exceptionalism"). Sociology, on Carter and Charles's reading, is built on this premise.
- **Bifurcation / the animal–human binary**: the dualistic carve-up nature/culture, genes/environment, facts/values, animal/human (Carter and Charles, 2011: 2, cited in slides).
- **Anthroponormativity**: Carter and Charles's term for sociology's conceptual vocabulary being silently configured around the human, analogous to heteronormativity or white normativity.
- **Companion species** (Haraway): humans become humans through co-shaping relations with other species; not "humans AND animals" but mutually constitutive becoming.
- **Becoming-with / rendering-capable** (Haraway, Despret): partners do not pre-exist the knot; they are made what they are through entanglement.
- **Multispecies sociology / multispecies ethnography**: research that treats animals as social actors and co-constituents of society, not as mute "nature."
- **Situated knowledge**: knowledge is always located in specific bodies and relations; pertinent here to "thinking with" rather than "about" animals (Kimbo, Despret).
- **Classical thinkers**: Durkheim (sociability as social, observable in animals); Weber (animals can "understand" but verifiable meaningful access is limited); Marx (productive labour, bees); Greene (horses as living machines, prime movers of industrial modernity); Parsons (consolidator of the strictly human social).

## 4. Information from the readings

**Carter and Charles, "The animal challenge to sociology" (2018).** The article argues that sociology has been "slow to take up the animal challenge" (p. 79), despite the obvious fact that "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Bryant 1979, cited p. 79). Three reasons are diagnosed. *First*, sociology has had a "fraught relationship with biology" (p. 80): in its late-nineteenth-century institutionalisation, the discipline defended "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12) against Darwinian "comprehensive naturalism", and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. *Second*, sociology rests on **human exceptionalism**: humans are treated as evolutionarily unique because of language and meaning-making, and "the human" is "usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Ingold 1994a, cited p. 81). *Third*, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe, where urban dwellers experienced themselves as "increasingly remote from most other animals" (p. 84). Yet, drawing on Greene (2008), Carter and Charles insist horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene 2008: 4, p. 84) and the "prime mover" of industrial development.

The article then argues for two conceptual revisions. **Society** must be redefined to include non-human animals as "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68). **Agency** must be reworked as relational: agency is not a Cartesian property of rational subjects but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90); it is "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). Their pheasant-shoot example dramatises a "complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92). Their concluding claim is uncompromising: "the social is animal" (p. 93). Sociology's whole conceptual vocabulary is "anthroponormative" (p. 88) — analogous to heteronormative and white-normative vocabularies — and must be reworked, not abandoned (as Latour and the new materialists would have it).

**Haraway, "Playing String Figures with Companion Species" (2016).** The chapter develops a method for thinking with animals through the metaphor of cat's cradle — "sf" doubles for science fiction, speculative fabulation, science fact, and string figures. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (p. 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (p. 13). Haraway's pigeons illustrate this: racing pigeons in Southern California are "competent agents — in the double sense of both delegates and actors" (p. 18). Her key empirical case is **PigeonBlog** (Beatriz da Costa, 2006), where racing pigeons, art-activists, working-class pigeon fanciers and DIY electronics co-produced air-pollution data over polluted Los Angeles neighbourhoods. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (p. 18) and the humans had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" (p. 22). Response-ability — a Despret term Haraway adopts — names the obligation to make others "capable" through specific entanglements (p. 12). Haraway explicitly refuses both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (p. 11). Project PigeonWatch with Washington DC schoolchildren shows recuperation across categories that racist iconography links — "rats with wings", "feral" — when the kids learn to see pigeons "as sociable birds with lives and deaths" (p. 24).

**Haraway, *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003).** Earlier articulation of the same project: dogs and people are not pre-given subjects encountering each other; rather "we are training each other in acts of communication we barely understand" (the manifesto's framing of becoming-with). Haraway insists on "significant otherness" — humans and dogs do not collapse into one another but co-shape one another through history, labour, evolution, breed politics, and everyday domestic practice. Domestic dogs are products of millennia of co-evolution with humans; they are not metaphors or symbols for humans. This grounds the multispecies sociology that Carter and Charles call for.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The lecture and the Carter and Charles reading share a single thesis: sociology has been so anxious to defend its disciplinary turf against biology that it amputated half of its empirical world. I think they are right, but the diagnosis matters more than the slogan. Carter and Charles do not just say "let's add animals in" — that would be stage three on the feminism analogy. They argue, in the article's most direct line, that **"the social *is* animal"** (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 93). The work is reconfiguring concepts, not extending coverage.

Their target is sharp: "the human is usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Carter and Charles, p. 81, citing Ingold). Sociology inherits this binary from Cartesian humanism, hardens it after Parsons, and then runs into trouble when confronted with horses ("biotechnology… living machines", Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 4 [VERIFY p.?]), pheasants in shoots, or wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). The Greene quote from the slides drives the point home: "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008, p. 170). Sociology stares at the Communist Manifesto and misses the horses dropping "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" in 1900 New York (Greene, 2008, p. 174).

The Weber excerpt the lecturer cites complicates the simple "classical sociology ignored animals" story. Weber concedes that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, pp. 15–16). But he immediately retreats: a "verifiable conception" of animals' state of mind is attainable "only within narrow limits" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, p. 17). Weber half-opens the door and then closes it on epistemological grounds — and twentieth-century sociology, post-Parsons, slammed it shut. Durkheim's *Rules* leaves a similar window: "animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common" (Durkheim, *Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 132). Sociability, for Durkheim, is not innately human.

Haraway pushes further. She refuses the "either/or" of human exceptionalism versus posthumanism. Her programmatic line is: "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 12). Companion species are not "ordinary beings-in-encounter" who already exist before they meet; "species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). This is what Carter and Charles, in their own register, mean when they say agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, p. 90).

The critical question is whether this expanded sociology can still do the political work sociology was meant to do. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: if sociology cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, it is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). Haraway makes the same wager: "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 29). I think this is right. A sociology that cannot name factory farming, zoonotic pandemics, biodiversity collapse, or the politics of beef and palm oil is a sociology that has unilaterally disarmed in the face of its century.

## 8. Essay answers

### 2024 (Q2): Why is it important to study animals within sociology?

Sociology emerged as the study of industrial modernity, and yet by my count the most important moving parts of that modernity — horses, livestock, lab animals, working dogs, insects pollinating the food system — were systematically excluded from the sociological imagination. I want to argue, with Carter and Charles, that studying animals within sociology is not a niche enrichment of the discipline but a remedy for a structural blind spot that has, until recently, made sociology unable to describe the very societies it claims to analyse.

The first reason is empirical. There is, as Bryant pointed out in 1979 and as Carter and Charles repeat, "virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 79). Greene's history of nineteenth-century cities makes this concrete. "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, 2008, p. 170). Industrial capitalism, the very object of classical sociology, ran on horsepower in the literal sense, on horses described by Greene as "living machines" (Greene, 2008, p. 4). The Communist Manifesto contains, as the lecturer pointedly notes, no horses. New York in 1900 produced "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" (Greene, 2008, p. 174). A discipline that misses an infrastructure that large is not a discipline that has settled on its proper object; it is a discipline that has misperceived its object.

The second reason is conceptual. Sociology defines social action, agency, subjectivity, reflexivity, and even society itself through a tacit "anthroponormativity" — Carter and Charles's term, modelled on heteronormativity and white normativity, for "the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences" being "configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). This is not a politically neutral choice. The Weberian inheritance is the key example: meaning-making, empathetic understanding, value-oriented action are all framed as exclusively human, even though Weber himself conceded that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility" non-instinctively and meaningfully (Weber, *Economy and Society*, pp. 15–16). When Parsons consolidates sociology in the mid-twentieth century, he hardens this into the doctrine that sociality is properly the realm of human interaction alone. To ascribe sociality to animals would, in this view, "threaten the grounding and specificity of the social" (lecture). I think this is exactly backwards: what threatens the specificity of the social is pretending it stops at the species boundary.

The third reason is political. Carter and Charles draw an explicit analogy with feminism. The trajectory is: pre-feminist neglect, critique, adding-women-in, then "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, p. 80, citing Walby, Oakley, Charles). Animal studies, they argue, is "experiencing the second and third stages" but has not yet reached the fourth. Studying animals within sociology is the work of moving the discipline from stage three to stage four — from animals as an exotic specialism to animals as constitutive of every sociological object. Just as feminism did not simply "add women" but reworked the categories of work, family, agency and the body, multispecies sociology reworks the categories of labour (lab animals in drug testing; horses pulling cabs; bees pollinating crops), the family (pets as kin in Haraway's sense of companion species), the body (zoonotic disease and COVID), and the political (animal-rights claims, vegan politics, the cosmopolitics Haraway elaborates).

The fourth reason is ecological and urgent. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: a sociology that cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, they warn, is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). I think this is right. Industrial agriculture clears the Amazon for beef; palm-oil plantations destroy orangutan habitat; intensive farming generates antibiotic resistance and zoonotic spillover. None of these are biological problems and only biological problems; they are social problems in which animals are agents, objects, and victims simultaneously. A sociology that defines its remit as exclusively human is not "rigorous"; it is wilfully partial.

Studying animals is also a methodological challenge. Donna Haraway insists that "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 12). Alexandra Kimbo, the lecturer's former PhD student, reframes this as a question: how to think *with* (and for) other animals, as opposed to *about* them? The shift is from animals as data points or symbols to animals as research partners whose ways of mattering have to be taken seriously. The PigeonBlog project — where racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over Los Angeles — shows what such collaboration looks like in practice: the pigeons had to "learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18) and the humans had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, p. 22).

To say "the social is animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93) is not anthropomorphism, and it is not flat ontology. It is a recognition that the entities sociology studies — labour, family, city, modernity, capitalism, politics — were always already multispecies. Studying animals within sociology therefore does not stretch the discipline; it returns it to its proper object.

### 2025 (Q2): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically assess this statement using the ideas set out by Carter and Charles in the key reading for this topic.

I will critically assess the claim that sociology is the study of human societies using the argument Carter and Charles develop in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018). The statement is true as a description of how the discipline has constituted itself, but Carter and Charles show it is false as a description of what the discipline should be — and that the gap matters politically, conceptually and ecologically.

Carter and Charles concede the historical accuracy of the statement. Sociology, in its institutionalisation between roughly 1890 and the 1930s, did define its object as human society and only human society. They give three reasons. First, sociology had a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, p. 80). To establish itself against psychology and biology, sociology insisted on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12, cited p. 82) and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. Second, sociology was built on "human exceptionalism" — the doctrine "that the human species is exempt from natural constraints… and is evolutionarily unique" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). Third, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe at precisely the moment when "urban dwellers experience themselves as increasingly remote from most other animals" (Carter and Charles, p. 84). The disappearance of horses and food-animals from city streets in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries coincided with sociology's professional formation. The discipline took the absence of visible animals for the absence of relevant animals.

The first stage of their critique is that this is empirically false. Animals were "central… to economy and society" (Carter and Charles, p. 84, citing Tovey 2003). Greene's case study of nineteenth-century American cities, which the lecturer foregrounds, shows that horses were "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene 2008: 4, cited p. 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile in the first part of the twentieth century" (Carter and Charles, p. 86). To leave horses out of an account of industrialisation is not a defensible analytic choice; it is a misdescription.

The second stage of their critique is conceptual, and it is where the article does its hardest work. Carter and Charles argue that "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (p. 88). They name this configuration **anthroponormativity**, by direct analogy with heteronormativity and white normativity. Society, agency, social action, intersubjectivity and reflexivity are all silently restricted to humans. Agency, on the Cartesian inheritance, is "associated with deeply rooted assumptions about self-conscious, individual self-transformation and the 'emancipated subject'" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). When sociology uses this vocabulary, it excludes animals not by argument but by definition.

Carter and Charles then offer their alternative. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (p. 90). It is "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). On this realist-relational view, non-human animals are agential beings — entangled, "enfolded in all sorts of relations with humans (many of which are fatal for them)" and finding "that their agential conditions are modified involuntarily" (p. 92). Their pheasant-shoot example is precise: a shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (p. 92). To call this human interaction alone, and to call the pheasants and dogs mere background, is to misperceive the practice.

The third stage is the political stake. Carter and Charles draw the analogy with feminism: sociology went through a pre-feminist phase, a critique, an "add women in" phase, and finally "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (p. 80). Animal studies, on their reading, is between phases two and three. The claim that "sociology is the study of human societies" is precisely the kind of definitional move that feminism, post-colonial sociology and queer theory have all dismantled in turn. What is at stake is whether sociology can describe its actual object — industrial capitalism, the post-colonial world, the Anthropocene — without continuing to lop off half of it.

Their concluding line is uncompromising: "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93). This is not animal advocacy; it is a methodological claim. "In considering animals as social actors and agents," they write, "we have provided an example of how this might be done" (p. 93). Their rejection of Latour and the new materialists is also worth noting: they do not think sociology has to be abandoned in favour of actor-network theory or post-humanism. The discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (p. 87) once its foundational concepts — society, agency, social action — are reworked.

So the statement "sociology is the study of human societies" is, on Carter and Charles's account, an inherited disciplinary self-description, not a discovered truth about what sociology can or should be. It is a description of where sociology has been, not where it has to go. To take their argument seriously is to accept that the boundary between humans and animals is not a methodological given but a political and conceptual choice — one that current ecological, ethical and epistemological pressures give us very good reasons to revise. I therefore reject the statement as a definition while accepting it as a description of past practice.

### 2023 (Q2): Why should sociology make efforts to study the relations between humans and animals?

I want to argue that sociology should make these efforts for four interlocking reasons: empirical fidelity, conceptual coherence, ethical and political relevance, and ecological necessity. The argument runs through Carter and Charles's *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018) and Donna Haraway's notion of companion species, with concrete examples ranging from horsepower in the Industrial Revolution to PigeonBlog in twenty-first-century Los Angeles.

**Empirical fidelity.** The first reason is that human–animal relations were always there, and a sociology that misses them misperceives its own object. Greene's history of nineteenth-century US cities, which features prominently in the lecture, makes this concrete: "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170). Modernity, industrialisation, capitalism — sociology's founding objects — ran on animals. Horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, p. 4). They became invisible to sociologists precisely because they were too central to be seen, the way we no longer notice internet cables or sewage infrastructure. As the lecturer put it, making the familiar strange is one of sociology's main tasks, and horses are a prime candidate.

The empirical case extends far beyond the nineteenth century. Lab animals are essential to the pharmaceutical industry. Factory farming reshapes the labour market, the land and the global food system; capitalist agriculture is unthinkable without the industrial breeding, feeding and slaughter of animals. Pets are deeply implicated in emotional labour, family structure and the welfare state — companion-animal therapy is now a recognised practice in mental health care. Honeybees and other insects pollinate the crops that feed cities. Zoonotic spillover, dramatised by COVID-19, has reshaped the world economy. Biodiversity collapse is unfolding as we speak. To leave all of this out of sociology because it is "biological" is to abandon the discipline's promise of grasping social totality.

**Conceptual coherence.** The second reason is that sociology's core concepts wobble badly without animals. Carter and Charles call this **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). Agency, in the Cartesian inheritance, belongs to rational subjects; objects (including animals) are passive matter available for human use. But this view is incoherent once we look closely. Carter and Charles's example of wolves in Europe shows that "the agency of wolves… changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). Their agential conditions are "powerfully shaped by their relations to humans (but not only these)" (p. 91). The wolf is not metaphorically an agent; the wolf's possibilities and constraints are real, relational, and politically structured. Sociology that does not recognise this cannot describe nature reserves, hunting laws, rewilding politics or animal-rights litigation.

**Ethical and political relevance.** The third reason is that human–animal relations are sites of domination, exploitation and care that sociology, on its own terms, should care about. Carter and Charles are explicit: their "key claim is that animals are agents entangled in relations with humans and that these relations are frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, p. 80). The sociology of factory farming sits naturally alongside the sociology of mass incarceration: both are bureaucratic systems of confinement that reduce living beings to inputs. The sociology of pets sits alongside the sociology of care and emotional labour. The sociology of circuses and zoos sits alongside the sociology of spectacle and entertainment. Veganism, in Greenebaum's three-type analysis (health, environmental, ethical), is a social movement with class, gender and racial dimensions.

**Ecological necessity.** The fourth reason is that the Anthropocene leaves us no choice. Carter and Charles end their article by warning that a sociology that cannot speak about a multispecies planet is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). Haraway makes the same wager in her language of "staying with the trouble": "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 29). Differential responsibility — by ecology, economy and species — is precisely a sociological problem.

Haraway's notion of companion species supplies a vocabulary for doing this work without falling into either anthropomorphism or flat ontology. Humans "become humans through their relations with animals" (lecture slide on companion species). "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, p. 12). The PigeonBlog project, where racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over polluted Los Angeles neighbourhoods, illustrates this in practice: the pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing"; the artist-researchers "had to render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, p. 22). Citizen-science programmes like Project PigeonWatch in Washington DC teach city children to re-see despised "rats with wings" as "sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, p. 24).

Sociology should make these efforts, then, because animals are everywhere in the social, because sociology's own concepts already require them, because human–animal relations are political relations, and because the planetary crisis demands a discipline capable of multispecies thinking. The alternative — defending the human/animal binary as if it were a discovered fact about the world — is not rigour. It is a refusal to look.

### 2021 (Q18): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically analyse the limitations of this statement.

The statement captures how sociology has historically defined itself but obscures three distinct limitations, which I will analyse in turn: a historical-empirical limitation, a conceptual limitation, and a normative limitation. I argue that none of these are minor blind spots; together they amount to a structural failure that the discipline now has to repair.

**The historical-empirical limitation.** The first limitation is that the statement misdescribes the societies sociology has actually studied. Carter and Charles trace the formation of the discipline to "modern industrial capitalist societies" of Western Europe and the United States (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 83). But those societies were emphatically multispecies. Greene's history shows that "horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170). Nineteenth-century New York had 131,000 horses producing up to 3,300 tons of manure daily (Greene, p. 174). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). The "absence of large animals" in twentieth-century cities is not a natural condition; it is the result of historical processes (food-animal removal in the second half of the nineteenth century, horse displacement by the automobile in the early twentieth) which sociology mistook for the disappearance of animals from social life altogether. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Durkheim's industrial division of labour contains no slaughterhouses. Weber's Protestant ethic contains no oxen. Carter and Charles point out that "the contribution of animals and even their centrality to processes of industrialisation have been erased in sociological conceptions of the social" (Carter and Charles, p. 84). The statement that sociology is the study of human societies is, in this sense, a self-fulfilling description of an empirical mistake.

**The conceptual limitation.** The second limitation is that the statement entrenches what Carter and Charles call **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). This is the same kind of move, they argue, that earlier critiques exposed under the names heteronormativity and white normativity — a tacit norm that pretends to be a neutral definition. Agency, social action, intersubjectivity, reflexivity and even society are all silently restricted to humans. Carter and Charles trace this back partly to a particular reading of Weber, partly to Parsons. The lecturer is more direct: "I blame Talcott Parsons!" — under whose influence twentieth-century sociologists insisted that "whatever sociality is, it is a production of humans, groups of humans or systems of humans" (lecture). Yet Weber himself wrote that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility and react to them in ways which are evidently often by no means purely instinctive and mechanical and in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, pp. 15–16). And Durkheim wrote that "it has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, *Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 132). The classical thinkers were less anthropocentric than their twentieth-century descendants. The "study of human societies" formula is a hardening, not a faithful inheritance.

Carter and Charles's positive proposal is a relational account of agency: "agency is not an attribute, or property, of humans or other animals or things, but an emergent product of the relations within which all things are placed" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). Their example of European wolves shows that agency is spatial and political: wolves are protected by EU law in one jurisdiction and shot under licence in another. Their pheasant-shoot example shows that ordinary practices are "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92). The conceptual limitation of the "human societies" definition is that it gives us no vocabulary for these realities except dismissal.

**The normative limitation.** The third limitation is ethical and ecological. The "human societies" definition lets sociology evade the politics of human–animal relations. Carter and Charles are explicit that these relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (p. 80). Factory farming, drug testing on lab animals, zoos, circuses, fur farming, the dog-meat trade, palm-oil-driven habitat destruction, COVID-style zoonotic spillover, antibiotic resistance born of industrial agriculture, biodiversity collapse — none of these can be properly described by a discipline that has defined them out of scope. Haraway's response is to refuse both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 11). Companion species, becoming-with, response-ability — her vocabulary is built precisely to do work that the "human societies" formula prevents.

There is one defence of the statement worth considering. Some sociologists — Carter and Charles cite Mead, and the lecturer adds Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard — argue that language, symbolism and reflexive meaning-making really do mark a categorical difference, and that the proper object of sociology really is the symbolic order produced by human meaning-makers. The reply, which I find persuasive, has two parts. Empirically, this categorical difference does not survive scrutiny: chimpanzees, bonobos, whales and other species are now widely accepted to have cultures that "are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, p. 88, citing de Waal). Pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, p. 18). Methodologically, the argument is circular — we define the social as symbolic, define the symbolic as human, and conclude that the social is human. Anthroponormativity does the work that empirical investigation should be doing.

The three limitations are connected. The historical-empirical limitation produces an inaccurate picture of past societies. The conceptual limitation produces analytic tools that cannot grasp present societies. The normative limitation produces a discipline ill-equipped for the multispecies crises of the future. The statement "sociology is the study of human societies" is not merely incomplete; it actively blocks sociology from doing what sociology is supposed to do. I therefore conclude, with Carter and Charles, that "the social *is* animal" (p. 93) — and that the definition we should defend is not "sociology is the study of human societies" but something closer to "sociology is the study of multispecies relations through which human-and-animal worlds are made."

### M1: To what extent is human exceptionalism the central obstacle to a sociology of human–animal relations?

I argue that human exceptionalism is the central obstacle, but only when we understand it not as a stand-alone error but as the keystone of a wider architecture — biology-avoidance, urban-industrial provincialism, anthroponormative concepts — that Carter and Charles diagnose in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*.

Human exceptionalism is "the idea that the human species is exempt from natural constraints (primarily because of its scientific and technological achievements) and is evolutionarily unique (because of its capacity for language and the capacities for meaning making and cultural development that are emergent from this)" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). The lecturer traces it back further, to the Judaeo-Christian Great Chain of Being (God–angels–humans–animals–elements–matter). It is the philosophical posture that animals are different in kind, not degree, from us. As the lecturer points out, this posture "may seem reasonable at first sight. But it presumes that humans are 'above', 'superior' to animals."

Human exceptionalism is the keystone because it does the silent work behind every other obstacle. Sociology's "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, p. 80) makes sense only if we already think humans are exceptional — otherwise treating biology as irrelevant to social life would be incoherent. Sociology's urban-industrial provincialism — its tendency to read industrial society's removal of food-animals and draught animals from city streets as their disappearance from social life — makes sense only if we already think that animals are "nature" rather than constituents of society. Anthroponormative concepts — agency, social action, reflexivity — make sense as exclusively human only if we already presume human exceptionalism.

This is why turning the question around, as the lecturer suggests, is so productive: "Why don't we want animals to have agency, subjectivity, the ability to use symbols? Why do we want to keep culture to ourselves? Why do we want to keep rights to ourselves?" The defensive posture, not the empirical evidence, is doing the work.

But I want to qualify the claim. Human exceptionalism is the central conceptual obstacle, but it is not the only obstacle, and the discipline's history shows that exceptionalism alone does not explain everything. The classical theorists were not uniformly exceptionalist. Durkheim wrote that "animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common" (*Rules*, p. 132). Weber conceded that animals "understand… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful" (*Economy and Society*, p. 15). It was twentieth-century consolidation — Carter and Charles emphasise Parsons — that hardened a softer classical position into doctrine. Industrialisation and urbanisation independently removed animals from sight in a way that made exceptionalism easier to maintain (Carter and Charles, p. 84). The association of biology with eugenics and scientific racism gave sociology an additional reason — a good political reason — to keep biology and animals at arm's length (p. 83).

Haraway adds a further twist. She refuses both human exceptionalism *and* a glib flattening of the human/animal distinction. "Companion species play string figure games where who is/are to be in/of the world is constituted in intra- and interaction" (Haraway, p. 13). The partners do not pre-exist the knotting. To dismantle human exceptionalism on Haraway's terms is not to declare humans-and-animals all-the-same but to attend carefully to specific entanglements: PigeonBlog's racing pigeons and their working-class fanciers, Kimbo and her dog Harald, lab rats and pharmaceutical researchers, sheep and pastoralists, wolves and EU jurisdictions.

So human exceptionalism is the central obstacle in the sense that removing it unlocks the other obstacles. But the unlocking is not a deletion. The biology problem still requires care (Carter and Charles do not want a return to eugenics). The urban-industrial blindness still requires effortful re-imagination (the lecturer's "making the familiar strange"). The anthroponormative concepts still need reworking, not just denouncing. The Anthropocene still needs sociological description, not just slogans.

The most useful summary is Carter and Charles's: "ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (Carter and Charles, p. 93). Once. That is the right word. Removing exceptionalism is the central move; it is also only the first move. To the extent of the question, then: yes, human exceptionalism is the central obstacle. But "central" should be read in the sense of "load-bearing" rather than "exclusive." Removing it brings the rest of the structure into view, where the actual sociological work — describing factory farms, pets, lab animals, horses, pigeons, wolves, bees — has to be done.

### M2: To what extent does industrial modernity, rather than classical sociological theory, explain the invisibility of animals in sociology?

I argue that industrial modernity and classical sociological theory are not rival explanations but two faces of a single explanation. Industrial modernity removed animals from the everyday sightlines of urban Europeans and North Americans; classical sociological theory consolidated that removal into a methodological principle. To rank them as causes is to miss that they were mutually reinforcing.

The case for industrial modernity is strong. Carter and Charles emphasise that "sociology has its origins in the investigation of modern industrial capitalist societies" (Carter and Charles, p. 83) and that "in this type of society… urban dwellers experience themselves as increasingly remote from most other animals" (p. 84). The historical specifics matter: "animals farmed for food were removed from cities during the second half of the nineteenth century and horses disappeared in the early part of the twentieth" (p. 84). Before this removal, London streets had been "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden, in addition, passers-by often encountered herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). After it, urban experience seemed animal-free in a way that made theoretical neglect feel natural.

The case for classical theory is also strong. The lecturer "blames Talcott Parsons" — under whose influence "sociologists… assume and insist that whatever sociality is, it is a production of humans, groups of humans or systems of humans" (lecture). Carter and Charles support this: Parsons is "the first major sociologist to hold a conception of the social that defines it unambiguously as the realm of *human* interaction; after Parsons, the question of what constitutes the social rarely surfaces" (Carter and Charles, p. 85). The Cartesian inheritance about agency — "the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive" (p. 89) — gave the human-only definition its philosophical scaffolding. Weber's emphasis on meaning, subjectively understood, gave it its methodological scaffolding. The struggle with behaviourism in mid-century gave it polemical urgency: sociology emphasised "the rational, reflexive nature of social action" partly because behaviourism threatened to dissolve the social into stimulus-response (p. 85).

But here is the point: these two stories interlock. Sociologists could maintain a human-only definition of the social because industrial modernity had made animals invisible to them. And industrial modernity could be theorised as a human-only phenomenon because sociology had already defined the social as human-only. The horses moving 1,300–3,300 tons of manure across New York every day (Greene, p. 174) were invisible *both* because the city's spatial organisation made them harder to see (they were stabled at night, used during work hours, removed when dead) *and* because the discipline's conceptual scheme made them theoretically uninteresting. Sociologists looked through them.

This is the move Carter and Charles make again and again: the structural and the conceptual are mutually constitutive. "By the early years of the twentieth century the belief that the object of sociology was the study of human society and that this object was primarily shaped by culture, with meanings and values at its core, was widely established" (p. 85). This was an inheritance, not a discovery. It locked in what industrialisation had already begun.

The Greene case is the lecturer's preferred illustration: horses were "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene, p. 4); they were "essential 'prime mover[s]' of industrial development" (Carter and Charles, p. 86). To miss horses in an account of nineteenth-century industrialisation is not a defensible analytic choice. But missing them was easy, in the sense that one inheritance reinforced another: the urban context made horses less salient, and the classical-theoretical inheritance made them theoretically inadmissible.

What about counter-examples? Rural sociology has not done much better than urban sociology on animals, despite the fact that "the centrality of animals to economy and society, and the continuing sense among rural residents that they are organically embedded in a larger than human world, are among the main elements which continue to distinguish rural from urban life" (Tovey 2003: 197, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). This suggests that classical theory's role is not just downstream of urban experience — even sociologists studying contexts full of visible animals tended to filter them out. So classical theory's effect is partly independent of urban experience.

Conversely, the lecturer's analogy with feminism cuts the other way: pre-feminist sociology ignored women not because women were physically invisible but because the discipline's categories were configured to make women's lives theoretically uninteresting (the wage-labour bias, the public/private division, the assumption that "the social" was the public sphere). So there is precedent for classical theory's role being predominant. Anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles, p. 88), like the classical theoretical neglect of gender, can operate independently of empirical visibility.

I therefore conclude that industrial modernity is a major part of the explanation — perhaps the precipitating cause — but classical sociological theory is what made the neglect permanent. Without industrial modernity, the theory might never have congealed. But without the theory, post-industrial sociology — which now has plenty of analytical access to pet-keeping, lab animals, factory farming, zoonotic disease and rewilding — would have repaired the gap by now. It hasn't yet, and that is on the theory.

### M3: Discuss with examples how animals can be considered agents in sociological terms.

I argue that animals can be considered agents in sociological terms once we abandon the Cartesian view of agency as a property of rational subjects and adopt the relational view Carter and Charles defend in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*. On this view, agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, p. 90). Three examples — horses in industrialisation, wolves in the EU, and racing pigeons in PigeonBlog — show what this looks like in practice.

The Cartesian view, which Carter and Charles take as their target, holds that "the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive, a distinction that favours a view of 'nature' and non-human animals as available for human exploitation" (Carter and Charles, p. 89). On this view, agency requires a rational subject capable of weighing means to ends. Only humans qualify; animals are matter that responds. The relational view, drawing on A. N. Whitehead and Margaret Archer, instead understands agency as "the constant accompaniment of material existence, the dynamic and relational outcome of the engagement with the world of all things, human and non-human" (Carter and Charles, p. 89). Importantly, action and agency are distinguished: action is what beings do, agency is the structure of possibilities and constraints within which they do it. Carter and Charles also draw on Burkitt and on Ahearn's definition of agency as "the socioculturally mediated capacity to act" (Ahearn 2001, cited Carter and Charles, p. 89), but extend "socioculturally mediated" to include cross-species mediation.

**First example: horses in industrialisation.** Greene's history shows that horses were not just used; they shaped what industrial cities could become. "Horses were bred for sport, war, work, transport; they stimulated the development of an array of technologies and devices: vehicles, equestrian equipment, breeders' logs; and horse power reinforced other sorts of power — military, political, religious, sexual" (Greene 2008: 5, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). The horse is an agent in this sense not because individual horses chose to pull cabs but because horses' capacities, breeding histories, biological rhythms and limits structured what industrialisation could be. The fact that horses needed to rest on Sundays shaped urban temporality. The fact that horses produced manure shaped urban sanitation. The fact that horses could not work indefinitely shaped the energy economy and ultimately the demand for steam and internal combustion. Horses' "purpose" — to live, eat, move, breed — engaged the contextual conditions of industrial demand to produce a specifically horse-shaped modernity. Take horses out of the explanation and you have to invent something to replace them; the explanation cannot be built from human action alone.

**Second example: wolves in the EU.** Carter and Charles use this example to show that "agency thus has a spatial dimension (which agential conditions are relevant will depend on where you are and the ways in which the space inhabited is traversed by human categories and ambitions)" (p. 91). Wolves in Sweden are EU-protected; wolves in the Sami territories of northern Sweden can be killed because they threaten reindeer herds; wolves in Norway, outside EU jurisdiction, can be hunted under licence. The wolves themselves do not know which jurisdiction they are crossing — but the same wolf is a different agent in each jurisdiction, with a different array of possibilities and constraints. Carter and Charles add: "individual wolves are not reducible to their relational co-ordinates as, say, a member of a protected species, a symbol of the wilderness, or a legitimate target for hunting" (p. 91). The wolf is an actor whose actions — territorial marking, hunting, mating — engage agential conditions structured by EU law, Sami pastoralism, Norwegian sporting culture, conservation biology and biodiversity politics. This is agency in the relational sense, and it is sociologically describable.

**Third example: racing pigeons in PigeonBlog.** Haraway's case-study of Beatriz da Costa's 2006 art-activism project shows agency as co-production. The pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over Los Angeles. They "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18). The artist-researchers had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, p. 22). This is "becoming-with" in Haraway's vocabulary: "becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, p. 12). The pigeons were not instruments; they were agents whose capacities — homing, navigation, urban familiarity — were essential to the project and would not have been activated without specific human cooperation. Equally, the people involved — working-class pigeon fanciers, art-activists, the engineer Bob Matsuyama — were rendered capable of new forms of citizen science only through the pigeons' cooperation. Agency is distributed across the entanglement.

These examples generalise. Pets exert agency in households by shaping routines, emotional rhythms and even housing decisions. Lab animals exert agency in pharmaceutical research by responding (or not) to compounds, by surviving (or not) procedures, by behaving (or not) as model organisms. Factory-farmed pigs and chickens exert a constrained agency — Carter and Charles note that "sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages; wolves and pheasants are unaware of the shooting season… and that their choices, when they are exercised, are already circumscribed" (Carter and Charles, p. 92). The constraint is the point: agency on the relational view is precisely about what possibilities are available given relational conditions.

There are objections. The strongest is that "agency" loses its meaning if it is extended to everything that responds to its environment. Carter and Charles's reply, which I find persuasive, is that they are not extending agency to "everything"; they are extending it to beings whose purposes engage agential conditions in socially-structured ways. Pheasants in a UK shoot are agents because their purposes (live, fly, breed) engage agential conditions (October–February shooting season, dog-handlers, gun licences, class-coded "sporting life") to produce a specifically multispecies practice (Carter and Charles, p. 92). A rock does not.

So animals can be considered agents in sociological terms — not as little humans, not as Cartesian rational subjects, but as beings whose purposes engage relational conditions in ways that constitute the social. This is what Carter and Charles mean by "the social is animal" (p. 93).

### M4: Compare Carter and Charles's "anthroponormativity" with Donna Haraway's "becoming-with" as critiques of mainstream sociology.

Carter and Charles's "anthroponormativity" and Donna Haraway's "becoming-with" are two distinct critical strategies aimed at the same target: a sociology that treats the human as the silent norm of the social. I argue that they are complementary — anthroponormativity is a diagnostic concept, becoming-with is a constructive method — and that the most useful sociology draws on both.

**Anthroponormativity as diagnosis.** Carter and Charles coin the term by analogy with heteronormativity and white normativity. "Much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). Heteronormativity does not just exclude queer people; it configures concepts of family, intimacy, household and respectability around heterosexual couples. White normativity does not just exclude people of colour; it configures concepts of citizenship, professionalism and reasonableness around whiteness. Anthroponormativity, analogously, configures society, agency, social action, intersubjectivity and reflexivity around humans. The exclusion of animals is not done by argument; it is done by definition. The point of the concept is to make visible what was previously invisible — the silent species-norm at the centre of sociology's vocabulary.

This is a powerful diagnostic move because it allows Carter and Charles to refuse the obvious counter-argument: "but sociology *is* the study of humans, so of course it excludes animals." That move treats a contingent disciplinary self-definition as a discovered necessity. Anthroponormativity calls the bluff: there is no neutral place to stand from which "the social = the human" is just true. It is a norm, and norms are contestable.

Carter and Charles use this diagnosis to reframe specific concepts. Agency is not a Cartesian property of rational subjects but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90). Society is not "the realm of *human* interaction" but a structure of social relationships in which "non-human animals" can be "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68, cited p. 86). The diagnosis enables conceptual repair.

**Becoming-with as constructive method.** Haraway's contribution is different. Where Carter and Charles diagnose the conceptual failure, Haraway builds a vocabulary for working past it. "Companion species are relentlessly becoming-with. The category companion species helps me refuse human exceptionalism without invoking posthumanism" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (p. 12). The metaphor is the cat's-cradle string figure: "the partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (p. 13).

Becoming-with does work that anthroponormativity cannot do on its own. It tells the researcher how to study multispecies practices. Pigeons and their fanciers in Haraway's PigeonBlog case do not pre-exist their cooperation; they become competent agents — pigeons become reliable racers, humans become talented fanciers — through specific, embodied, relational practices. The Despret quote Haraway uses says it precisely: "without the lover of pigeons… without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25). Becoming-with is the actual texture of multispecies sociality.

**Complementarity.** I want to insist on the complementarity. If we only had anthroponormativity, we would know what is wrong but not how to study what should replace it. If we only had becoming-with, we would have a beautiful method but no leverage on a discipline whose categories systematically deflect that method. Together, they are powerful.

Consider the example of pets. Anthroponormativity tells us why sociology has tended to reduce pets to symbols of human meaning-making (the slide on "Meanings, Symbols and Culture" makes this point explicitly: "as a result, animals become important as symbols, as bearers of meaning. For example: Pets"). Becoming-with tells us how to study pets non-reductively: as beings whose capacities for affection, distress, attention and recognition co-shape the humans they live with. Haraway's *Companion Species Manifesto* is precisely such a study, where humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand."

Consider factory farming. Anthroponormativity tells us why sociological accounts of agriculture have tended to focus on farmers, agribusiness and consumer culture while treating animals as inputs. Becoming-with tells us what is missing: the specific, embodied practices through which pigs and chickens are (de-)rendered capable, the labour of slaughterhouse workers, the affective and cognitive lives of animals in industrial confinement. Carter and Charles's "sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered" (p. 92) is a reminder that becoming-with can also be becoming-killed; multispecies relations are not all benign.

**Tensions.** There are real differences. Carter and Charles want to reform sociology from within; Haraway is more willing to displace it ("not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'", Haraway, p. 11). Carter and Charles preserve a realist ontology of structures and emergent properties; Haraway is more rhetorically playful, more comfortable with metaphor as method. Carter and Charles see Latour and the new materialists as overcorrecting in the direction of posthumanism; Haraway is closer to that tradition. These are not trivial differences. A sociologist deciding which to follow has to choose between disciplinary continuity and methodological adventure.

But I think the tensions are productive. The right multispecies sociology, in my view, uses anthroponormativity to clear the conceptual ground, and becoming-with to do the actual descriptive work. PigeonBlog needs both: the diagnostic that says "the standard sociology of citizen science would have erased the pigeons", and the method that says "here is how the pigeons were actually agents in the project." Pets need both: the diagnostic that says "treating pets as symbols anthroponormatively erases their lives", and the method that says "here is how a dog and a person become a household together." Factory farming needs both: the diagnostic that says "treating cows as inputs erases their agential entanglements", and the method that says "here are the specific ways in which dairy cows are bred, fed, milked, killed."

In short, Carter and Charles tell us what is missing; Haraway tells us how to write what should replace it. A multispecies sociology that uses only one of them will be either critically sharp but methodologically thin, or methodologically rich but disciplinarily marginalised. Used together, they constitute the most useful current toolkit for studying human–animal relations.

### M5: Compare Weber's and Durkheim's treatment of animals and assess what this tells us about sociology's "biological blindness."

I argue that Durkheim and Weber are both ambivalent about animals — neither is straightforwardly exceptionalist — and that the hardening of sociology's "biological blindness" (Benton 1991, cited Carter and Charles, p. 80) is a twentieth-century achievement, not a classical inheritance. The comparison matters because it shows that the human-only definition of the social is not anchored in the discipline's founders.

**Durkheim.** The lecturer cites three key passages from the *Rules of Sociological Method*. First: "It has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, *Rules*, p. 132). Second: "It is much more natural to see in it [sociability] a product of social life which has slowly become organised in us" (p. 132). Third, and most striking: "It is an observable fact that animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common or cause them to shun such a life" (p. 132). Durkheim is willing to say (i) human sociability is not biologically given but socially produced, (ii) animals are observably sociable, and (iii) sociability tracks environmental conditions for animals just as social facts track collective conditions for humans. This is not an exceptionalist position. It is closer to a comparative sociology of sociability in which humans are one case among others.

Why did Durkheim not develop this line? Carter and Charles point to two pressures. The first is methodological: Durkheim's struggle to establish sociology as a discipline distinct from psychology required him to insist on "the irreducible and *sui generis* realm of social relations" (Carter and Charles, p. 82). Animals threatened to drag the social back toward biology. The second is political: the late nineteenth century was the heyday of Spencer's Social Darwinism, eugenics and scientific racism, and Durkheim was at pains to refuse biological explanations of social inequality (p. 83). His own openness to animal sociability got squeezed by the disciplinary politics of his moment.

**Weber.** Weber's passages in *Economy and Society* (cited in the lecture from pp. 15–16) are even more striking. "Many animals 'understand' [*verstehen*] commands, anger, love, hostility and react to them in ways which are evidently often by no means purely instinctive and mechanical and in some sense both consciously meaningful [*sinnhaft*] and affected by experience." This is Weber using his core technical vocabulary — *Verstehen*, *sinnhaft* — for animals. He explicitly says animal behaviour can be conscious, meaningful and experience-shaped. This is a remarkable concession in the founding text of interpretive sociology.

But Weber immediately retreats. "A verifiable conception of the state of mind of these social animals [*sozialen Tierindividuen*] accessible to meaningful understanding [*sinnhaften 'Verstehens'*] of the world, would seem to be attainable even as an ideal goal only within narrow limits" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, p. 17). The retreat is epistemological, not ontological. Weber is not saying animals don't have meaningful mental states; he is saying we cannot reliably know them. The methodological caution becomes, over time, a substantive doctrine — by the mid-twentieth century, interpretive sociologists treat the human-only scope of *Verstehen* as a discovered truth about animals rather than a methodological caution about our access to them.

**What this tells us about biological blindness.** Carter and Charles are explicit that the discipline's "biological blindness" is not a founding feature but a defensive consolidation: "The source of the 'biological blindness' of the social sciences, for Benton, lay in their attempts in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to elaborate and defend 'the autonomy and specificity of human social life' (Benton, 1991: 12) and to establish sociology as a discipline with an object of study that would differentiate it from its rivals" (Carter and Charles, p. 82). The classical thinkers had complex, ambivalent positions. The consolidation came afterwards.

Three consequences follow. First, classical theory is a resource for multispecies sociology, not an obstacle. Durkheim's account of animal sociability is precisely the comparative move multispecies sociology now wants to make. Weber's *Verstehen*, taken at face value, is precisely the empathetic understanding Despret and Kimbo are developing. Second, the real culprit is mid-twentieth-century consolidation. Carter and Charles, following Halewood, identify Parsons as the key figure: "Parsons is the first major sociologist to hold a conception of the social that defines it unambiguously as the realm of *human* interaction; after Parsons, the question of what constitutes the social rarely surfaces" (Carter and Charles, p. 85). The lecturer's "I blame Talcott Parsons" is hyperbole, but the diagnosis is sound. Third, the contemporary task is to recover the classical ambivalence and develop it forward — not to invent multispecies sociology from scratch as if the founders had nothing to offer.

The biological-blindness story Benton tells is, at its core, a story about how a defensive disciplinary politics — biology equals eugenics equals racism, so let us avoid biology — combined with an offensive disciplinary politics — sociology must have its own *sui generis* object — to produce a human-only sociology that neither Durkheim nor Weber actually held. Sociology's biological blindness is, in this sense, self-imposed. It can also be self-removed. Durkheim's recognition that "animals are sociable or otherwise" and Weber's recognition that animals "understand… commands, anger, love, hostility" in meaningful ways are openings that multispecies sociology can walk through. The walking is what the discipline owes its founders.

### M6: To what extent does Haraway's PigeonBlog case in *Staying with the Trouble* succeed as a model for multispecies sociology?

I argue that PigeonBlog succeeds remarkably well as a model for multispecies sociology in three respects — methodological, political, and conceptual — but that its limitations as a model show why Haraway cannot stand alone and needs to be paired with Carter and Charles's structural realism.

**Methodological success.** PigeonBlog dramatises how to actually study multispecies practice without reducing it either to human action with non-human props or to a flat ontology of equivalent actors. Beatriz da Costa's 2006 project tied "homing pigeons, artists, engineers, and pigeon fanciers engaged in a grass-roots scientific data gathering initiative designed to collect and distribute information about air quality conditions" (Haraway, p. 21). The point Haraway emphasises is that none of the participants were pre-given. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18). The artist-researchers spent almost a year "building hands-on multispecies trust and knowledge essential to joining the birds, technology, and people" (p. 22). The fanciers had to be persuaded to risk the project despite fearing animal-rights backlash. Each of these is a sociologically describable practice of competence-building. The Despret quote Haraway leans on is the most concise statement of the method: "without the lover of pigeons (pigeon fancier), without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25).

This is exactly what becoming-with looks like as a method. The researcher does not ask "what does the pigeon mean to the humans?" (the symbolic reading) or "what is the human use-value of the pigeon?" (the instrumental reading). The researcher asks: through what specific entanglements were these particular pigeons rendered capable of flying air-pollution sensors over LA, and through what specific entanglements were these particular humans rendered capable of working with these particular birds? It is a methodological model.

**Political success.** PigeonBlog also succeeds politically. It targets the differential burden of air pollution — "rates of human (and other-than-human, but that is not rated) mortality and illness, often distributed by race and class" (Haraway, p. 20) — and produces data that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" cannot, because the official devices are "placed at fixed points away from high-traffic areas and known pollution sources and at altitudes higher than the zones in which people and lots of other plants and animals breathe" (p. 21). Multispecies practice here is environmental justice practice. The pigeons are not metaphors for justice; they are concrete co-producers of the data on which environmental justice claims can be made. This is sociology with an edge.

**Conceptual success.** PigeonBlog is also a conceptual achievement. Haraway uses it to refuse two opposed positions. Against human exceptionalism — the view that only humans are agents — she shows pigeons as "competent agents — in the double sense of both delegates and actors" (p. 18). Against flat ontology — the view that humans, animals and machines are all just "actants" in a network — she insists on specificity: "the partners do not precede the knotting" (p. 13), but the partners are nonetheless real, embodied, located, capable of suffering and flourishing. The pigeon is not interchangeable with the GPS unit it carries. "Not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, p. 11) is Haraway's compressed way of marking this difference.

**Limitations.** But PigeonBlog as a model has limitations. The first is scale. PigeonBlog is a beautifully specific case study; it shows what multispecies practice can look like in one art-activism project in one city in one year. It is harder to see how to scale this to the sociology of factory farming, which involves billions of animals per year and whose entanglements are structured by global capital, agribusiness consolidation, and state regulation. Haraway's vocabulary of becoming-with, response-ability, string figures and com-post is suited to small, specific, intimate entanglements. It is less obviously suited to large, anonymous, brutal entanglements. This is where Carter and Charles's structural-realist vocabulary — agency as "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, p. 90), with structures and emergent properties — does work Haraway cannot do.

The second limitation is normative. Haraway is suspicious of straightforward animal-rights politics. She refuses what she calls "prolife project[s]… in the chilling American sense of that term" (Haraway, p. 28). She praises the Batman Park pigeon loft in Melbourne not because it expands pigeon flourishing but because it implements "hatching control" — humans replace pigeon eggs with artificial ones to limit feral pigeon populations (Haraway, p. 28). This is a sophisticated position, but it makes Haraway uncomfortable with the kind of clear normative claims — "factory farming is wrong", "stop palm-oil deforestation", "no more cosmetic testing on rabbits" — that animal-studies politics often wants to make. Carter and Charles are more direct: animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (p. 80), full stop.

The third limitation is disciplinary. PigeonBlog is art-activism, not sociology. Haraway's whole intellectual style is happily outside the discipline. She invents vocabulary (Terrapolis, sf, com-post, response-ability, string figures) at a rate that sociologists trying to publish in *Sociology* or *European Journal of Social Theory* cannot match. Carter and Charles are explicit that they want to reform sociology from within rather than displace it (Carter and Charles, p. 87). PigeonBlog is a more useful model for what multispecies *practice* looks like than for what multispecies *sociology* looks like.

So PigeonBlog succeeds as a model methodologically, politically and conceptually, but its model status is local rather than universal. Used with Carter and Charles's structural-realist vocabulary, it shows how multispecies sociology can describe both intimate becoming-with and large-scale agential structures. Used alone, it gives us a brilliant case study without a discipline.

### M7: Discuss with examples why the Anthropocene makes a multispecies sociology not optional but necessary.

I argue that the Anthropocene — the geological epoch in which human activity has become the dominant force shaping Earth's systems — makes a multispecies sociology necessary in four registers: descriptive, causal, normative and reflexive. To stick with a human-only sociology in the Anthropocene is to be a discipline unable to describe its own time.

**Descriptive necessity.** The Anthropocene is a multispecies phenomenon by definition. Carter and Charles end their article with this warning: "continuing to direct the sociological gaze only at humans significantly limits the sociological imagination and is in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). The descriptive case is clear. Biodiversity collapse — the IPBES estimates a million species at risk of extinction — cannot be described as a purely human phenomenon. Zoonotic spillover, dramatised by COVID-19 but also by SARS, MERS, HIV, Ebola and avian influenza, is the leakage of pathogens from animal populations into human populations under conditions of habitat destruction and industrial livestock confinement. Antibiotic resistance is driven by the use of antibiotics in factory farms. Carbon emissions from livestock — particularly methane from ruminants — constitute roughly 14.5% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. None of these processes can be described without animals as central actors.

Concrete examples make this vivid. Palm-oil plantations in Indonesia and Malaysia destroy orangutan habitat, displace indigenous peoples, and generate carbon emissions; this is a multispecies process knotting capitalism, post-coloniality, deforestation and primate extinction. The lecturer flags this directly: "forests where Orangutans live are being cleared for Palm Oil." Beef production in the Amazon clears rainforest for cattle ranches that feed export markets in China and Europe; this is a multispecies process knotting capitalism, indigenous land rights, soybean monoculture and biodiversity loss. COVID-19 began, in the leading hypothesis, in a multispecies entanglement of bats, intermediate hosts, wild-meat markets and global travel.

**Causal necessity.** The Anthropocene also requires a multispecies sociology causally, because the mechanisms producing planetary change cross the species line. Factory farming is the obvious case. Industrial confinement of pigs, chickens and cattle is simultaneously a labour practice (with documented health and psychological costs for slaughterhouse workers), a capital-accumulation practice (with documented consolidation of agribusiness), an environmental practice (with documented impacts on water, soil, climate) and a multispecies practice (with documented systematic cruelty). To explain rising obesity in the Global North, you have to explain cheap meat. To explain cheap meat, you have to explain industrial livestock production. To explain industrial livestock production, you have to explain the conversion of animals into "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use" (Greene 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). Sociology cannot stop at the species line because the causal chains do not.

**Normative necessity.** The Anthropocene raises ethical and political questions that sociology, on its own self-understanding, should be able to address. Who suffers? Who benefits? Who bears risk? Carter and Charles are clear that animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (p. 80). The sociology of animal suffering is continuous with the sociology of human suffering. Slaughterhouse workers — disproportionately migrants, disproportionately racialised, disproportionately precarious — suffer documented harms (PTSD, repetitive strain, dangerous conditions) alongside the animals they slaughter. Indigenous peoples lose territories alongside the species those territories sustain. The lecturer's framing of veganism — Greenebaum's (2012) three-type analysis of health, environmental and ethical vegans — gestures toward this normative complexity: vegan politics is simultaneously about human health, planetary survival, and the ethical standing of animals. A sociology that cannot describe these intersections cannot do justice to its century.

**Reflexive necessity.** Finally, the Anthropocene requires a multispecies sociology reflexively, because the discipline's own categories were built in and for an earlier moment when human-only sociology seemed unproblematic. Carter and Charles's anthroponormativity diagnosis (p. 88) is the reflexive demand: stop treating "the social = the human" as a discovered fact and start treating it as a contestable definition. Haraway's call to "stay with the trouble" (the title of her book) is the same reflexive demand: the discipline cannot proceed as if its conceptual inheritance is adequate to its new historical situation. To pretend that pre-Anthropocene categories will serve in the Anthropocene is what Haraway, in another register, would call disciplinary bad faith.

There are objections. The strongest is that the Anthropocene is itself a contested concept — some scholars prefer "Capitalocene" (Jason Moore) or "Plantationocene" (Anna Tsing, Haraway) to emphasise the specific economic systems driving planetary change, and others worry that "Anthropocene" indicts humanity as a whole rather than the specific human institutions responsible. Haraway herself prefers "Chthulucene" (Haraway, p. 11). I take the point: the term is imperfect. But the underlying phenomenon — planetary change driven by specific human practices, with consequences that cross the species line — is real whatever we call it. And it requires a multispecies sociology.

Concrete examples again. COVID-19 demanded a sociology of the wet market, the bat, the intermediate host, the slaughterhouse, the supply chain, the lockdown and the vaccine — multispecies all the way down. Biodiversity collapse demands a sociology of habitat loss, species reintroduction, conservation politics and indigenous land rights. Factory farming demands a sociology of capital, labour, animal welfare and environmental regulation. Pet-keeping, in an era of separated-by-COVID lockdowns and rising mental-health concerns, demands a sociology of companion animals and emotional labour. None of these is optional.

So the Anthropocene makes multispecies sociology necessary not because it is a fashionable topic but because the discipline's own promises — to describe society, to explain causation, to support normative judgement, to remain reflexive — cannot be kept without it. Carter and Charles are right to make this their closing claim. Haraway is right to live and write in its register. The choice for sociology is to take the multispecies turn or to be a discipline that talks about a planet that no longer exists.

### M8: Critically assess the claim that animals can have culture and agency.

I argue, with Carter and Charles, that animals can have culture and agency in sociologically meaningful senses, but that defending the claim requires careful distinctions between strong and weak versions, and that the most defensible version uses a relational concept of agency and an empirically grounded concept of culture.

**The strong claim and the weak claim.** The strong claim is that some animals possess language, culture, consciousness, intentionality and morality in essentially the same senses that humans do. The weak claim is that some animals possess versions of these capacities sufficient to make them sociologically interesting agents and culture-bearers. I defend the weak claim and think the strong claim overreaches.

**Culture.** Carter and Charles note that primatology and ethology have, over the last several decades, accumulated evidence that "animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, p. 88, citing de Waal 2001). Tool use in chimpanzee populations, vocal dialects in killer whale pods, song traditions in birds — these are not folk-anthropomorphic projections but documented findings of careful field science. They demand a sociological response: if culture is the inter-generational transmission of learned, group-specific practice, then some non-human animals have culture. Carter and Charles cite this evidence to make the point that "these developments pose a problem for sociology's assumption that culture is uniquely human" (p. 88).

The lecturer makes the same case more provocatively: some sociologists insist "that only humans have language. And that only humans have culture" (lecture slide on "Some people want to retain an element of human exceptionalism"); but the reply is that "animals can communicate (though they don't obviously have human language)" and "animals have culture." The lecturer's rhetorical question is sharp: "Why do we want to keep culture to ourselves?"

**Agency.** Carter and Charles devote the most extended treatment to agency. They reject the Cartesian conception — agency as a property of rational, self-conscious individual subjects — and adopt instead a relational conception drawn from Whitehead, Archer and Despret. On this view, "agency is not an attribute, or property, of humans or other animals or things, but an emergent product of the relations within which all things are placed" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). It is "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). The wolf example is illustrative: a wolf's agency "changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (p. 91), with EU protection in some places and licensed hunting in others. The pheasant-shoot example is illustrative again: pheasants in a UK shoot are "social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (p. 92).

On this relational view, animals can have agency without being little humans. They have purposes (to live, eat, breed, navigate), they have capacities (to fly, hunt, mate, recognise), and these purposes and capacities engage relational conditions in ways that constitute social practices. The PigeonBlog pigeons have agency because their navigation capacities engaged the relational conditions of LA air pollution monitoring (Haraway, p. 22). The shooting-season pheasants have agency, even though it is heavily circumscribed: "the caged chicken cannot walk freely and the pheasant cannot fly safely within range of armed humans" (Carter and Charles, p. 92).

**Objections.** The strongest objection is from symbolic interactionism and from writers like Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard (the lecturer's list). The claim is that human social action is constituted by reflexive symbol-use of a kind unavailable to animals; therefore animal "culture" and "agency" are at best analogies. There are two replies. First, the empirical record on animal symbol-use is richer than the older symbolic interactionists assumed: pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, p. 18), great apes use sign language, dolphins demonstrate referential communication, octopuses do astonishing things. Second, even granting that human reflexive symbol-use is distinctive, this does not entail that animals lack agency and culture in any sense; it entails that animal agency and culture are differently structured. The Cartesian move — "different in structure" therefore "different in kind" therefore "not really agency and culture at all" — is the conceptual sleight that anthroponormativity exposes.

A second objection is that the concepts of agency and culture lose meaning if extended too far. If a pigeon has agency, why not a rock? The reply is that the relational conception of agency is precisely not the claim that everything is an agent. Agency, on this view, requires purposes that engage relational conditions. A pigeon has purposes; a rock does not. The line is empirical, not metaphysical.

**The case of personhood.** The lecturer flags a 2017 case in which a New York lawyer sought to have two chimpanzees declared "persons" in legal terms. This is the strong claim in legal-political form. I am inclined to think Haraway's caution applies here: "Perhaps we should turn these questions round. Why don't we want animals to have agency, subjectivity, the ability to use symbols?" The legal personhood debate is less about chimpanzees than about us — about whether we are willing to revise our categories. Carter and Charles are explicit that they are not engaged in animal advocacy (p. 93); their argument is sociological, not legal. But the sociological argument has political implications.

**The case of factory farming.** A complementary case from the opposite end is industrial agriculture. Factory-farmed animals have agency in the relational sense — they have purposes that engage conditions — but those conditions systematically thwart almost every purpose. "Sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages; wolves and pheasants are unaware of the shooting season" (Carter and Charles, p. 92). The recognition of agency here is not romantic; it is the recognition that the animals are being deprived of their agential conditions. This recognition is sociologically describable and politically actionable.

So I defend the weak version of the claim: animals can have culture and agency in sociologically meaningful senses, where "culture" means group-specific learned practice persisting across generations and "agency" means the relational engagement of purposes with conditions. This is enough to dismantle the human-only definition of the social and to make multispecies sociology possible. The strong version — animals as little humans — is not necessary for the work, and is in fact a hindrance, because it makes the disagreement turn on whether animals are like us rather than on how human–animal entanglements actually work.

### M9: To what extent can the bifurcation between humans and animals be defended within contemporary sociology?

I argue that the human/animal bifurcation cannot be defended in its strong form within contemporary sociology, but that some weaker species-specific distinctions remain useful. The question is not whether there are differences between humans and other animals — there are — but whether those differences justify a sociological definition that excludes animals from the social. Carter and Charles's argument shows that they do not.

**The bifurcation defined.** The lecture frames bifurcation as the assumption "that 'humans' and 'animals' are two categories that can be clearly distinguished from each other" (Carter and Charles 2011, cited in slides). The slide pairs this with allied bifurcations: nature/culture, genes/environment, facts/values. The animal/human binary is one move in a wider pattern of dualistic thinking that Carter and Charles, following Latour and others, see as constitutive of modernity itself: "an arrangement in which the natural and the social are regarded as discrete realms: the one composed of objects, the other of subjects" (Carter and Charles, p. 85, citing Latour). To defend the bifurcation is to defend this larger modernist arrangement.

**The strong defence.** The strongest defence is the one symbolic interactionism gives: humans are the only beings with reflexive symbol-use, and reflexive symbol-use is the precondition of meaningful social action; therefore the social as a domain of meaningful action is properly restricted to humans. The lecturer associates this position with Mead, Goffman, and (in a wider register) Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard. The case has force. Human language is, by any reasonable measure, structurally different from animal communication systems in complexity, productivity and recursion. Human moral reasoning, scientific theorising, legal institution-building and large-scale collective action are unmatched.

But the strong defence fails for two reasons. First, it slides between empirical and definitional claims. It is one thing to say "humans are linguistically and culturally distinctive"; it is another to say "therefore animals are not properly part of the social." The slide treats a difference of degree (sophisticated symbol-use) as a difference of kind (presence or absence of sociality). Carter and Charles call this anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (p. 88), and these configurations then masquerade as definitions of what is to count as social. Second, the strong defence makes empirical predictions that the evidence does not support. Animal cultures, animal communication, animal cognition, animal emotion — the empirical record across primatology, ethology and animal cognitive science is now substantial enough that the strong human/animal divide cannot be sustained on facts.

**The weak defence.** A weaker defence concedes that animals have some forms of culture and agency but maintains that the differences are sociologically important enough to justify methodological distinctions. This is more defensible. There really are species-specific capacities — human written language, mass democratic politics, modern science, historical self-consciousness — that have no obvious animal equivalents. Carter and Charles do not deny this. Their point is that recognising these distinctive human capacities is compatible with treating animals as sociological actors. The choice is not between "humans and animals are the same" and "humans and animals are sociologically separable"; it is between species-specific descriptions and a hard binary.

**The structural argument.** Carter and Charles add a structural argument. Even if we conceded — for the sake of argument — that humans are sociologically unique in their capacities, this would not justify excluding animals from sociology. Why? Because human social practices are entangled with animal lives in ways that cannot be described without animals as parts of the description. "Any adequate specification of societies as structures of social relationship or interaction must include reference to non-human animals as occupants of social positions and as terms in social relationships" (Benton 1993: 68, cited Carter and Charles, p. 86). The pheasant shoot, the slaughterhouse, the pet-owning household, the lab, the conservation reserve, the racing pigeon loft — all are human social practices that include animals as constitutive components. To describe these as if the animals were mere props is to misdescribe them.

**Haraway's argument.** Haraway pushes further. Bifurcation is incompatible with the actual practice of becoming-with. "The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, p. 13). Human capacities themselves are not species-given; they are species-relational, produced through entanglement with other species. The lecturer puts this directly: "Humans become humans through their relations with animals." The bifurcation assumes that there is a "human" that exists prior to engagement with animals, and an "animal" that exists prior to engagement with humans, and then a relation between them. Haraway's claim is that there are no such pre-given partners; there are only the entangled practices that produce both poles.

**Defences that survive.** Some bifurcations survive scrutiny. Legal status — animals do not have full citizenship — is a real bifurcation, but one that sociology can describe and critique rather than naturalise. Ethical responsibility — humans bear ethical responsibilities that animals do not, because of our reflective capacities — is a real asymmetry, but one that is internal to multispecies sociology, not opposed to it. Methodological access — we can interview humans and not animals — is a real practical difference, but one that demands new methods (Despret's ethology, Kimbo's "thinking with") rather than the wholesale exclusion of animals.

**Defences that fail.** Defences that fail are the ontological-categorical ones. "Humans are subjects, animals are objects" fails because animals are demonstrably agents in the relational sense. "Humans have culture, animals do not" fails because animal cultures are documented. "Society is human-only" fails because social practices are demonstrably multispecies. "Sociology is the study of human societies" fails as a defence because the discipline's actual objects — industrial capitalism, urban modernity, the post-colonial world, the Anthropocene — are themselves multispecies phenomena.

So the bifurcation cannot be defended within contemporary sociology in its strong, categorical form. It can be replaced by species-specific descriptions that recognise both differences and entanglements. As Carter and Charles conclude: "the social *is* animal" (p. 93). The bifurcation, in its modernist form, dissolves under the weight of its own empirical and conceptual failures.

### M10: Discuss with examples how factory farming and pet-keeping demand different sociological frameworks.

I argue that factory farming and pet-keeping are at opposite ends of the spectrum of human–animal relations and that they demand different but complementary sociological frameworks: a structural-political-economy framework for factory farming, and a micro-relational becoming-with framework for pet-keeping. The contrast is itself sociologically illuminating, because it shows that multispecies sociology is not one method but a family of methods, calibrated to different kinds of entanglement.

**Factory farming.** Factory farming is industrial-scale confinement, processing and slaughter of animals for human consumption. The scale is staggering — roughly 80 billion land animals are slaughtered each year globally — and the institutions are unmistakably those of industrial capitalism: vertically integrated agribusiness corporations, commodity supply chains, regulatory regimes, labour practices, advertising and consumer culture. The animals are systematically deprived of nearly every agential condition that would let them realise their species-specific purposes. As Carter and Charles put it: "chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages" (Carter and Charles, p. 92).

The sociological framework appropriate to factory farming is structural and political-economic. The questions are: how did this system come to exist? What economic forces sustain it? What labour practices does it require? What state regulations enable or constrain it? What consumer cultures and food cultures legitimate it? How does it intersect with class, race, gender, migration status and global trade? Carter and Charles's relational realism — agency as "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90) — fits naturally here, because the structural conditions are the conditions of action for both human workers and animal "products." Marxist political economy, Weberian rationalisation, Foucauldian biopolitics and the sociology of food and agriculture all have purchase. The animals are agents in the relational sense, but their agency is brutally curtailed.

Specific examples make this vivid. Industrial poultry production confines chickens by the tens of thousands in sheds where ammonia levels harm both birds and workers. Pig confinement systems use gestation crates that prevent sows from turning around. Cattle feedlots concentrate animals in numbers that produce methane emissions, manure lagoons and groundwater contamination. Slaughterhouse workers — disproportionately migrant, racialised and undocumented — suffer the highest non-fatal injury rates of any US industry and document elevated rates of PTSD. The sociology of factory farming is necessarily a sociology of capital, labour, race, environment and animals at once.

**Pet-keeping.** Pet-keeping is the opposite kind of practice: intimate, dyadic or small-group, organised around affection and care, embedded in households rather than industrial systems. Pets — dogs, cats, rabbits, birds, reptiles, fish — share domestic space with their humans, participate in family routines, are mourned when they die, and are increasingly recognised as significant in mental health, child development and elderly care. The economic scale is large (the global pet industry is enormous), but the structure is decentralised and household-based.

The sociological framework appropriate to pet-keeping is micro-relational. Haraway's becoming-with vocabulary fits here far better than for factory farming. Companion species "play string figure games where who is/are to be in/of the world is constituted in intra- and interaction" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). Humans become humans through specific relations with their dogs and cats; dogs and cats become the specific dogs and cats they are through specific relations with their humans. Haraway's earlier *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003) develops this for dog-human relations specifically: humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand." Kimbo's account of her dog Harald is in the same register: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless" (lecture slide on "Thinking with her companion (dog) Harald"). "If two individual bodies… open themselves up or attune to each other… shared experience is what matters foremost."

Specific examples make this vivid. Companion-animal therapy is a recognised practice in mental health treatment for veterans with PTSD, children on the autism spectrum, and elderly people with dementia. The sociology of pet-keeping has to describe the emotional labour involved in caring for a dying pet, the family dynamics of who feeds and walks the dog, the gendered patterns of pet care, and the household economic decisions involved in vet bills. It also has to describe the asymmetries: pets are not chosen partners in the way human partners are; they cannot consent to their lives; they are bred, bought, sold and neutered in ways that recall — at a softened scale — the structural conditions of factory farming.

**Why different frameworks.** Why do these two practices demand different frameworks? Because the scale, structure and entanglement-density of the practices differ. Factory farming is a structurally organised system in which individual animals are interchangeable inputs; the sociological work is at the level of structures. Pet-keeping is a dyadic intimate practice in which the specific animal is the point; the sociological work is at the level of relations and meanings. Carter and Charles's structural-realist vocabulary is built for the first; Haraway's becoming-with vocabulary is built for the second. Trying to use Haraway on a chicken farm produces sentimentality that flattens the brutality; trying to use Carter and Charles on a household dog produces categorical analysis that misses the texture of attachment.

**The connection.** But — and this is the point that makes multispecies sociology coherent — the two practices are connected. Pet food is a downstream product of the factory-farming industry; dogs and cats are major consumers of industrial meat. The pet industry markets specific breeds in ways that reproduce eugenic logics. Pet "abandonment" — dogs and cats surrendered to shelters, then often euthanised — is itself a low-grade version of the factory-farming production-and-disposal logic. A serious sociology of human–animal relations has to be able to move between scales, using Carter and Charles for structural analysis and Haraway for relational analysis.

**Common ground.** Both frameworks share an anti-anthroponormative commitment: animals are agents and culture-bearers, not props. Both reject the bifurcation that puts animals outside the social. Both accept that human social practice is multispecies practice. The difference is not in commitment but in calibration — different practices demand different conceptual tools.

So factory farming and pet-keeping demand different frameworks because they are different practices: one structural and industrial, the other relational and intimate. The sociology that can describe both is not a single theory but a family of approaches, anchored in the shared diagnostic of anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles) and the shared method of becoming-with (Haraway). Used together, they let sociology describe the full range of human–animal entanglements — from the slaughterhouse to the household, from the supply chain to the sofa, from the brutal to the loving.

### M11: Compare the four-stage feminist trajectory invoked by Carter and Charles with current animal studies — what stage are we at, and why?

I argue that animal studies, on Carter and Charles's own analogy, is currently caught somewhere between stages two and three of the feminist trajectory, but that the analogy is sharper as a diagnostic than as a prediction: the move from stage three to stage four will require structural changes the feminist trajectory itself only partly secured. Used carefully, the analogy clarifies both where we are and what the work still demands.

**The four stages.** Carter and Charles take the feminist trajectory directly from Walby, Oakley and Charles: a "pre-feminist" phase in which the discipline simply did not see women; a critique phase in which feminism diagnosed sociology's gender blindness; an "adding women in" phase in which empirical research on women's lives was incorporated; and a final stage of "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 80). Animal studies, they argue, "is experiencing the second and third stages." That is, the critique has been made — animals are missing from sociology and this is a problem — and empirical work on animals is now visible in journals, conferences and curricula, but the conceptual integration of animals into sociology's core vocabulary has not yet happened.

**The case for stage two.** Carter and Charles's own article is itself a stage-two intervention. The very fact that they have to argue for a "reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, p. 80) is evidence that the field is still defending its right to exist. Anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles, p. 88) has to be named because it is not yet acknowledged. The lecturer's framing of the lecture — animals as "a problem for Sociology", animals as "neglected within modernity" — is still in the register of critique rather than incorporation. The fact that the discipline can still publish major textbooks on social theory without an animals chapter is the unmistakable mark of stage two.

**The case for stage three.** At the same time, animal studies has plainly entered the "adding-in" stage. Multispecies ethnography is a recognised methodology. The lecturer's PhD student Alexandra Kimbo wrote a thesis explicitly on *Sociology, Sociality and Animals: Beyond the social/natural divide* — that is a stage-three achievement. Greenebaum's (2012) typology of health, environmental and ethical vegans is a stage-three contribution: empirical work on a multispecies social movement. The *European Journal of Social Theory* publishing Carter and Charles is itself a stage-three move. Multispecies sociology, Critical Animal Studies, Human–Animal Studies — these are now sub-fields with their own journals.

**Why we are not at stage four.** But we are emphatically not at stage four. Mainstream sociology — survey methods, classical theory teaching, methods textbooks, undergraduate curricula — still treats animals as a specialist topic rather than as integral to social analysis. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses (lecture). Most introductory sociology textbooks still define the discipline as the study of human societies. The Carter and Charles article appeared in 2018; seven years on, the discipline has not absorbed it as it absorbed the equivalent feminist arguments by the late 1980s.

**Where the analogy strains.** I think the analogy is sharper as diagnosis than as prediction, for three reasons. First, the feminist trajectory operated within a constituency — women — who could speak in their own voices in seminars and on editorial boards. Animals cannot. Multispecies sociology has to develop methods (Despret's ethology, Kimbo's "thinking with", Haraway's becoming-with) that the feminist trajectory did not need to invent. Second, the institutional incentives are different. Gender integration was driven partly by the entry of women into the academy as professionals; there is no analogous "entry of animals" into university faculty. The drivers of stage-three-to-stage-four progress will have to be ecological and political (Anthropocene, animal-rights movements, factory-farming critiques) rather than demographic. Third, the feminist trajectory benefited from a sequence of high-profile theoretical landmarks — Beauvoir, Mitchell, Smith, Butler — that conceptually reframed gender. Animal studies is still searching for its Butler. Haraway's *Staying with the Trouble* (2016) is the strongest candidate, but its reception inside sociology has been slower than its reception in science studies and the humanities.

**What stage-four animal studies would look like.** Stage-four animal studies would mean that a sociology textbook chapter on "work" automatically discussed lab animals, slaughterhouse animals, working dogs, racing pigeons and pollinator infrastructure alongside human labour. A chapter on "family" would automatically discuss companion animals and pet-keeping. A chapter on "globalisation" would automatically discuss livestock supply chains and zoonotic disease. A chapter on "the body" would automatically discuss multispecies entanglements of microbiome, contagion and food. The vocabulary of agency, society, social action, intersubjectivity and reflexivity — Carter and Charles's anthroponormative core — would be reworked, in Carter and Charles's own preferred direction (relational realism) or in Haraway's (becoming-with). The shift is not "add a chapter" but "rework the entire vocabulary."

**Counter-argument.** A skeptic might reply that animal studies has overreached the feminist analogy — that animals are not, in fact, like women in the relevant respect, because women are obviously social actors in the human sense while animals are at best agents in a weaker, relational sense. I think the reply concedes the analogy at exactly the level it operates. Carter and Charles do not claim animals are like women in their capacities; they claim that sociology's exclusion of animals operates structurally like its exclusion of women — through silent definitional moves that mask political choices as conceptual necessities. The analogy is about the structure of exclusion, not about the excluded.

**Conclusion.** Animal studies is between stages two and three because the critique is made and empirical work proceeds, but the conceptual integration has not happened. The move to stage four will require what feminist sociology required and more: theoretical landmarks, methodological innovation, institutional incentives, and the political pressure of the Anthropocene. Carter and Charles's 2018 article is, on its own terms, an attempt to push the field from stage two toward stage three; the next decade will test whether it succeeds. The diagnostic remains useful; the prediction is harder to make.

### M12: Discuss with examples why the Anthropocene makes a multispecies sociology not optional but necessary.

I argue that the Anthropocene — the proposed geological epoch in which human activity has become the dominant force shaping planetary systems — makes multispecies sociology a methodological obligation rather than a fashionable option. The case rests on the simple observation that the Anthropocene's defining phenomena cross the species line in their causes, their effects and their politics. A sociology that cannot follow those causal and political chains across that line is a sociology that cannot describe its own historical moment.

**The descriptive obligation.** Carter and Charles end *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* with this warning: "continuing to direct the sociological gaze only at humans significantly limits the sociological imagination and is in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). The descriptive case is straightforward. Biodiversity loss — the IPBES estimates around one million species threatened with extinction — is a sociological phenomenon because the proximate drivers (habitat conversion, industrial agriculture, urban expansion, climate change, pollution) are social practices. Zoonotic spillover events, from SARS and MERS to HIV, Ebola and COVID-19, are the leakage of pathogens from animal populations into human populations under conditions of habitat destruction, wildlife trade and industrial livestock confinement. Antibiotic resistance is in significant part a consequence of subtherapeutic antibiotic use in factory farming. Methane emissions from ruminant livestock account for roughly 14.5% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. To describe any of these phenomena adequately, animals have to enter the description not as background but as actors and victims.

**The causal obligation.** The Anthropocene also makes multispecies sociology necessary causally. The causal chains that produce planetary change run through human–animal entanglements. Take the global beef trade. Brazilian Amazon clearance for cattle pasture displaces indigenous communities and biodiversity, while feeding export markets in China and the EU. The causal mechanism is multispecies: bovine biology (rumen methane production, grass-and-soybean feeding regimes, breeding rhythms) couples with capitalist commodity logistics (supply chains, futures markets, slaughter capacity) and political-economic structures (Brazilian land law, deforestation enforcement, trade agreements) to produce a specific anthropogenic outcome (rainforest loss, climate change, indigenous dispossession). Stop the description at the species line and you cannot follow the chain. Greene's nineteenth-century horses prefigure this: horses were "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use" (Greene 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84), and the causal explanation of urban modernity required them. The Anthropocene generalises Greene's point across the entire planetary economy.

**The normative obligation.** The Anthropocene raises ethical and political questions that sociology, on its own self-understanding, should address. Carter and Charles are explicit that animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, p. 80). The sociology of suffering, justice and inequality is continuous across the species line. Slaughterhouse workers — disproportionately migrants, racialised, undocumented — suffer documented harms (PTSD, repetitive injury, dangerous chemical exposure) alongside the animals they slaughter. Indigenous peoples lose territories alongside the species those territories sustain. Pacific Islanders face displacement from climate change while their reef ecosystems collapse. Vegan politics, in Greenebaum's three-type analysis (health, environmental, ethical), captures this normative complexity: vegan movements are simultaneously about human health, planetary survival and the ethical standing of animals. A sociology that cannot describe these intersections cannot do justice to its century.

**The reflexive obligation.** Haraway's response is the call to "stay with the trouble" — the title of her book and her programmatic injunction against escapist or technological fixes. "We are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 29). The reflexive obligation is to recognise that the discipline's own categories were built in and for an earlier moment when human-only sociology seemed unproblematic. Anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles, p. 88) is the reflexive demand: stop treating "the social = the human" as a discovered fact and start treating it as a contestable definition. To carry pre-Anthropocene categories unrevised into the Anthropocene is, in Haraway's register, disciplinary bad faith.

**Concrete examples.** COVID-19 is the most vivid recent case. To describe COVID-19 sociologically, one needs the wet market or laboratory origin, the bat reservoir, the intermediate host, the urban supply chain, the lockdown, the vaccine rollout, the labour conditions of meat-processing plants where outbreaks clustered, and the differential racial and class burden of mortality. These are multispecies all the way down. Factory farming is another case: industrial livestock confinement is simultaneously a labour practice, a capital-accumulation practice, an environmental practice, an animal-welfare practice and a public-health practice. The Haraway PigeonBlog case shows the reverse — multispecies citizen science as a tool for measuring environmental injustice, where the pigeons were "competent agents — in the double sense of both delegates and actors" (Haraway, p. 18). Pet-keeping during COVID lockdowns generated a new sociology of companion-animal therapy and emotional labour, with measurable mental-health effects.

**Counter-argument.** A skeptic might reply that the Anthropocene is itself contested — Jason Moore prefers "Capitalocene"; Anna Tsing and Haraway propose "Plantationocene"; Haraway elsewhere prefers "Chthulucene" (Haraway, p. 11) — and that loading sociology with planetary ambitions overreaches the discipline's competence. I take the conceptual point but reject the implication. The naming dispute concerns who bears the responsibility (humanity in general or specific capitalist and colonial institutions); it does not dispute the underlying multispecies character of the planetary crisis. And the worry about overreach inverts the diagnosis: sociology's risk is irrelevance, not overreach, if it cannot describe the planet its societies actually inhabit.

**Conclusion.** The Anthropocene makes multispecies sociology necessary because its defining phenomena are multispecies in their description, their causation, their politics and their reflexive demands on the discipline. To stick with human-only sociology in this moment is not rigorous; it is wilfully partial. Carter and Charles are right that "the social *is* animal" (p. 93). Haraway is right to insist on staying with the trouble. The choice for sociology is to take the multispecies turn or to be a discipline that talks about a planet that no longer exists.

### M13: Critically assess the claim that animals can have culture and agency, drawing on Carter and Charles and Haraway.

I argue, drawing on Carter and Charles and Haraway, that animals can have culture and agency in sociologically meaningful senses, provided we use empirically grounded concepts of culture and a relational concept of agency. The strong claim — animals as little humans, with language, morality and consciousness identical to ours — overreaches; the weak claim is enough for the sociological work, and it is well supported.

**Two versions of the claim.** The strong version says some animals possess language, culture, consciousness, intentionality and morality in essentially the same senses humans do. The weak version says some animals possess versions of these capacities sufficient to make them sociologically interesting agents and culture-bearers. I defend the weak version. The strong version invites endless metaphysical squabbles about what "really" counts as language or consciousness; the weak version does the sociological work without those squabbles.

**Animal culture.** Carter and Charles cite the now-established finding that "animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 88, citing de Waal 2001). Chimpanzee tool-use traditions differ between populations and persist across generations; killer whale vocal dialects are pod-specific; some bird song traditions are inter-generationally transmitted and locally variable. These are not anthropomorphic projections but findings of careful ethology. If culture means "inter-generationally transmitted, group-specific learned practice", then some animals have culture. The lecturer presses the rhetorical question that follows: "Why do we want to keep culture to ourselves?" The defensive posture, not the empirical record, is doing the conceptual work.

**Animal agency.** Carter and Charles's most extended contribution concerns agency. They reject the Cartesian conception — agency as the property of self-conscious individual rational subjects — and replace it with a relational conception drawn from Whitehead, Archer, Ahearn and Despret. "Agency is not an attribute, or property, of humans or other animals or things, but an emergent product of the relations within which all things are placed" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). It is "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). On this view, agency requires purposes engaging relational conditions; it does not require Cartesian self-consciousness.

The European wolf example demonstrates the move. A wolf's agency "changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (p. 91). In Sweden the wolf is EU-protected; in northern Sami reindeer-herding areas it can be killed under licence; in Norway, outside EU jurisdiction, it can be hunted differently. The wolves do not know which jurisdiction they have crossed, but the relational conditions of their action differ. Their hunting, mating and territorial marking engage these different conditions to produce sociologically describable outcomes. This is agency in the relational sense, and it is real.

The pheasant-shoot example tightens the case. A UK pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (p. 92). Pheasants have purposes (live, fly, breed) which engage conditions (October–February shooting season, dog-handlers, gun licences, class-coded "sporting life") to produce a specific multispecies practice. The pheasants are agents even though their agency is heavily circumscribed; indeed the constraint is precisely what makes agency a useful sociological concept here.

**Haraway's contribution.** Haraway provides the constructive method. Becoming-with names the way agential capacities are produced through entanglement: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 12). The PigeonBlog case demonstrates: the racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18); the artist-researchers had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (p. 22). The pigeons' homing capacities, navigation skills and urban familiarity were essential to the project; their agency was inseparable from the specific human cooperation that activated it. The Despret line is the most compressed statement of the point: "without the lover of pigeons… without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25). Culture and agency, on Haraway's account, are co-produced through specific entanglements rather than discovered as pre-given properties.

**Objections.** The strongest objection is symbolic-interactionist: humans alone possess reflexive symbol-use of a kind that constitutes the social, so animal culture and agency are at best analogies. The reply is twofold. Empirically, the record on animal symbol-use is richer than Mead's generation assumed: pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, p. 18); great apes use sign language; cetaceans demonstrate referential communication. More importantly, even granting that human reflexive symbol-use is structurally distinctive, this does not entail that animals lack culture and agency in any sense — only that animal culture and agency are differently structured. The Cartesian move (different in structure, therefore different in kind, therefore not real culture and agency) is the anthroponormative sleight Carter and Charles expose.

A second objection: extending agency to animals dilutes the concept. If pigeons have agency, why not rocks? The reply is that the relational concept is not the claim that everything is an agent; it requires beings with purposes engaging relational conditions. Pigeons have purposes; rocks do not. The line is empirical, not metaphysical.

**Factory farming as a stress test.** Industrial agriculture stress-tests the concept. Factory-farmed pigs and chickens have agency in the relational sense — they have purposes engaging conditions — but those conditions systematically thwart almost every purpose. "Chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages" (Carter and Charles, p. 92). The recognition of agency here is not romantic; it is the recognition that the animals are being deprived of their agential conditions. This is sociologically describable and politically actionable. It also shows why the weak version is more useful than the strong: we do not need chickens to be little humans to recognise their agency being crushed; we need only the relational concept Carter and Charles supply.

**Conclusion.** Animals can have culture and agency in sociologically meaningful senses. Culture means group-specific, inter-generationally transmitted learned practice, and is empirically documented in several taxa. Agency means the relational engagement of purposes with conditions, and is exemplified in wolves, pheasants, racing pigeons and factory-farmed animals alike. Carter and Charles provide the diagnostic of anthroponormativity and the relational-realist concept of agency; Haraway provides the constructive vocabulary of becoming-with. Together they make the weak version of the claim defensible and sociologically productive. The strong version is unnecessary — and, as Haraway's caution about not collapsing into posthumanism shows, often counter-productive.
