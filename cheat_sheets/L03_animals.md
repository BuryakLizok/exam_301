## 2. Key concepts and thinkers

- **Human exceptionalism**: the doctrine that humans alone possess intention, morality, symbolism, consciousness, and so stand outside natural law (slide on "Human Exceptionalism"). Sociology, on Carter and Charles's reading, is built on this premise.
- **Bifurcation / the animal–human binary**: the dualistic carve-up nature/culture, genes/environment, facts/values, animal/human (Carter and Charles, 2011: 2, cited in slides).
- **Anthroponormativity**: Carter and Charles's term for sociology's conceptual vocabulary being silently configured around the human, analogous to heteronormativity or white normativity.
- **Companion species** (Haraway): humans become humans through co-shaping relations with other species; not "humans AND animals" but mutually constitutive becoming.
- **Becoming-with / rendering-capable** (Haraway, Despret): partners do not pre-exist the knot; they are made what they are through entanglement.
- **Multispecies sociology / multispecies ethnography**: research that treats animals as social actors and co-constituents of society, not as mute "nature."
- **Situated knowledge**: knowledge is always located in specific bodies and relations; pertinent here to "thinking with" rather than "about" animals (Kimbo, Despret).
- **Classical thinkers**: Durkheim (sociability as social, observable in animals); Weber (animals can "understand" but verifiable meaningful access is limited); Marx (productive labour, bees); Greene (horses as living machines, prime movers of industrial modernity); Parsons (consolidator of the strictly human social).

## 4. Information from the readings

**Carter and Charles, 'The animal challenge to sociology' (2018).** The article argues that sociology has been "slow to take up the animal challenge" (2018: 79), despite the obvious fact that "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Bryant 1979, cited 2018: 79). Three reasons are diagnosed. *First*, sociology has had a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80): in its late-nineteenth-century institutionalisation, the discipline defended "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12) against Darwinian "comprehensive naturalism", and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. *Second*, sociology rests on **human exceptionalism**: humans are treated as evolutionarily unique because of language and meaning-making, and "the human" is "usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Ingold 1994a, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). *Third*, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe, where urban dwellers experienced themselves as "increasingly remote from most other animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). Yet, drawing on Greene (2008), Carter and Charles insist horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84) and the "prime mover" of industrial development.

The article then argues for two conceptual revisions. **Society** must be redefined to include non-human animals as "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68). **Agency** must be reworked as relational: agency is not a Cartesian property of rational subjects but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90); it is "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Their pheasant-shoot example dramatises a "complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Their concluding claim is uncompromising: "the social is animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). Sociology's whole conceptual vocabulary is "anthroponormative" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), analogous to heteronormative and white-normative vocabularies, and must be reworked, not abandoned (as Latour and the new materialists would have it).

**Haraway, 'Playing String Figures with Companion Species' (2016).** The chapter develops a method for thinking with animals through the metaphor of cat's cradle: "sf" doubles for science fiction, speculative fabulation, science fact, and string figures. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13). Haraway's pigeons illustrate this: racing pigeons in Southern California are "competent agents, in the double sense of both delegates and actors" (Haraway, 2016: 18). Her key empirical case is **PigeonBlog** (Beatriz da Costa, 2006), where racing pigeons, art-activists, working-class pigeon fanciers and DIY electronics co-produced air-pollution data over polluted Los Angeles neighbourhoods. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) and the humans had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22). Response-ability, a Despret term Haraway adopts, names the obligation to make others "capable" through specific entanglements (Haraway, 2016: 12). Haraway explicitly refuses both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11). Project PigeonWatch with Washington DC schoolchildren shows recuperation across categories that racist iconography links ("rats with wings", "feral") when the kids learn to see pigeons "as sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, 2016: 24).

**Haraway, *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003).** Earlier articulation of the same project: dogs and people are not pre-given subjects encountering each other; rather "we are training each other in acts of communication we barely understand" (the manifesto's framing of becoming-with). Haraway insists on "significant otherness": humans and dogs do not collapse into one another but co-shape one another through history, labour, evolution, breed politics, and everyday domestic practice. Domestic dogs are products of millennia of co-evolution with humans; they are not metaphors or symbols for humans. This grounds the multispecies sociology that Carter and Charles call for.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The lecture and the Carter and Charles reading share a single thesis: sociology has been so anxious to defend its disciplinary turf against biology that it amputated half of its empirical world. I think they are right, but the diagnosis matters more than the slogan. Carter and Charles do not just say "let's add animals in" (that would be stage three on the feminism analogy). They argue, in the article's most direct line, that **"the social *is* animal"** (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). The work is reconfiguring concepts, not extending coverage.

Their target is sharp: "the human is usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81, citing Ingold). Sociology inherits this binary from Cartesian humanism, hardens it after Parsons, and then runs into trouble when confronted with horses ("biotechnology… living machines", Greene, 2008: 4), pheasants in shoots, or wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). The Greene quote from the slides drives the point home: "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Sociology stares at the Communist Manifesto and misses the horses dropping "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" in 1900 New York (Greene, 2008: 174).

The Weber excerpt the lecturer cites complicates the simple "classical sociology ignored animals" story. Weber concedes that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). But he immediately retreats: a "verifiable conception" of animals' state of mind is attainable "only within narrow limits" (Weber, 1922: 17). Weber half-opens the door and then closes it on epistemological grounds, and twentieth-century sociology, post-Parsons, slammed it shut. Durkheim's *Rules* leaves a similar window: "animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common" (Durkheim, 1895: 132). Sociability, for Durkheim, is not innately human.

Haraway pushes further. She refuses the "either/or" of human exceptionalism versus posthumanism. Her programmatic line is: "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). Companion species are not "ordinary beings-in-encounter" who already exist before they meet; "species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, 2016: 13). This is what Carter and Charles, in their own register, mean when they say agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90).

The critical question is whether this expanded sociology can still do the political work sociology was meant to do. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: if sociology cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, it is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Haraway makes the same wager: "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29). I think this is right. A sociology that cannot name the horses that built nineteenth-century cities, the pheasant-shoot entanglements of the contemporary countryside, the Amazon clearance for beef production or the palm-oil clearance of orangutan habitat that the lecturer flags on slide 35 is a sociology that has unilaterally disarmed in the face of its century.

## 8. Essay answers

### 2025 (Q2): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically assess this statement using the ideas set out by Carter and Charles in the key reading for this topic.

I will critically assess the claim using Carter and Charles's *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018). The statement is true as a description of how the discipline has constituted itself but false as a description of what the discipline should be, and the gap matters politically, conceptually and ecologically.

Carter and Charles concede the historical accuracy of the statement. Sociology, institutionalising, defined its object as human society alone. Three reasons. First, sociology had a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80), insisting on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton, 1991: 12, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 82) and later distancing itself from biology because of its association with eugenics and scientific racism. Second, sociology was built on "human exceptionalism", the doctrine that "the human species is exempt from natural constraints… and is evolutionarily unique" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Third, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe when "urban dwellers experience themselves as increasingly remote from most other animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The removal of food-animals and horses from early-twentieth-century cities coincided with sociology's professional formation. The discipline took the absence of visible animals for the absence of relevant animals.

The first stage of their critique is that this is empirically false. Greene's horses were "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). To leave horses out is misdescription. Factory farming today extends the point.

The second stage is conceptual. Carter and Charles argue "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). They name this **anthroponormativity**, by direct analogy with heteronormativity and white normativity. Society, agency, social action, intersubjectivity and reflexivity are silently restricted to humans. The vocabulary excludes animals by definition rather than argument. Read alongside Halewood's account of bifurcation as the "master modern concept" (Halewood, 2014: 1), the human/animal cut is one instance of the deeper Cartesian carve-up of nature/culture, fact/value and subject/object. Anthroponormativity is the species-specific shape bifurcation takes inside twentieth-century sociology, and Halewood's lecture-long argument that the discipline still has not metabolised Whitehead's critique gives Carter and Charles's diagnosis its philosophical backbone.

Their alternative reworks agency as "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Animals are agential beings, "enfolded in all sorts of relations with humans (many of which are fatal for them)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The pheasant-shoot example is precise: a shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). To call this human interaction alone is to misperceive the practice.

The third stage is the political stake. Carter and Charles draw the analogy with feminism: pre-feminist, critique, "add women in", then "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Animal studies sits between phases two and three. The claim that "sociology is the study of human societies" is the kind of definitional move feminism and post-colonial sociology dismantled in turn. What is at stake is whether sociology can describe its actual object (industrial capitalism, the post-colonial world, the Anthropocene with its climate change and biodiversity loss) without lopping off half of it. The COVID-19 moment, a zoonotic disease whose social shape ran straight through human-animal contact, makes the point: a discipline that cannot see those entanglements cannot explain the pandemic.

The concluding line is uncompromising: "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). This is not animal advocacy; it is a methodological claim. Carter and Charles reject Latour and the new materialists: sociology need not be abandoned for actor-network theory or post-humanism. The discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87) once its foundational concepts are reworked.

So the statement is an inherited disciplinary self-description, not a discovered truth. It describes where sociology has been, not where it has to go. The boundary between humans and animals is not a methodological given but a political and conceptual choice, one that current ecological pressures give very good reasons to revise. I reject the statement as a definition while accepting it as a description of past practice.

### 2024 (Q2): Why is it important to study animals within sociology?

Sociology emerged as the study of industrial modernity, yet the most important moving parts of that modernity (Greene's horses, the food animals removed from cities, the pollinating insects) were systematically excluded from the sociological imagination. With Carter and Charles, studying animals within sociology is not a niche enrichment but a remedy for a structural blind spot that has made sociology unable to describe the very societies it claims to analyse.

The first reason is empirical. There is, as Bryant pointed out in 1979, "virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 79). Greene's history makes this concrete. "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Industrial capitalism, the object of classical sociology, ran literally on horsepower, on "living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4). The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. London's streets, on Ritvo's account, "were crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden, in addition, passers-by often encountered herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo, 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). A discipline that misses an infrastructure that large has misperceived its object. Factory farming today and the pet food brands that mark the commodified-companion economy show that the human social runs through animal lives at every scale.

The second reason is conceptual. Sociology defines social action, agency, subjectivity, reflexivity and society itself through a tacit "anthroponormativity": "the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences" being "configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). The Weberian inheritance is the key example: meaning-making, empathetic understanding, value-oriented action are framed as exclusively human, even though Weber himself conceded that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). Parsons hardens this into the doctrine that sociality is the realm of human interaction alone. The lecturer's slide 40 is direct: ascribing sociality to animals would "threaten the grounding and specificity of the social." This is backwards: what threatens the specificity of the social is pretending it stops at the species boundary.

The third reason is political. Carter and Charles's feminist analogy: pre-feminist neglect, critique, adding-women-in, then "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Animal studies sits between stages two and three. As feminism did not simply "add women" but reworked the categories of work, family, agency and the body, multispecies sociology reworks labour (horses pulling cabs, pit ponies; Marx's bees as foil), family (pets in Haraway's companion-species sense, and the pet-food industry built around them), and the political (the pheasant-shoot example).

The fourth reason is ecological. Carter and Charles warn that a sociology unable to speak about a multispecies planet is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Climate change and biodiversity loss are general concerns whose social shape runs straight through human-animal relations. Foucault's biopower, the management of populations through "biopolitics" rather than the discipline of individual bodies (Lynch, 2011: 26), is the obvious extension once the species line is opened: factory farming, herd management and breeding regimes are the very statistical techniques Foucault analyses applied beyond the human, and they cannot be described while sociology pretends animal life is biology's problem. COVID-19 was a zoonotic-disease moment; nature documentaries have, for decades, kept ordinary publics aware that the social and natural are not separable. None of this is biological alone; it is social, with animals as agents and victims simultaneously.

Studying animals is also a methodological challenge. Haraway insists "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game" (Haraway, 2016: 12). Kimbo, on slide 28, reframes this: how to think *with* (and for) other animals as opposed to *about* them? PigeonBlog (Beatriz da Costa, 2006), where racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over Los Angeles, shows what such collaboration looks like: the pigeons had to "learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) and the artist-researchers had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22).

To say "the social is animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) is neither anthropomorphism nor flat ontology. It is a recognition that the entities sociology studies (labour, family, city, modernity, capitalism, politics) were always already multispecies. Studying animals does not stretch the discipline; it returns it to its proper object.

### 2023 (Q2): Why should sociology make efforts to study the relations between humans and animals?

Sociology should make these efforts for four interlocking reasons: empirical fidelity, conceptual coherence, ethical-political relevance, and ecological necessity. The argument runs through Carter and Charles (2018) and Haraway's companion species, with examples from Greene's horses, the pheasant shoot, and PigeonBlog.

**Empirical fidelity.** Human-animal relations were always there. Greene: "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Modernity, industrialisation, capitalism (sociology's founding objects) ran on animals. Horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4). They became invisible to sociologists precisely because they were too central to be seen. Making the familiar strange is one of sociology's main tasks. The empirical case extends to insect pollinators, pets and the pet-food industry built around them, and factory-farming systems as the dominant mode of animal-protein production. Carter and Charles describe wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91): protected in some places, shot under licence in others, including by Sami reindeer herders. To leave this out as "biological" is to abandon sociology's promise.

**Conceptual coherence.** Sociology's core concepts wobble without animals. Carter and Charles call this **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Agency, on the Cartesian inheritance, belongs to rational subjects; objects are passive matter. Halewood treats this Cartesian carve-up as the master modern move: "bifurcation" of nature/culture, fact/value, subject/object is "the master modern concept" sociology inherits and rarely names (Halewood, 2014: 1). The human/animal binary is one bifurcation among several, and Carter and Charles's repair operates at the level Halewood's lecture identifies as decisive. The wolves' "agential conditions… are powerfully shaped by their relations to humans (but not only these)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). The wolf's possibilities are real, relational and politically structured. A sociology that misses this cannot describe nature reserves, hunting licences, or attempts to win legal personhood for chimpanzees (lecture slide 31).

**Ethical and political relevance.** Human-animal relations are sites of domination, exploitation and care. Carter and Charles: their "key claim is that animals are agents entangled in relations with humans and that these relations are frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). "Sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages; wolves and pheasants are unaware of the shooting season" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Greenebaum's three-type vegan analysis on slide 34 (health vegans, environmental vegans, ethical vegans) picks out one social movement in which these questions become political identities with class, gender and racial dimensions.

**Ecological necessity.** The Anthropocene leaves no choice. Carter and Charles warn that a sociology that cannot speak about a multispecies planet is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Climate change and biodiversity loss are general concerns whose social shape runs through human-animal relations. COVID-19 was a zoonotic-disease moment forcing this point on every government. Haraway: "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways" (Haraway, 2016: 29). Differential responsibility by ecology, economy and species is a sociological problem.

Haraway's companion species supplies a vocabulary for doing this work without anthropomorphism or flat ontology. Humans "become humans through their relations with animals" (slide 26). "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). PigeonBlog (Beatriz da Costa, 2006) illustrates this: racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over Los Angeles neighbourhoods where "official government air pollution monitoring devices" (Haraway, 2016: 21) were placed away from the working-class areas most affected. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing"; the artist-researchers had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds and the fanciers (Haraway, 2016: 22). Project PigeonWatch teaches city children to re-see despised "rats with wings" as "sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, 2016: 24).

Sociology should make these efforts because animals are everywhere in the social, because sociology's own concepts already require them, because human-animal relations are political relations, and because the planetary crisis demands a discipline capable of multispecies thinking. The alternative is not rigour; it is a refusal to look.

### 2021 (Q18): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically analyse the limitations of this statement.

The statement captures how sociology has historically defined itself but obscures three distinct limitations: historical-empirical, conceptual and normative. Together they amount to a structural failure the discipline has to repair.

**The historical-empirical limitation.** The statement misdescribes the societies sociology has actually studied. Carter and Charles trace the discipline's formation to "modern industrial capitalist societies" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 83). Those societies were emphatically multispecies. "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo, 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The absence of large animals in twentieth-century cities is the result of historical processes sociology mistook for the disappearance of animals from social life. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Carter and Charles: "the contribution of animals and even their centrality to processes of industrialisation have been erased in sociological conceptions of the social" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The statement is a self-fulfilling description of an empirical mistake.

**The conceptual limitation.** The statement entrenches what Carter and Charles call **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). This is the same move earlier critiques exposed under heteronormativity and white normativity: a tacit norm pretending to be a neutral definition. Carter and Charles trace this to Parsons via Halewood (2014), "the first major sociologist to hold a conception of the social that defines it unambiguously as the realm of human interaction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). Yet Weber: "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). Durkheim: "it has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, 1895: 132). The classical thinkers were less anthropocentric than their twentieth-century descendants.

Carter and Charles propose a relational account: agency is "an emergent product of the relations within which all things are placed" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). The wolf example shows agency is spatial and political: "in Sweden, wolves are protected but in the north, where the Sami live, they can be killed because they might threaten the Sami's herds of reindeer" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). The pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The "human societies" formula gives no vocabulary for these realities except dismissal.

**The normative limitation.** The third limitation is ethical and ecological. The definition lets sociology evade the politics of human-animal relations, "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Factory farming, intensive agriculture, the spectacle of biodiversity loss made visible in nature documentaries, the climate-change pressure on multispecies ecologies: none of these can be properly described by a discipline that has defined them out of scope. Foucault's account of biopower as the "macro-level of populations" managed through statistical knowledge and aggregate intervention (Lynch, 2011: 26) extends directly to factory-farm cohorts, breeding regimes and herd-management policy; if biopower describes governance of human populations through hygiene and nutrition, it describes a fortiori the governance of animal populations through veterinary regimes and slaughter quotas. Haraway refuses both human exceptionalism and flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11). Companion species, becoming-with, response-ability are built precisely to do work the formula prevents.

One defence is worth considering. Some sociologists (slide 31 lists Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard) argue language and symbolism mark a categorical difference. The reply is two-part. Empirically, the difference does not survive scrutiny: Carter and Charles cite de Waal that "animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales… have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Haraway notes pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, 2016: 18). Methodologically, the argument is circular: define the social as symbolic, the symbolic as human, conclude the social is human. Anthroponormativity does the work empirical investigation should.

The three limitations connect. The historical-empirical gives an inaccurate picture of past societies. The conceptual produces analytic tools that cannot grasp present societies. The normative leaves a discipline ill-equipped for multispecies questions. I conclude, with Carter and Charles, that "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93), and the definition we should defend is closer to "sociology is the study of multispecies relations through which human-and-animal worlds are made."

### M1: "The social *is* animal." Discuss.

The line is Carter and Charles's (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93), the most compressed statement of a sociological revolution the discipline has not yet absorbed. I unpack what it does not say, defend what it does say, and test it on Greene's horses and Haraway's PigeonBlog.

The claim is not anthropomorphism: Carter and Charles are not saying chickens hold opinions. Nor is it flat ontology; they reject the Latourian move to treat humans, animals and machines as symmetrical "actants" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87). It says that the categories sociology uses (society, agency, labour, family, modernity) were always already multispecies in their referents, and pretending otherwise is anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), the definitional sleight that once made heteronormativity feel like a neutral default.

The positive content sits in reworked agency. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Animals are agents because they have purposes engaging relational conditions. The pheasant-shoot example crystallises this: a UK shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Pheasants are not props; they are "bred in their hundreds of thousands so that they can be shot" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), and their breeding cycles and flight capacities co-constitute the practice alongside the dogs who beat or pick up.

The empirical test is Greene's challenge to Marxism. Industrial modernity ran on horses ("Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically", Greene, 2008: 170), yet the Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Marx's productive labour and metabolism with nature ran on the "living machines" he never wrote about (Greene, 2008: 4). Greene insists horses "stimulated the development of an array of technologies and devices: vehicles, equestrian equipment, breeders' logs; and horse power reinforced other sorts of power, military, political, religious, sexual" (Greene, 2008: 5, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The slogan recovers what classical sociology amputated.

The wolves example sharpens the politics. "The agency of wolves… changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91): in Sweden protected, in the north where the Sami live killable because they threaten reindeer herds, in Norway outside EU legislation. A wolf is not the kind of being a human-only sociology can describe in its political register. "The social *is* animal" is the only formula that holds these things together.

Haraway refuses the bad alternatives. "Not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11) keeps species specificity while refusing exceptionalism. Companion species "are relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13); the partners "do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13). The continuity with Haraway's earlier "Situated Knowledges" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is direct: refusing "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" is what allows her to refuse human-exceptionalist vantage too, since the human-only gaze is itself one of the unmarked god-tricks Carter and Charles's anthroponormativity diagnoses. PigeonBlog (Beatriz da Costa, 2006) shows the social as animal in micro: racing pigeons, working-class fanciers, art-activists and DIY sensors co-produced air-pollution data over Los Angeles neighbourhoods that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" (Haraway, 2016: 21) could not reach. Despret puts the becoming-with: "without the lover of pigeons (pigeon fancier), without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25).

The recurring objection is that extending the social to animals dissolves sociology into biology. Carter and Charles answer: reform, not abandon; the discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87) once its categories are reworked. The social/biological boundary is exactly what the slogan calls into question. Defending it as fixed is defending a disciplinary self-description against a discoverable fact. The Anthropocene's pressures (climate change, biodiversity loss, factory farming and intensive agriculture, the COVID-19 zoonotic moment) make the cost of refusal palpable.

So I take "the social *is* animal" as a programme rather than slogan: rework society, agency and social action to take animals seriously as constitutive, then use the resulting vocabulary on horsepower, the pheasant shoot, the EU wolf, and PigeonBlog. Carter and Charles supply the diagnostic; Haraway supplies the method.

### M2: What does Haraway mean by companion species, and why does she treat it as ontologically prior to "human" or "animal"?

Haraway means by companion species not a list of cute domesticated animals but a relational ontology in which partners come into being through their entanglement rather than meeting as pre-formed kinds. She insists on this priority because the alternative (starting from "human" and "animal" as given categories and asking how they relate) smuggles human exceptionalism back under the cover of taxonomy. I will explain the concept and defend the priority claim.

The clearest articulation is Haraway's string-figure metaphor. "Companion species play string figure games where who is/are to be in/of the world is constituted in intra- and interaction. The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, 2016: 13). In a cat's cradle there is no string-pattern prior to the hands that pass it; the figure is the knotting. Likewise, there are no "humans" and "animals" prior to the histories of co-domestication, co-evolution, labour, breeding and affection that make them what they are. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13).

The constructive term is becoming-with; its operational sister is rendering-capable, from Despret: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). The compressed statement is Despret's, on Matali Crasset's 2003 pigeon loft at Caudry: "without the lover of pigeons (pigeon fancier), without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25). The homing pigeon does not pre-exist the practice that produced it; neither does the fancier.

Why is this ontologically prior to "human" and "animal"? Three reasons.

First, the empirical record cuts against pre-given kinds. Domestic pigeons "(Columba livia domestica) emerged from birds native to western and southern Europe, North Africa, and western and southern Asia" through "becoming-with people for several thousand years" (Haraway, 2016: 15). The *Manifesto* (2003) makes the same case for dogs: humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand." The lecturer's slide 26 is in the same register: humans "become humans through their relations with animals." Similar arguments apply to beings Carter and Charles cite from de Waal as having "cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88).

Second, treating "human" and "animal" as prior is what generates human exceptionalism in the first place. The Cartesian inheritance ("the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive", Carter and Charles, 2018: 89) depends on already having a "human" and an "animal" to assign properties to. Haraway dissolves the problem at root: entities are constituted through the practices producing their properties. Hence "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11): posthumanism still names a "human" to be overcome.

Third, the priority claim does political work. PigeonBlog is unintelligible on a kinds-first ontology. The racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18); the artist-researchers had "to render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22); the fanciers, "frankly afraid of animal rights people and tactics" (Haraway, 2016: 24), had to be persuaded. None of these competences existed in advance. Pigeons became air-quality scientists; humans became multispecies citizen-scientists; both produced data that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" could not generate (Haraway, 2016: 21).

Does the priority claim flatten species differences? No. Haraway insists on "significant otherness" (the *Manifesto* subtitle) and graduated response-ability: "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29). The priority of relation does not erase the specificity of relata. Her companion-species ontology is continuous with the "Situated Knowledges" position she articulated earlier: knowledge is "from somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196), and the somewheres include other species. Refusing "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is what allows entanglement to count as a producer of kinds.

A second objection comes from Carter and Charles, who want a realist commitment to structures and emergent properties that Haraway's playful idiom risks losing (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87). The right reading is complementary: Carter and Charles supply disciplinary repair; Haraway supplies the relational ontology that explains why repair is needed.

So companion species is the ontological claim that kinds are consequent upon entanglement: relation prior to relata. Without it, multispecies sociology slides back into "humans plus animals", the anthroponormativity Carter and Charles name.

### M3: Horsepower, industrialisation and the limits of Marxism.

The horse is not a footnote to Marxism but a stress test for it: Marx's productive labour, his metabolism with nature, and his political economy of industrial capitalism cannot be told without horses, and the fact that he tells them without horses exposes the anthroponormativity at the heart of his apparatus.

Begin with empirical scale. "Horses jammed the streets… They hauled streetcars, omnibuses, drays, delivery wagons, and private vehicles. Horses delivered raw materials to factories and trucked away the finished products… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo, 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). The slide 22 list (war-horse, race-horse, plough horse, pit ponies) reinforces the scale.

Now the limit. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Marx theorises productive labour through the human worker selling labour-power to capital, with non-human nature appearing only as raw material. The bee turns up in *Capital* only to be denied the architect's intentionality, the foil that makes human productive labour distinctive. Greene cuts through: horses are "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The horse is neither raw material nor human worker. It is a labouring being whose biological rhythms, breeding histories and species-specific capacities co-shaped what industrial production could be. Horses needed rest, hay, stabling and care; their working lives shaped urban infrastructure (stables, smithies, knackers' yards) and urban temporality. A purely human labour theory of value cannot price this in.

Greene's wider claim sharpens the test. Horses "stimulated the development of an array of technologies and devices: vehicles, equestrian equipment, breeders' logs; and horse power reinforced other sorts of power, military, political, religious, sexual" (Greene, 2008: 5, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The "technological network that horses represent" is precisely what human-only Marxism cannot describe. Carter and Charles add that this extends to "the whole colonial project" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84): horses were imperial agents too.

The conceptual repair is Carter and Charles's relational agency. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). The horse is an agent in industrial modernity because its purposes engage agential conditions (capitalist demand for traction, urban density, omnibus rhythms, feed costs) to produce outcomes humans alone could not. The erasure is anthroponormative (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), and Halewood's diagnosis sharpens the point: Marx is one inheritor of the Cartesian "bifurcation" of nature and culture that Halewood identifies as the master modern move (Halewood, 2014: 1), and Marxist political economy reproduces that bifurcation by treating animal labour as nature rather than as social.

What survives of Marxism? More than the title suggests. Capital accumulation, exploitation, class struggle and crisis remain powerful, extended to multispecies labour processes. The factory-farming complex and intensive agriculture are obvious twenty-first-century cases where Marxist categories (surplus extraction, the reserve army of labour, the metabolic rift) want extending to animal lives. The wolves example, with its jurisdictional layering (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91), shows political economy structuring animal lives. The pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), with breeding regimes, gun rooms and beaters' wages calibrated to pheasants as the agents around which the practice is organised.

Haraway's contribution is becoming-with. Horse and human are not separate kinds plugging into industrial production; they are companion species whose joint capacities were produced through millennia of domestication, breeding and labour. The carthorse pulling a brewer's dray is not a "natural" entity meeting an "industrial" entity; it is co-produced inside an entanglement that is social-and-natural together. "The partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13) describes the historical record more accurately than the Manifesto's human-only protagonists.

The limits of Marxism are the limits of its anthroponormative inheritance, showing up exactly where industrial modernity is most obviously multispecies. Marxism is not refuted by horses; it is forced to grow. A multispecies political economy keeps what is best in Marx (exploitation, accumulation, crisis) while losing a contingent disciplinary blinker. Greene's horses are the test case the discipline still has to pass.

### M4: How convincing do you find Carter and Charles's diagnosis of anthroponormativity in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*?

I find the diagnosis very convincing as a diagnostic move and persuasive but incomplete as a programme for repair. Its strength is that it names a structural exclusion sociology has treated as methodological commonplace; its weakness is that it does most of its work negatively. On balance, highly successful.

Anthroponormativity is defined explicitly: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). The analogies do real work. Heteronormativity is not the claim some people are heterosexual; it is the claim family, intimacy and respectability are silently configured around heterosexual couples in ways that exclude queer lives by definition. White normativity does the same around whiteness, and the L8 lecture's archive of scientific racism (Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* dividing Homo sapiens into four sub-species, 1735; Blumenbach's five-race skull typology) shows the human/animal hierarchy was racialised from the start: the orangutan-and-Negro frontispiece Gilroy reads (Gilroy, 1998: 254) is exactly the modernity-coloniality-animal nexus the diagnosis names. Anthroponormativity configures society, agency, social action and reflexivity around humans, with exclusion of animals operating as a definitional move rather than an empirical finding.

The diagnostic power is in calling the bluff. The obvious counter ("sociology is the study of humans, so of course it excludes animals") treats a contingent disciplinary self-description as a discovered necessity. There is no neutral place from which "the social = the human" is just true. It is a norm, and norms are contestable: the same move feminist sociology made against "sociology is the study of public, paid, male life" and post-colonial sociology made against "sociology is the study of Western modernity."

Once the diagnosis is in hand, the conceptual repair becomes legible. Agency is not a Cartesian property ("the human subject is active... while the non-human object is passive", Carter and Charles, 2018: 89) but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Wolves whose agency "changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91) are agents; pheasants in "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92) are agents; "sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), but they are agents whose conditions thwart their purposes.

Three limits, none fatal.

First, the concept does not tell you how to do multispecies research. The methodological vocabulary (becoming-with, rendering-capable, response-ability) comes from Haraway and Despret. Carter and Charles diagnose; Haraway demonstrates through PigeonBlog. Kimbo on her dog Harald (slide 29) shows the method-level work the diagnosis alone does not do.

Second, the concept is in places under-theorised. It is unclear how anthroponormativity intersects with heteronormativity and white normativity in concrete sites, even though Carter and Charles flag the link (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88).

Third, institutional reception has been slow. On the four-stage feminist analogy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80) animal studies is still between stages two and three. This is partly a problem of the diagnosis (animals cannot enter the academy as graduate students the way women could) and partly a measure of how deep anthroponormativity runs.

Despite these limits the diagnosis is convincing. Empirically, the cases it organises (Greene's horses, the pheasant shoot, EU wolves, factory farming) are exactly the cases mainstream sociology has neglected, and the concept explains the pattern rather than treating each as isolated oversight. Conceptually, it locates the problem at the right level: the categories, not the data. Politically, it gives critical animal studies a name to fight under, as "heteronormativity" once gave queer theory a name.

The line I find most persuasive is the closing one: "ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). The single move opens the discipline up. I find the diagnosis highly convincing, enough to make the rest of the work intelligible.

### M5: Critically assess Carter and Charles's argument with reference to a contemporary case of human-animal entanglement (pheasant shoots, wolves under EU jurisdiction, or industrial food production).

Carter and Charles argue that sociology has misperceived its object by silently restricting "the social" to the human, and that the empirical, conceptual and political costs of this are now too great to defend. I test the argument against the pheasant shoot, which they themselves develop in the article's closing pages.

Their first move: "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 79). Their second: sociology's neglect has historical causes, a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80), urban-industrial provincialism, human exceptionalism. Their third: the vocabulary is anthroponormative (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Their fourth: repair is relational realism where agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), and animals are "occupants of social positions" (Benton, 1993: 68). The slogan: "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93).

A British pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The pheasants are "an introduced species" who are "bred in their hundreds of thousands so that they can be shot" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Alongside them are dogs and handlers, "either beating (flushing out the pheasants) or picking up (retrieving them once they have been shot and 'despatching'… those that are not dead)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92).

The empirical move holds. Pheasants are not background; they are the agents around whom the practice is organised. Their breeding regimes, release cycles and flight capacities co-constitute the shoot. Foucault's biopower, the "macro-level of populations" governed through statistical knowledge and aggregate intervention (Lynch, 2011: 26), is the natural genealogy: rearing hundreds of thousands of pheasants for autumn release applies the same population-level rationality Foucault traces in public health to the species line itself, turning the shoot into multispecies biopolitics. Dogs are not tools; "as actors, we are not reducible to our agential entanglements" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), and their trained capacities co-constitute the practice. The claim that animals are "occupants of social positions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86) is here a literal description.

The conceptual move holds, but only with the revised agency concept. Cartesian agency cannot describe a pheasant whose entire life cycle is structured by breeding for shoot. Relational agency can: the pheasant has purposes (eat, fly, mate, evade predators) engaging conditions (release pen, beating line, gun line, season). Carter and Charles: "wolves and pheasants are unaware of the shooting season" and "their choices, when they are exercised, are already circumscribed (the caged chicken cannot walk freely and the pheasant cannot fly safely within range of armed humans)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Agency recognised in being denied lets sociology describe the system rather than deplore it. Factory farming and intensive agriculture extend the same logic.

The political move holds with sharpening. Animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The pheasant shoot is a class practice ("power, status, gender, technologies, notions of 'a sporting life', the 'place of animals'", 2018: 92), a gendered practice, and a multispecies practice in which pheasants are bred in industrial quantities to be killed. None of this is describable inside a human-only sociology except as scattered specialisms; Carter and Charles's framework holds the pieces together.

Where the argument needs supplementation is at the methodological-affective level. The structural-realist idiom is right for system analysis but thin on what it is like to watch a pheasant break cover. Haraway's becoming-with supplies the missing register. PigeonBlog shows partners "rendered capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22), and Despret's pigeon-fancier line ("without the lover of pigeons… without the transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs", Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25) gives a vocabulary for the practices that make pheasants what they are.

A residual worry is normative reach. Carter and Charles state that "this is not animal advocacy" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). But the analytic recognition that the shoot is a structured practice of domination over agential beings makes normative judgement nearly unavoidable. This is a strength: ethical questions follow from the sociology rather than being imposed.

The argument holds up well under the test. It explains the phenomenon, supplies the vocabulary and articulates the politics. Where it needs help it gets it from Haraway. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) is the most economical description of what the pheasant shoot actually is.

### M6: Why does the lecturer "blame Talcott Parsons" for the disappearance of animals from twentieth-century sociology? Answer with reference to Carter and Charles and to the Durkheim and Weber excerpts.

The lecturer's slide 40 is direct: "I blame Talcott Parsons!" The charge is justified: Carter and Charles back it up with a textually careful account of how Parsons consolidated the human-only social, and the Durkheim and Weber excerpts show how much narrower Parsons made things than the classical thinkers had been.

Begin with the lecturer's framing. On slide 40, "for most of the 20th century, sociologists (influenced by Parsons) seem to assume and insist that whatever sociality is, it is a production of humans, groups of humans or systems of humans. Animals were rarely, if ever, considered. To ascribe genuine sociality to animals would seem to threaten the grounding and specificity of the social, which should, it is claimed, be restricted to the human realm alone." Parsons hardened sociology around a definitional move: the social is human.

Carter and Charles, citing Halewood (2014), make the same case more carefully. "Halewood has recently made a plausible case for seeing Parsons as the key figure in attaining the first objective" of "assembling a stable object of sociology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). "For Halewood, Parsons is the first major sociologist to hold a conception of the social that defines it unambiguously as the realm of human interaction; after Parsons, the question of what constitutes the social rarely surfaces. Moreover, this is a notion of the social from which the non-human (other animals, technologies, things) has been expelled" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). By giving sociology a stable object, Parsons excluded animals from its conceptual reach by definition.

By the early 1930s, sociology had "identified the features which radically distinguished it from the objects studied by the natural sciences. Principally, these were features held to be characteristic of people as human beings, features that allegedly marked them out from other animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). Their core was "claims about consciousness, subjectivity and reflexivity" and the belief that language and culture were possible only because human beings were "self-reflexively aware" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). This is the Parsonian moment: the door closes.

The Durkheim and Weber excerpts show the classical thinkers had left it ajar. Durkheim, in the *Rules*: "it has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, 1895: 132, slide 38), and "it is an observable fact that animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common" (Durkheim, 1895: 132). Sociability is a product of social life, not a species trait. The category is capable of being predicated of non-human animals when their conditions warrant.

Weber goes further. On slide 39: "many animals 'understand' [verstehen] commands, anger, love, hostility and react to them in ways which are evidently often by no means purely instinctive and mechanical and in some sense both consciously meaningful [sinnhaft] and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). He lets animals into the territory of meaningful action, then narrows: "a verifiable conception of the state of mind of these social animals… accessible to meaningful understanding… would seem to be attainable even as an ideal goal only within narrow limits" (Weber, 1922: 17). Weber half-opens the door on ontological grounds and closes it on epistemological grounds.

Parsons collapses the Weberian half-open door into a definitional closure. Methodological caution about access becomes categorical exclusion. The Durkheimian recognition that sociability is environmental becomes Parsonian insistence that sociality is human. Classical complexity is replaced by a tidy disciplinary boundary. Foucault's account of disciplinary power (Foucault, 1977) lets us name what Parsons was doing as a disciplinary move in the strict sense: producing a "stable object" through partitioning and classification, the same techniques Foucault traces in the prison and the clinic. Parsonian sociology disciplines itself by expelling the non-human, with anthroponormativity as the resulting norm of professional competence.

The charge is well-founded. Carter and Charles's positive argument: the price was too high. Once sociology had its object, it lost the conceptual flexibility to describe horses in nineteenth-century cities (Greene, 2008), wolves under EU law (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91), pheasants on shoots (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), or the factory-farming entanglements that dominate contemporary food systems. Anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88) is the conceptual habit Parsons installed. COVID-19 as a zoonotic moment shows how unworkable a human-only social has become.

So the lecturer's joke is a serious historical claim. Parsons did not start the human-only social, but he made it doctrinal. The Durkheim and Weber excerpts show how much was lost. Carter and Charles's diagnosis is the bill come due.

### M7: Is animal studies the next step in sociology or a category mistake?

I argue animal studies is the next step in sociology, modelled on Carter and Charles's account on the analogous step feminism took, and that the category-mistake objection rests on a circular definition. The field's work is to move from stage two to stage four of the feminist trajectory; the objection defends a definitional move that has not survived analogous challenges.

The category-mistake objection: sociology is the study of human societies; animals are not human; therefore animal studies misclassifies a biological topic as a sociological one. It is the silent assumption of introductory textbooks.

Carter and Charles's reply: the major premise is a contingent disciplinary self-description, not a discovered fact, and calling it a category boundary smuggles a normative move into a pretended definition. "Much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). This is anthroponormativity, analogous to heteronormativity and white normativity. The category-mistake objection is not a category judgement; it is anthroponormativity dressed up as one.

The "next step" framing is Carter and Charles's. The four-stage feminist trajectory: pre-feminist neglect; critique; adding women in; "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Animal studies "is experiencing the second and third stages" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The critique is made; empirical work proceeds (multispecies ethnography, the pheasant shoot, Greenebaum's vegan typology, Kimbo's PhD on *Sociology, Sociality and Animals*). What remains is conceptual integration: reworking agency, society, labour and modernity around the recognition that the social is multispecies. The post-colonial parallel sharpens the point: Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* and Blumenbach's skull typology classified humans on the same scientific scaffolding that ranked species (Linnaeus, 1735; Blumenbach in slide 8 of L8), and Gilroy's reading of the orangutan-and-Negro frontispiece (Gilroy, 1998: 254) shows the human/animal hierarchy was always racialised. Decolonising the discipline and de-anthroponormativising it are continuous projects.

The empirical case is strong. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) is supported by work on horsepower (Greene, 2008), wolf jurisdictional politics (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91), the British pheasant shoot (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), PigeonBlog (Haraway, 2016) and Project PigeonWatch (Haraway, 2016: 24). Pets and the commodified-companion economy of pet food brands, factory farming, intensive agriculture, the climate change and biodiversity loss pressures of the Anthropocene, the COVID-19 zoonotic-disease moment: a discipline that cannot describe these is misperceiving its object.

The conceptual case is stronger. Carter and Charles's relational agency ("an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action", Carter and Charles, 2018: 90) is a sociological move, not a biological one. The pheasant shoot as "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92) is a sociological description. PigeonBlog: pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) in a project that produced data official monitors could not (Haraway, 2016: 21). Neither collapses sociology into biology.

Two sophisticated forms of the objection. First, the symbolic-interactionist line: humans alone possess reflexive symbol-use, so the social as symbolic order is properly human. Slide 31 names the constituency: Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure, Baudrillard. The reply is partly empirical: Carter and Charles cite de Waal that "chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88); Haraway notes pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, 2016: 18). And partly conceptual: even if human reflexive symbol-use is structurally distinctive, that does not entail animals lack any agency or culture; the slide from "different in structure" to "not really sociological" is anthroponormative. Second, the disciplinary-identity worry. Carter and Charles want sociology reformed, not replaced (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87), rejecting Latourian symmetry as overcorrection.

The analogy with feminism is sharper as diagnosis than as prediction. Women entered the academy and spoke for themselves; animals cannot. The methodological vocabulary of multispecies research had to be invented (Despret, Haraway, Kimbo). The drivers of stage-three-to-stage-four progress will be ecological and political (the Anthropocene framing, 2018: 81), not demographic.

So animal studies is the next step, on the trajectory gender, race and post-coloniality took. The category-mistake charge is the contemporary version of charges once made against women's studies. Each relied on a definitional move that did not survive scrutiny; anthroponormativity is the name of the move when the boundary is species. The work has begun.

### M8: Compare Haraway's companion species with Carter and Charles's "constitutive of society" framing.

The two framings are complementary moves toward the same multispecies sociology, operating at different registers: Haraway supplies a relational ontology, Carter and Charles supply structural-realist disciplinary reform. The most useful sociology uses both.

Carter and Charles's framing is the claim that sociology requires "a reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Constitutive does precise work: not "animals plus humans equals society" (which still treats each as pre-given) nor "animals replace humans as sociology's object" (category-swap not category-critique). Society as a structure of social positions includes animals as occupants: "non-human animals" can be "occupants of social positions" (Benton, 1993: 68, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). The pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92).

Their agency repair underwrites this. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Animals have purposes engaging relational conditions; this makes them agents without requiring Cartesian subjecthood. The wolf example shows agency is jurisdictional: "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91).

Haraway's companion species is a different move. "The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, 2016: 13). The claim is ontological: the kinds "human" and "animal" are not given prior to the practices that produce them. Becoming-with is the operational vocabulary: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). The illustration: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25). The continuity with her L10 "Situated Knowledges" essay is unbroken: refusing "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is what makes the priority of relation thinkable, since every standpoint is partial and embodied, and species are one axis along which the partial vantage gets made.

Compare on three axes. First, register: Carter and Charles operate at the disciplinary level (sociology's vocabulary); Haraway at the ontological level (kinds-first metaphysics). Carter and Charles want reform; Haraway is closer to displacement. Second, scale: Carter and Charles's framework is built for structural analysis (Greene's horses, EU wolf policy, the pheasant shoot, factory farming and intensive agriculture as system-level practices); Haraway's for situated micro-analysis (PigeonBlog, dog-human training in the *Manifesto*, PigeonWatch's DC schoolchildren learning to see pigeons as "sociable birds with lives and deaths", Haraway, 2016: 24). Out of scale each loses traction. Third, normative orientation: Carter and Charles state they are not engaged in animal advocacy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93); Haraway articulates response-ability as graduated obligation across "ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29), wary of some animal-rights framings ("not a prolife project… in the chilling American sense of that term", Haraway, 2016: 28).

Complementarity is straightforward. Carter and Charles tell us animals are inside the social and rework sociology to describe them; Haraway tells us the kinds we work with are themselves produced through the entanglements we describe. Without Carter and Charles, Haraway risks slipping outside sociology. Without Haraway, Carter and Charles's structural realism risks reifying "humans" and "animals" as the kinds they want to relate.

Consider PigeonBlog. Carter and Charles would describe it as multispecies practice in which racing pigeons are occupants of social positions co-constituted by working-class fanciers, art-activist Beatriz da Costa, and the city's pollution geography. Haraway describes how the pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) and how partners were "rendered capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22): the texture the structural account leaves undescribed. Consider Greene's horses. Carter and Charles describe the system: the horse as "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4) inside industrial capitalism's "prime mover" role (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). Haraway describes what is foreclosed when the horse is industrialised: the becoming-with domestication produced, the response-ability carthorse and driver owe each other.

The two framings are not rivals. They are layers: disciplinary repair from Carter and Charles, relational ontology from Haraway. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) and "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13) say the same thing from different angles. Read one without the other and the sociology is partial.

### M9: Can sociology decentre the human without dissolving its disciplinary identity?

Yes, and Carter and Charles's article demonstrates that the decentring can be done from inside the discipline rather than at its expense. The worry is that taking animals seriously will collapse sociology into biology, Latourian flat ontology, or philosophical post-humanism. All three slides are avoidable if we rework concepts rather than abandon them, and the resulting discipline is more, not less, recognisably sociological.

The worry is reasonable. Sociology was built partly through defence against biology. The discipline insisted on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton, 1991: 12, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 82) and later distanced itself from biology because of its association with eugenics and scientific racism (Carter and Charles, 2018: 83). Durkheim's struggle against Spencer, Weber's against social Darwinism, mid-twentieth-century opposition to behaviourism: all were boundary-defences.

The reply has three parts.

First, Carter and Charles do not advocate dissolution. They reject Latour and the new materialists, who treat humans, animals and machines as symmetrical "actants". Sociology is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87) once its concepts are reworked. Reform, not replacement. Their relational realism keeps emergent structures, social positions and causal explanation; it loses only the silent species-norm.

Second, the decentring runs through the existing sociological agenda. Every standard object becomes more intelligible once animals are admitted. Greene's horses make industrialisation legible at the level Marx missed; the pheasant shoot makes class and leisure legible as "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92); wolves under EU law make conservation and indigenous livelihood legible as multispecies political economy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91); PigeonBlog makes environmental justice legible as multispecies citizen science (Haraway, 2016: 21). Factory farming and intensive agriculture are sociological objects of obvious importance that a human-only discipline cannot fully describe.

Third, the analogy with previous decentrings shows the worry was always overstated. Feminist sociology decentred the male subject without dissolving sociology; post-colonial sociology decentred the Western subject; queer theory the heterosexual subject. The worry ("expand the subject, the discipline collapses") has each time been an artefact of the previously narrow definition. Animal studies is between stages two and three of the feminist trajectory (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80); stage four does not dissolve sociology any more than feminist stage four did. Halewood's recovery of bifurcation as the master modern problem (Halewood, 2014: 1) reframes the worry: what is being decentred is not "the human" but a specific Cartesian carve-up of subject and object that sociology inherited and which Whitehead long ago indicated could be dropped without losing the discipline.

Two cases sharpen the answer. The pheasant shoot: a human-only sociology describes a country sport. A multispecies sociology describes a single practice where breeding regimes, beating dogs, gun lines, gendered etiquette and the pheasants' bred-for-shoot life cycles form an interlocking whole. More sociological, not less. Companion-animal relations: a human-only sociology describes pet-keeping as a leisure or symbolic practice (slide 13 names this the human-have-culture-animals-do-not premise). A multispecies sociology, adding becoming-with, describes a relational entanglement in which dog and human are rendered capable of each other. Kimbo's slide 29 applies: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless."

A residual methodological question. Animals do not fill out questionnaires; sociology's standard repertoire is built around interviews and quantitative analysis. Multispecies methods are being built: Despret's ethology, Kimbo's "thinking with" (slide 28), Haraway's situated multispecies ethnography. None dissolves sociological method; they extend it.

Disciplinary identity as institution: the worry is that admitting animals will make sociology indistinguishable from anthropology or environmental studies. The reply: neighbouring disciplines already do multispecies work, and the traffic strengthens sociology when it contributes its distinctive resources (structural analysis, political economy, relational realism). Climate change and biodiversity loss are framing problems no single discipline owns; sociology's contribution is the political economy of how human-animal relations are organised.

Carter and Charles's closing line names the wager. "Ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). Change, not dissolve. Sociology is what survives the change; what falls away is anthroponormativity, not the discipline.

### M10: "Animals can communicate, but they don't obviously have human language." Discuss with reference to at least two readings.

The quotation, from slide 31 on "retaining an element of human exceptionalism", is a useful starting point for an argument that the language line is not where the action is in multispecies sociology. With reference to Carter and Charles and Haraway, the distinction between communication and human language is real but does not justify excluding animals from sociology, because language is not the basis on which either includes animals in the social.

The quotation acknowledges what the empirical record shows. Carter and Charles, citing de Waal, note that "animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Haraway documents pigeon capacities. Pigeons "pass mirror tests" and "share this capacity with, at least, human children over two years old, rhesus macaques, chimpanzees, magpies, dolphins, and elephants" (Haraway, 2016: 18). Animal communication is structured differently from human language, not unstructured. Television nature documentaries have, for decades, shown ordinary publics that this is so.

The quotation retains the claim that human language has structural features (recursion, productivity, displacement, full syntax) that animal systems do not match. By current evidence this is mostly correct. The question is what follows.

The symbolic-interactionist tradition (slide 31: Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure, Baudrillard) takes language-distinctiveness as a basis for excluding animals: meaningful social action requires reflexive symbol-use only humans possess. Carter and Charles flag Mead's foundational role: "Alger and Alger argue that the centrality of Mead to the sociological tradition and his assumption that other animals are incapable of symbolic thought explains sociology's lack of attention to animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81).

Carter and Charles's reply is conceptual. The slide from "humans have distinctive language" to "therefore the social is properly human" is anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). It is a definitional configuration pretending to be an inference.

Their positive move redefines agency in non-linguistic terms. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Linguistic competence is not the criterion; relational engagement is. A wolf under EU jurisdiction is an agent (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91); a pheasant in a shoot is an agent (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92); a chicken in a battery cage on a factory farm is an agent whose conditions thwart its purposes. None of this requires the animal to be a language-user.

Animal culture is the related case. The de Waal claim ("animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales… have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations", Carter and Charles, 2018: 88) shows that if culture means inter-generationally transmitted group-specific learned practice, animals have culture without human language. The lecturer presses the right question on slide 32: "Why do we want to keep culture to ourselves?"

Haraway deflates the language question by changing the level of analysis. Becoming-with is constituted through embodied co-attunement that exceeds linguistic communication. PigeonBlog's racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18); the artist-researchers had "to render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22). The competences are practical and embodied, and Berger's L10 *Ways of Seeing* opening line ("Seeing comes before words", Berger, 1972: 7) underwrites the point: pre-linguistic embodied attunement is a real register of social life, with animals as much as with humans. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7) is the multispecies sociology's epistemological starting line.

Could the language line be defended on weaker grounds ("human social practice is distinctive enough to merit its own discipline")? More defensible but less interesting. Sociology can recognise human linguistic distinctiveness and still include animals as relational agents. The objection that extending sociology dilutes "social action" misfires: the relational view distinguishes agents (beings with purposes engaging conditions) from non-agents. The chicken qualifies; the rock does not.

So the quotation is true but undercuts itself. Animals communicate without human language; Carter and Charles and Haraway both build multispecies sociology on grounds that do not require animals to share human language. The language line is worth preserving as a fact; it is not a justification for anthroponormative exclusion. The lecturer's slide 31 makes the point: animals "can communicate (though they don't obviously have human language)" and "animals have culture." That sentence ends the case for language-based exclusion.

### M11: Using the work of at least two authors, assess whether animal welfare and animal rights demand the same sociological framework, given Haraway's wariness of certain "prolife" framings (Haraway, 2016: 28).

Drawing on Carter and Charles and Haraway, animal welfare and animal rights do not demand the same political framework, but both fit inside a multispecies sociology whose categories accommodate them while preserving the political distinctness of each. Sociology can describe both without endorsing either.

Begin with the distinction. Animal welfare is the position that humans may use animals (for food, labour, research, companionship) but must minimise suffering. Animal rights is the position that animals have interests grounding duties humans owe them, including duties to prohibit certain uses entirely. Slide 31 flags the live case: "a New York lawyer went to court to try to get two chimpanzees awarded 'personhood'." Welfare accepts use if reformed; rights denies the use in principle.

These are not the same problem with different reform programmes. Welfare treats animals as objects-with-interests; rights as subjects-with-claims. Welfare works through state and corporate regulation; rights through movement advocacy and litigation. A sociology that conflates them misperceives the political field.

But both projects presume animals are agents with morally relevant interests, and Carter and Charles's relational realism lets sociology describe both. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). A battery hen on a factory farm has agency-thwarted-by-conditions; a pheasant in a shoot has agency entangled in a class-and-species practice (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92); a wolf has agency that shifts across the EU/Sweden/Sami/Norway boundary (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). Welfare works on conditions inside the use; rights on the use itself. Both are intelligible as politics of agential conditions. Foucault's biopower, "the macro-level of populations" managed through statistical knowledge (Lynch, 2011: 26), names the techniques both interventions contest: welfare reforms population-level conditions, rights litigates against the population-level use itself.

The shared stake: animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The sociology can describe strategic choices, institutional partnerships, and cultural dynamics including the pet food brands and commodified-companion economy. Greenebaum's three-type analysis (slide 34: health, environmental, ethical vegans) maps onto these distinctions. Health vegans sit closer to welfare logics; environmental vegans foreground factory farming and intensive agriculture as drivers of climate change and biodiversity loss; ethical vegans land closer to rights.

Haraway adds two moves. First, she is explicitly wary of certain rights framings. Writing about the Batman Park pigeon loft in Melbourne: "This pigeon loft is not a prolife project; in my view, no serious animal-human becoming-with can be a prolife project in the chilling American sense of that term" (Haraway, 2016: 28). The Batman Park scheme replaces pigeon eggs with artificial ones, controlling feral populations while inviting them to roost. It is multispecies management framed by "the response-ability of staying with the trouble. Response-ability is about both absence and presence, killing and nurturing, living and dying" (Haraway, 2016: 28). This is neither welfare regulation nor rights advocacy; it is graduated ecological politics. "We are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29).

Second, becoming-with cuts across the welfare-rights distinction. PigeonBlog's fanciers might or might not endorse animal-rights politics; "the first contact person was interested but frankly afraid of animal rights people and tactics" (Haraway, 2016: 24). Yet they "love and care for their birds" in ways neither regulation nor litigation fully describes. "Rendered capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, 2016: 22): Haraway captures a register political philosophy does not.

The assessment. Welfare and rights do not demand the same political-philosophical scaffolding; they make different ethical claims. They do demand the same disciplinary apparatus for describing multispecies practice: both are intelligible inside Carter and Charles's relational realism and Haraway's relational ontology.

Two cases sharpen the point. The Batman Park egg-replacement scheme is closer to welfare in accepting population control, but Haraway frames it as "a small, practical enactment and a reminder to further opening to the response-ability of staying with the trouble" (Haraway, 2016: 28), showing that the welfare/rights distinction is not exhaustive of the political possibilities. The chimpanzee personhood case is closer to rights, asserting that the legitimacy of certain uses is in question; the sociology of that litigation is still describable by Carter and Charles's apparatus.

So welfare and rights are politically distinct but sociologically compatible. Different politics, shared sociology. Haraway's wariness of "prolife" framings (Haraway, 2016: 28) shows the welfare/rights binary is not the only normative terrain. The sociology keeps all the projects intelligible.

### M12: What does the Greenebaum (2012) typology of vegans add to the Carter and Charles diagnosis of the animal challenge to sociology?

Slide 34 introduces Greenebaum's three-type typology alongside "Should we eat animals?": health vegans (lose weight, improve health), environmental vegans (concerned with the impact of industrial/intensive farming, though they may buy leather rather than plastic), and ethical vegans (moral and political views). The typology adds three concrete things to Carter and Charles's diagnosis: anthroponormativity's contemporary register, the limits of any single ethical framing, and the Anthropocene stake at which Carter and Charles end.

Carter and Charles's diagnosis is that sociology is built on anthroponormativity, "the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences" being "configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), and that this has to be reworked because human-animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80) and because, in the Anthropocene, a human-only sociology is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). What the diagnosis lacks at this level of generality is a sense of what taking it seriously looks like as everyday practice.

Greenebaum supplies this. Veganism is one of the most visible contemporary practices in which human-animal relations are reorganised, and the typology shows it is not one practice but several. Health vegans rework their relation to animals on the terms of their own bodies; the animal as such may not be the primary object of concern. Environmental vegans frame the relation in Anthropocene register: factory farming and intensive agriculture carry environmental costs (climate change, biodiversity loss) that justify dietary change. The leather-not-plastic note on slide 34 captures the typology's tensions: an environmental vegan may prioritise petrochemical avoidance over animal-product avoidance, an internally coherent but contested choice. Ethical vegans take the animal as the object of moral concern directly, lining up with the framing of agential beings "frequently" subject to "domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80).

The first thing the typology adds is anthroponormativity's contemporary register. Anthroponormativity is a diagnosis of conceptual habits; the typology shows what contestation looks like in the consumer practices of ordinary people. The three vegan types are three different breaches of the silent species-norm that human eating is a category apart from animal lives.

The second is the limits of any single ethical framing. Carter and Charles state they are not engaged in animal advocacy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93); their analytic recognition of domination does not prescribe a politics. The three types are not jointly determinate of a single programme: health veganism does not generate animal-rights politics; environmental veganism does not generate welfare politics in the regulatory sense; ethical veganism may or may not endorse the "prolife" framings Haraway distances herself from ("no serious animal-human becoming-with can be a prolife project in the chilling American sense of that term", Haraway, 2016: 28). The typology displays the plurality multispecies sociology has to keep open.

The third is the Anthropocene stake. Slide 35's "Green Criminology" framing pushes the environmental category into Carter and Charles's closing concern. If sociology cannot describe a planet shared with other species, it is in danger of irrelevance. Environmental vegans are precisely those reorganising their daily practice in response to that planetary recognition. Halewood's diagnosis of bifurcation as the master modern move (Halewood, 2014: 1) names the conceptual mistake at issue: the nature/culture cut on which industrial agriculture rests is the same cut sociology inherits, and environmental veganism is one of the everyday practices through which the bifurcation is being refused. Haraway's response-ability names the moral attention.

The typology has limits. It is a typology of vegans, not of human-animal relations more generally; it does not cover the pheasant shoot, the pigeon fancier, or the dog-walker. It treats individual consumer practice as the unit of analysis, where Carter and Charles work at the level of social structures. Greenebaum is an addition, not a replacement: it gives the diagnosis a downstream presence in lived practice.

One further connection. The pheasant shoot is a "complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The typology suggests the same is true on the consumption side: even within "veganism", the practice is multiply motivated and multiply political. Multispecies sociology has to describe both production-side entanglements (Greene's horses, the pheasant shoot, PigeonBlog, factory farming) and consumption-side typologies without flattening either.

So Greenebaum (2012) adds concreteness, plurality and Anthropocene urgency to the diagnosis. The typology does not by itself rework sociology's concepts; that is Carter and Charles's work. It shows the diagnosis landing in ordinary practice, which makes it matter beyond the academy.

### M13: "Pets are kin." Discuss the politics and ethics of treating companion animals as family with reference to Haraway and Kimbo.

The phrase condenses one of the most politically and ethically charged sites in human-animal relations. Through Haraway's companion species and the lecturer's presentation of Kimbo's work, treating companion animals as family is sociologically defensible and politically productive, but only if held alongside an honest recognition of the structural asymmetries that distinguish pet-keeping from human family relations.

Begin with the empirical reality. Slide 26 is autobiographical: the lecturer's dog Roasted Lop illustrates the claim that humans "become humans through their relations with animals." Slide 29 on Kimbo: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless. Thus, regardless of how 'different' individuals are from each other, experience can still be shared." Kinship is lived practice. The pet-food industry and the brands organising the commodified-companion economy are the political-economic backdrop against which everyday kin-claims happen.

Haraway gives the developed language. Companion species "are relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13). The *Companion Species Manifesto* (Haraway, 2003): humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand", co-shaped through millennia of co-evolution, breeding, labour and domestic life. Haraway insists on "significant otherness": humans and dogs co-shape each other without collapsing. The Despret line transfers: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (cited Haraway, 2016: 25). The dog is not pre-given prior to training, walking, feeding and naming.

Kimbo develops this affectively. Slide 29: "If two individual bodies (or centres of experiences and flows of feeling) open themselves up or attune to each other… shared experience is what matters foremost." Attunement is the ethical mode. Slide 28's question ("How to think with (and for) other animals, as opposed to about them?") suggests a methodology for kinship: thinking with the pet. Haraway's "Situated Knowledges" (Haraway, 1991: 189) underwrites this: refusing "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" is what makes the pet's standpoint count, and Haraway's own line that "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196) applies as much to dog-human kinship as to feminist epistemology.

Carter and Charles support the framing disciplinarily. The dog is an agent whose purposes engage household conditions; agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). A dog choosing where to sleep is an agent in the sociological sense. Treating that agent as kin is recognition rather than anthropomorphism. Slide 13's critique of reducing pets to symbols is the negative case.

The politics. Treating pets as kin contests the species-citizenship boundary, puts pressure on legal categories (the chimpanzee personhood case on slide 31), and valorises feminised, unpaid emotional labour. Slide 7: "perhaps it is only possible for us to be human, and to live in a society, through our relations with animals."

The ethics. The strongest claim is Haraway's response-ability: "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29). Kinning a pet is graduated response-ability the pet cannot reciprocate in the same way but is no less a real ethical relation.

But the asymmetries are real. Pets are bred, bought, sold and euthanised on human decisions; pet-keeping is class-stratified, and the pet-food industry profits from the asymmetry. Human-animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80), pets included. Kinning is a corrective inside an unequal practice, not a neutralisation.

Two objections. First, kin-pets divert attention from animals worse-treated. Reply: on slide 26's becoming-with, kinship generates ethical attention outward. Second, the kinship claim risks Haraway's "prolife" register ("no serious animal-human becoming-with can be a prolife project in the chilling American sense", Haraway, 2016: 28). Pet kinship has to be graduated response-ability, not absolute.

Carter and Charles's anthroponormativity diagnosis (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88) underwrites the broader claim. Refusing kinship to companion animals because they are non-human is the definitional move anthroponormativity makes. "Pets are kin" is not stretching family beyond recognition; it is recognising that family was always already capable of multispecies extension.

So the politics and ethics of pet-kinship are defensible and productive, provided the asymmetries are held in view. Pets are kin: real, ethical, politically charged, but also asymmetric, class-stratified and non-chosen. The slogan is true; the work is in the qualifications.
