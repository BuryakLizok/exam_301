## 2. Key concepts and thinkers

- **Human exceptionalism**: the doctrine that humans alone possess intention, morality, symbolism, consciousness, and so stand outside natural law (slide on "Human Exceptionalism"). Sociology, on Carter and Charles's reading, is built on this premise.
- **Bifurcation / the animal–human binary**: the dualistic carve-up nature/culture, genes/environment, facts/values, animal/human (Carter and Charles, 2011: 2, cited in slides).
- **Anthroponormativity**: Carter and Charles's term for sociology's conceptual vocabulary being silently configured around the human, analogous to heteronormativity or white normativity.
- **Companion species** (Haraway): humans become humans through co-shaping relations with other species; not "humans AND animals" but mutually constitutive becoming.
- **Becoming-with / rendering-capable** (Haraway, Despret): partners do not pre-exist the knot; they are made what they are through entanglement.
- **Multispecies sociology / multispecies ethnography**: research that treats animals as social actors and co-constituents of society, not as mute "nature."
- **Situated knowledge**: knowledge is always located in specific bodies and relations; pertinent here to "thinking with" rather than "about" animals (Kimbo, Despret).
- **Classical thinkers**: Durkheim (sociability as social, observable in animals); Weber (animals can "understand" but verifiable meaningful access is limited); Marx (productive labour, bees); Greene (horses as living machines, prime movers of industrial modernity); Parsons (consolidator of the strictly human social).

## 4. Information from the readings

**Carter and Charles, "The animal challenge to sociology" (2018).** The article argues that sociology has been "slow to take up the animal challenge" (p. 79), despite the obvious fact that "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Bryant 1979, cited p. 79). Three reasons are diagnosed. *First*, sociology has had a "fraught relationship with biology" (p. 80): in its late-nineteenth-century institutionalisation, the discipline defended "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12) against Darwinian "comprehensive naturalism", and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. *Second*, sociology rests on **human exceptionalism**: humans are treated as evolutionarily unique because of language and meaning-making, and "the human" is "usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Ingold 1994a, cited p. 81). *Third*, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe, where urban dwellers experienced themselves as "increasingly remote from most other animals" (p. 84). Yet, drawing on Greene (2008), Carter and Charles insist horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene 2008: 4, p. 84) and the "prime mover" of industrial development.

The article then argues for two conceptual revisions. **Society** must be redefined to include non-human animals as "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68). **Agency** must be reworked as relational: agency is not a Cartesian property of rational subjects but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90); it is "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). Their pheasant-shoot example dramatises a "complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92). Their concluding claim is uncompromising: "the social is animal" (p. 93). Sociology's whole conceptual vocabulary is "anthroponormative" (p. 88) — analogous to heteronormative and white-normative vocabularies — and must be reworked, not abandoned (as Latour and the new materialists would have it).

**Haraway, "Playing String Figures with Companion Species" (2016).** The chapter develops a method for thinking with animals through the metaphor of cat's cradle — "sf" doubles for science fiction, speculative fabulation, science fact, and string figures. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (p. 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (p. 13). Haraway's pigeons illustrate this: racing pigeons in Southern California are "competent agents — in the double sense of both delegates and actors" (p. 18). Her key empirical case is **PigeonBlog** (Beatriz da Costa, 2006), where racing pigeons, art-activists, working-class pigeon fanciers and DIY electronics co-produced air-pollution data over polluted Los Angeles neighbourhoods. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (p. 18) and the humans had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" (p. 22). Response-ability — a Despret term Haraway adopts — names the obligation to make others "capable" through specific entanglements (p. 12). Haraway explicitly refuses both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (p. 11). Project PigeonWatch with Washington DC schoolchildren shows recuperation across categories that racist iconography links — "rats with wings", "feral" — when the kids learn to see pigeons "as sociable birds with lives and deaths" (p. 24).

**Haraway, *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003).** Earlier articulation of the same project: dogs and people are not pre-given subjects encountering each other; rather "we are training each other in acts of communication we barely understand" (the manifesto's framing of becoming-with). Haraway insists on "significant otherness" — humans and dogs do not collapse into one another but co-shape one another through history, labour, evolution, breed politics, and everyday domestic practice. Domestic dogs are products of millennia of co-evolution with humans; they are not metaphors or symbols for humans. This grounds the multispecies sociology that Carter and Charles call for.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The lecture and the Carter and Charles reading share a single thesis: sociology has been so anxious to defend its disciplinary turf against biology that it amputated half of its empirical world. I think they are right, but the diagnosis matters more than the slogan. Carter and Charles do not just say "let's add animals in" — that would be stage three on the feminism analogy. They argue, in the article's most direct line, that **"the social *is* animal"** (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 93). The work is reconfiguring concepts, not extending coverage.

Their target is sharp: "the human is usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Carter and Charles, p. 81, citing Ingold). Sociology inherits this binary from Cartesian humanism, hardens it after Parsons, and then runs into trouble when confronted with horses ("biotechnology… living machines", Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 4 [VERIFY p.?]), pheasants in shoots, or wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). The Greene quote from the slides drives the point home: "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008, p. 170). Sociology stares at the Communist Manifesto and misses the horses dropping "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" in 1900 New York (Greene, 2008, p. 174).

The Weber excerpt the lecturer cites complicates the simple "classical sociology ignored animals" story. Weber concedes that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, pp. 15–16). But he immediately retreats: a "verifiable conception" of animals' state of mind is attainable "only within narrow limits" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, p. 17). Weber half-opens the door and then closes it on epistemological grounds — and twentieth-century sociology, post-Parsons, slammed it shut. Durkheim's *Rules* leaves a similar window: "animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common" (Durkheim, *Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 132). Sociability, for Durkheim, is not innately human.

Haraway pushes further. She refuses the "either/or" of human exceptionalism versus posthumanism. Her programmatic line is: "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 12). Companion species are not "ordinary beings-in-encounter" who already exist before they meet; "species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). This is what Carter and Charles, in their own register, mean when they say agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, p. 90).

The critical question is whether this expanded sociology can still do the political work sociology was meant to do. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: if sociology cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, it is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). Haraway makes the same wager: "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 29). I think this is right. A sociology that cannot name factory farming, zoonotic pandemics, biodiversity collapse, or the politics of beef and palm oil is a sociology that has unilaterally disarmed in the face of its century.

## 8. Essay answers

### 2024 (Q2): Why is it important to study animals within sociology?

Sociology emerged as the study of industrial modernity, and yet by my count the most important moving parts of that modernity — horses, livestock, lab animals, working dogs, insects pollinating the food system — were systematically excluded from the sociological imagination. I want to argue, with Carter and Charles, that studying animals within sociology is not a niche enrichment of the discipline but a remedy for a structural blind spot that has, until recently, made sociology unable to describe the very societies it claims to analyse.

The first reason is empirical. There is, as Bryant pointed out in 1979 and as Carter and Charles repeat, "virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 79). Greene's history of nineteenth-century cities makes this concrete. "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, 2008, p. 170). Industrial capitalism, the very object of classical sociology, ran on horsepower in the literal sense, on horses described by Greene as "living machines" (Greene, 2008, p. 4). The Communist Manifesto contains, as the lecturer pointedly notes, no horses. New York in 1900 produced "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" (Greene, 2008, p. 174). A discipline that misses an infrastructure that large is not a discipline that has settled on its proper object; it is a discipline that has misperceived its object.

The second reason is conceptual. Sociology defines social action, agency, subjectivity, reflexivity, and even society itself through a tacit "anthroponormativity" — Carter and Charles's term, modelled on heteronormativity and white normativity, for "the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences" being "configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). This is not a politically neutral choice. The Weberian inheritance is the key example: meaning-making, empathetic understanding, value-oriented action are all framed as exclusively human, even though Weber himself conceded that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility" non-instinctively and meaningfully (Weber, *Economy and Society*, pp. 15–16). When Parsons consolidates sociology in the mid-twentieth century, he hardens this into the doctrine that sociality is properly the realm of human interaction alone. To ascribe sociality to animals would, in this view, "threaten the grounding and specificity of the social" (lecture). I think this is exactly backwards: what threatens the specificity of the social is pretending it stops at the species boundary.

The third reason is political. Carter and Charles draw an explicit analogy with feminism. The trajectory is: pre-feminist neglect, critique, adding-women-in, then "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, p. 80, citing Walby, Oakley, Charles). Animal studies, they argue, is "experiencing the second and third stages" but has not yet reached the fourth. Studying animals within sociology is the work of moving the discipline from stage three to stage four — from animals as an exotic specialism to animals as constitutive of every sociological object. Just as feminism did not simply "add women" but reworked the categories of work, family, agency and the body, multispecies sociology reworks the categories of labour (lab animals in drug testing; horses pulling cabs; bees pollinating crops), the family (pets as kin in Haraway's sense of companion species), the body (zoonotic disease and COVID), and the political (animal-rights claims, vegan politics, the cosmopolitics Haraway elaborates).

The fourth reason is ecological and urgent. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: a sociology that cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, they warn, is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). I think this is right. Industrial agriculture clears the Amazon for beef; palm-oil plantations destroy orangutan habitat; intensive farming generates antibiotic resistance and zoonotic spillover. None of these are biological problems and only biological problems; they are social problems in which animals are agents, objects, and victims simultaneously. A sociology that defines its remit as exclusively human is not "rigorous"; it is wilfully partial.

Studying animals is also a methodological challenge. Donna Haraway insists that "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 12). Alexandra Kimbo, the lecturer's former PhD student, reframes this as a question: how to think *with* (and for) other animals, as opposed to *about* them? The shift is from animals as data points or symbols to animals as research partners whose ways of mattering have to be taken seriously. The PigeonBlog project — where racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over Los Angeles — shows what such collaboration looks like in practice: the pigeons had to "learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18) and the humans had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, p. 22).

To say "the social is animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93) is not anthropomorphism, and it is not flat ontology. It is a recognition that the entities sociology studies — labour, family, city, modernity, capitalism, politics — were always already multispecies. Studying animals within sociology therefore does not stretch the discipline; it returns it to its proper object.

### 2025 (Q2): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically assess this statement using the ideas set out by Carter and Charles in the key reading for this topic.

I will critically assess the claim that sociology is the study of human societies using the argument Carter and Charles develop in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018). The statement is true as a description of how the discipline has constituted itself, but Carter and Charles show it is false as a description of what the discipline should be — and that the gap matters politically, conceptually and ecologically.

Carter and Charles concede the historical accuracy of the statement. Sociology, in its institutionalisation between roughly 1890 and the 1930s, did define its object as human society and only human society. They give three reasons. First, sociology had a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, p. 80). To establish itself against psychology and biology, sociology insisted on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12, cited p. 82) and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. Second, sociology was built on "human exceptionalism" — the doctrine "that the human species is exempt from natural constraints… and is evolutionarily unique" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). Third, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe at precisely the moment when "urban dwellers experience themselves as increasingly remote from most other animals" (Carter and Charles, p. 84). The disappearance of horses and food-animals from city streets in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries coincided with sociology's professional formation. The discipline took the absence of visible animals for the absence of relevant animals.

The first stage of their critique is that this is empirically false. Animals were "central… to economy and society" (Carter and Charles, p. 84, citing Tovey 2003). Greene's case study of nineteenth-century American cities, which the lecturer foregrounds, shows that horses were "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene 2008: 4, cited p. 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile in the first part of the twentieth century" (Carter and Charles, p. 86). To leave horses out of an account of industrialisation is not a defensible analytic choice; it is a misdescription.

The second stage of their critique is conceptual, and it is where the article does its hardest work. Carter and Charles argue that "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (p. 88). They name this configuration **anthroponormativity**, by direct analogy with heteronormativity and white normativity. Society, agency, social action, intersubjectivity and reflexivity are all silently restricted to humans. Agency, on the Cartesian inheritance, is "associated with deeply rooted assumptions about self-conscious, individual self-transformation and the 'emancipated subject'" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). When sociology uses this vocabulary, it excludes animals not by argument but by definition.

Carter and Charles then offer their alternative. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (p. 90). It is "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). On this realist-relational view, non-human animals are agential beings — entangled, "enfolded in all sorts of relations with humans (many of which are fatal for them)" and finding "that their agential conditions are modified involuntarily" (p. 92). Their pheasant-shoot example is precise: a shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (p. 92). To call this human interaction alone, and to call the pheasants and dogs mere background, is to misperceive the practice.

The third stage is the political stake. Carter and Charles draw the analogy with feminism: sociology went through a pre-feminist phase, a critique, an "add women in" phase, and finally "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (p. 80). Animal studies, on their reading, is between phases two and three. The claim that "sociology is the study of human societies" is precisely the kind of definitional move that feminism, post-colonial sociology and queer theory have all dismantled in turn. What is at stake is whether sociology can describe its actual object — industrial capitalism, the post-colonial world, the Anthropocene — without continuing to lop off half of it.

Their concluding line is uncompromising: "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93). This is not animal advocacy; it is a methodological claim. "In considering animals as social actors and agents," they write, "we have provided an example of how this might be done" (p. 93). Their rejection of Latour and the new materialists is also worth noting: they do not think sociology has to be abandoned in favour of actor-network theory or post-humanism. The discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (p. 87) once its foundational concepts — society, agency, social action — are reworked.

So the statement "sociology is the study of human societies" is, on Carter and Charles's account, an inherited disciplinary self-description, not a discovered truth about what sociology can or should be. It is a description of where sociology has been, not where it has to go. To take their argument seriously is to accept that the boundary between humans and animals is not a methodological given but a political and conceptual choice — one that current ecological, ethical and epistemological pressures give us very good reasons to revise. I therefore reject the statement as a definition while accepting it as a description of past practice.

### 2023 (Q2): Why should sociology make efforts to study the relations between humans and animals?

I want to argue that sociology should make these efforts for four interlocking reasons: empirical fidelity, conceptual coherence, ethical and political relevance, and ecological necessity. The argument runs through Carter and Charles's *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018) and Donna Haraway's notion of companion species, with concrete examples ranging from horsepower in the Industrial Revolution to PigeonBlog in twenty-first-century Los Angeles.

**Empirical fidelity.** The first reason is that human–animal relations were always there, and a sociology that misses them misperceives its own object. Greene's history of nineteenth-century US cities, which features prominently in the lecture, makes this concrete: "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170). Modernity, industrialisation, capitalism — sociology's founding objects — ran on animals. Horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, p. 4). They became invisible to sociologists precisely because they were too central to be seen, the way we no longer notice internet cables or sewage infrastructure. As the lecturer put it, making the familiar strange is one of sociology's main tasks, and horses are a prime candidate.

The empirical case extends far beyond the nineteenth century. Lab animals are essential to the pharmaceutical industry. Factory farming reshapes the labour market, the land and the global food system; capitalist agriculture is unthinkable without the industrial breeding, feeding and slaughter of animals. Pets are deeply implicated in emotional labour, family structure and the welfare state — companion-animal therapy is now a recognised practice in mental health care. Honeybees and other insects pollinate the crops that feed cities. Zoonotic spillover, dramatised by COVID-19, has reshaped the world economy. Biodiversity collapse is unfolding as we speak. To leave all of this out of sociology because it is "biological" is to abandon the discipline's promise of grasping social totality.

**Conceptual coherence.** The second reason is that sociology's core concepts wobble badly without animals. Carter and Charles call this **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). Agency, in the Cartesian inheritance, belongs to rational subjects; objects (including animals) are passive matter available for human use. But this view is incoherent once we look closely. Carter and Charles's example of wolves in Europe shows that "the agency of wolves… changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). Their agential conditions are "powerfully shaped by their relations to humans (but not only these)" (p. 91). The wolf is not metaphorically an agent; the wolf's possibilities and constraints are real, relational, and politically structured. Sociology that does not recognise this cannot describe nature reserves, hunting laws, rewilding politics or animal-rights litigation.

**Ethical and political relevance.** The third reason is that human–animal relations are sites of domination, exploitation and care that sociology, on its own terms, should care about. Carter and Charles are explicit: their "key claim is that animals are agents entangled in relations with humans and that these relations are frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, p. 80). The sociology of factory farming sits naturally alongside the sociology of mass incarceration: both are bureaucratic systems of confinement that reduce living beings to inputs. The sociology of pets sits alongside the sociology of care and emotional labour. The sociology of circuses and zoos sits alongside the sociology of spectacle and entertainment. Veganism, in Greenebaum's three-type analysis (health, environmental, ethical), is a social movement with class, gender and racial dimensions.

**Ecological necessity.** The fourth reason is that the Anthropocene leaves us no choice. Carter and Charles end their article by warning that a sociology that cannot speak about a multispecies planet is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, p. 81). Haraway makes the same wager in her language of "staying with the trouble": "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 29). Differential responsibility — by ecology, economy and species — is precisely a sociological problem.

Haraway's notion of companion species supplies a vocabulary for doing this work without falling into either anthropomorphism or flat ontology. Humans "become humans through their relations with animals" (lecture slide on companion species). "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, p. 12). The PigeonBlog project, where racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over polluted Los Angeles neighbourhoods, illustrates this in practice: the pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing"; the artist-researchers "had to render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, p. 22). Citizen-science programmes like Project PigeonWatch in Washington DC teach city children to re-see despised "rats with wings" as "sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, p. 24).

Sociology should make these efforts, then, because animals are everywhere in the social, because sociology's own concepts already require them, because human–animal relations are political relations, and because the planetary crisis demands a discipline capable of multispecies thinking. The alternative — defending the human/animal binary as if it were a discovered fact about the world — is not rigour. It is a refusal to look.

### 2021 (Q18): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically analyse the limitations of this statement.

The statement captures how sociology has historically defined itself but obscures three distinct limitations, which I will analyse in turn: a historical-empirical limitation, a conceptual limitation, and a normative limitation. I argue that none of these are minor blind spots; together they amount to a structural failure that the discipline now has to repair.

**The historical-empirical limitation.** The first limitation is that the statement misdescribes the societies sociology has actually studied. Carter and Charles trace the formation of the discipline to "modern industrial capitalist societies" of Western Europe and the United States (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 83). But those societies were emphatically multispecies. Greene's history shows that "horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170). Nineteenth-century New York had 131,000 horses producing up to 3,300 tons of manure daily (Greene, p. 174). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). The "absence of large animals" in twentieth-century cities is not a natural condition; it is the result of historical processes (food-animal removal in the second half of the nineteenth century, horse displacement by the automobile in the early twentieth) which sociology mistook for the disappearance of animals from social life altogether. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Durkheim's industrial division of labour contains no slaughterhouses. Weber's Protestant ethic contains no oxen. Carter and Charles point out that "the contribution of animals and even their centrality to processes of industrialisation have been erased in sociological conceptions of the social" (Carter and Charles, p. 84). The statement that sociology is the study of human societies is, in this sense, a self-fulfilling description of an empirical mistake.

**The conceptual limitation.** The second limitation is that the statement entrenches what Carter and Charles call **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, p. 88). This is the same kind of move, they argue, that earlier critiques exposed under the names heteronormativity and white normativity — a tacit norm that pretends to be a neutral definition. Agency, social action, intersubjectivity, reflexivity and even society are all silently restricted to humans. Carter and Charles trace this back partly to a particular reading of Weber, partly to Parsons. The lecturer is more direct: "I blame Talcott Parsons!" — under whose influence twentieth-century sociologists insisted that "whatever sociality is, it is a production of humans, groups of humans or systems of humans" (lecture). Yet Weber himself wrote that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility and react to them in ways which are evidently often by no means purely instinctive and mechanical and in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, *Economy and Society*, pp. 15–16). And Durkheim wrote that "it has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, *Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 132). The classical thinkers were less anthropocentric than their twentieth-century descendants. The "study of human societies" formula is a hardening, not a faithful inheritance.

Carter and Charles's positive proposal is a relational account of agency: "agency is not an attribute, or property, of humans or other animals or things, but an emergent product of the relations within which all things are placed" (Carter and Charles, p. 91). Their example of European wolves shows that agency is spatial and political: wolves are protected by EU law in one jurisdiction and shot under licence in another. Their pheasant-shoot example shows that ordinary practices are "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92). The conceptual limitation of the "human societies" definition is that it gives us no vocabulary for these realities except dismissal.

**The normative limitation.** The third limitation is ethical and ecological. The "human societies" definition lets sociology evade the politics of human–animal relations. Carter and Charles are explicit that these relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (p. 80). Factory farming, drug testing on lab animals, zoos, circuses, fur farming, the dog-meat trade, palm-oil-driven habitat destruction, COVID-style zoonotic spillover, antibiotic resistance born of industrial agriculture, biodiversity collapse — none of these can be properly described by a discipline that has defined them out of scope. Haraway's response is to refuse both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 11). Companion species, becoming-with, response-ability — her vocabulary is built precisely to do work that the "human societies" formula prevents.

There is one defence of the statement worth considering. Some sociologists — Carter and Charles cite Mead, and the lecturer adds Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard — argue that language, symbolism and reflexive meaning-making really do mark a categorical difference, and that the proper object of sociology really is the symbolic order produced by human meaning-makers. The reply, which I find persuasive, has two parts. Empirically, this categorical difference does not survive scrutiny: chimpanzees, bonobos, whales and other species are now widely accepted to have cultures that "are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, p. 88, citing de Waal). Pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, p. 18). Methodologically, the argument is circular — we define the social as symbolic, define the symbolic as human, and conclude that the social is human. Anthroponormativity does the work that empirical investigation should be doing.

The three limitations are connected. The historical-empirical limitation produces an inaccurate picture of past societies. The conceptual limitation produces analytic tools that cannot grasp present societies. The normative limitation produces a discipline ill-equipped for the multispecies crises of the future. The statement "sociology is the study of human societies" is not merely incomplete; it actively blocks sociology from doing what sociology is supposed to do. I therefore conclude, with Carter and Charles, that "the social *is* animal" (p. 93) — and that the definition we should defend is not "sociology is the study of human societies" but something closer to "sociology is the study of multispecies relations through which human-and-animal worlds are made."

### M1: "The social *is* animal." Discuss.

The line is Carter and Charles's (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 93), and I take it as the most compressed statement of a sociological revolution the discipline has not yet absorbed. I want to unpack what it does not say, defend what it does say, and test it on factory farming and COVID-19 — the two cases where the social cannot be told without the animal.

The claim is not anthropomorphism: Carter and Charles are not saying chickens hold opinions. Nor is it flat ontology; they explicitly reject the Latourian move to treat humans, animals and machines as symmetrical "actants" (p. 87). What it says is that the categories sociology uses — society, agency, social action, labour, family, modernity — were always already multispecies in their referents, and pretending otherwise is anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (p. 88), the definitional sleight that once made heteronormativity feel like a neutral default.

The positive content sits in their reworked agency. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). Animals are agents because they have purposes engaging relational conditions, not because they are little humans. Their pheasant-shoot example crystallises this: a UK shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (p. 92). The pheasants are not props; their breeding cycles, flight capacities and seasonal vulnerabilities co-constitute the practice.

The empirical test is the test Greene poses against Marxism. Industrial modernity ran on horses — "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170) — and yet, as the lecturer dryly observes, the Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Marx's productive labour and his metabolism with nature ran on living machines (Greene, p. 4) he never wrote about. The slogan recovers what classical sociology amputated.

The contemporary test is sharper. Factory farming makes the social-is-animal claim undeniable. Roughly 80 billion land animals are slaughtered annually inside vertically integrated agribusiness chains whose labour, capital, environmental footprint and consumer cultures are multispecies in their causal logic. "Chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages" (Carter and Charles, p. 92). Slaughterhouse workers — disproportionately migrant and racialised — suffer documented PTSD rates alongside the animals they kill, a single multispecies labour process whatever sociology pretends.

COVID-19 puts the point in the register of pandemic. The bat reservoir, the intermediate host, the wet-market or laboratory origin, the meat-processing plants where outbreaks clustered, the lockdown surge in pet adoption: the whole pandemic is multispecies in its causal chain and political fallout. A sociology of human societies in 2020 could not describe what was happening.

Haraway sharpens the point by refusing the bad alternatives. "Not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 11) keeps species specificity while refusing exceptionalism. Companion species "are relentlessly becoming-with" (p. 13); the partners "do not precede the knotting" (p. 13). PigeonBlog — Beatriz da Costa's 2006 air-pollution project — shows the social as animal in micro: racing pigeons, working-class fanciers, art-activists and DIY sensors co-produce data that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" cannot generate (Haraway, p. 21).

The recurring objection is that extending the social to animals dissolves sociology into biology. Carter and Charles answer it: they want to reform sociology, not abandon it; the discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (p. 87) once its categories are reworked. The social/biological boundary is exactly what the slogan calls into question. Defending it as fixed is defending a disciplinary self-description against a discoverable fact.

So I take "the social *is* animal" as a programme rather than a slogan: rework society, agency and social action to take animals seriously as constitutive, then use the resulting vocabulary on horsepower, factory farming, COVID, pet-keeping and biodiversity collapse. Carter and Charles supply the diagnostic; Haraway supplies the method; the discipline supplies the rest if it wants to.

### M2: What does Haraway mean by companion species, and why does she treat it as ontologically prior to "human" or "animal"?

Haraway means by companion species not a list of cute domesticated animals but a relational ontology in which partners come into being through their entanglement rather than meeting as pre-formed kinds. She insists on this priority because the alternative — starting from "human" and "animal" as given categories and asking how they relate — smuggles human exceptionalism back under the cover of taxonomy. I will explain the concept and defend the priority claim.

The clearest articulation is Haraway's string-figure metaphor. "Companion species play string figure games where who is/are to be in/of the world is constituted in intra- and interaction. The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). In a cat's cradle there is no string-pattern prior to the hands that pass it; the figure is the knotting. Likewise, there are no "humans" and "animals" prior to the histories of co-domestication, co-evolution, labour, breeding and affection that make them what they are. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (p. 13).

The constructive term is becoming-with; its operational sister is rendering-capable, taken from Vinciane Despret: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (p. 12). The compressed statement of the claim is Despret's: "without the lover of pigeons (pigeon fancier), without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25). The homing pigeon does not pre-exist the practice that produced it; neither does the fancier.

Why is this ontologically prior to "human" and "animal"? Three reasons.

First, the empirical record cuts against pre-given kinds. Dogs and humans share a deep co-evolutionary history; wolf-to-dog domestication unfolds over tens of thousands of years and reshapes both. The *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003) makes the case explicitly: humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand." The lecturer's slide is in the same register: humans "become humans through their relations with animals." Similar arguments apply to chimpanzee tool cultures, octopus cognition, and microbiomes.

Second, treating "human" and "animal" as prior is what generates human exceptionalism in the first place. The Cartesian inheritance — "the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 89) — depends on already having a "human" and an "animal" to assign properties to. Haraway dissolves the problem at root: entities are constituted through the practices producing their properties. Hence "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, p. 11) — posthumanism still names a "human" to be overcome.

Third, the priority claim does political work. PigeonBlog is unintelligible on a kinds-first ontology. The racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18); the artist-researchers had "to render each other capable of mutual trust" (p. 22); the fanciers had to be persuaded to risk the project. None of these competences existed in advance. Pigeons became air-quality scientists; humans became multispecies citizen-scientists; both produced environmental-justice data that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" could not generate (p. 21).

Does the priority claim flatten species differences? No. Haraway insists on "significant otherness" (the *Manifesto* subtitle) and graduated response-ability: "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (p. 29). The priority of relation does not erase the specificity of relata.

A second objection comes from Carter and Charles themselves, who want a realist commitment to structures and emergent properties that Haraway's playful idiom risks losing (Carter and Charles, p. 87). The right reading is complementary: Carter and Charles supply structural-realist disciplinary repair; Haraway supplies the relational ontology that explains why repair is needed.

So companion species is the ontological claim that kinds are consequent upon entanglement — relation prior to relata. Without it, multispecies sociology slides back into "humans plus animals", the anthroponormativity Carter and Charles diagnose.

### M3: Horsepower, industrialisation and the limits of Marxism.

The title is provocative and I take it seriously. My argument is that the horse is not a footnote to Marxism but a stress test for it: Marx's productive labour, his metabolism with nature, and his political economy of industrial capitalism cannot be told without horses, and the fact that he tells them without horses exposes the anthroponormativity at the heart of his apparatus.

Begin with empirical scale. "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170). Nineteenth-century New York housed roughly 131,000 horses producing 1,300–3,300 tons of manure daily (Greene, p. 174). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile" (Carter and Charles, p. 86).

Now the limit. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Marx theorises productive labour through the human worker selling labour-power to capital, with non-human nature appearing only as raw material. Greene cuts through this: horses are "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84). The horse is neither raw material nor human worker. It is a labouring being whose biological rhythms, breeding histories and species-specific capacities co-shaped what industrial production could be. Horses needed rest, hay, stabling and care; their working lives shaped urban infrastructure (stables, smithies, knackers' yards, manure removal) and urban temporality. A purely human labour theory of value cannot price this in.

Marx's metabolic rift points the right direction but stops short. The rift was lived by manure: 3,300 tons a day had to be moved, and its absence from rural fields helped drive soil exhaustion and the demand for guano imports. Yet the rift literature still treats animals largely as substrate. Greene's intervention insists on horses as agents: they "stimulated the development of an array of technologies and devices: vehicles, equestrian equipment, breeders' logs; and horse power reinforced other sorts of power — military, political, religious, sexual" (Greene 2008: 5, cited Carter and Charles, p. 84).

The conceptual repair is Carter and Charles's relational agency. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90). The horse is an agent in industrial modernity because its purposes (live, eat, move, reproduce) engage agential conditions (capitalist demand for traction, urban density, omnibus rhythms, feed costs) to produce outcomes humans alone could not. The erasure is Marx's, but it is also Durkheim's, Weber's and Parsons's — what Carter and Charles label anthroponormativity (p. 88).

What survives of Marxism? More than the title suggests. Capital accumulation, exploitation, class struggle and crisis remain powerful — but extended to multispecies labour processes. Factory farming is the obvious case: industrial breeding of pigs, broilers and dairy cattle is capital accumulation through the exploitation of both human labour (migrant slaughterhouse workers) and animal bodies. Barbara Noske calls this the "animal-industrial complex"; the term updates Marx by recognising animals as exploited co-producers.

Haraway's contribution to the same limit is becoming-with. Horse and human are not separate kinds plugging into industrial production; they are companion species whose joint capacities were produced through millennia of domestication, breeding and labour. The carthorse pulling a brewer's dray is not a "natural" entity meeting an "industrial" entity; it is a co-produced being inside an entanglement that is social-and-natural together. "The partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13) describes the historical record more accurately than the Manifesto's human-only protagonists.

The limits of Marxism, then, are the limits of its anthroponormative inheritance, and they show up exactly where industrial modernity is most obviously multispecies. Marxism is not refuted by horses; it is forced to grow. A multispecies political economy — capital, labour, animals, ecology — keeps what is best in Marx (exploitation, accumulation, crisis) while losing what was always a contingent disciplinary blinker. Greene's horses are the test case the discipline still has to pass.

### M4: How convincing do you find Carter and Charles's diagnosis of anthroponormativity in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*?

I find the diagnosis very convincing as a diagnostic move and persuasive but incomplete as a programme for repair. Its strength is that it names a structural exclusion sociology has treated as methodological commonplace; its weakness is that it does most of its work negatively. On balance I judge it highly successful.

Anthroponormativity is defined explicitly: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 88). The analogies do real work. Heteronormativity is not the claim that some people are heterosexual; it is the claim that family, intimacy and respectability have been silently configured around heterosexual couples in ways that exclude queer lives by definition. White normativity does the same for citizenship, professionalism and reasonableness around whiteness. Anthroponormativity, analogously, configures society, agency, social action and reflexivity around humans, with the exclusion of animals operating as a definitional move rather than an empirical finding.

The diagnostic power is in calling the bluff. The obvious counter — "sociology is the study of humans, so of course it excludes animals" — treats a contingent disciplinary self-description as a discovered necessity. There is no neutral place from which "the social = the human" is just true. It is a norm, and norms are contestable — the same move feminist sociology made against "sociology is the study of public, paid, male life" and post-colonial sociology made against "sociology is the study of Western modernity."

Once the diagnosis is in hand, the conceptual repair becomes legible. Agency is not a Cartesian property — "the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive" (p. 89) — but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). Wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (p. 91) are agents; pheasants in "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92) are agents; chickens in a battery cage are agents whose conditions thwart their purposes.

Three limits are worth registering, none fatal.

First, the concept does not by itself tell you how to do multispecies research. The fine-grained methodological vocabulary — becoming-with, rendering-capable, response-ability, situated knowledge — comes from Haraway and Despret. Carter and Charles diagnose; Haraway demonstrates. Alexandra Kimbo's PhD work on her dog Harald — "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless" (lecture slide) — shows the method-level work the diagnosis does not by itself do.

Second, the concept is in places under-theorised. It is unclear how anthroponormativity intersects with heteronormativity and white normativity in concrete sites — the slaughterhouse where racialised migrant labour and animal lives are extinguished together, or the dog-breeding world where pedigree politics map onto eugenic logics. Carter and Charles gesture but do not deliver these intersections.

Third, institutional reception has been slow. Carter and Charles published in 2018; the discipline has not absorbed the diagnosis as it absorbed equivalent feminist arguments by the late 1980s. This is partly a problem of the diagnosis (animals cannot enter the academy as graduate students the way women could) and partly a measure of how deep anthroponormativity runs.

Despite these limits the diagnosis is convincing. Empirically, the cases it organises — horses in industrialisation, pets, lab animals, factory-farmed animals, wolves under EU law, pheasants in shoots — are exactly the cases mainstream sociology has neglected, and the concept explains the pattern rather than treating each as isolated oversight. Conceptually, it locates the problem at the right level: the categories, not the data. Politically, it gives critical animal studies a name to fight under, as "heteronormativity" once gave queer theory a name.

The line I find most persuasive is the closing one: "ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (p. 93). Once. The single move opens the discipline up. I find the diagnosis highly convincing — convincing enough to make the rest of the work intelligible.

### M5: Critically assess Carter and Charles's argument in "The Animal Challenge to Sociology" in light of contemporary factory farming.

Carter and Charles's argument is that sociology has misperceived its object by silently restricting "the social" to the human, and that the empirical, conceptual, political and ecological costs of this restriction are now too great to defend. Factory farming is the contemporary case where the argument is at its sharpest. I will test the argument's four moves against it.

Their first move: "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 79, citing Bryant). Their second: sociology's neglect has historical causes — a "fraught relationship with biology" (p. 80), urban-industrial provincialism, the inheritance of human exceptionalism. Their third: the vocabulary is anthroponormative (p. 88). Their fourth: the repair is a relational realism where agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90), and animals can be "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68, cited p. 86). The slogan is "the social *is* animal" (p. 93).

Factory farming tests the argument on every move. Roughly 80 billion land animals are slaughtered each year inside vertically integrated agribusiness chains: industrial poultry sheds with tens of thousands of broilers, sow gestation crates that prevent turning around, cattle feedlots producing manure lagoons and methane plumes. The system is structurally industrial (labour, capital, regulation, supply chains, consumer markets) and structurally biological (bovine rumen physiology, avian disease ecology, antibiotic-resistance dynamics). Anthroponormative sociology can describe one side or the other; only Carter and Charles's framework can describe both as a single multispecies practice.

The empirical move holds up. Migrant slaughterhouse workers — disproportionately Latino in the US, Eastern European in the UK, with the highest non-fatal injury rates of any US industry and documented PTSD elevations — are co-producers of the meat coming off the line. Their labour is structured around animal bodies; line speed, cuts, chemicals, chilling and cleaning calibrate to the species being processed. The claim that animals are "occupants of social positions" (p. 86) is here a literal description.

The conceptual move holds up too, but only with the revised agency concept. Cartesian agency cannot describe a sow whose entire reproductive life is structured by a gestation crate. Relational agency can: the sow has purposes (nurse, move, socialise) engaging agential conditions (bars, lighting, feed schedule, slaughter age). "Sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages" (p. 92). Agency is recognised in being denied — more analytically powerful than welfarist sympathy because it lets sociology describe the system rather than just deplore it.

The political move holds up with sharpening. Animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (p. 80). The politics is not just about animals; it is racialised migrant labour, public health (antibiotic resistance, COVID-19 outbreaks clustered in US meat plants in 2020), climate change (livestock methane is roughly 14.5% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions), biodiversity loss (Amazon clearance for cattle and soy) and consumer culture. The framework lets sociology describe these as interlocking multispecies questions rather than disconnected specialisms.

Where the argument needs supplementation is at the methodological-affective level. The structural-realist idiom is right for system analysis but thin on what it is like to interview slaughterhouse workers, visit a CAFO, write about pigs in a sow stall. Haraway's becoming-with and "staying with the trouble" supply the missing register. Haraway is harder to deploy on factory farming than on intimate cases, but her insistence that "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 29) is the right normative orientation.

A residual worry is normative reach. Carter and Charles state they are not engaged in animal advocacy (p. 93). But the analytic recognition that factory farming is structured domination of agential beings makes a normative judgement nearly unavoidable. This is a strength: "factory farming is wrong" follows from the sociology rather than being imposed on it.

The argument holds up brilliantly under the factory-farming test. It explains the phenomenon, supplies the vocabulary, articulates the politics and frames the ecology. Where it needs help it gets it from Haraway. "The social *is* animal" (p. 93) is not rhetorical inflation; it is the most economical description of what factory farming actually is.

### M6: How does multispecies sociology help us understand the COVID-19 pandemic? Answer with reference to Haraway and Carter and Charles.

COVID-19 is the case that breaks human-only sociology and demands multispecies sociology — its causal chain, its labour politics, its mortality patterns and its companion-animal afterlife are all multispecies all the way down. Haraway supplies the relational ontology; Carter and Charles supply the disciplinary repair. Together they make the pandemic intelligible in ways a sociology of human societies cannot.

Begin with origins. The leading hypothesis traces SARS-CoV-2 to a bat coronavirus, possibly via an intermediate host, surfacing in a Wuhan wet market or laboratory environment. The competing hypotheses do not change the multispecies character of the question: zoonotic spillover happens when a pathogen leaks from animal populations into human populations under conditions of habitat destruction, wildlife trade, industrial confinement or scientific manipulation. Carter and Charles's framing applies: "the agency of wolves… changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 91) generalises to bats and pangolins whose ecological possibilities are restructured by deforestation and wildlife markets. The bat is an agent in the relational sense; the pandemic is what those restructured conditions allowed.

Now the supply chain. Some of the most severe 2020 outbreaks clustered in industrial meat-processing plants — Smithfield in South Dakota, JBS plants, German pork facilities. The plants combine high-density labour, cold processing environments that favour aerosols, and a migrant workforce pressured to continue working through illness. Animals are "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68, cited p. 86) literally and politically: animal lives, labour processes, disease ecology and the racial-class structure of the workforce form a single multispecies system. A sociology of pandemic labour that does not include the pigs and chickens cannot explain why these particular plants were the hotspots.

Mortality patterns. COVID-19 mortality is famously stratified by class, race and age, and also by multispecies entanglements. Indigenous communities lost elders alongside the ecological knowledge they carried. Care-home residents died in part because the staffing pipeline (precarious carers moving between facilities) is itself a multispecies labour structure. The differential burden of air pollution that PigeonBlog targeted — "rates of human (and other-than-human, but that is not rated) mortality and illness, often distributed by race and class" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 20) — was a documented co-factor for COVID severity.

Companion-animal afterlife. The pandemic generated a surge in pet adoption, sometimes followed by a return surge as people went back to offices. Companion species "play string figure games where who is/are to be in/of the world is constituted in intra- and interaction" (Haraway, p. 13). The pandemic produced new household entanglements — humans and dogs becoming-with through lockdown walks, humans and cats through home offices — and partly dissolved them. Kimbo's framing fits: "shared experience is what matters foremost" (lecture slide on Harald) when two centres of feeling attune through cohabitation. Pandemic pet-keeping is multispecies emotional labour mental-health sociology now has to take seriously.

Haraway's wider principle helps with the politics. "We are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (p. 29). The pandemic distributed response-ability unevenly: low-wage workers could not work from home; meat-plant workers could not avoid exposure; pets could not consent to adoption or relinquishment; bats were the original conditions of possibility.

Carter and Charles supply the disciplinary anchor. Their warning that human-only sociology is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (p. 81) is borne out by COVID. A sociology of the pandemic that omits bats, intermediate hosts, industrial livestock, slaughterhouse animals and companion animals is missing the description, not the supplementary detail. Their relational agency concept (p. 90) extends naturally to the pandemic ecology; anthroponormativity (p. 88) names the habit that has stopped much pandemic sociology from doing this work.

The objection that this overloads sociology with biology misses the point. The social/biological boundary is precisely what the pandemic crossed. Carter and Charles want sociology reformed, not replaced (p. 87); the discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" once its categories are reworked. COVID is the test of whether the reworking has begun in earnest.

So multispecies sociology helps us understand COVID by letting us describe it accurately: as a zoonotic-industrial-environmental-affective event whose causal logic, labour politics and household afterlife are all multispecies. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93) is not a slogan when the social happens to be a pandemic. It is the only description that fits.

### M7: Is animal studies the next step in sociology or a category mistake?

I argue that animal studies is the next step in sociology — explicitly modelled, on Carter and Charles's own account, on the analogous step feminism took — and that the category-mistake objection rests on a circular definition. The field's work is to move from stage two to stage four of the feminist trajectory; the objection's work is to defend a definitional move that has not survived analogous challenges.

The category-mistake objection: sociology is the study of human societies; animals are not human; therefore animals are not the proper object of sociology, and "animal studies" misclassifies a biological topic as a sociological one. It is the silent assumption of introductory textbooks that define the discipline in human-only terms.

Carter and Charles's reply is that the major premise is a contingent disciplinary self-description, not a discovered fact, and calling it a category boundary smuggles a normative move into what pretends to be a definition. "Much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 88). This is anthroponormativity — the silent species-norm analogous to heteronormativity and white normativity. The category-mistake objection is not a category judgement; it is anthroponormativity dressed up as one.

The "next step" framing is Carter and Charles's own. The four-stage feminist trajectory from Walby, Oakley and Charles: pre-feminist neglect; critique; adding women in; and "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (p. 80). Animal studies "is experiencing the second and third stages." The critique is made (anthroponormativity is now a published term); empirical work proceeds (multispecies ethnography, critical animal studies, Greenebaum's vegan typology, Kimbo's PhD on Sociology, Sociality and Animals). What remains is conceptual integration: reworking agency, society, labour, family and modernity around the recognition that the social is multispecies.

The empirical case is strong. "The social *is* animal" (p. 93) is supported by accumulating work on horsepower (Greene), pet-keeping, pheasant shoots, wolf jurisdictional politics, factory farming, lab animals, conservation ecology and zoonotic disease. Industrial modernity ran on horses (Greene, *Horses at Work*, p. 170); contemporary capitalism runs on broilers and pigs; COVID-19 ran on bats and meat plants; the climate crisis runs in part on livestock methane. A discipline that cannot describe these is not maintaining a category; it is misperceiving its object.

The conceptual case is stronger. Carter and Charles's relational agency — "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90) — is a sociological move, not a biological one. The pheasant shoot as "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92) is a sociological description in good standing. So is PigeonBlog: pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 18) in a project that produced data official monitors could not (p. 21). Neither collapses sociology into biology.

Two sophisticated forms of the objection. First, the symbolic-interactionist line: humans alone possess reflexive symbol-use, so the social as symbolic order is properly human. The reply is partly empirical (animal communication, mirror tests in pigeons, chimpanzee tool cultures, octopus cognition) and partly conceptual: even if human reflexive symbol-use is structurally distinctive, that does not entail that animals lack any agency or culture; the slide from "different in structure" to "not really sociological" is anthroponormative. Second, the disciplinary-identity worry. Carter and Charles want sociology reformed, not replaced (p. 87), and reject Latourian symmetry as overcorrection.

The analogy with feminism is sharper as diagnosis than as prediction. Women entered the academy and spoke for themselves; animals cannot. The methodological vocabulary of multispecies research had to be invented (Despret, Haraway, Kimbo). The drivers of stage-three-to-stage-four progress will be ecological and political (Anthropocene, factory-farming critiques, animal-rights pressure) rather than demographic.

So animal studies is the next step, on the trajectory gender, race and post-coloniality took. The category-mistake charge is the contemporary version of charges once made against women's studies, Black studies and queer theory. Each relied on a definitional move that did not survive scrutiny; anthroponormativity is the name of the move when the boundary is species. The work has begun.

### M8: Compare Haraway's companion species with Carter and Charles's "constitutive of society" framing.

I argue that the two framings are complementary moves toward the same multispecies sociology, operating at different registers: Haraway supplies a relational ontology, Carter and Charles supply structural-realist disciplinary reform. The most useful sociology uses both.

Carter and Charles's framing is in their key claim: sociology requires "a reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 80). Constitutive does precise work: not "animals plus humans equals society" (which still treats each as pre-given) nor "animals replace humans as sociology's object" (which is category-swap not category-critique). It is the claim that society as a structure of social positions includes animals as occupants: "non-human animals" can be "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68, cited p. 86). The pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (p. 92).

Their agency repair underwrites this. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). Animals have purposes engaging relational conditions; this makes them agents without requiring Cartesian subjecthood. The wolf example shows agency is jurisdictional: a wolf's "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (p. 91).

Haraway's companion species is a different move. "The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). The claim is ontological: the kinds "human" and "animal" are not given prior to the practices that produce them. Becoming-with is the operational vocabulary: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (p. 12). The clearest illustration: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25).

Compare on three axes. First, register: Carter and Charles operate at the disciplinary level (sociology's vocabulary); Haraway operates at the ontological level (kinds-first metaphysics). Carter and Charles want reform; Haraway is closer to displacement. Second, scale: Carter and Charles's framework is built for structural analysis (factory farming, EU wolf policy, pheasants and dogs in shoots); Haraway's is built for situated micro-analysis (PigeonBlog, dog-human training, Project PigeonWatch's DC schoolchildren learning to see pigeons as "sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, p. 24)). Out of scale each loses traction. Third, normative orientation: Carter and Charles state they are not engaged in animal advocacy (p. 93); Haraway articulates response-ability as graduated obligation across "ecologies, economies, species, lives" (p. 29), wary of some animal-rights framings (she praises Batman Park's pigeon-egg-replacement programme as ecological management, not rights advocacy).

Complementarity is straightforward. Carter and Charles tell us animals are inside the social and rework sociology to describe them; Haraway tells us the kinds we work with are themselves produced through the entanglements we describe. Without Carter and Charles, Haraway risks slipping outside sociology. Without Haraway, Carter and Charles's structural realism risks reifying "humans" and "animals" as the kinds they want to relate.

Consider PigeonBlog. Carter and Charles describe it as multispecies practice in which racing pigeons are occupants of social positions co-constituted by working-class fanciers, art-activists and the city's pollution geography. Haraway describes how the pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (p. 18) and how partners were "rendered capable of mutual trust" (p. 22) — the texture the structural account leaves undescribed. Consider factory farming. Carter and Charles describe the system: capital, labour, regulation, the relational agency of pigs in gestation crates whose conditions thwart their purposes (p. 92). Haraway describes what is destroyed: the becoming-with industrial breeding forecloses, the response-ability slaughterhouse workers are denied as much as the pigs.

The two framings are not rivals. They are layers — disciplinary repair from Carter and Charles, relational ontology from Haraway. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93) and "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, p. 13) are saying the same thing from different angles. Read one without the other and the sociology is partial.

### M9: Can sociology decentre the human without dissolving its disciplinary identity?

Yes — and Carter and Charles's article is a careful demonstration that the decentring can be done from inside the discipline rather than at its expense. The worry is that taking animals seriously will collapse sociology into biology, into Latourian flat ontology, or into philosophical post-humanism. All three slides are avoidable if we rework concepts rather than abandon them, and the resulting discipline is more, not less, recognisably sociological than its anthroponormative predecessor.

The worry is reasonable. Sociology was built partly through defence against biology. The discipline insisted on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12, cited Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 82) and later distanced itself from biology because of its association with eugenics and scientific racism (p. 83). Durkheim's struggle against Spencer, Weber's against social Darwinism, mid-twentieth-century opposition to behaviourism — all were boundary-defences.

The reply has three parts.

First, Carter and Charles do not advocate dissolution. They reject Latour and the new materialists, who treat humans, animals and machines as symmetrical "actants". Sociology is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (p. 87) once its concepts are reworked. Reform, not replacement. Their relational realism keeps emergent structures, social positions and causal explanation; it loses only the silent species-norm.

Second, the decentring runs through the existing sociological agenda. Every standard object becomes more intelligible once animals are admitted. Greene's horses make industrialisation legible at the level Marx missed; the pheasant shoot makes class and leisure legible as "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92); the slaughterhouse makes the labour-and-migration sociology of meat legible as a single multispecies process; the pandemic makes urban public health legible as a zoonotic-industrial system; PigeonBlog makes environmental justice legible as multispecies citizen science (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 21).

Third, the analogy with previous decentrings shows the worry was always overstated. Feminist sociology decentred the male subject without dissolving sociology; post-colonial sociology decentred the Western subject; queer theory decentred the heterosexual subject. The worry — "if we expand the subject, the discipline collapses" — has each time been an artefact of the previously narrow definition. Animal studies is between stages two and three of the feminist trajectory (p. 80); stage four does not dissolve sociology any more than feminist stage four did.

Two cases sharpen the answer. Factory farming: a human-only sociology describes meat-processing as a labour-and-food-policy issue. A multispecies sociology — Carter and Charles plus Haraway — describes the same plant as a unified process where migrant labour, animal lives, antibiotic ecology, public health and consumer culture form a single system. More sociological, not less. Companion-animal therapy: a human-only sociology describes it as a mental-health intervention. A multispecies sociology — adding becoming-with — describes a relational entanglement in which dog and human are rendered capable of each other. Kimbo's framing applies: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless" (lecture slide).

A residual question about method. Animals do not fill out questionnaires; sociology's standard repertoire is built around interviews, human ethnography and quantitative analysis. Multispecies methods are being built: Despret's ethology, Kimbo's "thinking with" companion animals, Haraway's situated multispecies ethnography, Anna Tsing's slow ecological observation. None dissolves sociological method; they extend it. Situated knowledge is itself a sociological inheritance through feminist standpoint theory.

Disciplinary identity as institution — departments, journals, curricula. The worry is that admitting animals will make sociology indistinguishable from anthropology, geography or environmental studies. The reply is that neighbouring disciplines already do multispecies work, and the traffic strengthens sociology when it contributes its distinctive resources — structural analysis, political economy, the relational realism Carter and Charles articulate.

Carter and Charles's closing line names the wager. "Ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (p. 93). Change, not dissolve. Sociology is what survives the change; what falls away is anthroponormativity, not the discipline. Sociology's identity is in its questions and modes of explanation, not the species of its objects. The discipline that decentres the human without dissolving its identity is the one that finally answers its own questions accurately.

### M10: "Animals can communicate, but they don't obviously have human language." Discuss with reference to at least two readings.

The quotation comes from the lecturer's slide on "Some people want to retain an element of human exceptionalism", and I take it as a useful starting point for an argument that the language line is not where the action is in multispecies sociology. With reference to Carter and Charles and Haraway, the distinction between communication and human language is real but does not justify excluding animals from sociology — because language is not the basis on which either includes animals in the social.

The quotation acknowledges what the empirical record now shows: animals communicate. Chimpanzees and bonobos have learned sign and lexigram communication; great apes and corvids pass theory-of-mind tests; cetaceans demonstrate referential signalling and pod-specific dialects; pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 18); octopuses exhibit cognition that has surprised everyone. Animal communication is structured differently from human language, not unstructured.

What the quotation retains is the claim that human language has structural features — recursion, productivity, displacement, full syntax — that animal systems do not match. By current evidence this is mostly correct. The question is what follows.

The symbolic-interactionist tradition the lecturer associates with Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard takes language-distinctiveness as a basis for excluding animals: meaningful social action requires reflexive symbol-use only humans possess. Mead's *Mind, Self and Society* is the clearest classical statement.

Carter and Charles's reply is on conceptual ground. The slide from "humans have distinctive language" to "therefore the social is properly human" is anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 88). The slide is a definitional configuration that pretends to be an inference.

Their positive move redefines agency in non-linguistic terms. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (p. 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (p. 92). Linguistic competence is not the criterion; relational engagement of purposes with conditions is. A wolf in a Swedish EU-protected forest is an agent (p. 91); a pheasant in a UK shoot is an agent in "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (p. 92); a chicken in a battery cage is an agent whose conditions thwart its purposes. None of this requires the animal to be a language-user.

Animal culture is the related case. "Animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (p. 88, citing de Waal). Chimpanzee tool-use traditions differ between populations; killer whale vocal dialects are pod-specific. If culture means inter-generationally transmitted group-specific learned practice, animals have culture without human language. The lecturer presses the right question: "Why do we want to keep culture to ourselves?"

Haraway deflates the language question by changing the level of analysis. Becoming-with is constituted through embodied co-attunement that includes but exceeds linguistic communication. PigeonBlog's racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, p. 18); the artist-researchers had "to render each other capable of mutual trust" (p. 22). This trust is built through training, feeding, handling, repeated joint flights, not human language. The Despret line is decisive: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25). The competences are practical and embodied, not primarily linguistic.

Could the language line be defended on weaker grounds — "human social practice is distinctive enough to merit its own discipline"? More defensible but less interesting. Sociology can recognise human linguistic distinctiveness and still include animals as relational agents. Carter and Charles deny only that distinctiveness justifies definitional exclusion. The counter-objection that extending sociology dilutes "social action" misfires: the relational view distinguishes agents (beings with purposes engaging conditions) from non-agents (rocks, gas). The chicken qualifies; the gas does not.

So the quotation is true but undercuts itself. Animals communicate without human language. Carter and Charles and Haraway both build multispecies sociology on grounds that do not require animals to share human language. The language line is worth preserving as a fact about the world; it is not a justification for anthroponormative exclusion. The lecturer's own framing pushes the point: animals "can communicate (though they don't obviously have human language)" and "animals have culture." That sentence ends the case for the language-based exclusion.

### M11: Using the work of at least two authors, assess whether animal welfare and animal rights demand the same sociological framework.

I argue, drawing on Carter and Charles and Haraway, that animal welfare and animal rights do not demand the same political framework — but both fit inside a more general multispecies sociology whose categories accommodate them while preserving the political distinctness of each. Welfare and rights name two ethical-political projects with different histories, institutional carriers and disciplinary affinities; sociology can describe both without endorsing either.

Begin with the distinction. Animal welfare is the position that humans may use animals (for food, labour, research, companionship, entertainment) but must minimise their suffering. It accepts the use and regulates the conditions. Its expressions include the UK's Animal Welfare Act, EU slaughter and transport regulations, the Five Freedoms, RSPCA codes, certified-humane labelling. Animal rights is the position that animals have interests of their own that ground duties humans owe them — duties that may include prohibiting certain uses entirely. Tom Regan, Peter Singer (utilitarian rather than rights strictly), Gary Francione, and the New York lawyer who in 2017 sought legal personhood for chimpanzees defend versions of the position. Welfare typically accepts factory farming if reformed; rights typically denies it in principle.

These are not the same problem with different reform programmes. Welfare treats animals as objects-with-interests; rights as subjects-with-claims. Welfare aims at better cages, rights at empty cages. Welfare works through state and corporate regulation; rights through movement advocacy and litigation. A sociology that conflates them misperceives the political field.

But both projects presume animals are agents with morally relevant interests, and the relational-realist concept lets sociology describe both. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 90). A battery hen has agency-thwarted-by-conditions; a sanctuary chicken has agency-enabled-by-conditions. Welfare works on conditions inside the use; rights on the use itself. Both are describable as politics of agential conditions — a single sociological category accommodating two political projects.

The shared stake: animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (p. 80). The sociology can describe the strategic choices, institutional partnerships (rights with movement organisations, welfare with regulators), cultural dynamics (Greenebaum's three-type vegan analysis maps onto these distinctions), and contradictions (welfare reforms may legitimise continued use; rights demands may slow welfare gains).

Haraway adds two moves. First, she is wary of certain rights framings, refusing what she calls "prolife project[s]… in the chilling American sense of that term" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 28). Her example is the Batman Park pigeon loft in Melbourne, where humans replace pigeon eggs with artificial ones — multispecies management, not rights advocacy. "We are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (p. 29). Graduated response-ability gives a vocabulary for distinguishing the welfare-rights field from a more thoroughgoing ecological politics neither fully captures.

Second, becoming-with cuts across the distinction. PigeonBlog's working-class fanciers might or might not endorse animal-rights politics; they certainly love and care for their birds in ways neither welfare regulation nor rights litigation fully describes. "Rendered capable of mutual trust" with the birds (p. 22), the Despret line that without the fancier "what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (p. 25) — Haraway captures a register political philosophy does not.

The assessment. Welfare and rights do not demand the same political-philosophical scaffolding; they make different ethical claims. They do demand the same disciplinary apparatus for describing multispecies practice — both are intelligible inside Carter and Charles's relational realism and Haraway's relational ontology.

Two cases sharpen the point. Cage-free eggs is a welfare reform improving agential conditions inside continued use; the sociology — supply chains, consumer demand, certified labels, class-stratified purchasing — is Carter and Charles's toolkit applied to a multispecies industry. Closing zoos is closer to rights politics, questioning the legitimacy of the use itself; the sociology — advocacy movements, urban public space, imperial collection, zookeeper labour, captive welfare — is the same multispecies sociology describing a more radical project. Haraway's wariness of pure rights idioms applies: closing zoos may help or harm species conservation depending on what replaces them.

So welfare and animal rights are politically distinct but sociologically compatible. Different politics, shared sociology. The sociology keeps both projects intelligible, and the differences between them are part of what it must describe.

### M12: Does generative AI demand a multispecies framework too?

The question is sharp and I want to take it seriously. My answer is that generative AI does not literally demand a multispecies framework — large language models are not species — but it does demand a relational framework very close in shape to multispecies sociology, and the conceptual tools Carter and Charles and Haraway developed for animals transfer with surprisingly little distortion. The answer is a hedged yes.

Begin with the obvious disanalogy. Animals are evolved, embodied, sentient beings with purposes (live, eat, breed, navigate) engaging relational conditions. Carter and Charles's agency concept is calibrated to this: "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 90). Generative AI systems are not evolved, not embodied (except as silicon and electricity), and almost certainly not sentient — the consensus is that current models do not have the unified experience animals demonstrably possess. Applying "agency" to GPT-4 in the same sense it applies to a wolf is to mis-extend the concept.

But the relevant question is whether the framework that lets us think relationally about animals also lets us think relationally about AI. Largely yes. Carter and Charles's deepest move — against the Cartesian inheritance that treats agency as a property of self-conscious rational subjects, in favour of an emergent-relational view — is structurally suited to thinking about distributed socio-technical systems regardless of whether the non-human components are biological.

Anthroponormativity transfers cleanly. Sociology's species-norm has an analogous silent norm in some treatments of AI: only human reasoning counts as cognition; AI outputs are either anthropomorphic projections or mere computation. The slide from "different in structure" to "not really sociologically relevant" is the same anthroponormative move (p. 88). Calling ChatGPT "just a stochastic parrot" can be the right call or a definitional move that pre-empts empirical investigation.

Becoming-with is the most directly transferable concept. "The partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13). Humans using generative AI are demonstrably becoming-with the system: writing habits change, prompting becomes a craft, the boundaries between "my work" and "the model's contribution" blur in ways that recall the boundary-blurring for humans and dogs. The Despret line — without the lover of pigeons, without apprenticeship and transmission of practices, "what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, p. 25) — has an analogue: without users, fine-tuners and deploying organisations, what would remain would be model weights but not the AI systems users actually engage with.

The labour question is where the analogy is most useful. AI systems are trained on data produced by human workers — annotators, content moderators, RLHF raters — often poorly paid and globally distributed, and on data produced by all of us writing on the internet without explicit consent. The migrant slaughterhouse worker and the Kenyan content moderator are sociologically describable as occupants of related positions in supply chains where non-human components (pigs, large language models) are co-produced by and co-shape human labour. The framework that lets us see both owes a lot to "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, p. 93) — minus the species claim, plus the structural insight.

The Anthropocene framing also transfers. Generative AI data centres consume significant energy; their water and carbon footprints are non-trivial. Haraway's "we are not all response-able in the same ways" (p. 29) extends to AI infrastructure that is itself already multispecies (data-centre cooling affects water tables, fish, birds and ecosystems).

A sharper distinction is worth registering. AI systems do not suffer; their "interests" are not morally analogous to animal interests; the politics of AI is not a politics of agential conditions the way welfare politics is. Treating AI as a companion species can flatter the technology and obscure the human power behind it. Haraway's distinction between "com-post" and posthumanism (p. 11) applies: we should not slide into flat ontology.

So generative AI demands a relational sociology in the shape of multispecies sociology — the same anti-anthroponormative diagnosis, relational agency concept, becoming-with method, Anthropocene framing — applied to socio-technical systems rather than biological species. What does the work in Carter and Charles and Haraway is not "animality" per se but the relational ontology animals forced into view. Once that ontology is on the table, it serves any non-human system whose practical entanglements with humans co-produce social outcomes. The multispecies turn taught sociology to think relationally; the AI turn tests whether the lesson generalises. It does, with care.

### M13: "Pets are kin." Discuss the politics and ethics of treating companion animals as family.

The phrase condenses one of the most politically and ethically charged sites in human–animal relations. My argument, through Haraway's companion species and Carter and Charles's relational sociology, is that treating companion animals as family is sociologically defensible and politically productive — but only if held alongside an honest recognition of the structural asymmetries that distinguish pet-keeping from human family relations.

Begin with the empirical reality. In contemporary Britain about half of households include a pet; in the US the figure is higher. Many of these animals are referred to, mourned for, and legally provided for as family; pet-trust law allows bequests; companion-animal therapy is recognised in mental-health practice. The lecturer's slide on Kimbo's dog Harald captures the register: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless." Kinship here is lived practice, not metaphor.

Haraway gives the most developed language. Companion species "are relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (p. 13). The *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003) makes the case: humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand", and the dog is not metaphor but a being co-shaped through millennia of co-evolution, breeding, labour and domestic life. Haraway insists on "significant otherness" — humans and dogs do not collapse but co-shape each other.

Carter and Charles support this disciplinarily. The dog in the household is an agent whose purposes engage household conditions; agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*, p. 90). A dog choosing to sleep on a particular sofa, greet a particular person, refuse to walk in the rain, is an agent in the sociological sense. Treating that agent as kin is recognition rather than anthropomorphism.

The politics. Treating pets as kin contests the species-citizenship boundary, puts pressure on legal categories (custody battles increasingly treat pets less as property than as quasi-dependents), and valorises emotional labour traditionally feminised and unpaid. Greenebaum's vegan typology (health, environmental, ethical) partly aligns; ethical vegans extend kinship logic from pets to all animals, refusing the "kin in the house, food on the plate" asymmetry.

The ethics. The strongest claim is response-ability: "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter — in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, p. 29). Kinning a pet is graduated response-ability — feed, walk, train, attend, mourn — that the pet cannot reciprocate in the same way but is no less a real ethical relation. Kimbo: "If two individual bodies… open themselves up or attune to each other… shared experience is what matters foremost" (lecture slide). Attunement is the ethical mode.

But the asymmetries are real. Pets are bred, bought, sold, neutered, euthanised on human decisions; their kinship is non-chosen. Pet ownership is class-stratified. The pedigree industry reproduces eugenic logics (brachycephaly in pugs, hip dysplasia in retrievers) for cosmetic ends. The pet food industry is an arm of the factory-farming complex; companion animals are major consumers of industrial meat. Pet abandonment is a low-grade version of factory-farming production-and-disposal logic. Dog-breeding has ties to colonial taxonomy and eugenic ideologies imported from human racial pseudo-science. Pet care labour is feminised, often tracking childcare. Companion-animal therapy for veterans, autistic children and the elderly raises questions about whether pets fill gaps public services should fill.

Two objections. First, treating pets as kin diverts attention from animals worse-treated. The reply: kinship logic, fully extended, generates ethical attention to those other animals; pet-keeping is a school of multispecies attunement that expands outward. Second, pet-kinship is a Global North luxury. Empirically false: pet-keeping is widespread globally in different forms, and the vocabulary travels with modifications.

Carter and Charles's anthroponormativity diagnosis (p. 88) underwrites the broader claim. Refusing kinship to companion animals because they are non-human is exactly the definitional move anthroponormativity makes. Saying "pets are kin" is not stretching family beyond recognition; it is recognising that family was always already capable of multispecies extension, as human–dog co-evolution shows.

So the politics and ethics of treating companion animals as family are defensible and productive — provided the kinship claim is held with the asymmetries in full view. Pets are kin; the kinship is real, ethical and politically charged. It is also asymmetric, class-stratified, racially complicated, gendered, and entangled with the factory-farming complex it appears to oppose. The slogan is true; the work is in the qualifications.
