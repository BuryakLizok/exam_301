## 2. Key concepts and thinkers

- **Human exceptionalism**: the doctrine that humans alone possess intention, morality, symbolism, consciousness, and so stand outside natural law (slide on "Human Exceptionalism"). Sociology, on Carter and Charles's reading, is built on this premise.
- **Bifurcation / the animal–human binary**: the dualistic carve-up nature/culture, genes/environment, facts/values, animal/human (Carter and Charles, 2011: 2, cited in slides).
- **Anthroponormativity**: Carter and Charles's term for sociology's conceptual vocabulary being silently configured around the human, analogous to heteronormativity or white normativity.
- **Companion species** (Haraway): humans become humans through co-shaping relations with other species; not "humans AND animals" but mutually constitutive becoming.
- **Becoming-with / rendering-capable** (Haraway, Despret): partners do not pre-exist the knot; they are made what they are through entanglement.
- **Multispecies sociology / multispecies ethnography**: research that treats animals as social actors and co-constituents of society, not as mute "nature."
- **Situated knowledge**: knowledge is always located in specific bodies and relations; pertinent here to "thinking with" rather than "about" animals (Kimbo, Despret).
- **Classical thinkers**: Durkheim (sociability as social, observable in animals); Weber (animals can "understand" but verifiable meaningful access is limited); Marx (productive labour, bees); Greene (horses as living machines, prime movers of industrial modernity); Parsons (consolidator of the strictly human social).

## 4. Information from the readings

**Carter and Charles, 'The animal challenge to sociology' (2018).** The article argues that sociology has been "slow to take up the animal challenge" (2018: 79), despite the obvious fact that "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Bryant 1979, cited 2018: 79). Three reasons are diagnosed. *First*, sociology has had a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80): in its late-nineteenth-century institutionalisation, the discipline defended "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton 1991: 12) against Darwinian "comprehensive naturalism", and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. *Second*, sociology rests on **human exceptionalism**: humans are treated as evolutionarily unique because of language and meaning-making, and "the human" is "usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Ingold 1994a, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). *Third*, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe, where urban dwellers experienced themselves as "increasingly remote from most other animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). Yet, drawing on Greene (2008), Carter and Charles insist horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84) and the "prime mover" of industrial development.

The article then argues for two conceptual revisions. **Society** must be redefined to include non-human animals as "occupants of social positions" (Benton 1993: 68). **Agency** must be reworked as relational: agency is not a Cartesian property of rational subjects but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90); it is "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Their pheasant-shoot example dramatises a "complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Their concluding claim is uncompromising: "the social is animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). Sociology's whole conceptual vocabulary is "anthroponormative" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), analogous to heteronormative and white-normative vocabularies, and must be reworked, not abandoned (as Latour and the new materialists would have it).

**Haraway, 'Playing String Figures with Companion Species' (2016).** The chapter develops a method for thinking with animals through the metaphor of cat's cradle: "sf" doubles for science fiction, speculative fabulation, science fact, and string figures. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13). Haraway's pigeons illustrate this: racing pigeons in Southern California are "competent agents, in the double sense of both delegates and actors" (Haraway, 2016: 18). Her key empirical case is **PigeonBlog** (Beatriz da Costa, 2006), where racing pigeons, art-activists, working-class pigeon fanciers and DIY electronics co-produced air-pollution data over polluted Los Angeles neighbourhoods. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) and the humans had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22). Response-ability, a Despret term Haraway adopts, names the obligation to make others "capable" through specific entanglements (Haraway, 2016: 12). Haraway explicitly refuses both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11). Project PigeonWatch with Washington DC schoolchildren shows recuperation across categories that racist iconography links ("rats with wings", "feral") when the kids learn to see pigeons "as sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, 2016: 24).

**Haraway, *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003).** Earlier articulation of the same project: dogs and people are not pre-given subjects encountering each other; rather "we are training each other in acts of communication we barely understand" (the manifesto's framing of becoming-with). Haraway insists on "significant otherness": humans and dogs do not collapse into one another but co-shape one another through history, labour, evolution, breed politics, and everyday domestic practice. Domestic dogs are products of millennia of co-evolution with humans; they are not metaphors or symbols for humans. This grounds the multispecies sociology that Carter and Charles call for.

## 5. Critical analysis with embedded key quotes

The lecture and the Carter and Charles reading share a single thesis: sociology has been so anxious to defend its disciplinary turf against biology that it amputated half of its empirical world. I think they are right, but the diagnosis matters more than the slogan. Carter and Charles do not just say "let's add animals in" (that would be stage three on the feminism analogy). They argue, in the article's most direct line, that **"the social *is* animal"** (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). The work is reconfiguring concepts, not extending coverage.

Their target is sharp: "the human is usually defined in contradistinction to 'the animal'" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81, citing Ingold). Sociology inherits this binary from Cartesian humanism, hardens it after Parsons, and then runs into trouble when confronted with horses ("biotechnology… living machines", Greene, 2008: 4), pheasants in shoots, or wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). The Greene quote from the slides drives the point home: "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Sociology stares at the Communist Manifesto and misses the horses dropping "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" in 1900 New York (Greene, 2008: 174).

The Weber excerpt the lecturer cites complicates the simple "classical sociology ignored animals" story. Weber concedes that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). But he immediately retreats: a "verifiable conception" of animals' state of mind is attainable "only within narrow limits" (Weber, 1922: 17). Weber half-opens the door and then closes it on epistemological grounds, and twentieth-century sociology, post-Parsons, slammed it shut. Durkheim's *Rules* leaves a similar window: "animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common" (Durkheim, 1895: 132). Sociability, for Durkheim, is not innately human.

Haraway pushes further. She refuses the "either/or" of human exceptionalism versus posthumanism. Her programmatic line is: "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). Companion species are not "ordinary beings-in-encounter" who already exist before they meet; "species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, 2016: 13). This is what Carter and Charles, in their own register, mean when they say agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90).

The critical question is whether this expanded sociology can still do the political work sociology was meant to do. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: if sociology cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, it is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Haraway makes the same wager: "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29). I think this is right. A sociology that cannot name the horses that built nineteenth-century cities, the pheasant-shoot entanglements of the contemporary countryside, the Amazon clearance for beef production or the palm-oil clearance of orangutan habitat that the lecturer flags on slide 35 is a sociology that has unilaterally disarmed in the face of its century.

## 8. Essay answers

### 2025 (Q2): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically assess this statement using the ideas set out by Carter and Charles in the key reading for this topic.

I will critically assess the claim that sociology is the study of human societies using the argument Carter and Charles develop in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018). The statement is true as a description of how the discipline has constituted itself, but Carter and Charles show it is false as a description of what the discipline should be, and that the gap matters politically, conceptually and ecologically.

Carter and Charles concede the historical accuracy of the statement. Sociology, in its institutionalisation, did define its object as human society and only human society. They give three reasons. First, sociology had a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). To establish itself, sociology insisted on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton, 1991: 12, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 82) and later distanced itself from biology because of biology's association with eugenics and scientific racism. Second, sociology was built on "human exceptionalism", the doctrine "that the human species is exempt from natural constraints… and is evolutionarily unique" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Third, sociology emerged in urban-industrial Western Europe at precisely the moment when "urban dwellers experience themselves as increasingly remote from most other animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The removal of food-animals from cities "during the second half of the nineteenth century" and of horses "in the early part of the twentieth" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84) coincided with sociology's professional formation. The discipline took the absence of visible animals for the absence of relevant animals.

The first stage of their critique is that this is empirically false. Animals were "central… to economy and society" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84, citing Tovey, 2003). Greene's case study of nineteenth-century American cities, which the lecturer foregrounds, shows that horses were "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile in the first part of the twentieth century" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). To leave horses out of an account of industrialisation is not a defensible analytic choice; it is a misdescription.

The second stage of their critique is conceptual, and it is where the article does its hardest work. Carter and Charles argue that "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). They name this configuration **anthroponormativity**, by direct analogy with heteronormativity and white normativity. Society, agency, social action, intersubjectivity and reflexivity are all silently restricted to humans. Agency, on the Cartesian inheritance, is "associated with deeply rooted assumptions about self-conscious, individual self-transformation and the 'emancipated subject'" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). When sociology uses this vocabulary, it excludes animals not by argument but by definition.

Carter and Charles then offer their alternative. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action relevant to that purpose" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). It is "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). On this realist-relational view, non-human animals are agential beings: entangled, "enfolded in all sorts of relations with humans (many of which are fatal for them)" and finding "that their agential conditions are modified involuntarily" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Their pheasant-shoot example is precise: a shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). To call this human interaction alone, and to call the pheasants and dogs mere background, is to misperceive the practice.

The third stage is the political stake. Carter and Charles draw the analogy with feminism: sociology went through a pre-feminist phase, a critique, an "add women in" phase, and finally "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Animal studies, on their reading, is between phases two and three. The claim that "sociology is the study of human societies" is precisely the kind of definitional move that feminism and post-colonial sociology dismantled in turn. What is at stake is whether sociology can describe its actual object (industrial capitalism, the post-colonial world, the Anthropocene) without continuing to lop off half of it.

Their concluding line is uncompromising: "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). This is not animal advocacy; it is a methodological claim. "In considering animals as social actors and agents," they write, "we have provided an example of how this might be done" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). Their rejection of Latour and the new materialists is also worth noting: they do not think sociology has to be abandoned in favour of actor-network theory or post-humanism. The discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87) once its foundational concepts (society, agency, social action) are reworked.

So the statement "sociology is the study of human societies" is, on Carter and Charles's account, an inherited disciplinary self-description, not a discovered truth about what sociology can or should be. It is a description of where sociology has been, not where it has to go. To take their argument seriously is to accept that the boundary between humans and animals is not a methodological given but a political and conceptual choice, one that current ecological pressures (the Anthropocene framing on which Carter and Charles end) give us very good reasons to revise. I therefore reject the statement as a definition while accepting it as a description of past practice.

### 2024 (Q2): Why is it important to study animals within sociology?

Sociology emerged as the study of industrial modernity, and yet the most important moving parts of that modernity (the horses Greene describes, the food animals removed from cities in the second half of the nineteenth century, the insect pollinators the lecturer flags on slide 18) were systematically excluded from the sociological imagination. I want to argue, with Carter and Charles, that studying animals within sociology is not a niche enrichment of the discipline but a remedy for a structural blind spot that has, until recently, made sociology unable to describe the very societies it claims to analyse.

The first reason is empirical. There is, as Bryant pointed out in 1979 and as Carter and Charles repeat, "virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 79). Greene's history of nineteenth-century cities makes this concrete. "Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Industrial capitalism, the very object of classical sociology, ran on horsepower in the literal sense, on horses described by Greene as "living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4). The Communist Manifesto contains, as the lecturer pointedly notes, no horses. New York in 1900 had 131,000 horses producing "1,300–3,300 tons of horse manure daily" (Greene, 2008: 174). London's streets, on Ritvo's account cited by Carter and Charles, "were crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden, in addition, passers-by often encountered herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo, 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). A discipline that misses an infrastructure that large is not a discipline that has settled on its proper object; it is a discipline that has misperceived its object.

The second reason is conceptual. Sociology defines social action, agency, subjectivity, reflexivity, and even society itself through a tacit "anthroponormativity", Carter and Charles's term, modelled on heteronormativity and white normativity, for "the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences" being "configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). This is not a politically neutral choice. The Weberian inheritance is the key example: meaning-making, empathetic understanding, value-oriented action are all framed as exclusively human, even though Weber himself conceded that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility… in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). When Parsons consolidates sociology in the mid-twentieth century, he hardens this into the doctrine that sociality is properly the realm of human interaction alone. The lecturer's own framing on slide 40 is direct: to ascribe sociality to animals would, on the Parsonian view, "threaten the grounding and specificity of the social." I think this is exactly backwards: what threatens the specificity of the social is pretending it stops at the species boundary.

The third reason is political. Carter and Charles draw an explicit analogy with feminism. The trajectory is: pre-feminist neglect, critique, adding-women-in, then "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80, citing Walby, Oakley, Charles). Animal studies, they argue, is "experiencing the second and third stages" but has not yet reached the fourth. Studying animals within sociology is the work of moving the discipline from stage three to stage four, from animals as an exotic specialism to animals as constitutive of every sociological object. Just as feminism did not simply "add women" but reworked the categories of work, family, agency and the body, multispecies sociology reworks the categories of labour (horses pulling cabs, omnibuses and pit ponies in mines; bees that Marx mentioned only as the foil for human productive labour), the family (pets in Haraway's sense of companion species), and the political (the pheasant-shoot example with which Carter and Charles close their article).

The fourth reason is ecological. Carter and Charles end with the Anthropocene: a sociology that cannot speak about a planet shared with other species, they warn, is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). I think this is right. The lecturer's slide 35 flags, as concrete instances, the clearance of the Amazon for beef production and of orangutan-inhabited forests for palm oil. None of these are biological problems alone; they are social problems in which animals are agents, objects, and victims simultaneously. A sociology that defines its remit as exclusively human is not "rigorous"; it is wilfully partial.

Studying animals is also a methodological challenge. Donna Haraway insists that "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game" (Haraway, 2016: 12). Alexandra Kimbo, the lecturer's former PhD student whose work is presented on slide 28, reframes this as a question: how to think *with* (and for) other animals, as opposed to *about* them? The shift is from animals as data points or symbols to animals as research partners whose ways of mattering have to be taken seriously. The PigeonBlog project, where Beatriz da Costa's racing pigeons carried CO and NOx sensors over Los Angeles, shows what such collaboration looks like in practice: the pigeons had to "learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) and the artist-researchers and pigeon fanciers had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, 2016: 22).

To say "the social is animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) is not anthropomorphism, and it is not flat ontology. It is a recognition that the entities sociology studies (labour, family, city, modernity, capitalism, politics) were always already multispecies. Studying animals within sociology therefore does not stretch the discipline; it returns it to its proper object.

### 2023 (Q2): Why should sociology make efforts to study the relations between humans and animals?

I want to argue that sociology should make these efforts for four interlocking reasons: empirical fidelity, conceptual coherence, ethical-political relevance, and ecological necessity. The argument runs through Carter and Charles's *The Animal Challenge to Sociology* (2018) and Donna Haraway's companion species, with concrete examples drawn from Greene's horses, the pheasant shoot, and the PigeonBlog project in twenty-first-century Los Angeles.

**Empirical fidelity.** The first reason is that human–animal relations were always there, and a sociology that misses them misperceives its own object. Greene's history of nineteenth-century US cities, which the lecturer foregrounds on slides 19–22, makes this concrete: "Horses jammed the streets… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Modernity, industrialisation, capitalism (sociology's founding objects) ran on animals. Horses were "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4). They became invisible to sociologists precisely because they were too central to be seen, the way, as the lecturer puts it on slide 23, we no longer notice internet cables. Making the familiar strange is one of sociology's main tasks, and horses are a prime candidate.

The empirical case extends beyond the nineteenth century. The lecturer's slide 18 makes the point with insects: coffee, apples, almonds, watermelon, zucchini and kiwifruit are listed as crops directly dependent on pollinators. Pets, the focus of slide 16, are part of household and emotional life. Food and farming (slide 17) and the slide 35 references to Amazon-beef clearance and palm-oil orangutan-habitat clearance close the empirical loop. Carter and Charles describe wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91): protected by EU law in some places, shot under licence in others, including by Sami reindeer herders in northern Sweden. To leave all of this out of sociology because it is "biological" is to abandon the discipline's promise of grasping social totality.

**Conceptual coherence.** The second reason is that sociology's core concepts wobble badly without animals. Carter and Charles call this **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Agency, on the Cartesian inheritance, belongs to rational subjects; objects (including animals) are passive matter available for human use. But this view is incoherent once we look closely. The wolves' "agential conditions… are powerfully shaped by their relations to humans (but not only these)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). The wolf is not metaphorically an agent; the wolf's possibilities and constraints are real, relational and politically structured. Sociology that does not recognise this cannot describe nature reserves, hunting licences, or the New York lawyer's attempt the lecturer references on slide 31 to win legal personhood for two chimpanzees.

**Ethical and political relevance.** The third reason is that human–animal relations are sites of domination, exploitation and care that sociology, on its own terms, should care about. Carter and Charles are explicit: their "key claim is that animals are agents entangled in relations with humans and that these relations are frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). "Sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages; wolves and pheasants are unaware of the shooting season" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Greenebaum's three-type vegan analysis (health vegans, environmental vegans, ethical vegans), the lecturer's slide 34, picks out one social movement in which these ethical questions become political identities with class, gender and racial dimensions.

**Ecological necessity.** The fourth reason is that the Anthropocene leaves us no choice. Carter and Charles end their article by warning that a sociology that cannot speak about a multispecies planet is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant in the age of the Anthropocene" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). Haraway makes the same wager in her language of "staying with the trouble": "we are all responsible to and for shaping conditions for multispecies flourishing in the face of terrible histories… but we are not all response-able in the same ways" (Haraway, 2016: 29). Differential responsibility (by ecology, economy and species) is precisely a sociological problem.

Haraway's notion of companion species supplies a vocabulary for doing this work without falling into either anthropomorphism or flat ontology. Humans "become humans through their relations with animals" (lecture slide 26 on companion species). "Becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). PigeonBlog (Beatriz da Costa, 2006) illustrates this in practice: racing pigeons carried air-pollution sensors over Los Angeles neighbourhoods where "official government air pollution monitoring devices" (Haraway, 2016: 21) were placed away from the high-traffic working-class areas most affected. The pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing"; the artist-researchers had to "render each other capable of mutual trust" with the birds and with pigeon fancier Bob Matsuyama (Haraway, 2016: 22). Project PigeonWatch in Washington DC teaches city children to re-see despised "rats with wings" as "sociable birds with lives and deaths" (Haraway, 2016: 24).

Sociology should make these efforts, then, because animals are everywhere in the social, because sociology's own concepts already require them, because human–animal relations are political relations, and because the planetary crisis demands a discipline capable of multispecies thinking. The alternative, defending the human/animal binary as if it were a discovered fact about the world, is not rigour. It is a refusal to look.

### 2021 (Q18): "Sociology is the study of human societies." Critically analyse the limitations of this statement.

The statement captures how sociology has historically defined itself but obscures three distinct limitations, which I will analyse in turn: a historical-empirical limitation, a conceptual limitation, and a normative limitation. I argue that none of these are minor blind spots; together they amount to a structural failure that the discipline now has to repair.

**The historical-empirical limitation.** The first limitation is that the statement misdescribes the societies sociology has actually studied. Carter and Charles trace the formation of the discipline to "modern industrial capitalist societies" of Western Europe and the United States (Carter and Charles, 2018: 83). But those societies were emphatically multispecies. Greene's history shows that "horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Nineteenth-century New York had 131,000 horses producing up to 3,300 tons of manure daily (Greene, 2008: 174). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo, 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The "absence of large animals" in twentieth-century cities is not a natural condition; it is the result of historical processes (food-animal removal in the second half of the nineteenth century, horse displacement by the automobile in the early twentieth) which sociology mistook for the disappearance of animals from social life altogether. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Carter and Charles point out that "the contribution of animals and even their centrality to processes of industrialisation have been erased in sociological conceptions of the social" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The statement that sociology is the study of human societies is, in this sense, a self-fulfilling description of an empirical mistake.

**The conceptual limitation.** The second limitation is that the statement entrenches what Carter and Charles call **anthroponormativity**: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). This is the same kind of move, they argue, that earlier critiques exposed under the names heteronormativity and white normativity: a tacit norm that pretends to be a neutral definition. Agency, social action, intersubjectivity, reflexivity and even society are all silently restricted to humans. Carter and Charles trace this back partly to a particular reading of Weber, partly, drawing on Halewood (2014), to Parsons, who is "the first major sociologist to hold a conception of the social that defines it unambiguously as the realm of human interaction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). The lecturer is more direct on slide 40: "I blame Talcott Parsons!", under whose influence twentieth-century sociologists insisted that "whatever sociality is, it is a production of humans, groups of humans or systems of humans" (lecture). Yet Weber himself wrote that "many animals 'understand'… commands, anger, love, hostility and react to them in ways which are evidently often by no means purely instinctive and mechanical and in some sense both consciously meaningful… and affected by experience" (Weber, 1922: 15–16). And Durkheim wrote that "it has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, 1895: 132). The classical thinkers were less anthropocentric than their twentieth-century descendants. The "study of human societies" formula is a hardening, not a faithful inheritance.

Carter and Charles's positive proposal is a relational account of agency: "agency is not an attribute, or property, of humans or other animals or things, but an emergent product of the relations within which all things are placed" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). Their wolf example shows that agency is spatial and political: wolves are protected by EU law in one jurisdiction and shot under licence in another, and "in Sweden, wolves are protected but in the north, where the Sami live, they can be killed because they might threaten the Sami's herds of reindeer" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). Their pheasant-shoot example shows that ordinary practices are "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The conceptual limitation of the "human societies" definition is that it gives us no vocabulary for these realities except dismissal.

**The normative limitation.** The third limitation is ethical and ecological. The "human societies" definition lets sociology evade the politics of human–animal relations. Carter and Charles are explicit that these relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The horses dying in nineteenth-century streets whose carcasses Greene says "often lay for several days before being removed" (Greene, 2008: 174), the chickens in battery cages and sheep at slaughter Carter and Charles invoke (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), the Amazon clearance for beef and palm-oil clearance of orangutan habitat the lecturer flags on slide 35: none of these can be properly described by a discipline that has defined them out of scope. Haraway's response is to refuse both human exceptionalism and a flat posthumanism: "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11). Companion species, becoming-with, response-ability: her vocabulary is built precisely to do work that the "human societies" formula prevents.

There is one defence of the statement worth considering. Some sociologists (the lecturer's slide 31 lists Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard) argue that language, symbolism and reflexive meaning-making really do mark a categorical difference, and that the proper object of sociology really is the symbolic order produced by human meaning-makers. The reply, which I find persuasive, has two parts. Empirically, the categorical difference does not survive scrutiny: Carter and Charles cite de Waal in arguing that "animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Haraway notes that pigeons pass mirror tests, sharing "this capacity with, at least, human children over two years old, rhesus macaques, chimpanzees, magpies, dolphins, and elephants" (Haraway, 2016: 18). Methodologically, the argument is circular: we define the social as symbolic, define the symbolic as human, and conclude that the social is human. Anthroponormativity does the work that empirical investigation should be doing.

The three limitations are connected. The historical-empirical limitation produces an inaccurate picture of past societies. The conceptual limitation produces analytic tools that cannot grasp present societies. The normative limitation produces a discipline ill-equipped for multispecies questions of the future. The statement "sociology is the study of human societies" is not merely incomplete; it actively blocks sociology from doing what sociology is supposed to do. I therefore conclude, with Carter and Charles, that "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93), and that the definition we should defend is not "sociology is the study of human societies" but something closer to "sociology is the study of multispecies relations through which human-and-animal worlds are made."

### M1: "The social *is* animal." Discuss.

The line is Carter and Charles's (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93), and I take it as the most compressed statement of a sociological revolution the discipline has not yet absorbed. I want to unpack what it does not say, defend what it does say, and test it on Greene's horses and on Haraway's PigeonBlog, the two cases the course materials use to dramatise the claim.

The claim is not anthropomorphism: Carter and Charles are not saying chickens hold opinions. Nor is it flat ontology; they explicitly reject the Latourian move to treat humans, animals and machines as symmetrical "actants" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87). What it says is that the categories sociology uses (society, agency, social action, labour, family, modernity) were always already multispecies in their referents, and pretending otherwise is anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), the definitional sleight that once made heteronormativity feel like a neutral default.

The positive content sits in their reworked agency. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Animals are agents because they have purposes engaging relational conditions, not because they are little humans. Their pheasant-shoot example crystallises this: a UK shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The pheasants are not props; they are an "introduced species" who are "bred in their hundreds of thousands so that they can be shot" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), and their breeding cycles and flight capacities co-constitute the practice alongside the dogs who beat or pick up and their handlers.

The empirical test is the test Greene poses against Marxism. Industrial modernity ran on horses ("Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically", Greene, 2008: 170), and yet, as the lecturer dryly observes on slide 20, the Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Marx's productive labour and his metabolism with nature ran on the "living machines" Greene describes (Greene, 2008: 4) he never wrote about. Greene insists horses "stimulated the development of an array of technologies and devices: vehicles, equestrian equipment, breeders' logs; and horse power reinforced other sorts of power, military, political, religious, sexual" (Greene, 2008: 5, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The slogan recovers what classical sociology amputated.

The wolves example sharpens the political point. "The agency of wolves… changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91); in Sweden they are protected, in the north where the Sami live they can be killed because they might threaten reindeer herds, in Norway they are not protected by EU legislation. A wolf is not the kind of being a human-only sociology can describe in its political register; it is a being whose conditions are produced by state, law, livelihood and habitat. "The social *is* animal" is the only formula that holds these things together.

Haraway sharpens the point by refusing the bad alternatives. "Not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11) keeps species specificity while refusing exceptionalism. Companion species "are relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13); the partners "do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13). PigeonBlog (Beatriz da Costa's 2006 art-activist project) shows the social as animal in micro: racing pigeons, working-class fanciers including Bob Matsuyama, art-activists and DIY GPS/GSM/CO/NOx sensors co-produced air-pollution data over Los Angeles neighbourhoods that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" (Haraway, 2016: 21) could not reach. Despret puts the becoming-with succinctly: "without the lover of pigeons (pigeon fancier), without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25).

The recurring objection is that extending the social to animals dissolves sociology into biology. Carter and Charles answer it: they want to reform sociology, not abandon it; the discipline is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87) once its categories are reworked. The social/biological boundary is exactly what the slogan calls into question. Defending it as fixed is defending a disciplinary self-description against a discoverable fact.

So I take "the social *is* animal" as a programme rather than a slogan: rework society, agency and social action to take animals seriously as constitutive, then use the resulting vocabulary on horsepower, the pheasant shoot, the EU wolf, PigeonBlog, the pets and pollinators the lecturer foregrounds, and the Amazon-and-palm-oil pressures Haraway's *Staying with the Trouble* opens up. Carter and Charles supply the diagnostic; Haraway supplies the method; the discipline supplies the rest if it wants to.

### M2: What does Haraway mean by companion species, and why does she treat it as ontologically prior to "human" or "animal"?

Haraway means by companion species not a list of cute domesticated animals but a relational ontology in which partners come into being through their entanglement rather than meeting as pre-formed kinds. She insists on this priority because the alternative (starting from "human" and "animal" as given categories and asking how they relate) smuggles human exceptionalism back under the cover of taxonomy. I will explain the concept and defend the priority claim.

The clearest articulation is Haraway's string-figure metaphor. "Companion species play string figure games where who is/are to be in/of the world is constituted in intra- and interaction. The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, 2016: 13). In a cat's cradle there is no string-pattern prior to the hands that pass it; the figure is the knotting. Likewise, there are no "humans" and "animals" prior to the histories of co-domestication, co-evolution, labour, breeding and affection that make them what they are. Companion species are "relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13).

The constructive term is becoming-with; its operational sister is rendering-capable, taken from Vinciane Despret: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). The compressed statement of the claim is Despret's, in the passage Haraway cites about Matali Crasset's 2003 pigeon loft at Caudry in France: "without the lover of pigeons (pigeon fancier), without the knowledge and know-how of men and birds, without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25). The homing pigeon does not pre-exist the practice that produced it; neither does the fancier.

Why is this ontologically prior to "human" and "animal"? Three reasons.

First, the empirical record cuts against pre-given kinds. Domestic pigeons "(Columba livia domestica) emerged from birds native to western and southern Europe, North Africa, and western and southern Asia" through "becoming-with people for several thousand years" (Haraway, 2016: 15). The *Companion Species Manifesto* (2003) makes the same case for dogs explicitly: humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand." The lecturer's slide 26 is in the same register: humans "become humans through their relations with animals." Similar arguments apply to the wider list of beings that Carter and Charles cite from de Waal as having "cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88).

Second, treating "human" and "animal" as prior is what generates human exceptionalism in the first place. The Cartesian inheritance ("the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive", Carter and Charles, 2018: 89) depends on already having a "human" and an "animal" to assign properties to. Haraway dissolves the problem at root: entities are constituted through the practices producing their properties. Hence "not 'posthuman' but 'com-post'" (Haraway, 2016: 11): posthumanism still names a "human" to be overcome.

Third, the priority claim does political work. PigeonBlog is unintelligible on a kinds-first ontology. The racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18); the artist-researchers had "to render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22); the fanciers, who were "frankly afraid of animal rights people and tactics" (Haraway, 2016: 24), had to be persuaded to risk the project. None of these competences existed in advance. Pigeons became air-quality scientists; humans became multispecies citizen-scientists; both produced environmental-justice data that "official government air pollution monitoring devices" could not generate (Haraway, 2016: 21).

Does the priority claim flatten species differences? No. Haraway insists on "significant otherness" (the *Manifesto* subtitle) and graduated response-ability: "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29). The priority of relation does not erase the specificity of relata.

A second objection comes from Carter and Charles themselves, who want a realist commitment to structures and emergent properties that Haraway's playful idiom risks losing (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87). The right reading is complementary: Carter and Charles supply structural-realist disciplinary repair; Haraway supplies the relational ontology that explains why repair is needed.

So companion species is the ontological claim that kinds are consequent upon entanglement: relation prior to relata. Without it, multispecies sociology slides back into "humans plus animals", the anthroponormativity Carter and Charles diagnose.

### M3: Horsepower, industrialisation and the limits of Marxism.

The title is provocative and I take it seriously. My argument is that the horse is not a footnote to Marxism but a stress test for it: Marx's productive labour, his metabolism with nature, and his political economy of industrial capitalism cannot be told without horses, and the fact that he tells them without horses exposes the anthroponormativity at the heart of his apparatus.

Begin with empirical scale. "Horses jammed the streets… They hauled streetcars, omnibuses, drays, delivery wagons, and private vehicles. Horses delivered raw materials to factories and trucked away the finished products. Horses delivered building materials to construction sites, dug foundations, powered cranes, and hauled away the dirt from excavations… Horses provided virtually all the power for the internal circulation of city life because no other prime mover could compete with them technologically" (Greene, 2008: 170). Nineteenth-century New York housed 131,000 horses producing 1,300–3,300 tons of manure daily (Greene, 2008: 174). London streets were "crowded with horses and dogs that served as draft animals and beasts of burden" alongside "herds of cattle and sheep being driven to the Smithfield live-stock market" (Ritvo, 1987: 125, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The horse was "an essential 'prime mover' of industrial development before it was finally superseded by the automobile" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). The slide 22 list (war-horse, race-horse, plough horse, pit ponies) reinforces the scale.

Now the limit. The Communist Manifesto contains no horses. Marx theorises productive labour through the human worker selling labour-power to capital, with non-human nature appearing only as raw material. The bee turns up in *Capital* only to be denied the architect's intentionality, the foil that makes human productive labour distinctive. Greene cuts through this: horses are "biotechnology, or organisms altered for human use. Through the process of domestication, horses became living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The horse is neither raw material nor human worker. It is a labouring being whose biological rhythms, breeding histories and species-specific capacities co-shaped what industrial production could be. Horses needed rest, hay, stabling and care; their working lives shaped urban infrastructure (stables, smithies, knackers' yards, manure removal) and urban temporality. A purely human labour theory of value cannot price this in.

Greene's wider claim sharpens the test. Horses "stimulated the development of an array of technologies and devices: vehicles, equestrian equipment, breeders' logs; and horse power reinforced other sorts of power, military, political, religious, sexual" (Greene, 2008: 5, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84). The "technological network that horses represent" (Greene, 2008: 5) is precisely what a human-only Marxism cannot describe. Carter and Charles add that this extends to "the whole colonial project" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 84): horses were not only metropolitan but imperial agents.

The conceptual repair is Carter and Charles's relational agency. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). The horse is an agent in industrial modernity because its purposes (live, eat, move, reproduce) engage agential conditions (capitalist demand for traction, urban density, omnibus rhythms, feed costs) to produce outcomes humans alone could not. The erasure is Marx's, but it is also Durkheim's, Weber's and Parsons's, what Carter and Charles label anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88).

What survives of Marxism? More than the title suggests. Capital accumulation, exploitation, class struggle and crisis remain powerful, but extended to multispecies labour processes. Carter and Charles's wolves example, with its EU/Swedish/Norwegian/Sami jurisdictional layering (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91), already shows how political economy structures animal lives. Their pheasant-shoot example shows that ordinary practices in the contemporary British countryside are "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), with breeding regimes, gun rooms and beaters' wages all calibrated to pheasants as the agents around which the practice is organised.

Haraway's contribution to the same limit is becoming-with. Horse and human are not separate kinds plugging into industrial production; they are companion species whose joint capacities were produced through millennia of domestication, breeding and labour. The carthorse pulling a brewer's dray is not a "natural" entity meeting an "industrial" entity; it is a co-produced being inside an entanglement that is social-and-natural together. "The partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13) describes the historical record more accurately than the Manifesto's human-only protagonists.

The limits of Marxism, then, are the limits of its anthroponormative inheritance, and they show up exactly where industrial modernity is most obviously multispecies. Marxism is not refuted by horses; it is forced to grow. A multispecies political economy (capital, labour, animals, ecology) keeps what is best in Marx (exploitation, accumulation, crisis) while losing what was always a contingent disciplinary blinker. Greene's horses are the test case the discipline still has to pass.

### M4: How convincing do you find Carter and Charles's diagnosis of anthroponormativity in *The Animal Challenge to Sociology*?

I find the diagnosis very convincing as a diagnostic move and persuasive but incomplete as a programme for repair. Its strength is that it names a structural exclusion sociology has treated as methodological commonplace; its weakness is that it does most of its work negatively. On balance I judge it highly successful.

Anthroponormativity is defined explicitly: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). The analogies do real work. Heteronormativity is not the claim that some people are heterosexual; it is the claim that family, intimacy and respectability have been silently configured around heterosexual couples in ways that exclude queer lives by definition. White normativity does the same for citizenship, professionalism and reasonableness around whiteness. Anthroponormativity, analogously, configures society, agency, social action and reflexivity around humans, with the exclusion of animals operating as a definitional move rather than an empirical finding.

The diagnostic power is in calling the bluff. The obvious counter ("sociology is the study of humans, so of course it excludes animals") treats a contingent disciplinary self-description as a discovered necessity. There is no neutral place from which "the social = the human" is just true. It is a norm, and norms are contestable: the same move feminist sociology made against "sociology is the study of public, paid, male life" and post-colonial sociology made against "sociology is the study of Western modernity."

Once the diagnosis is in hand, the conceptual repair becomes legible. Agency is not a Cartesian property ("the human subject is active (and therefore exercises agency) while the non-human object is passive", Carter and Charles, 2018: 89) but "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Wolves whose "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91) are agents; pheasants in "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92) are agents; "sheep do not decide when they are to be slaughtered; chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), but they are agents whose conditions thwart their purposes.

Three limits are worth registering, none fatal.

First, the concept does not by itself tell you how to do multispecies research. The fine-grained methodological vocabulary (becoming-with, rendering-capable, response-ability, situated knowledge) comes from Haraway and Despret. Carter and Charles diagnose; Haraway demonstrates through PigeonBlog and Project PigeonWatch. Alexandra Kimbo's PhD work on her dog Harald ("Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless", lecture slide 29) shows the method-level work the diagnosis does not by itself do.

Second, the concept is in places under-theorised. It is unclear how anthroponormativity intersects with heteronormativity and white normativity in concrete sites, even though Carter and Charles flag the link ("it is also unsurprising that these forms of normativity are commonly bound together, leading to the argument that different forms of oppression are linked", Carter and Charles, 2018: 88, citing Adams). They gesture but do not fully deliver these intersections.

Third, institutional reception has been slow. Carter and Charles published in 2018; on their own four-stage feminist analogy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80) animal studies is still between stages two and three. The discipline has not absorbed the diagnosis as it absorbed equivalent feminist arguments. This is partly a problem of the diagnosis (animals cannot enter the academy as graduate students the way women could) and partly a measure of how deep anthroponormativity runs.

Despite these limits the diagnosis is convincing. Empirically, the cases it organises (Greene's horses in industrialisation, the pheasant shoot, EU wolves, Carter and Charles's "chickens do not decide to live in cramped and insanitary battery cages") are exactly the cases mainstream sociology has neglected, and the concept explains the pattern rather than treating each as isolated oversight. Conceptually, it locates the problem at the right level: the categories, not the data. Politically, it gives critical animal studies a name to fight under, as "heteronormativity" once gave queer theory a name.

The line I find most persuasive is the closing one: "ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). Once. The single move opens the discipline up. I find the diagnosis highly convincing, convincing enough to make the rest of the work intelligible.

### M5: Critically assess Carter and Charles's argument with reference to a contemporary case of human–animal entanglement (pheasant shoots, wolves under EU jurisdiction, or industrial food production).

Carter and Charles's argument is that sociology has misperceived its object by silently restricting "the social" to the human, and that the empirical, conceptual and political costs of this restriction are now too great to defend. I will test the argument against the pheasant shoot, the case they themselves develop in the article's closing pages, because it is the case in which their four key moves can be seen working together.

Their first move: "there is virtually no area of social life that is untouched by animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 79, citing Bryant, 1979). Their second: sociology's neglect has historical causes, a "fraught relationship with biology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80), urban-industrial provincialism, the inheritance of human exceptionalism. Their third: the vocabulary is anthroponormative (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Their fourth: the repair is a relational realism where agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), and animals can be "occupants of social positions" (Benton, 1993: 68, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). The slogan is "the social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93).

A British pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The pheasants themselves are "an introduced species" who are "bred in their hundreds of thousands so that they can be shot" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Alongside them are dogs and their handlers, "either beating (flushing out the pheasants) or picking up (retrieving them once they have been shot and 'despatching' – i.e. killing – those that are not dead)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). The shoot tests the argument on every move.

The empirical move holds up. The pheasants are not background; they are the agents around whom the entire practice is organised. Their breeding regimes, release cycles, flight capacities and seasonal vulnerabilities (the UK pheasant shooting season runs October to February, Carter and Charles note in footnote 4) co-constitute the shoot. The dogs are not tools; "as actors, we are not reducible to our agential entanglements" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), and the dogs' trained capacities for beating or picking up co-constitute the practice too. The claim that animals are "occupants of social positions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86) is here a literal description.

The conceptual move holds up too, but only with the revised agency concept. Cartesian agency cannot describe a pheasant whose entire life cycle is structured by breeding for shoot. Relational agency can: the pheasant has purposes (eat, fly, mate, evade predators) engaging agential conditions (release pen, beating line, gun line, season). Carter and Charles spell it out: "wolves and pheasants are unaware of the shooting season" and find "their choices, when they are exercised, are already circumscribed (the caged chicken cannot walk freely and the pheasant cannot fly safely within range of armed humans)" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Agency is recognised in being denied, more analytically powerful than welfarist sympathy because it lets sociology describe the system rather than just deplore it.

The political move holds up with sharpening. Animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The pheasant shoot is at once a class practice (the agential conditions Carter and Charles list include "power, status, gender, technologies, notions of 'a sporting life', the 'place of animals'", 2018: 92), a gendered practice (the gun line is heavily male), and a multispecies practice in which the pheasants are bred in industrial quantities to be killed. None of this is describable inside a human-only sociology except as scattered specialisms; Carter and Charles's framework lets a single sociology hold the pieces together.

Where the argument needs supplementation is at the methodological-affective level. The structural-realist idiom is right for system analysis but thin on what it is like to watch a pheasant break cover or to handle a retrieved bird. Haraway's becoming-with and "staying with the trouble" supply the missing register. Her account of PigeonBlog shows how partners can be "rendered capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22), and Despret's pigeon-fancier line ("without the lover of pigeons… without the transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs", Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25) gives a vocabulary for the practices that make pheasants what they are at a shoot.

A residual worry is normative reach. Carter and Charles state explicitly that "this is not animal advocacy" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). But the analytic recognition that the shoot is a structured practice of domination over agential beings makes a normative judgement nearly unavoidable. This is a strength: ethical questions follow from the sociology rather than being imposed on it. Greenebaum's three-type vegan analysis (slide 34) is one set of political responses; Haraway's response-ability is another, more graduated, one.

The argument holds up well under the pheasant-shoot test. It explains the phenomenon, supplies the vocabulary and articulates the politics. Where it needs help it gets it from Haraway. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) is not rhetorical inflation; it is the most economical description of what the pheasant shoot actually is.

### M6: Why does the lecturer "blame Talcott Parsons" for the disappearance of animals from twentieth-century sociology? Answer with reference to Carter and Charles and to the Durkheim and Weber excerpts.

The lecturer's slide 40 is direct: "So where did the animals go? Why did we stop thinking about them? I blame Talcott Parsons!" My argument is that this charge is justified, that Carter and Charles back it up with a textually careful account of how Parsons consolidated the human-only social, and that the Durkheim and Weber excerpts the lecturer cites on slides 38 and 39 show how much narrower Parsons made things than the classical thinkers themselves had been.

Begin with the lecturer's own framing. On slide 40, "for most of the 20th century, sociologists (influenced by Parsons) seem to assume and insist that whatever sociality is, it is a production of humans, groups of humans or systems of humans. Animals were rarely, if ever, considered. To ascribe genuine sociality to animals would seem to threaten the grounding and specificity of the social, which should, it is claimed, be restricted to the human realm alone." The charge is that Parsons hardened sociology around a definitional move: the social is human.

Carter and Charles, citing Halewood (2014), make the same case more carefully. "Halewood has recently made a plausible case for seeing Parsons as the key figure in attaining the first objective" of "assembling a stable object of sociology" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). "For Halewood, Parsons is the first major sociologist to hold a conception of the social that defines it unambiguously as the realm of human interaction; after Parsons, the question of what constitutes the social rarely surfaces. Moreover, this is a notion of the social from which the non-human (other animals, technologies, things) has been expelled" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). The Parsonian achievement, in other words, is also the Parsonian closure: by giving sociology a stable object, he excluded animals from its conceptual reach by definition rather than by argument.

Carter and Charles place this inside the wider story of "the social, the cultural and the human." By the early 1930s, sociology had "identified the features which radically distinguished it from the objects studied by the natural sciences. Principally, these were features held to be characteristic of people as human beings, features that allegedly marked them out from other animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). At their core were "claims about consciousness, subjectivity and reflexivity" and the belief that language and culture, "the two accomplishments that appeared to be most fundamentally constitutive of the human", were possible only because human beings were "self-reflexively aware" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 85). This is the Parsonian moment: the door closes.

The Durkheim and Weber excerpts the lecturer cites show that the classical thinkers themselves had left the door ajar. Durkheim, in the *Rules of Sociological Method*, writes that "it has not even been proved at all that the tendency to sociability was originally a congenital instinct of the human race" (Durkheim, *Rules*: 132, on slide 38), and that "it is an observable fact that animals are sociable or otherwise, depending on whether their environmental conditions force them to live in common or cause them to shun such a life" (Durkheim, *Rules*: 132). Sociability is a product of social life, not a species trait. The category of "the social" is, for Durkheim, capable of being predicated of non-human animals when their conditions warrant.

Weber goes further still. On slide 39: "many animals 'understand' [verstehen] commands, anger, love, hostility and react to them in ways which are evidently often by no means purely instinctive and mechanical and in some sense both consciously meaningful [sinnhaft] and affected by experience" (Weber, *Economy and Society*: 15–16). Weber lets animals into the territory of meaningful action. He then narrows: "a verifiable conception of the state of mind of these social animals [sozialen Tiernindividuen] accessible to meaningful understanding [sinnhaften 'Verstehens'] of the world, would seem to be attainable even as an ideal goal only within narrow limits" (Weber, *Economy and Society*: 17). Weber half-opens the door on ontological grounds and then closes it on epistemological grounds: animals are doing something meaningful, but we cannot fully access it.

What Parsons does, on the lecturer's and Carter and Charles's reading, is collapse the Weberian half-open door into a definitional closure. The methodological caution about access becomes a categorical exclusion of animals from the social. The Durkheimian recognition that sociability is environmental and observable in other species becomes a Parsonian insistence that sociality is human. The classical complexity is replaced by a tidy disciplinary boundary.

The lecturer's "I blame Parsons" is therefore well-founded, but it is also an indictment of how disciplinary stability gets bought. Carter and Charles's positive argument is that the price was too high. Once sociology had its object (human social action), it lost the conceptual flexibility to describe horses in nineteenth-century cities (Greene, 2008), wolves under EU law (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91), pheasants on shoots (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), or the Amazon and palm-oil entanglements the lecturer's slide 35 flags. Anthroponormativity (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88) is the name of the conceptual habit Parsons installed.

So the lecturer's joke is a serious historical claim. Parsons did not start the human-only social, but he made it doctrinal. The Durkheim and Weber excerpts show how much was lost in the move. Carter and Charles's diagnosis is the bill come due.

### M7: Is animal studies the next step in sociology or a category mistake?

I argue that animal studies is the next step in sociology, explicitly modelled, on Carter and Charles's own account, on the analogous step feminism took, and that the category-mistake objection rests on a circular definition. The field's work is to move from stage two to stage four of the feminist trajectory; the objection's work is to defend a definitional move that has not survived analogous challenges.

The category-mistake objection: sociology is the study of human societies; animals are not human; therefore animals are not the proper object of sociology, and "animal studies" misclassifies a biological topic as a sociological one. It is the silent assumption of introductory textbooks that define the discipline in human-only terms.

Carter and Charles's reply is that the major premise is a contingent disciplinary self-description, not a discovered fact, and calling it a category boundary smuggles a normative move into what pretends to be a definition. "Much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human. When sociologists use this vocabulary, they exclude animals and the non-human more generally" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). This is anthroponormativity, the silent species-norm analogous to heteronormativity and white normativity. The category-mistake objection is not a category judgement; it is anthroponormativity dressed up as one.

The "next step" framing is Carter and Charles's own. The four-stage feminist trajectory they draw on Walby (1988), Oakley (1989) and Charles (1993) gives: pre-feminist neglect; critique; adding women in; and "the full theoretical integration of gender into the discipline" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Animal studies "is experiencing the second and third stages" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The critique is made (anthroponormativity is now a published term); empirical work proceeds (multispecies ethnography, the pheasant shoot, Greenebaum's vegan typology, Kimbo's PhD on *Sociology, Sociality and Animals*). What remains is conceptual integration: reworking agency, society, labour and modernity around the recognition that the social is multispecies.

The empirical case is strong. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) is supported by accumulating work on horsepower (Greene, 2008), wolf jurisdictional politics (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91), the British pheasant shoot (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92), the racing-pigeon-and-air-pollution PigeonBlog project (Haraway, 2016), and Project PigeonWatch in Washington DC (Haraway, 2016: 24). The lecturer adds insects as pollinators of coffee, apples, almonds, watermelon, zucchini and kiwifruit (slide 18), pets (slide 16) and the Amazon-beef and palm-oil-orangutan harm slide 35 flags. A discipline that cannot describe these is not maintaining a category; it is misperceiving its object.

The conceptual case is stronger. Carter and Charles's relational agency ("an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action", Carter and Charles, 2018: 90) is a sociological move, not a biological one. The pheasant shoot as "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92) is a sociological description in good standing. So is PigeonBlog: pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) in a project that produced data official monitors could not (Haraway, 2016: 21). Neither collapses sociology into biology.

Two sophisticated forms of the objection. First, the symbolic-interactionist line: humans alone possess reflexive symbol-use, so the social as symbolic order is properly human. The lecturer's slide 31 names the constituency, including Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard. The reply is partly empirical: Carter and Charles cite de Waal in noting that "chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88); Haraway notes that pigeons pass mirror tests (Haraway, 2016: 18). And partly conceptual: even if human reflexive symbol-use is structurally distinctive, that does not entail that animals lack any agency or culture; the slide from "different in structure" to "not really sociological" is anthroponormative. Second, the disciplinary-identity worry. Carter and Charles want sociology reformed, not replaced (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87), and reject Latourian symmetry as overcorrection.

The analogy with feminism is sharper as diagnosis than as prediction. Women entered the academy and spoke for themselves; animals cannot. The methodological vocabulary of multispecies research had to be invented (Despret, Haraway, Kimbo). The drivers of stage-three-to-stage-four progress will be ecological and political (the Anthropocene framing Carter and Charles use to close the article, 2018: 81) rather than demographic.

So animal studies is the next step, on the trajectory gender, race and post-coloniality took. The category-mistake charge is the contemporary version of charges once made against women's studies. Each relied on a definitional move that did not survive scrutiny; anthroponormativity is the name of the move when the boundary is species. The work has begun.

### M8: Compare Haraway's companion species with Carter and Charles's "constitutive of society" framing.

I argue that the two framings are complementary moves toward the same multispecies sociology, operating at different registers: Haraway supplies a relational ontology, Carter and Charles supply structural-realist disciplinary reform. The most useful sociology uses both.

Carter and Charles's framing is in their key claim: sociology requires "a reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Constitutive does precise work: not "animals plus humans equals society" (which still treats each as pre-given) nor "animals replace humans as sociology's object" (which is category-swap not category-critique). It is the claim that society as a structure of social positions includes animals as occupants: "non-human animals" can be "occupants of social positions" (Benton, 1993: 68, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). The pheasant shoot is "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species, all of whom are enmeshed in overlapping webs of agential conditions" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92).

Their agency repair underwrites this. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Animals have purposes engaging relational conditions; this makes them agents without requiring Cartesian subjecthood. The wolf example shows agency is jurisdictional: a wolf's "agency therefore changes depending on whether they are in one or another national jurisdiction" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91).

Haraway's companion species is a different move. "The partners do not precede the knotting; species of all kinds are consequent upon worldly subject- and object-shaping entanglements" (Haraway, 2016: 13). The claim is ontological: the kinds "human" and "animal" are not given prior to the practices that produce them. Becoming-with is the operational vocabulary: "becoming-with, not becoming, is the name of the game; becoming-with is how partners are, in Vinciane Despret's terms, rendered capable" (Haraway, 2016: 12). The clearest illustration: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25).

Compare on three axes. First, register: Carter and Charles operate at the disciplinary level (sociology's vocabulary); Haraway operates at the ontological level (kinds-first metaphysics). Carter and Charles want reform; Haraway is closer to displacement. Second, scale: Carter and Charles's framework is built for structural analysis (Greene's industrial horses, EU wolf policy, the pheasant shoot); Haraway's is built for situated micro-analysis (PigeonBlog, dog-human training in the *Manifesto*, Project PigeonWatch's DC schoolchildren learning to see pigeons as "sociable birds with lives and deaths", Haraway, 2016: 24). Out of scale each loses traction. Third, normative orientation: Carter and Charles state they are not engaged in animal advocacy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93); Haraway articulates response-ability as graduated obligation across "ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29), wary of some animal-rights framings (she praises Batman Park's pigeon-egg-replacement programme in Melbourne as multispecies management, "not a prolife project… in the chilling American sense of that term", Haraway, 2016: 28).

Complementarity is straightforward. Carter and Charles tell us animals are inside the social and rework sociology to describe them; Haraway tells us the kinds we work with are themselves produced through the entanglements we describe. Without Carter and Charles, Haraway risks slipping outside sociology. Without Haraway, Carter and Charles's structural realism risks reifying "humans" and "animals" as the kinds they want to relate.

Consider PigeonBlog. Carter and Charles would describe it as multispecies practice in which racing pigeons are occupants of social positions co-constituted by working-class fanciers like Bob Matsuyama, art-activist Beatriz da Costa, and the city's pollution geography. Haraway describes how the pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18) and how partners were "rendered capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22): the texture the structural account leaves undescribed. Consider Greene's horses. Carter and Charles describe the system: the horse as "biotechnology… living machines" (Greene, 2008: 4) inside industrial capitalism's "prime mover" role (Carter and Charles, 2018: 86). Haraway describes what is foreclosed when the horse is industrialised: the becoming-with that domestication produced, the response-ability the carthorse and its driver owe each other.

The two framings are not rivals. They are layers: disciplinary repair from Carter and Charles, relational ontology from Haraway. "The social *is* animal" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93) and "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13) are saying the same thing from different angles. Read one without the other and the sociology is partial.

### M9: Can sociology decentre the human without dissolving its disciplinary identity?

Yes, and Carter and Charles's article is a careful demonstration that the decentring can be done from inside the discipline rather than at its expense. The worry is that taking animals seriously will collapse sociology into biology, into Latourian flat ontology, or into philosophical post-humanism. All three slides are avoidable if we rework concepts rather than abandon them, and the resulting discipline is more, not less, recognisably sociological than its anthroponormative predecessor.

The worry is reasonable. Sociology was built partly through defence against biology. The discipline insisted on "the autonomy and specificity of human social life" (Benton, 1991: 12, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 82) and later distanced itself from biology because of its association with eugenics and scientific racism (Carter and Charles, 2018: 83). Durkheim's struggle against Spencer, Weber's against social Darwinism, mid-twentieth-century opposition to behaviourism (Camic, 1986, cited Carter and Charles, 2018: 84): all were boundary-defences.

The reply has three parts.

First, Carter and Charles do not advocate dissolution. They reject Latour and the new materialists, who treat humans, animals and machines as symmetrical "actants". Sociology is "capable of accommodating the non-human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 87) once its concepts are reworked. Reform, not replacement. Their relational realism keeps emergent structures, social positions and causal explanation; it loses only the silent species-norm.

Second, the decentring runs through the existing sociological agenda. Every standard object becomes more intelligible once animals are admitted. Greene's horses make industrialisation legible at the level Marx missed; the pheasant shoot makes class and leisure legible as "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92); Carter and Charles's wolves under EU law make the politics of conservation and indigenous livelihood (the Sami reindeer herders, 2018: 91) legible as multispecies political economy; PigeonBlog makes environmental justice legible as multispecies citizen science (Haraway, 2016: 21).

Third, the analogy with previous decentrings shows the worry was always overstated. Feminist sociology decentred the male subject without dissolving sociology; post-colonial sociology decentred the Western subject; queer theory decentred the heterosexual subject. The worry ("if we expand the subject, the discipline collapses") has each time been an artefact of the previously narrow definition. Animal studies is between stages two and three of the feminist trajectory (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80); stage four does not dissolve sociology any more than feminist stage four did.

Two cases sharpen the answer. The pheasant shoot: a human-only sociology describes a country sport. A multispecies sociology (Carter and Charles plus Haraway) describes a single multispecies practice where breeding regimes, beating dogs, gun lines, gendered etiquette and the pheasants' bred-for-shoot life cycles form an interlocking whole. More sociological, not less. Companion-animal relations in the household: a human-only sociology describes pet-keeping as a leisure or symbolic practice (slide 13 calls this out as the result of the human-have-culture-animals-do-not premise). A multispecies sociology (adding becoming-with) describes a relational entanglement in which dog and human are rendered capable of each other. Kimbo's framing on slide 29 applies: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless."

A residual question about method. Animals do not fill out questionnaires; sociology's standard repertoire is built around interviews, human ethnography and quantitative analysis. Multispecies methods are being built: Despret's ethology (cited throughout Haraway), Kimbo's "thinking with" (slide 28) companion animals, Haraway's situated multispecies ethnography. None dissolves sociological method; they extend it. The Stengers-via-Haraway commitment that "decisions must take place somehow in the presence of those who will bear their consequences" (Haraway, 2016: 12) is itself a methodological pressure sociology can absorb.

Disciplinary identity as institution: departments, journals, curricula. The worry is that admitting animals will make sociology indistinguishable from anthropology, geography or environmental studies. The reply is that neighbouring disciplines already do multispecies work, and the traffic strengthens sociology when it contributes its distinctive resources (structural analysis, political economy, the relational realism Carter and Charles articulate, drawing on Archer, 2000 and 2003).

Carter and Charles's closing line names the wager. "Ideas about what it means to be human, about what society is, about what the limits of the social might be taken to be, and about the sustainability of life on a planet shared with other species change once they no longer presume human exceptionalism" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93). Change, not dissolve. Sociology is what survives the change; what falls away is anthroponormativity, not the discipline. The discipline that decentres the human without dissolving its identity is the one that finally answers its own questions accurately.

### M10: "Animals can communicate, but they don't obviously have human language." Discuss with reference to at least two readings.

The quotation comes from the lecturer's slide 31 on "Some people want to retain an element of human exceptionalism", and I take it as a useful starting point for an argument that the language line is not where the action is in multispecies sociology. With reference to Carter and Charles and Haraway, the distinction between communication and human language is real but does not justify excluding animals from sociology, because language is not the basis on which either includes animals in the social.

The quotation acknowledges what the empirical record shows. Carter and Charles, citing de Waal, note that "animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales (to name but a few) have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). Haraway documents pigeon capacities at length. Pigeons "pass mirror tests" and "share this capacity with, at least, human children over two years old, rhesus macaques, chimpanzees, magpies, dolphins, and elephants" (Haraway, 2016: 18). At Keio University's Laboratory of Comparative Cognitive Neuroscience under Shigeru Watanabe, "pigeons could tell the difference between paintings by Monet or Picasso, and even generalize to discriminate unfamiliar paintings from different styles and schools by various painters" (Haraway, 2016: 18). Carter and Charles also cite Cheke and Clayton on Eurasian jays "overcoming their current desires to anticipate two distinct future needs and plan for them appropriately" (Carter and Charles, 2018: reference list, citing Biology Letters 8). Animal communication is structured differently from human language, not unstructured.

What the quotation retains is the claim that human language has structural features (recursion, productivity, displacement, full syntax) that animal systems do not match. By current evidence this is mostly correct. The question is what follows.

The symbolic-interactionist tradition the lecturer associates on slide 31 with Mead, Goffman, Barthes, Saussure and Baudrillard takes language-distinctiveness as a basis for excluding animals: meaningful social action requires reflexive symbol-use only humans possess. Mead's foundational role here is the one Carter and Charles flag explicitly: "Alger and Alger argue that the centrality of Mead to the sociological tradition and his assumption that other animals are incapable of symbolic thought explains sociology's lack of attention to animals" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81).

Carter and Charles's reply is on conceptual ground. The slide from "humans have distinctive language" to "therefore the social is properly human" is anthroponormativity: "much of the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences is configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88). The slide is a definitional configuration that pretends to be an inference.

Their positive move redefines agency in non-linguistic terms. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90), "always agency in relation to other agents" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92). Linguistic competence is not the criterion; relational engagement of purposes with conditions is. A wolf in a Swedish EU-protected forest is an agent (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91); a pheasant in a UK shoot is an agent in "a complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92); a chicken in a battery cage is an agent whose conditions thwart its purposes. None of this requires the animal to be a language-user.

Animal culture is the related case. The de Waal claim Carter and Charles cite ("animals such as chimpanzees, bonobos and whales… have cultures which are peculiar to specific social groups and persist across generations", Carter and Charles, 2018: 88) shows that if culture means inter-generationally transmitted group-specific learned practice, animals have culture without human language. The lecturer presses the right question on slide 32: "Why do we want to keep culture to ourselves?"

Haraway deflates the language question by changing the level of analysis. Becoming-with is constituted through embodied co-attunement that includes but exceeds linguistic communication. PigeonBlog's racing pigeons "had to learn what their humans were interested in seeing" (Haraway, 2016: 18); the artist-researchers had "to render each other capable of mutual trust" (Haraway, 2016: 22). This trust is built through training, feeding, handling, repeated joint flights, not human language. The Despret line is decisive: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25). The competences are practical and embodied, not primarily linguistic.

Could the language line be defended on weaker grounds ("human social practice is distinctive enough to merit its own discipline")? More defensible but less interesting. Sociology can recognise human linguistic distinctiveness and still include animals as relational agents. Carter and Charles deny only that distinctiveness justifies definitional exclusion. The counter-objection that extending sociology dilutes "social action" misfires: the relational view distinguishes agents (beings with purposes engaging conditions) from non-agents. The chicken qualifies; the rock does not.

So the quotation is true but undercuts itself. Animals communicate without human language. Carter and Charles and Haraway both build multispecies sociology on grounds that do not require animals to share human language. The language line is worth preserving as a fact about the world; it is not a justification for anthroponormative exclusion. The lecturer's own framing on slide 31 pushes the point: animals "can communicate (though they don't obviously have human language)" and "animals have culture." That sentence ends the case for the language-based exclusion.

### M11: Using the work of at least two authors, assess whether animal welfare and animal rights demand the same sociological framework, given Haraway's wariness of certain "prolife" framings (Haraway, 2016: 28).

I argue, drawing on Carter and Charles and Haraway, that animal welfare and animal rights do not demand the same political framework, but both fit inside a more general multispecies sociology whose categories accommodate them while preserving the political distinctness of each. Welfare and rights name two ethical-political projects with different histories and disciplinary affinities; sociology can describe both without endorsing either.

Begin with the distinction. Animal welfare is the position that humans may use animals (for food, labour, research, companionship) but must minimise suffering, regulating conditions. Animal rights is the position that animals have interests grounding duties humans owe them, including duties to prohibit certain uses entirely. The lecturer's slide 31 flags the live case: "a New York lawyer went to court to try to get two chimpanzees awarded 'personhood'." Welfare typically accepts use if reformed; rights typically denies the use in principle.

These are not the same problem with different reform programmes. Welfare treats animals as objects-with-interests; rights as subjects-with-claims. Welfare works through state and corporate regulation; rights through movement advocacy and litigation. A sociology that conflates them misperceives the political field.

But both projects presume animals are agents with morally relevant interests, and the relational-realist concept lets sociology describe both. Agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). A battery hen has agency-thwarted-by-conditions; a pheasant in a shoot has agency entangled in a class-and-species practice (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92); a wolf has agency that shifts across the EU/Sweden/Sami/Norway boundary (Carter and Charles, 2018: 91). Welfare works on conditions inside the use; rights on the use itself. Both are describable as politics of agential conditions, a single sociological category accommodating two political projects.

The shared stake: animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The sociology can describe the strategic choices, institutional partnerships (rights with movement organisations, welfare with regulators), and cultural dynamics. Greenebaum's three-type vegan analysis (lecture slide 34: health vegans, environmental vegans, ethical vegans) maps onto these distinctions. Health vegans sit closer to welfare logics (the question is bodily good, not animal rights); environmental vegans take Haraway's Anthropocene framing seriously without necessarily embracing rights; ethical vegans articulate moral-political views that often land closer to rights positions.

Haraway adds two moves. First, she is explicitly wary of certain rights framings. Writing about the Batman Park pigeon loft in Melbourne, she insists: "This pigeon loft is not a prolife project; in my view, no serious animal-human becoming-with can be a prolife project in the chilling American sense of that term" (Haraway, 2016: 28). The Batman Park scheme replaces pigeon eggs with artificial ones to brood, controlling feral pigeon populations while still inviting them to roost. It is multispecies management framed by "the response-ability of staying with the trouble. Response-ability is about both absence and presence, killing and nurturing, living and dying" (Haraway, 2016: 28). This is neither welfare regulation in the welfarist sense nor rights advocacy; it is graduated ecological politics. "We are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29).

Second, becoming-with cuts across the welfare-rights distinction. PigeonBlog's racing-pigeon fanciers like Bob Matsuyama might or might not endorse animal-rights politics; "the first contact person was interested but frankly afraid of animal rights people and tactics" (Haraway, 2016: 24). Yet the fanciers "love and care for their birds" in ways neither welfare regulation nor rights litigation fully describes. "Rendered capable of mutual trust" with the birds (Haraway, 2016: 22), and Despret's line that without the fancier "what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Haraway, 2016: 25): Haraway captures a register political philosophy does not.

The assessment. Welfare and rights do not demand the same political-philosophical scaffolding; they make different ethical claims. They do demand the same disciplinary apparatus for describing multispecies practice: both are intelligible inside Carter and Charles's relational realism and Haraway's relational ontology.

Two cases sharpen the point. The Batman Park egg-replacement scheme is closer to welfare than rights in its acceptance of human population control, but Haraway's framing of it as "a small, practical enactment and a reminder to further opening to the response-ability of staying with the trouble" (Haraway, 2016: 28) shows that the welfare/rights distinction is not exhaustive of the political possibilities. The chimpanzee personhood case the lecturer cites on slide 31 is closer to rights, asserting that the legitimacy of certain uses is in question; the sociology of that litigation is still describable by Carter and Charles's apparatus (the campaign, the courts, the chimpanzees as agents in the New York Supreme Court hearing).

So welfare and animal rights are politically distinct but sociologically compatible. Different politics, shared sociology. Haraway's wariness of "prolife" framings (Haraway, 2016: 28) shows that even the welfare/rights binary is not the only normative terrain. The sociology keeps all the projects intelligible.

### M12: What does the Greenebaum (2012) typology of vegans add to the Carter and Charles diagnosis of the animal challenge to sociology?

The lecturer introduces Greenebaum (2012) on slide 34 alongside the question "Should we eat animals?", offering a three-type typology of vegans: health vegans (lose weight, improve health), environmental vegans (concerned with the impact of industrial/intensive farming, though they may buy leather rather than plastic), and ethical vegans (moral and political views). My argument is that Greenebaum's typology adds three concrete things to the Carter and Charles diagnosis: it gives anthroponormativity a contemporary political register; it shows the limits of any single ethical framing; and it dramatises the Anthropocene stake at which Carter and Charles end their article.

Carter and Charles's diagnosis is that sociology is built on anthroponormativity, "the conceptual vocabulary of the social sciences" being "configured around assumptions about the human" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88), and that this configuration has to be reworked because human–animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80) and because, in the Anthropocene, a human-only sociology is "in danger of rendering it irrelevant" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 81). What the diagnosis lacks at this level of generality is a sense of what taking it seriously looks like as everyday social practice.

Greenebaum supplies this. Veganism is one of the most visible contemporary social practices in which human–animal relations are reorganised, and the typology shows it is not one practice but several. Health vegans rework their relation to animals on the terms of their own bodies; the practice is internally focused, and the animal as such may not be the primary object of concern. Environmental vegans frame the relation in Carter and Charles's Anthropocene register: industrial and intensive farming carries environmental costs that justify dietary change. The lecturer's slide 35 connects this directly to the harms in view: Amazon clearance for beef production, palm-oil clearance of orangutan habitat. The leather-not-plastic note on slide 34 captures the typology's tensions: an environmental vegan may prioritise petrochemical avoidance over animal-product avoidance, an internally coherent but politically contested choice. Ethical vegans take the animal as the object of moral concern directly, lining up with the framing of agential beings "frequently" subject to "domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80).

The first thing the typology adds is anthroponormativity's contemporary register. Anthroponormativity is a diagnosis of conceptual habits; the Greenebaum typology shows what contestation of those habits looks like in the consumer practices of ordinary people. The three vegan types are three different breaches of the silent species-norm that human eating is a category apart from animal lives. Each breach implies a different sociology of how the breach was reached.

The second thing the typology adds is the limits of any single ethical framing. Carter and Charles state they are not engaged in animal advocacy (Carter and Charles, 2018: 93); their analytic recognition of domination does not by itself prescribe a politics. The Greenebaum typology shows why this is methodologically responsible. The three types are reasons internal to the vegan; they are not jointly determinate of a single political programme. Health veganism does not generate an animal-rights politics; environmental veganism does not generate a welfare politics in the regulatory sense; ethical veganism may or may not endorse the kinds of "prolife" framings Haraway distances herself from in the Batman Park discussion ("no serious animal-human becoming-with can be a prolife project in the chilling American sense of that term", Haraway, 2016: 28). The typology displays the political plurality multispecies sociology has to keep open.

The third thing the typology adds is the Anthropocene stake. Slide 35's "Green Criminology" framing pushes Greenebaum's environmental category into Carter and Charles's closing concern. If sociology cannot describe a planet shared with other species, it is in danger of irrelevance. Greenebaum's environmental vegans are precisely those reorganising their daily practice in response to that planetary recognition. Haraway's response-ability ("we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives", Haraway, 2016: 29) names what such a reorganisation involves at the level of moral attention.

The typology also has limits that are worth registering. It is a typology of vegans, not of human–animal relations more generally; it does not cover the pheasant shoot, the racing pigeon fancier, or the dog-walker. It treats individual consumer practice as the unit of analysis, where Carter and Charles work at the level of social structures. Greenebaum's typology is therefore an addition rather than a replacement: it gives the diagnosis a downstream presence in lived practice without supplanting the structural analysis Carter and Charles do.

There is one further connection worth drawing. Carter and Charles's pheasant-shoot example (Carter and Charles, 2018: 92) shows that one practice can be a "complex entanglement of social actors from various classes, genders and species." Greenebaum's typology suggests the same is true on the consumption side: even within "veganism" as a label, the practice is multiply motivated, multiply situated, and multiply political. Multispecies sociology has to be capable of describing both the production-side entanglements (Greene's horses, the pheasant shoot, PigeonBlog) and the consumption-side typologies (Greenebaum's vegans) without flattening either to the other.

So Greenebaum (2012) adds concreteness, plurality and Anthropocene urgency to the Carter and Charles diagnosis. The typology does not by itself rework sociology's concepts; that is Carter and Charles's work. But it shows the diagnosis landing in ordinary social practice, which is what makes the diagnosis matter beyond the academy.

### M13: "Pets are kin." Discuss the politics and ethics of treating companion animals as family with reference to Haraway and Kimbo.

The phrase condenses one of the most politically and ethically charged sites in human–animal relations. My argument, through Haraway's companion species and the lecturer's presentation of Alexandra Kimbo's work, is that treating companion animals as family is sociologically defensible and politically productive, but only if held alongside an honest recognition of the structural asymmetries that distinguish pet-keeping from human family relations.

Begin with the empirical reality on the slides. Slide 16 reads simply "Pets", with images of a dog, a cat, a rabbit and a guinea pig. The lecturer's slide 26 is autobiographical: "Thumper", the lecturer's own dog, illustrates the companion-species claim that humans "become humans through their relations with animals." Slide 29 on Kimbo's work develops the register: "Harald and I may express love differently, and each have unique perspectives, but the possibility of loving exists nonetheless. Thus, regardless of how 'different' individuals are from each other, experience can still be shared." Kinship here is lived practice, not metaphor.

Haraway gives the most developed language. Companion species "are relentlessly becoming-with" (Haraway, 2016: 13); "the partners do not precede the knotting" (Haraway, 2016: 13). The *Companion Species Manifesto* (Haraway, 2003) makes the case: humans and dogs are "training each other in acts of communication we barely understand", and the dog is not metaphor but a being co-shaped through millennia of co-evolution, breeding, labour and domestic life. Haraway insists on "significant otherness": humans and dogs do not collapse but co-shape each other. The Despret line about pigeon fanciers transfers: "without the lover of pigeons… without selection, apprenticeship, without transmission of practices, what then would remain would be pigeons, but not homing pigeons, not voyageurs" (Despret, cited Haraway, 2016: 25). What goes for the homing pigeon goes for the household dog: the dog is not pre-given prior to the practices of training, walking, feeding and naming.

Kimbo develops this affectively. Her slide 29 second formulation is decisive: "If two individual bodies (or centres of experiences and flows of feeling) open themselves up or attune to each other, thus feeling-for mutual possibilities, shared experience is what matters foremost." Attunement is the ethical mode. The slide 28 question Kimbo poses ("How to think (theorise/conceptualise) with (and for) other animals, as opposed to about them?") suggests a methodology for kinship: thinking with the pet, not about it.

Carter and Charles support the framing disciplinarily. The dog in the household is an agent whose purposes engage household conditions; agency is "an emergent product of the engagement between purpose and the contextual conditions of action" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 90). A dog choosing where to sleep, whom to greet, whether to walk in the rain, is an agent in the sociological sense. Treating that agent as kin is recognition rather than anthropomorphism. The lecturer's slide 13 critique of the interpretivist tendency to reduce pets to symbols is the negative case: pets-as-symbols is what kinship-as-recognition replaces.

The politics. Treating pets as kin contests the species-citizenship boundary, puts pressure on legal categories (the chimpanzee personhood case the lecturer flags on slide 31 is the limit case), and valorises emotional labour traditionally feminised and unpaid. The lecturer's slide 7 framing ("perhaps it is only possible for us to be human, and to live in a society, through our relations with animals. The question is how to make these relations 'fair', or at least, not exploitative") names the political question precisely.

The ethics. The strongest claim is Haraway's response-ability: "we are not all response-able in the same ways. The differences matter, in ecologies, economies, species, lives" (Haraway, 2016: 29). Kinning a pet is graduated response-ability (feed, walk, train, attend, mourn) that the pet cannot reciprocate in the same way but is no less a real ethical relation. Kimbo's slide 29 closing claim is in the same register: "shared experience is what matters foremost" when two centres of feeling attune.

But the asymmetries are real. Pets are bred, bought, sold and euthanised on human decisions; their kinship is non-chosen. Pet-keeping is class-stratified. Carter and Charles's reminder that human–animal relations are "frequently ones of domination and exploitation" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80) applies to pets as much as to livestock. The pet is the lecturer's slide 13 example of the symbolic move pets-as-symbols-of-meaning, exactly the move Haraway's "significant otherness" formula is meant to refuse. Treating the pet as kin in the Haraway sense is therefore a corrective inside an unequal practice, not a neutralisation of the inequality.

Two objections. First, treating pets as kin diverts attention from animals worse-treated. The reply: kinship logic, on the lecturer's slide 26 framing of becoming-with, generates ethical attention to other animals; pet-keeping is, on Kimbo's account, a school of multispecies attunement that extends outward through the question "how to take into account what matters for nonhuman animals?" (slide 28). Second, the kinship claim risks slipping into Haraway's "prolife" register, the framing she explicitly distances herself from in the Batman Park pigeon-loft discussion: "no serious animal-human becoming-with can be a prolife project in the chilling American sense of that term" (Haraway, 2016: 28). Pet kinship has to be held as graduated response-ability, not as an absolute claim.

Carter and Charles's anthroponormativity diagnosis (Carter and Charles, 2018: 88) underwrites the broader claim. Refusing kinship to companion animals because they are non-human is exactly the definitional move anthroponormativity makes. Saying "pets are kin" is not stretching family beyond recognition; it is recognising that family was always already capable of multispecies extension, as human–dog co-evolution shows.

So the politics and ethics of treating companion animals as family are defensible and productive, provided the kinship claim is held with the asymmetries in full view. Pets are kin; the kinship is real, ethical and politically charged. It is also asymmetric, class-stratified and non-chosen. The slogan is true; the work is in the qualifications.
