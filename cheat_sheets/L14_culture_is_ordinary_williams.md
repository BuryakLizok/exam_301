# L14. Culture is Ordinary (Raymond Williams, British Cultural Studies)

## §1. One-line thesis

Raymond Williams's argument that culture is ordinary refounds the study of culture as the analysis of a whole way of life, common meanings made and remade by working people themselves, and, together with Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy*, Thompson on history from below, and Hall's encoding/decoding model, launches British Cultural Studies as a tradition that rejects both elite "high culture" and the Marxist base-superstructure reflection thesis, treating culture instead as a political site of struggle where hegemony is contested in the lived experience of class.

## §2. Why this lecture matters in SC301

L14 is the spine of the Spring term's "culture, class, media" arc and it sits at the joint between two earlier moves on the SC301 syllabus: the critique of mass-society pessimism (Adorno, Frankfurt School) and the Marxist account of ideology (base/superstructure). British Cultural Studies (BCS) is the moment when sociology stops asking "what does high culture mean?" and starts asking "what do working-class lives mean to those who live them?", and decides that the answer is not "they are dupes of capitalism," but that ordinary people make culture and remake themselves through it. This matters for SC301 because the lecture supplies a vocabulary, structure of feeling, the long revolution, flow (in TV), residual/dominant/emergent, encoding/decoding, hegemony, common sense, history from below, the moral economy, the working-class autodidact, that you will need across the media, class, race, and modernity essays. It also sets up L17 (Stuart Hall on race and representation), the gender lectures (Skeggs and others on the slide-deck legacy list), and any question about whether modernity homogenises culture. Williams's biographical claim, "I was born and grew up halfway along that bus journey" (Williams, 1958: 10), is doing methodological work: he insists that the analyst belongs to the culture under analysis, that the sociologist of culture is not Clive Bell in his Cambridge teashop but the railway signalman's son going back home. The lecture is, finally, a teachable counter to cultural pessimism: it refuses the people-without-history thesis and insists "that there are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18).

## §4. Williams's argument in *Culture is Ordinary*, step by step

1. **Biography as method.** Williams opens with a bus journey from Hereford cathedral up the Black Mountains to a Welsh farming valley. His grandfather a farm labourer turned roadman; his father a railway signalman; he himself the grammar-school boy who goes to Cambridge. "Culture is ordinary: that is where we must start" (Williams, 1958: 11).
2. **Two senses of culture, both insisted on.** "We use the word culture in these two senses: to mean a whole way of life, the common meanings; to mean the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort. Some writers reserve the word for one or other of these senses; I insist on both, and on the significance of their conjunction" (Williams, 1958: 11).
3. **Against the teashop.** At Cambridge Williams encounters "culture" as "the outward and emphatically visible sign of a special kind of people, cultivated people" (Williams, 1958: 12). He refuses this: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12).
4. **Against Clive Bell.** Bell's *Civilisation* divides "ordinary people and ordinary work" from culture as with "a park wall" (Williams, 1958: 12). Williams: at home "we met and made music, listened to it, recited and listened to poems, valued fine language" (Williams, 1958: 12).
5. **Against the new "argot" of the culture-vultures.** A new petty class of "do-gooders and highbrows and superior prigs" has "invented and disseminated the argot, in an attempt to influence ordinary people" (Williams, 1958: 13).
6. **Two serious influences he keeps in dialogue with.** "When I got to Cambridge I encountered two serious influences which have left a very deep impression on my mind. The first was Marxism, the second the teaching of Leavis" (Williams, 1958: 14).
7. **What he takes from Marxism, what he refuses.** He accepts that "a culture is a whole way of life, and the arts are part of a social organisation which economic change clearly radically affects" (Williams, 1958: 14). He refuses the "directive element", the idea "that if you honestly want socialism you must write, think, learn in certain prescribed ways" (Williams, 1958: 15).
8. **What he takes from Leavis, what he refuses.** He accepts that an "old, mainly agricultural England" has been replaced by "a modern, organised, industrial state" (Williams, 1958: 16). He refuses Leavis's verdict that the new commercial culture is total and that the masses are lost. Of mass-trinket nostalgia he says: "not in a million years would you make us give up this power", plumbing, tractors, medicines, contraceptives (Williams, 1958: 17).
9. **Four legacies to be cleared away.** Williams identifies "a legacy of two false equations, one false analogy, and one false proposition" (Williams, 1958: 17): (i) ugliness as price of power, false; (ii) popular education breeds commercial culture, false; (iii) bad popular culture proves the inner badness of its consumers, false; (iv) Gresham's Law in culture, bad driving out good, false.
10. **The "masses" formula.** "There are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18). Mass is a class projection.
11. **Three wishes.** Williams ends with three: that education be recognised as ordinary; that public provision for arts and adult learning be increased ("we now spend £20,000,000 annually on … the Arts Council … At the same time we spend £365,000,000 annually on advertising", Williams, 1958: 22); and that mass culture's keystone, advertising revenue, be addressed.
12. **The closing image.** The bus journey, again: "where the language changes but the voice is the same" (Williams, 1958: 24).

## §5. The wider BCS field (Hoggart, Thompson, Hall) and the contemporary relevance

**Hoggart.** *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957) is a book of two halves: the 1930s "lived," "self-made" working-class culture of his Leeds childhood, and the 1950s "world where things are done for the people." Hoggart's scholarship boy is "at the friction-point of two cultures," "sad and also solitary." The "Juke Box Boy" passage condemns the commercial cafe culture as "a peculiarly thin and pallid form of dissipation, a sort of spiritual dry-rot amid the odour of boiled milk." Hoggart's loss is more cautious than Adorno's; he insists the moral failure lies in "the culture provided for the working class," not in working-class people themselves.

**Thompson.** *The Making of the English Working Class* (Thompson, 1963): class made itself, in struggle, in chapels and pubs and Corresponding Societies and food riots; it was not the deposit of an economic structure. Thompson supplies the slogan "history from below" and the concept of the moral economy. His insistence on lived experience and human agency is the BCS counter to structuralist Marxism.

**Hall.** Drawing on Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), Hall's 1973 essay "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Hall, 1980) demolishes the transmission model of media: producers encode, audiences decode, with dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, and oppositional positions available. "The Whites of Their Eyes" (Hall, 1981a) reads the grammar of race in 1970s sitcoms like *Love Thy Neighbour*: the comedy "protects/defends viewer from acknowledging racism." *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) turns the moral panic over "mugging" into a case study of how state, media and policing manufacture ideological responses to the 1970s organic crisis.

**Worked examples already given in the materials**, keep these ready: the bus journey from Hereford cathedral up through the Black Mountains into the Welsh farming valley as Williams's founding image of culture as ordinary (Williams, 1958: 10); ## §8. Full essays

### Essay 1. "Culture is ordinary." Critically assess Raymond Williams's claim.

I want to argue that Williams's claim that culture is ordinary is the founding move of British Cultural Studies because it does two things at once: it democratises the *object* of cultural analysis, and it democratises the *position* of the analyst. The slogan is not a celebration of pop culture and it is not an attack on Shakespeare. It is a demand that we hold both senses of culture together, "a whole way of life, the common meanings" and "the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort" (Williams, 1958: 11), and refuse the class division that puts them in different rooms.

Williams's argument is staged biographically. He opens the essay on a bus from Hereford cathedral up into the Black Mountains, past Norman castles and the line of early iron workings, into a farming valley where his grandfather worked as a farm labourer until he was "turned out of his cottage and, in his fifties, became a roadman" (Williams, 1958: 11). His father became a railway signalman; he himself went to the village school, the grammar school, and Cambridge. The opening matters because it puts the sociologist of culture inside the culture under analysis. When he arrives in the Cambridge teashop, he finds "culture, not in any sense I knew, but in a special sense: the outward and emphatically visible sign of a special kind of people, cultivated people" (Williams, 1958: 12). His verdict is famous: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12). He has been on the other end of the bus route.

The argument then proceeds by clearing two rival positions. The first is the cultural pessimism Williams calls "Leavis": the diagnosis that "an old, mainly agricultural England" has been replaced by "a modern, organised, industrial state" whose institutions "deliberately cheapen our natural human responses" (Williams, 1958: 16). Williams accepts the diagnosis as a "personal crisis" but rejects its conclusion. The working people he knows are not the cultural casualties of industrial mechanisation; the Industrial Revolution brought "the gift of power that is everything to men who have worked with their hands" (Williams, 1958: 16), plumbing, electricity, contraceptives, canned food. "Not in a million years would you make us give up this power" (Williams, 1958: 17). The second target is Marxist economism: the view that culture is a reflection of the economic base, with prescribed correct forms. Williams agrees that culture and production are related, but refuses the "directive element, this insistence that if you honestly want socialism you must write, think, learn in certain prescribed ways" (Williams, 1958: 15).

What is left, once both rivals are cleared, is the positive thesis: culture is made by living, "by living, made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance" (Williams, 1958: 15). This makes culture political without being prescribed. It also makes the "masses" a category mistake. Williams's most quoted line, "there are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18), is a sociological knife. Mass is a name imposed by those whose money "gave them access to the new communication techniques" (Williams, 1958: 18) on the people they did not know.

The empirical test Williams himself runs is the strongest demonstration of the thesis. Returning home, he sits "in a house with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" and asks whether, "talking, as we were, about our own lives, about people, about feelings," he can find the "lack of quality" the cultural pessimists insist must be there (Williams, 1958: 19). He cannot. He records instead "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19). His father, a "satisfied reader of the *Daily Herald*," is shown working out from company reports a clear account of "combine and interlocking ownership in British industry" that Williams "had had made easy for me in two or three academic essays" (Williams, 1958: 19). The empirical demonstration of culture as ordinary is that ordinary people in fact do the thinking the teashop assumes they cannot do.

The critical limits are real. Williams writes before the cultural turn that took race and gender seriously; the "we" of his Welsh valley is a particular kind of common, and the slide-17 legacy (Mirza, 1992; Skeggs, 1997; McKenzie, 2015) is needed precisely to repair the gender and race blind spots. He also assumes a stable national frame ("England", "Englishness") whose limits he himself begins to notice. But the underlying move, refuse the teashop, refuse the dying-culture verdict, refuse to mass the people you do not know, is still, I think, the best opening sentence British sociology of culture ever wrote. Culture is ordinary; that is where we must still start.

### Essay 2. Compare Williams's "whole way of life" with Hoggart on working-class culture.

I want to argue that although Williams and Hoggart are usually shelved together as the founders of British Cultural Studies, their accounts of working-class culture diverge in tone and politics in ways that matter for revision. Williams's "whole way of life" is generous, expansive, and democratic; Hoggart's portrait in *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957) is elegiac and at moments moralistic. Both refuse the Adorno-style verdict that the masses are dupes, but they refuse it differently, and Williams's version travels better into the twenty-first century.

Hoggart's book is famously a book of two halves. The first half describes the "lived," "self-made" working-class culture of his Leeds boyhood in the 1930s: its proverbs, its parlour singing, its co-operative women's networks, its quiet moralism. The second half describes the same culture under threat from the new commercial entertainments of the 1950s, "a world where things are done for the people." On the "Juke Box Boy" page Hoggart turns sharp: the milk-bar boy is "living to a large extent in a myth world … perhaps most of them are rather less intelligent than the average … directionless and tamed helots of a machine-minding class." Hoggart wants to defend working-class people from a commercial culture that he thinks is corroding them. He is careful: "Not so much a moral decline in the working class as it is a decline in the moral seriousness of the culture provided for the working class." But the structure of feeling of the second half is loss.

Williams, in *Culture is Ordinary*, will not have it. The Welsh valley he describes is not a museum being lost. It is a living formation that took the Industrial Revolution as a gift: "I know, from the most ordinary experience, that the interest is there, the capacity is there" (Williams, 1958: 12). When he sits in a house "with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" and listens to them read, watch, talk, he reports finding "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience" as he found anywhere else (Williams, 1958: 19). The empirical claim is identical to Hoggart's first half. The verdict on the new mass-cultural material is much less alarmed.

The deepest difference is conceptual. Hoggart's framework is literary, "uses of literacy", and his sociological imagination is tied to the cultural form of the printed page being supplanted by jukeboxes and tabloids. Williams's framework is anthropological-political, "a whole way of life", and so it is far more elastic. It can include the steam engine and the trade-union branch his father started, "without bitterness, of the 'kept men' of the new politics" (Williams, 1958: 11). It can include the Labour Party group in the village. It does not need a golden age of self-made culture to anchor it. Hoggart has Stuart Hall's "common sense" but only as folk wisdom; Williams already, here, has structure of feeling.

The test cuts in Williams's favour even within the materials the lecture sets side by side. Hoggart's "Invitation to a Candy Floss World" reads the new commercial culture, citing D. H. Lawrence's "anti-life," as "a corrupt brightness, of improper appeals and moral evasions" (Hoggart, 1957, slide 10). Faced with the same 1950s milk bar Williams would have asked, instead, what the youth in it are *making* of it: what relationships, idioms and small meanings the customers manufacture against and through the jukebox. Williams's own report on the people he listens to back home is precisely this kind of reading: "now they read, they watch, this work we are talking about; some of them quite critically, others with a good deal of pleasure" (Williams, 1958: 19). Williams can hold the contradictions; Hoggart's literary aesthetics keeps pulling him towards loss.

Williams himself is generous about this. He records that Leavis (and behind Leavis, the sensibility Hoggart shares) "knows more than any Marxist I have met about the real relations between art and experience. We have all learned from him in this" (Williams, 1958: 16). But the politics differ: Hoggart wants to defend the people; Williams wants the people to govern. His third wish in the essay is exactly that "the ordinary people should govern; that culture and education are ordinary; that there are no masses to save, to capture, or to direct" (Williams, 1958: 24).

For analysing British class culture, Williams's "whole way of life" is the better tool because it can register working-class cultural agency without elegy: the great working-class political and industrial institutions Williams names, trade unions, the Co-op, friendly societies, the Labour movement (Williams, 1958: 15), and the slide-17 legacy of Willis (1977), Mirza (1992), Skeggs (1997) and McKenzie (2015) inherit the framework on exactly Williams's terms. Hoggart gave us the scholarship boy at the "friction-point of two cultures" (Hoggart, 1957, slide 9); Williams gave us the bus journey on which the friction is reconciled.

### Essay 3. Williams on structure of feeling.

I want to argue that Williams's notion of structure of feeling is one of the most useful tools in cultural sociology because it names something specific that other concepts cannot quite reach: a shared, emergent way of sensing and understanding the world that has not yet hardened into formal ideology, institutions, or political programme. The slide-deck definition is the working one: "pre-emergent cultural experiences of a class not yet articulated in formal ideology" (lecture slide 6).

Williams develops the conceptual material across *Culture and Society* (Williams, 1958) and the later books named in the teacher summary, *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974). The seed is here in *Culture is Ordinary*: a culture is "common meanings, the product of a whole people, and offered individual meanings, the product of a man's whole committed personal and social experience" (Williams, 1958: 15). Such meanings, he insists, "cannot in any way be prescribed; they are made by living, made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance" (Williams, 1958: 15). Structure of feeling, in the slide-deck and teacher-summary formulation, names the in-between zone where new common meanings are being made but are not yet expressed in dominant ideology.

Three features matter. First, it is collective, not individual: it is not a mood, it is a structure. Second, it is felt before it is theorised, present in tone, vocabulary, gesture, popular culture, before it appears in manifestos. Third, it is "pre-emergent" in the slide's wording, meaning it belongs to what is coming into being rather than to what is established or surviving (lecture slide 6).

A first illustration sits inside Williams's own essay. The grandfather, a "big hard labourer," weeps "while he spoke, finely and excitedly, at the parish meeting, of being turned out of his cottage" (Williams, 1958: 11). The father, "not long before he died, spoke quietly and happily of when he had started a trade-union branch and a Labour Party group in the village, and, without bitterness, of the 'kept men' of the new politics" (Williams, 1958: 11). These are not yet "ideology" in any formal sense; they are felt meanings, articulated in voice and tone in a particular farming valley at a particular moment, that are nonetheless the cultural raw material from which an entire socialist-democratic political tradition is going to be made. Williams's autobiographical scene is a textbook structure of feeling in operation.

A second illustration is the structure of feeling registered on the slide-6 image grid itself: Dickens's *Hard Times*, 1970s punks on the Kings Road, miners after a shift, English flags on a suburban street. The lecture invites the student to see, across these four images, the same conceptual operation working in four different historical scenes: a class experience that the formal politics of the day has not yet articulated. The Victorian industrial novel articulates an emergent industrial-working-class feeling before it has a labour party; the punks articulate a feeling of post-imperial disrepair before any party has named it; the miners and the flags articulate a deindustrial English structure of feeling whose political housing remains contested.

A third illustration is Hoggart's first half of *The Uses of Literacy*: the 1930s "lived, self-made" working-class culture that Hoggart describes (Hoggart, 1957, slide 8) is structure of feeling at the granular level, proverbs, parlour singing, neighbourhood mutual obligation, caught at the moment when it is dense with shared meaning and not yet under sustained commercial pressure. Hoggart's second half then catches the next structure of feeling, the 1950s "world where things are done for the people" (slide 8), at the very moment when it is emerging and not yet ideologically settled.

The methodological pay-off is large. Structure of feeling tells the cultural sociologist to look first at the texts and practices, at how people read the *Daily Herald*, at how they speak at the parish meeting, at how a milk bar arranges its furniture and its jukebox, *before* the formal political articulation arrives, because that is where the emergent meanings are being lived. It also keeps Williams's politics in view: he wants the analyst to register what ordinary people are making of their conditions, not to score them against an ideology in advance. "The making of a society is the finding of common meanings and directions, and its growth is an active debate and amendment under the pressures of experience, contact, and discovery, writing themselves into the land" (Williams, 1958: 11). Structure of feeling is the name for the moment when those meanings have begun to be written but have not yet been read aloud.

### Essay 4. Why the autobiographical frame matters for British Cultural Studies.

I want to argue that Williams's decision to begin *Culture is Ordinary* with a bus journey is not literary decoration but methodological insistence: it is the founding gesture of British Cultural Studies precisely because it refuses the position of detached observer that elite literary criticism and structuralist Marxism both presupposed. The "I" of the essay is doing sociological work.

The opening is concrete and slow. Williams describes the Mappa Mundi at Hereford cathedral, the chained library where he had to "cajole a verger" for an hour, the cinema across the street advertising the *Six-Five Special*, the bus arriving "with a driver and a conductress deeply absorbed in each other" (Williams, 1958: 10). He goes "out of the city, over the old bridge, and on through the orchards and the green meadows, and the fields red under the plough" (Williams, 1958: 10). He climbs into the Black Mountains; the rock changes under the bus; he passes Norman castles, the iron workings, the farming valleys, and arrives in the valley where he grew up. The grandfather is a farm labourer turned roadman. The father is a railway signalman. He himself went to the village school where "a curtain divided the two classes," then to the local grammar school, then to Cambridge.

Why does this matter for BCS? First, it locates the analyst inside the culture under analysis. Williams is not a Cambridge don describing the curious folkways of the working class from outside; he is the signalman's son returning. When he says "culture is ordinary: that is where we must start" (Williams, 1958: 11), the "we" is not the academy but a "we" that includes the bus driver and the conductress. This is methodologically loaded. It commits cultural sociology to what would later be called positionality: the analyst is part of the social formation she analyses, and that situation is a resource, not a contaminant. Stuart Hall's later writing on race and Caribbean diaspora will inherit exactly this move.

Second, it commits BCS to lived experience as a primary category. Thompson's history from below and Hoggart's "uses of literacy" share the gesture. Against Althusserian structuralism, which will arrive in the 1960s and claim that culture is the operation of impersonal ideological apparatuses, Williams's bus journey insists that culture is *lived*: by his grandfather, who "wept while he spoke, finely and excitedly, at the parish meeting, of being turned out of his cottage" (Williams, 1958: 11); by his father, who, "not long before he died, spoke quietly and happily of when he had started a trade-union branch and a Labour Party group in the village" (Williams, 1958: 11). The unit of analysis is the man at the parish meeting, not the apparatus.

Third, it refuses elite literary culture's geography. Williams notes that he was "not, by the way, oppressed by Cambridge" (Williams, 1958: 12); he had come "from a country with twenty centuries of history written visibly into the earth." Cambridge's "bookcases I now sit to work at in Oxford are of the same design as those in the chained library." Learning was not exotic in his family: "Learning was ordinary; we learned where we could." This refuses Leavis's geography (true culture preserved in a Cambridge minority) and refuses Bloomsbury's (culture as a London teashop) and refuses Marxist sociology's (workers as the object, intellectuals as the subject). It claims a different geography: the bus route between cathedral and farming valley as the proper space of British culture.

Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, it makes the writer accountable. When Williams confesses that he had to "look, my training has done that for me" at his own family, "talking, as we were, about our own lives, about people, about feelings, could I in fact find this lack of quality we are discussing? … I can only say that I found as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19), he is reporting an empirical test conducted on his own people. He could have lied. He could have either elegised them (Hoggart's temptation) or pitied them (Leavis's). He does neither. The autobiographical frame is what keeps him honest.

What follows in British Cultural Studies, Hoggart's scholarship boy, Hall's Caribbean intellectual, Beverley Skeggs's working-class women, Lisa McKenzie's Nottingham estates, is the long continuation of this gesture. The sociologist of culture is permitted, and indeed required, to write from somewhere. The "view from nowhere" is refused. That refusal is what made BCS the first really popular sociology of culture in Britain, and it is what gives Williams's bus journey, sixty-eight years on, its continuing force.

### Essay 5. "There are no masses": Williams against Frankfurt School pessimism.

I want to argue that Williams's "there are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18) is one of the sharpest single sentences in twentieth-century sociology, and that it stages a complete alternative to the cultural pessimism of the Frankfurt School. Where Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* sees the culture industry producing an undifferentiated mass of administered subjects, Williams sees a class projection by those whose money "gave them access to the new communication techniques" (Williams, 1958: 18).

The Frankfurt position, as the SC301 module has staged it earlier in the year, is grim: jazz, cinema, popular newspapers and radio are reading instructions for consent; the masses produced by the culture industry are the "doped mass" Williams himself names on the last page (Williams, 1958: 24). Adorno's verdict is that the masses cannot tell because they have been made unable to tell. The political consequence is that intellectuals are the only surviving site of critique. Williams's response is, exactly, that this picture mistakes a method of seeing for a fact about people.

His argument runs in three steps. First, historical: the word "mass" enters English only with the Industrial Revolution. "The names of our social classes, and the word 'class' itself in this sense, date only from the Industrial Revolution" (Williams, 1958: 18). When industrialisation breaks the old face-to-face village social organisation, "it became a matter of difficult personal experience that we were constantly seeing people we did not know, and it was tempting to mass them, as 'the others', in our minds" (Williams, 1958: 18). Mass, in other words, is what people become when you do not know them.

Second, sociological: the new communications technologies amplified this mistake. "The improvement in communications, in particular the development of new forms of multiple transmission of news and entertainment, created unbridgeable divisions between transmitter and audience, which again led to the audience being interpreted as an unknown mass" (Williams, 1958: 18). The transmitter sees a mass because that is the only thing the transmission technique allows him to see; the technical position becomes a sociological theory. This is exactly Adorno's position. Williams names it as a position, not as an observation.

Third, empirical: Williams tests the verdict on his own family. He spent a few weeks "in a house with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" (Williams, 1958: 19). Could he find, in conversation with them, the "lack of quality" the culture-industry analysis says must be there? He found instead "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19). The Frankfurt verdict fails the empirical test.

Williams does concede the problem Adorno was naming. He admits the surprising fact "that people whose quality of personal living is high are apparently satisfied by a low quality of printed feeling and opinion" (Williams, 1958: 19). He does not deny that mass cultural products are often shoddy. What he denies is the inference that the shoddy product reveals the shoddy consumer. "Clearly there is something in the psychology of print and image that none of us has yet quite grasped" (Williams, 1958: 19). The puzzle is real; the Frankfurt solution to the puzzle is wrong.

A test from inside the essay makes the point. Williams's own father, "a satisfied reader of the *Daily Herald*," got "simply from reading the company reports a clear idea, based on names, of the rapid development of combine and interlocking ownership in British industry" (Williams, 1958: 19). A Frankfurt-style verdict would have to read this man as ideologically captured by his daily paper. Williams's verdict, by contrast, looks at the actual practice of reading, the company reports being mined for their political content, the opinion columns being set against the facts in academic essays the son had written, and refuses to mass this reader as a doped mass. The same point will later be formalised by Stuart Hall as the encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1980): the same encoded text can be read in dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, or oppositional ways, with the audience as active decoder. Hall's model is impossible without Williams's prior refusal of the masses thesis.

The pay-off for SC301 is methodological. Frankfurt pessimism produces a sociology that cannot listen, because it has already decided what the listener is going to find. Williams's refusal of "masses" is what permits the listening that British Cultural Studies and its sociological inheritors (Willis, 1977; Mirza, 1992; Skeggs, 1997; McKenzie, 2015) have done since. The sentence does the work of a method.

The closing of *Culture is Ordinary* makes the political stakes explicit. "The biggest difficulty is in accepting, deep in our minds, the values on which they depend: that the ordinary people should govern; that culture and education are ordinary; that there are no masses to save, to capture, or to direct, but rather this crowded people in the course of an extraordinarily rapid and confusing expansion of their lives" (Williams, 1958: 24). The Frankfurt School wanted to save the masses. Williams says the masses do not exist; the people do; and they govern themselves better than anyone is saving them.

### Essay 6. Williams on flow as the cultural form of television.

I want to argue that Williams's concept of flow, set out in *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974), is the single most useful concept produced by British Cultural Studies for analysing how broadcast media produce meaning, and that it is inseparable from his broader refusal of technological determinism and his demand that the political economy of communication be analysed alongside the texts.

Williams's argument in 1974 was directed against two then-dominant ways of thinking about television. The first was technological determinism: the view, as the teacher-summary puts it, that "media technologies themselves dictate social outcomes." Williams insisted instead that "technologies are developed and used within specific social and political contexts." Television's flow is not a technical property of cathode-ray tubes but a programming decision made by broadcasters embedded in particular political economies, public-service BBC, commercial ITV, US network television each producing different flows. The second target was the programme-by-programme analysis inherited from film and literature. Williams insisted, against this, that television is experienced not as a series of separable objects but as a flow, a continuous sequence in which the news bulletin, the trailer for next week's drama, the advert, the moment of music, and the next programme run into one another. The viewer turns on the box and is delivered into a sequence she did not choose. The unit of cultural analysis, Williams said, is not the programme; it is the evening. As the teacher-summary records: "broadcasting operates as a continuous sequence rather than a series of discrete programmes, shaping how audiences experience media in everyday life."

The conceptual pay-off is the claim about meaning. Because the viewer experiences sequence, "meanings emerge cumulatively rather than in isolation" (teacher-summary, citing Williams, 1974). A racist joke in a sitcom is not just that programme; it is a meaning produced by the joke *plus* the news bulletin half an hour earlier *plus* the advert *plus* tomorrow night's programming. This is exactly the kind of cumulative cultural form Stuart Hall would later analyse in "The Whites of Their Eyes": the "grammar of race" is not a single image but a structure built up across the flow, with comedy "protect[ing] / defend[ing] viewer from acknowledging racism" because the comic frame is followed and surrounded by other materials that license it (Hall, 1981a). Hall's argument is, in this sense, a direct application of Williams's flow concept to the analysis of racial ideology on television.

Three further consequences matter for the SC301 student. First, flow makes media political-economic in Williams's specific sense. *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974) both insist that the financing of broadcasting, and behind that the financing of the press, the £20,000,000 on the Arts Council against £365,000,000 on advertising (Williams, 1958: 22), is the determining structural fact about how flow is built. The advert is not an interruption of the flow; it is, in commercial television, what the flow is for.

Second, flow refuses the transmission model of communication. The viewer is not a passive recipient of discrete messages; she is the inhabitant of a sequence whose cumulative effect on her common meanings is the proper object of analysis. This is the same move Williams makes against the "masses" thesis in *Culture is Ordinary*: the audience is not a mass to be administered with ideology, it is a "crowded people in the course of an extraordinarily rapid and confusing expansion of their lives" (Williams, 1958: 24).

Third, flow does the conceptual work that lets the analyst read media as embedded in everyday life. The teacher-summary's formulation is precise: this approach treats "media as embedded in social relations rather than as neutral tools or autonomous forces." That sentence is the BCS alternative to both the Frankfurt School's culture industry and the older liberal "press as fourth estate" picture. Media are neither autonomous machines administering ideology nor neutral conduits of information; they are cultural forms whose flow is a programming decision with social and economic conditions.

The limit of the concept, candidly, is that Williams in 1974 was analysing scarce-channel broadcasting in which one flow was imposed on all viewers within a national broadcast schedule. The unit of analysis Williams names, the evening, assumes a relatively common cultural form. Where contemporary media diverge into individualised sequences, the framework still asks the right question (what is the cumulative effect of the sequence on common meanings?) but the unit of the answer has to be reconstructed. The question, however, "what is the flow doing to the cumulative production of meaning?", is still Williams's, and it is still the right one. Look at the sequence, not the unit; look at who programmed the sequence and on what economic basis; refuse both technological determinism and the discrete-programme picture. That is the cultural form of media analysis BCS gave us.

### Essay 7. Using Hall's encoding/decoding model to read *Love Thy Neighbour*.

I want to argue that Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model, set out in his 1973 essay "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Hall, 1980), remains the indispensable analytic instrument for British cultural sociology of media, and that the ITV sitcom *Love Thy Neighbour*, which Hall himself analyses in "The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media" (Hall, 1981a), is the textbook case both because Hall picks it and because its operations are exactly transferable to other television texts.

Hall's model rejects the linear-transmission picture of communication. Media messages are not parcels of meaning that producers pack and audiences unpack intact. Instead, producers *encode* messages using "frameworks of knowledge," "relations of production," and "technical infrastructure" (lecture slide 13); the message circulates as "programme as 'meaningful' discourse"; and audiences *decode* using their own frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, and technical infrastructure. Encoding and decoding are not symmetrical: there is no guarantee that the meaning encoded is the meaning decoded. Hall then identifies three positions a viewer can take: the dominant-hegemonic reading, in which the viewer takes the preferred meaning straight; the negotiated reading, in which the viewer broadly accepts the dominant frame but inflects it with her own situation; and the oppositional reading, in which the viewer reads the message within an alternative framework (teacher-summary; Hall, 1980). The model is underwritten by Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971): dominant readings are not neutral but ideological, secured by common sense, contestable.

*Love Thy Neighbour* is the ITV sitcom built around "a white working class couple (Eddie and Joan Booth) living in Twickenham, in the outer London suburbs, and a black couple (Bill and Barbie Reynolds) as their next-door neighbours" (teacher-summary; Hall, 1981a). Hall's argument is that the show encodes what he calls a "grammar of race": negative racial stereotypes, slave figure, native, clown / entertainer, circulating as traces "even if not so apparent," and race itself framed as "a problem" (lecture slide 14). Frameworks of knowledge: post-imperial Britain's commonsense racial categories. Relations of production: ITV as commercial popular broadcasting in the 1970s. Technical infrastructure: the studio-sitcom form with live laughter track.

A dominant-hegemonic reading of *Love Thy Neighbour* accepts the preferred meaning as something like "this is a comedy about two ordinary couples whose racial banter is good-natured English fun." Watching from this position, the viewer reads Eddie Booth's slurs as harmless jokes, hears the laughter track as confirmation, and treats the sitcom's grammar of race as comic rather than ideological. Hall's point is that this reading is precisely what the encoding makes available: the slave figure, the native, the clown / entertainer are encoded as "traces" through which racial stereotypes can be reproduced under the cover of comic licence.

A negotiated reading accepts that the show is "just a comedy" while inflecting it. A working-class viewer in Twickenham, recognising her own street in the programme, might find the Booths sympathetic and the Reynoldses funny, but might also negotiate the racial content: she may laugh at Eddie's stupidity rather than at the racial slur, or she may take the show as a chance to think about her own neighbours. The negotiated decoder accepts the dominant frame in outline but reorganises its emphases.

An oppositional reading detotalises the encoded preferred meaning. From an oppositional position, *Love Thy Neighbour* is itself an exhibit in the racial ideology Hall is naming: its comic frame is precisely what does the ideological work, because, in Hall's formulation, "comedy protects / defends viewer from acknowledging racism" (Hall, 1981a; lecture slide 14). On this reading, the laughter track is not innocent and the "good-natured" banter is not innocent; the encoding produces the grammar of race even where viewers report not noticing it. Hall calls the resulting structure one of "contradictions and ambivalence": "decoded by viewers in ways that might contradict the encoding," and yet still doing the ideological work of installing the slave-figure / native / clown grammar (Hall, 1981a).

Hall's argument extends to a second study in the same module of materials: *Policing the Crisis: Mugging, the State, and Law and Order* (Hall et al., 1978). There the encoding is the press-and-state construction of "mugging" as a moral panic, and the cumulative dominant reading is the racialised criminal-on-the-streets common sense that secured authoritarian state response to the 1970s organic crisis. The same conceptual machinery, encoding, dominant / negotiated / oppositional decoding, hegemony as the form in which the dominant reading is secured, does the work.

The pay-off of Hall's model is that all three readings are real audience responses, occurring simultaneously, and the sociologist's job is not to declare one correct but to map the field of decoding. The model also implies that the preferred encoding is *not* guaranteed to land. A teenage Black British viewer of *Love Thy Neighbour* in 1972 was not obliged to receive the encoded grammar of race; her oppositional decoding was, and is, exactly the empirical fact the model was built to handle. Encoding/decoding remains, fifty-three years after the essay, the right tool, and Hall's *Love Thy Neighbour* analysis is the model case-study because the encoding, decoding, contradiction and ambivalence are all visible in a single half-hour cultural form.

### Essay 8. Hall's grammar of race and racial ideology on television.

I want to argue that Stuart Hall's "The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media" (Hall, 1981a) provides the foundational BCS framework for analysing race in popular television, and that the framework's strength lies in three specific moves: the identification of a "grammar of race" in the encoded image, the demonstration that ideological work proceeds through *traces* rather than overt slurs, and the insight that comedy structurally licenses the racist content of the comic text.

Hall's 1981 argument identified a "grammar of race" in 1970s British television (lecture slide 14). Negative racial stereotypes were encoded through three principal figures: the slave figure, the native, and the clown / entertainer. These were often present as "traces" even when not overt; race was framed as "a problem"; and decoding produced "contradictions and ambivalence", meanings that could "contradict the encoding," and a comic frame that, in Hall's central insight, "protects / defends viewer from acknowledging racism" (Hall, 1981a; lecture slide 14). The hegemonic work of racist representation could proceed precisely because the comic framing licensed a "we don't really mean it" decoding. The grammar of race did not require crude vilification; it required structural positioning of Black characters in the slave / native / entertainer slots.

The textbook case is *Love Thy Neighbour*: the ITV sitcom built around "a white working class couple (Eddie and Joan Booth) living in Twickenham, in the outer London suburbs, and a black couple (Bill and Barbie Reynolds) as their next-door neighbours" (teacher-summary; Hall, 1981a). Hall's reading is structural rather than content-focused. The grammar of race is not located in any single slur but in the way the Booth-Reynolds pairing positions the Reynoldses as the comic objects whose blackness is the *occasion* for the joke. The encoding produces traces of the entertainer position even where individual scenes look "balanced." The decoding splits: a dominant reading takes the show as harmless; a negotiated reading laughs at Eddie's stupidity rather than at the racial content; an oppositional reading, especially from Black British viewers, names the comic frame as the ideological mechanism.

A second case in the lecture materials is *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978), where the grammar of race operates not in comedy but in the press construction of "mugging" as a racialised street-crime moral panic. As the teacher-summary records, the study shows "how a relatively limited phenomenon, a moral panic, street robbery, labelled as 'mugging' became a major political and moral issue in 1970s Britain," with "media, policing, and state power interact[ing] to produce ideological responses to wider social and economic crises." The slide-16 image grid, petrol shortage forecourt, National Front rally, "Suspicious? Call the Police" poster, uncollected Winter-of-Discontent refuse, "Britain on Half Time" headline, the policing of a Black man on a London street, NUR pickets, Enoch Powell's "stop this flow of coloured immigrants" press cutting, an anti-police-violence protest, assembles the visual conditions in which the grammar of race is being deployed across the 1970s organic crisis. The same hegemonic operation Hall identified in *Love Thy Neighbour* is operating here on a larger canvas: race is framed as a problem, encoded with traces of older imperial stereotypes, and read in contradiction and ambivalence by audiences who may not fully recognise what they are receiving.

The conceptual sharpness of Hall's framework is that it links three pieces. First, encoding/decoding (Hall, 1980) supplies the model of media meaning: producers encode using frameworks of knowledge, relations of production and technical infrastructure, and audiences decode through their own. Second, Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), which Hall draws on heavily according to the teacher-summary, supplies the political theory: dominant groups secure consent by making their meanings feel like common sense. Third, the grammar of race (Hall, 1981a) supplies the specific cultural mechanism on television: slave figure, native, clown / entertainer, all as traces, all licensed by comedy or by the "problem" frame. Together these three pieces let the sociologist of culture analyse race in popular television without either reducing it to overt content or evaporating it into discourse.

The limit, candidly acknowledged inside BCS, is that Hall is writing about the racial ideology of a particular British televisual moment. The framework asks to be extended, and the slide-17 sociological legacy, Mirza's *Young, Female and Black* (1992) and the rest, is the place where the extension was done. But the analytical engine is Hall's, and it still works: the grammar of race is encoded, decoded in contradictions and ambivalence, and comedy still protects viewers from acknowledging racism. Hall is not yet historical; he is the toolkit.

### Essay 9. Thompson on history from below and the sociology of culture.

I want to argue that E. P. Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* (Thompson, 1963) supplies British Cultural Studies with its most important historiographical commitment, history from below, and that the move matters for the contemporary sociology of culture because it refuses to let either economic structures or elite ideologies do the explanatory work that lived experience and cultural agency must do.

Thompson's preface to *The Making* contains the most quoted sentence in British social history: he wanted to rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the "obsolete" hand-loom weaver, the "utopian" artisan, even the deluded follower of Joanna Southcott, from "the enormous condescension of posterity." The methodological commitment is then unmistakable. The English working class did not appear as the deposit of an economic structure; it *made itself*, in active cultural and political work, in chapels, friendly societies, Corresponding Societies, food riots, radical pamphlets, hymns and trade-union banners. Class is, in Thompson's formula, "a relationship, not a thing", something people do, in struggle, with one another and against others.

Three concepts in Thompson matter for L14. First, the lived experience of class: class is felt before it is structured. Thompson's working-class artisans experience exploitation as a moral as well as economic outrage and respond in idiom, religious, customary, traditional, that does not look "modern" but is in fact the work of cultural self-making. Second, history from below: the historian's responsibility is to write from the standpoint of those who did not leave parliamentary speeches behind, who appear in the archive as criminal records, parish reports, magistrates' notes, and seditious pamphlets. Third, the moral economy: in his 1971 essay "The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century," Thompson showed that eighteenth-century food riots were not blind hunger eruptions but principled enforcement of customary fair-price norms against new market behaviour. The crowd had a theory; the historian has to take it seriously.

The pay-off for the sociology of culture is fourfold. First, against structuralist Marxism (Althusser arrived in Britain in the late 1960s), Thompson supplied an account of class in which agency and culture were not afterthoughts to be "explained" by the relations of production but the very medium in which class came into being. The famous Thompson-Althusser exchange in *The Poverty of Theory* (1978) ran on exactly this point.

Second, against Frankfurt School pessimism, Thompson's working class was not a "mass" administered by ideology but a self-organising cultural formation. Williams's "no masses" thesis and Thompson's "the class made itself" are the same move from different disciplinary angles, sociology of culture from one side, social history from the other.

Third, Thompson supplied a method of reading sources from below. The minute book of a Friendly Society, the chapel hymn-book, the pamphlet sold for a penny, the inscriptions on a union banner, these are now legitimate documents of cultural analysis. The slide-17 inheritors, Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977), Heidi Safia Mirza's *Young, Female and Black* (1992), Beverley Skeggs's *Formations of Class and Gender* (1997) and Lisa McKenzie's *Getting By: Estates, Class and Culture in Austerity Britain* (2015), all draw on Thompson's licence to read non-canonical cultural materials seriously.

Fourth, the moral economy concept travels into the cultural sociology the rest of the lecture maps. Williams's working-class institutions, trade-union branch, Co-op, friendly society, the Labour movement, the "kept men" his father remarked on (Williams, 1958: 11, 15), are moral-economy formations in Thompson's sense: customary norms about how working people should be treated, defended in idioms that exceed pure economic calculation. Hoggart's first half of *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957), in the slide-8 description, is a portrait of 1930s "lived, self-made" working-class culture organised around precisely such customary norms; Hoggart's worry in the second half is that the "world where things are done for the people" is dissolving them.

Two cases inside the lecture materials show Thompson's framework operating. The first is the slide-16 image grid: NUR pickets, Winter-of-Discontent refuse, anti-police-violence protests. To read these only through industrial-relations data is to miss the moral-economy claim being made, the customary norm of fair pay and decent policing being defended through collective action. The second is *Policing the Crisis* itself (Hall et al., 1978), where the criminalisation of the "mugger" is precisely the breaking of an older moral-economic settlement between communities and the state, and where the popular response can be read as moral-economic resistance to the new authoritarian frame.

The limits are real. Thompson wrote about white English men in the early industrial revolution; gender and race are largely absent in *The Making*. The slide-17 corrections, Mirza on young, female and Black experience; Skeggs on class and gender; McKenzie on estates under austerity, make clear what Thompson's "below" excluded. But these are corrections from within his method, not refutations of it. History from below remains the right commitment for the sociology of culture. It tells us that the proper unit of analysis is not the apparatus, the ideology, or the structure, but the lived activity by which working people make themselves into something they were not before.

### Essay 10. "Culture is a site of struggle." Discuss with reference to two BCS thinkers.

I want to argue that the BCS slogan "culture is a site of struggle" is best understood by reading Williams and Hall together: Williams supplies the *ontology* (culture is the whole way of life in which struggles over meaning happen), Hall supplies the *mechanics* (encoding, decoding, hegemony, common sense, moral panic). The phrase is not a vague claim that "culture is political"; it is a specific claim about how power operates in late capitalism.

Williams's contribution is to refuse the two positions that would deny that culture is a site of struggle. The first is the elite literary view that treats culture as a finished inheritance to be transmitted to those capable of receiving it. Williams's Cambridge teashop is full of "cultivated people" who "showed you they had it" (Williams, 1958: 12). If culture is what is already settled in the teashop, then it is not a site of struggle, it is a site of curatorship. Williams refuses: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12). The second position is the reflectionist Marxism which treats culture as the secondary expression of an economic base. If culture is a reflection of production relations, then it is not a site of struggle in its own right; it is at best a symptom. Williams accepts that "a culture is a whole way of life, and the arts are part of a social organisation which economic change clearly radically affects" (Williams, 1958: 14). He refuses the "directive element" that follows. Culture is *both* shaped by production *and* a domain of struggle in which production relations are contested, naturalised, refused, lived.

The struggle, in Williams's account, is fought through common meanings. "A culture is common meanings, the product of a whole people, and offered individual meanings, the product of a man's whole committed personal and social experience" (Williams, 1958: 15). Common meanings are not given; they are "made and remade." The struggle is over which meanings become common and on whose terms. His own argument with the "do-gooder" / "highbrow" argot, with Bell's *Civilisation*, with the Cambridge teashop, with Leavis's dying-culture diagnosis and with the Marxist directive on prescribed forms is itself a series of struggles over what "culture" will be allowed to mean. Concepts like residual / dominant / emergent and the long revolution name a dynamic field, not a static base/superstructure schema but a culture in motion in which struggle is ongoing.

Hall picks up Williams's ontology and gives it Gramscian mechanics. Hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), the process by which dominant groups secure consent rather than relying on force, by which power operates through common sense, names the form the struggle takes. The dominant order does not have to win by police repression; it wins by making its meanings feel natural, obvious, "common sense." The site of that struggle is exactly the cultural domain Williams named. The encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1980) gives the struggle a precise location: in the encoding moment, dominant frameworks of knowledge work to install a preferred reading; in the decoding moment, audiences may take the preferred reading whole, negotiate it, or oppose it. The struggle is not metaphorical; it is happening in living rooms in front of the television, in the press, in classrooms, in the encoded text and the decoding moment.

*Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) is the textbook case. Hall and his colleagues show how a relatively limited social phenomenon, street robbery, became, through media labelling, policing intensification, and political signal-sending, the "mugging" moral panic of the mid-1970s. The struggle is over what counts as the public problem: is it racial criminality (the dominant frame) or is it youth unemployment, deindustrialisation, and a state in crisis (an oppositional frame)? The hegemonic outcome was the former; the cost of that outcome was the closing-down of alternative political readings of the 1970s organic crisis. Culture, here, is not a reflection of the political-economic crisis; it is the site at which the crisis is *resolved* into one ideological story rather than another. This is what "culture is a site of struggle" actually means.

Two applications inside the lecture materials make the model concrete. First, *Love Thy Neighbour* is a site-of-struggle case at sitcom scale: the encoded preferred reading installs the grammar of race in the comic frame; negotiated and oppositional decodings contest the meaning in the living room (Hall, 1981a). The struggle is over whether the show is "just a comedy" or whether the comic frame is the ideological mechanism. Hall's argument is that the hegemonic reading wins on television in 1972 precisely because the comic licence makes the racial encoding feel like common sense rather than ideology.

Second, the *Policing the Crisis* mugging panic (Hall et al., 1978) is a site-of-struggle case at national scale. The struggle is over what counts as the public problem of the 1970s organic crisis: is it racialised criminality on the streets (the dominant frame, secured by press encoding and policing intensification) or is it youth unemployment, deindustrialisation, and an authoritarian state response to declining post-war settlement (an oppositional frame)? The hegemonic outcome was the former; the slide-16 image grid (petrol shortage forecourt, National Front rally, "Suspicious? Call the Police," Winter-of-Discontent refuse, "Britain on Half Time," Black-man-being-policed, NUR picket, Powell's "stop this flow of coloured immigrants," anti-police-violence protest) shows the visual material out of which the dominant reading was assembled.

The cost of treating culture as a site of struggle is that the sociology of culture becomes harder. Reflection theory was easier, find the base, derive the superstructure. Williams-Hall analysis requires close reading of cultural texts, attention to encoding regimes and decoding practices, ethnography of audiences, and historical placement in residual / dominant / emergent fields. It also runs a political risk: if culture is the site at which power is contested, then cultural intervention (a TV drama, a song, a meme) is taken seriously as politics, which can flatter the cultural producer into believing he is the revolution. Williams and Hall both knew this; both warned against it. But the risk is the price of taking culture, and ordinary people, seriously.

### Essay 11. "There is a distinct working-class way of life." The politics of British Cultural Studies.

I want to argue that Williams's sentence "There is a distinct working-class way of life, which I for one value" (Williams, 1958: 15) is the most precise single statement of the politics of British Cultural Studies. The sentence does three things at once: it asserts the existence of a class culture; it values that culture without sentimentalising it; and it makes the analyst declare himself politically. All three moves are constitutive of BCS as a project.

The first move, assertion of distinct working-class culture, refuses two erasures. It refuses the elite literary erasure (working people have no culture worth the name; they consume what their betters make for them). It also refuses the determinist Marxist erasure (working-class culture is just bourgeois culture imposed on the workers; there is no autonomous form). Williams insists that the working-class way of life exists in its own right, "with its emphases of neighbourhood, mutual obligation, and common betterment, as expressed in the great working-class political and industrial institutions" (Williams, 1958: 15). The empirical claim is contestable but at minimum points to actual social formations, trade unions, friendly societies, the Co-op, the Labour movement, the Nonconformist chapel network, and the working-men's adult-education institutions Williams names in his third wish (Williams, 1958: 22), which were not bourgeois inventions and which had distinct internal cultures.

The second move, valuing without sentimentalising, is harder. Hoggart, working three years earlier than Williams, slides easily into elegy: the 1930s as a lived self-made culture being killed by 1950s candy-floss. Thompson, working five years later, slides at moments into heroic identification with the early working class as the agent of history. Williams declines both. He values the working-class way of life "not only because I was bred in it, for I now, in certain respects, live differently. I think this way of life … is in fact the best basis for any future English society" (Williams, 1958: 15). The valuation is forward-looking, not nostalgic: it is the basis for the future, not a relic to be preserved. He is also clear-eyed about what was hard: "dirty water, an earth bucket, a fourmile walk each way to work, headaches, broken women, hunger and monotony of diet" (Williams, 1958: 17). The working-class way of life is not romanticised.

The third move, the analyst declares himself, is the constitutive BCS gesture. "I for one value" makes the writer accountable. He is not reporting a neutral fact; he is taking a side. The political position is socialist-democratic, attached to the institutions and ethos of the labour movement, and committed to the proposition that "the ordinary people should govern" (Williams, 1958: 24). The "socialist intellectual" has a job: "to attack the clamps on that energy", the energy of the ordinary people, "in industrial relations, public administration, education, for a start" (Williams, 1958: 24).

This declared politics is what distinguishes BCS from later, more academic Cultural Studies. By the 1980s and 1990s the field had become more methodologically sophisticated but in places less politically anchored: discourse analysis without a labour movement, semiotic readings of consumer goods without an account of the production relations that made them. Williams's sentence is a reminder of the original commitment. The cultural analyst is on the side of the working people whose culture she analyses.

There are real costs to this politics. The "we" of Williams's Welsh valley is gendered male, white, and Christian-secular; women, racialised workers, and non-British migrants are not adequately included. Beverley Skeggs's *Formations of Class and Gender* (1997) and Heidi Safia Mirza's *Young, Female and Black* (1992) corrected the gender and race blindness; Stuart Hall's writing on race and the New Left did similar work from within. The working-class movement Williams "valued" has also fragmented since 1958: deindustrialisation, the destruction of the mining communities in the 1980s, the decline of the labour-movement institutions he named (trade unions, Co-op, friendly societies, working men's clubs). Lisa McKenzie's *Getting By: Estates, Class and Culture in Austerity Britain* (2015) documents what the working-class way of life looks like after thirty years of neoliberalism: more atomised, more precarious, less institutionally rooted, but still, McKenzie insists, in Williams's voice, distinct, valuable, and worth analysing on its own terms.

The empirical force of the sentence rests on the institutions Williams names a few lines later: "neighbourhood, mutual obligation, and common betterment, as expressed in the great working-class political and industrial institutions" (Williams, 1958: 15). These are not abstract categories; they are the trade-union branch and the Labour Party group his father started in the village (Williams, 1958: 11), the working men's institutions through which an entire educational and political tradition was built outside the bourgeois university, the Friendly Societies and chapels Thompson would later trace as the site at which the working class "made itself" (Thompson, 1963), and the 1930s neighbourhood culture Hoggart documents in the first half of *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957, slide 8). When Williams "for one" values this way of life he is valuing a specific cultural-institutional formation that the rest of the lecture can read in detail.

The risk Williams himself names is sentimentality. "I would not expect the working people of England to support works which, after proper and patient preparation, they could not accept" (Williams, 1958: 22). He is not flattering working-class taste; he is committing to extending and changing cultural provision in a way that the working people themselves will help to shape. The politics of British Cultural Studies, at its best, is exactly this commitment: distinct culture, valued, by an analyst on its side, working to extend it.

### Essay 12. What does British Cultural Studies put in place of high culture and reflection theory, and at what cost?

I want to argue that British Cultural Studies replaces two earlier orthodoxies, elite literary "high culture" and the Marxist base-superstructure reflection thesis, with a unified alternative: culture as ordinary, lived, contested, hegemonic, encoded and decoded. The replacement is a real intellectual advance but has costs that are still being paid: a tendency to overstate cultural agency, a relative neglect of political economy, and a difficulty in distinguishing analytically meaningful cultural struggles from sociologically trivial ones.

The two orthodoxies that BCS displaces are the elite literary position (associated in Williams's text with Clive Bell, the Cambridge teashop, and a softer Leavisite reading) and the Marxist reflectionist position (associated with the Frankfurt School in the SC301 module and with crude British communist cultural orthodoxy in Williams's text). The elite position says that culture is the special preserve of cultivated people who possess it as "the outward and emphatically visible sign" of their cultivation (Williams, 1958: 12). The reflectionist position says that culture is the secondary expression of an economic base. Williams refuses both with a single move: "We use the word culture in these two senses: to mean a whole way of life, the common meanings; to mean the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort. … I insist on both, and on the significance of their conjunction" (Williams, 1958: 11).

What BCS puts in place is a four-part alternative. First, an ontology: culture is the whole way of life of a people, in which both common meanings and special processes of discovery happen. Hoggart's "uses of literacy," Thompson's "lived experience of class," Hall's "hegemony" all sit inside Williams's "whole way of life." Second, a method: history from below (Thompson, 1963), close reading of working-class cultural materials (Hoggart, 1957), structure-of-feeling analysis (Williams, 1961; 1977), encoding/decoding analysis of media texts (Hall, 1980). Third, a politics: socialist-democratic, on the side of ordinary people, committed to the proposition that the ordinary people should govern. Fourth, an institutional location: the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS) at Birmingham, founded in 1964, which becomes the laboratory for the new approach.

This alternative achieves three things the displaced orthodoxies could not. It makes working-class and popular cultural materials sociologically legible without elegy. It accounts for media without technological determinism (Williams on TV) or transmission-of-ideology determinism (Hall on encoding/decoding). And it permits political reading of culture without prescribing correct cultural content, Williams refuses the "directive element" in Marxism, Hall refuses to declare any audience reading "false." The intellectual gain is large.

The costs are also real. The first cost is the *over-extension of cultural agency*. Once "everything is culture" and "audiences negotiate or oppose," it becomes tempting to read every consumption choice as resistance, every act of decoding as politically significant, every popular text as a site of struggle. The empirical fact that audiences decode actively, established by Hall (1980), does not entail that every act of decoding is politically meaningful, and BCS in its weaker variants slid towards that conclusion. The strongest BCS work, including Hall's own *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978), was sharply disciplined about distinguishing decodings that mattered politically from those that did not.

The second cost is the *relative neglect of political economy* outside Williams himself. BCS analysed media texts brilliantly, and Williams's *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974) do attend to who owns the media and on what economic basis (Williams's £20,000,000 vs £365,000,000 figures, Williams, 1958: 22, are the kind of political-economic accounting BCS needs to keep doing). But the broader tradition was less consistent in following this lead, with cultural-text analysis sometimes detached from the relations of production Williams insisted were determining.

The third cost is the *difficulty of distinguishing significant from trivial cultural struggles*. If culture is a site of struggle and every text encodes and is decoded, then the field is flat: how do we tell the *Policing the Crisis* mugging panic, clearly a struggle of national-historical weight, from a comic exchange on a long-forgotten sitcom? Williams's residual / dominant / emergent triad helps; Thompson's moral economy helps; Hall's hegemony helps. But the analyst still has to make a judgement, and BCS gives only partial guidance on how to make it.

The fourth, and most discussed, cost is the *category of class itself*. BCS was built on a robust account of British class culture, Williams's signalman father, Hoggart's 1930s neighbourhood (Hoggart, 1957, slide 8), Thompson's hand-loom weavers (Thompson, 1963). That class culture has been deeply transformed by deindustrialisation, neoliberalism, and the destruction of the labour-movement institutions Williams "valued" at the close of *Culture is Ordinary*. The slide-17 inheritors (Willis, 1977; Mirza, 1992; Skeggs, 1997; McKenzie, 2015) have updated the class analysis; but the framework itself has to keep adapting to post-industrial cultural conditions Williams could not foresee.

A case from inside the lecture materials shows both the achievement and the cost. *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) can be analysed by a BCS framework with real power: as a moral panic articulated through press encoding and decoded in multiple ways, under hegemonic conditions in which "common sense" about racialised street crime was built across the 1970s organic crisis. This is intellectually serious sociology of culture. But a fuller analysis also needs the political economy of post-war Britain, the petrol shortage, the Winter of Discontent, the slide-16 visual conditions of crisis, and the institutional analysis of state-press-police interaction that the *Policing the Crisis* authors themselves provide. The point, then, is not that BCS is wrong but that the strongest BCS work has always integrated cultural and political-economic analysis, and that the cost of the tradition's diffusion has been a sometimes uneven inheritance of that integration.

I would conclude: BCS replaced two intellectually exhausted orthodoxies with a tradition that has now itself become orthodox. Williams's "culture is ordinary" is sixty-eight years old; Hall's encoding/decoding is fifty-three years old; Thompson's *Making* is sixty-three years old. They remain the right starting point, and the cost of replacing them with anything else would be enormous, back to the teashop, back to the reflection, back to the masses. But each of the three founders would have been the first to insist that the tradition keep moving. Culture is ordinary, and so is its analysis: made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance.
