# L14. Culture is Ordinary (Raymond Williams, British Cultural Studies)

## §1. One-line thesis

Raymond Williams's argument that culture is ordinary refounds the study of culture as the analysis of a whole way of life, common meanings made and remade by working people themselves, and, together with Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy*, Thompson on history from below, and Hall's encoding/decoding model, launches British Cultural Studies as a tradition that rejects both elite "high culture" and the Marxist base-superstructure reflection thesis, treating culture instead as a political site of struggle where hegemony is contested in the lived experience of class.

## §2. Why this lecture matters in SC301

L14 is the spine of the Spring term's "culture, class, media" arc and it sits at the joint between two earlier moves on the SC301 syllabus: the critique of mass-society pessimism (Adorno, Frankfurt School) and the Marxist account of ideology (base/superstructure). British Cultural Studies (BCS) is the moment when sociology stops asking "what does high culture mean?" and starts asking "what do working-class lives mean to those who live them?", and decides that the answer is not "they are dupes of capitalism," but that ordinary people make culture and remake themselves through it. This matters for SC301 because the lecture supplies a vocabulary, structure of feeling, the long revolution, flow (in TV), residual/dominant/emergent, encoding/decoding, hegemony, common sense, history from below, the moral economy, the working-class autodidact, that you will need across the media, class, race, and modernity essays. It also sets up L17 (Stuart Hall on race and representation), the gender lectures (Skeggs and others on the slide-deck legacy list), and any question about whether modernity homogenises culture. Williams's biographical claim, "I was born and grew up halfway along that bus journey" (Williams, 1958: 10), is doing methodological work: he insists that the analyst belongs to the culture under analysis, that the sociologist of culture is not Clive Bell in his Cambridge teashop but the railway signalman's son going back home. The lecture is, finally, a teachable counter to cultural pessimism: it refuses the people-without-history thesis and insists "that there are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18).

## §4. Williams's argument in *Culture is Ordinary*, step by step

1. **Biography as method.** Williams opens with a bus journey from Hereford cathedral up the Black Mountains to a Welsh farming valley. His grandfather a farm labourer turned roadman; his father a railway signalman; he himself the grammar-school boy who goes to Cambridge. "Culture is ordinary: that is where we must start" (Williams, 1958: 11).
2. **Two senses of culture, both insisted on.** "We use the word culture in these two senses: to mean a whole way of life, the common meanings; to mean the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort. Some writers reserve the word for one or other of these senses; I insist on both, and on the significance of their conjunction" (Williams, 1958: 11).
3. **Against the teashop.** At Cambridge Williams encounters "culture" as "the outward and emphatically visible sign of a special kind of people, cultivated people" (Williams, 1958: 12). He refuses this: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12).
4. **Against Clive Bell.** Bell's *Civilisation* divides "ordinary people and ordinary work" from culture as with "a park wall" (Williams, 1958: 12). Williams: at home "we met and made music, listened to it, recited and listened to poems, valued fine language" (Williams, 1958: 12).
5. **Against the new "argot" of the culture-vultures.** A new petty class of "do-gooders and highbrows and superior prigs" has "invented and disseminated the argot, in an attempt to influence ordinary people" (Williams, 1958: 13).
6. **Two serious influences he keeps in dialogue with.** "When I got to Cambridge I encountered two serious influences which have left a very deep impression on my mind. The first was Marxism, the second the teaching of Leavis" (Williams, 1958: 14).
7. **What he takes from Marxism, what he refuses.** He accepts that "a culture is a whole way of life, and the arts are part of a social organisation which economic change clearly radically affects" (Williams, 1958: 14). He refuses the "directive element", the idea "that if you honestly want socialism you must write, think, learn in certain prescribed ways" (Williams, 1958: 15).
8. **What he takes from Leavis, what he refuses.** He accepts that an "old, mainly agricultural England" has been replaced by "a modern, organised, industrial state" (Williams, 1958: 16). He refuses Leavis's verdict that the new commercial culture is total and that the masses are lost. Of mass-trinket nostalgia he says: "not in a million years would you make us give up this power", plumbing, tractors, medicines, contraceptives (Williams, 1958: 17).
9. **Four legacies to be cleared away.** Williams identifies "a legacy of two false equations, one false analogy, and one false proposition" (Williams, 1958: 17): (i) ugliness as price of power, false; (ii) popular education breeds commercial culture, false; (iii) bad popular culture proves the inner badness of its consumers, false; (iv) Gresham's Law in culture, bad driving out good, false.
10. **The "masses" formula.** "There are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18). Mass is a class projection.
11. **Three wishes.** Williams ends with three: that education be recognised as ordinary; that public provision for arts and adult learning be increased ("we now spend £20,000,000 annually on … the Arts Council … At the same time we spend £365,000,000 annually on advertising", Williams, 1958: 22); and that mass culture's keystone, advertising revenue, be addressed.
12. **The closing image.** The bus journey, again: "where the language changes but the voice is the same" (Williams, 1958: 24).

## §5. The wider BCS field (Hoggart, Thompson, Hall) and the contemporary relevance

**Hoggart.** *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957) is a book of two halves: the 1930s "lived," "self-made" working-class culture of his Leeds childhood, and the 1950s "world where things are done for the people." Hoggart's scholarship boy is "at the friction-point of two cultures," "sad and also solitary." The "Juke Box Boy" passage condemns the commercial cafe culture as "a peculiarly thin and pallid form of dissipation, a sort of spiritual dry-rot amid the odour of boiled milk." Hoggart's loss is more cautious than Adorno's; he insists the moral failure lies in "the culture provided for the working class," not in working-class people themselves.

**Thompson.** *The Making of the English Working Class* (Thompson, 1963): class made itself, in struggle, in chapels and pubs and Corresponding Societies and food riots; it was not the deposit of an economic structure. Thompson supplies the slogan "history from below" and the concept of the moral economy. His insistence on lived experience and human agency is the BCS counter to structuralist Marxism.

**Hall.** Drawing on Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), Hall's 1973 essay "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Hall, 1980) demolishes the transmission model of media: producers encode, audiences decode, with dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, and oppositional positions available. "The Whites of Their Eyes" (Hall, 1981a) reads the grammar of race in 1970s sitcoms like *Love Thy Neighbour*: the comedy "protects/defends viewer from acknowledging racism." *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) turns the moral panic over "mugging" into a case study of how state, media and policing manufacture ideological responses to the 1970s organic crisis.

## §8. Full essays

### Essay 1. "Culture is ordinary." Critically assess Raymond Williams's claim.

Williams's claim that culture is ordinary is the founding move of British Cultural Studies because it democratises both the *object* of cultural analysis and the *position* of the analyst. The slogan is not a celebration of pop culture, nor an attack on Shakespeare. It is a demand that we hold both senses of culture together, "a whole way of life, the common meanings" and "the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort" (Williams, 1958: 11), and refuse the class division that puts them in different rooms.

The argument is staged biographically. Williams opens with a bus from Hereford cathedral up into the Black Mountains, past the Mappa Mundi and Norman castles, into a farming valley where his grandfather was "turned out of his cottage and, in his fifties, became a roadman" (Williams, 1958: 11). His father became a railway signalman; he himself went to grammar school and Cambridge. The opening puts the sociologist of culture inside the culture under analysis. When he arrives in the Cambridge teashop, he finds "culture, not in any sense I knew, but in a special sense: the outward and emphatically visible sign of a special kind of people, cultivated people" (Williams, 1958: 12). His verdict: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12). He has been on the other end of the bus route.

The argument then clears two rival positions. The first is the cultural pessimism Williams calls "Leavis": the diagnosis that "an old, mainly agricultural England" has been replaced by "a modern, organised, industrial state" whose institutions "deliberately cheapen our natural human responses" (Williams, 1958: 16). Williams rejects the conclusion. The Industrial Revolution brought "the gift of power that is everything to men who have worked with their hands" (Williams, 1958: 16), plumbing, electricity, contraceptives, canned food. "Not in a million years would you make us give up this power" (Williams, 1958: 17). The second target is Marxist economism: the view that culture is a reflection of the economic base, with prescribed correct forms. Williams refuses the "directive element, this insistence that if you honestly want socialism you must write, think, learn in certain prescribed ways" (Williams, 1958: 15).

What is left is the positive thesis: culture is "made by living, made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance" (Williams, 1958: 15). This makes culture political without being prescribed, and makes the "masses" a category mistake. Williams's most quoted line, "there are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18), is a sociological knife. Mass is a name imposed by those whose money "gave them access to the new communication techniques" (Williams, 1958: 18) on the people they did not know.

The empirical test is the strongest demonstration. Returning home, Williams sits "in a house with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" and asks whether he can find the "lack of quality" pessimists insist must be there (Williams, 1958: 19). He cannot. He records "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19). His father, a "satisfied reader of the *Daily Herald*," works out from company reports an account of "combine and interlocking ownership in British industry" (Williams, 1958: 19). Ordinary people do the thinking the teashop assumes they cannot do.

The thesis still travels. Working-class popular culture today, from *Coronation Street* and *EastEnders* to Stormzy and Oasis, from rugby league to the *Great British Bake Off*, is the same field of common meanings Williams named as ordinary. The Open University, food banks under the cost-of-living crisis, and streaming platforms as a shared cultural environment all need the Williams move: refuse to mass these people, attend to what they make of their conditions.

The limits are real. Williams writes before the cultural turn took race and gender seriously; the "we" of his Welsh valley is a particular common, and the slide-17 legacy (Mirza, 1992; Skeggs, 1997; McKenzie, 2015) repairs the blind spots. But the underlying move, refuse the teashop, refuse the dying-culture verdict, refuse to mass the people you do not know, is still the best opening British sociology of culture ever wrote. Culture is ordinary; that is where we must still start.

### Essay 2. Compare Williams's "whole way of life" with Hoggart on working-class culture.

Although Williams and Hoggart are usually shelved together as founders of British Cultural Studies, their accounts of working-class culture diverge. Williams's "whole way of life" is generous and democratic; Hoggart's portrait in *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957) is elegiac and at moments moralistic. Both refuse the Adorno-style verdict that the masses are dupes, but Williams's version travels better.

Hoggart's book is famously a book of two halves. The first describes the "lived," "self-made" working-class culture of his 1930s Leeds boyhood: its proverbs, parlour singing, co-operative women's networks, quiet moralism. The second describes the same culture under threat from the new commercial entertainments of the 1950s, "a world where things are done for the people." On the "Juke Box Boy" page Hoggart turns sharp: the milk-bar boy is "living to a large extent in a myth world … directionless and tamed helots of a machine-minding class" (Hoggart, 1957). He is careful: "Not so much a moral decline in the working class as it is a decline in the moral seriousness of the culture provided for the working class." But the structure of feeling of the second half is loss.

Williams will not have it. The Welsh valley he describes is not a museum being lost; it is a living formation that took the Industrial Revolution as a gift: "I know, from the most ordinary experience, that the interest is there, the capacity is there" (Williams, 1958: 12). Sitting in a house with "a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help," he finds "as much natural fineness of feeling" as anywhere else (Williams, 1958: 19). The empirical claim is identical to Hoggart's first half; the verdict on new mass-cultural material is much less alarmed.

The deepest difference is conceptual. Hoggart's framework is literary, "uses of literacy", and his sociological imagination is tied to the printed page being supplanted by jukeboxes and tabloids. Williams's framework is anthropological-political, "a whole way of life", and so far more elastic. It can include the steam engine, the trade-union branch and the Labour Party group his father started in the village, the "kept men" of the new politics (Williams, 1958: 11). It does not need a golden age of self-made culture to anchor it. Hoggart has folk wisdom; Williams already has structure of feeling.

The test cuts in Williams's favour even within the lecture materials. Hoggart's "Invitation to a Candy Floss World" reads the new commercial culture as "a corrupt brightness, of improper appeals and moral evasions" (Hoggart, 1957). Faced with the same milk bar, Williams would have asked what the youth in it are *making* of it: what idioms and small meanings the customers manufacture against and through the jukebox. His own report on the people back home is precisely this kind of reading: "now they read, they watch, this work we are talking about; some of them quite critically, others with a good deal of pleasure" (Williams, 1958: 19). Williams can hold the contradictions; Hoggart's literary aesthetics keeps pulling him towards loss.

The test runs on contemporary materials. *Coronation Street*, *EastEnders* and *Strictly Come Dancing* would invite the Hoggart of the second half to lament; the Williams move asks instead what the audience makes of them, and arrives at neighbourhood recognition and the negotiated pleasures of popular form. Streaming platforms and TikTok cottage-core sit on the Williams side: not loss but lived cultural production.

Williams is generous about Hoggart's lineage. He records that Leavis "knows more than any Marxist I have met about the real relations between art and experience. We have all learned from him in this" (Williams, 1958: 16). But the politics differ: Hoggart wants to defend the people; Williams wants the people to govern. His third wish is exactly that "the ordinary people should govern; that culture and education are ordinary; that there are no masses to save, to capture, or to direct" (Williams, 1958: 24).

For analysing British class culture, Williams's "whole way of life" is the better tool because it registers working-class agency without elegy: the trade unions, Co-op, friendly societies and Labour movement (Williams, 1958: 15), and the slide-17 legacy of Willis (1977), Mirza (1992), Skeggs (1997) and McKenzie (2015) inherit the framework on Williams's terms. Hoggart gave us the scholarship boy at the "friction-point of two cultures" (Hoggart, 1957); Williams gave us the bus journey on which the friction is reconciled.

### Essay 3. Williams on structure of feeling.

Williams's notion of structure of feeling is among the most useful tools in cultural sociology because it names something specific other concepts cannot reach: a shared, emergent way of sensing and understanding the world that has not yet hardened into formal ideology, institution, or political programme. The slide-deck definition is the working one: "pre-emergent cultural experiences of a class not yet articulated in formal ideology" (lecture slide 6).

Williams develops the concept across *Culture and Society* (Williams, 1958), *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974). The seed is in *Culture is Ordinary*: a culture is "common meanings, the product of a whole people, and offered individual meanings, the product of a man's whole committed personal and social experience" (Williams, 1958: 15). Such meanings "cannot in any way be prescribed; they are made by living, made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance" (Williams, 1958: 15). Structure of feeling names the in-between zone where new common meanings are being made but are not yet expressed in dominant ideology.

Three features matter. First, it is collective, not individual: not a mood, a structure. Second, it is felt before it is theorised, present in tone, vocabulary, gesture, popular culture before it appears in manifestos. Third, it is "pre-emergent": it belongs to what is coming into being rather than to what is established or surviving (lecture slide 6).

A first illustration sits inside Williams's own essay. The grandfather, a "big hard labourer," weeps "while he spoke, finely and excitedly, at the parish meeting, of being turned out of his cottage" (Williams, 1958: 11). The father, "not long before he died, spoke quietly and happily of when he had started a trade-union branch and a Labour Party group in the village, and, without bitterness, of the 'kept men' of the new politics" (Williams, 1958: 11). These are not yet "ideology"; they are felt meanings, articulated in voice and tone, that are the raw material from which an entire socialist-democratic tradition is going to be made.

A second illustration is the slide-6 image grid: Dickens's *Hard Times*, 1970s punks, miners after a shift, English flags on a suburban street. Across these four images the same operation runs: a class experience the formal politics of the day has not yet articulated. The Victorian industrial novel articulates an emergent industrial-working-class feeling before it has a labour party; the punks articulate post-imperial disrepair before any party has named it; the miners and flags articulate a deindustrial English structure of feeling whose political housing remains contested.

A third illustration is Hoggart's first half: the 1930s "lived, self-made" working-class culture is structure of feeling at the granular level, proverbs, parlour singing, neighbourhood mutual obligation, caught when dense with shared meaning and not yet under commercial pressure. Hoggart's second half then catches the next structure of feeling, the 1950s "world where things are done for the people," at the moment when it is emerging and not yet ideologically settled (Hoggart, 1957).

Contemporary uses are easy to find. The Brexit vote articulated a structure of feeling about post-imperial English place that was felt long before a political party named it. The Andrew Tate / manosphere phenomenon articulates a pre-emergent young-male structure of feeling about post-industrial labour and gender that formal politics has not yet absorbed. TikTok cottage-core articulates a structure of feeling about pastoral retreat under climate anxiety. *Adolescence* (Netflix) and *Black Mirror* are exactly the kind of contemporary cultural materials a Williams analyst would read for their pre-emergent structures of feeling. Each is felt before it is theorised, and each is collective rather than individual.

The methodological pay-off is large. Structure of feeling tells the cultural sociologist to look first at texts and practices, at how people read the *Daily Herald*, at how they speak at the parish meeting, at how a milk bar arranges its jukebox, *before* formal political articulation arrives, because that is where emergent meanings are being lived. It keeps Williams's politics in view: register what ordinary people are making of their conditions, not score them against an ideology in advance. "The making of a society is the finding of common meanings and directions, and its growth is an active debate and amendment under the pressures of experience, contact, and discovery, writing themselves into the land" (Williams, 1958: 11). Structure of feeling is the name for the moment when those meanings have begun to be written but not yet read aloud.

### Essay 4. Why the autobiographical frame matters for British Cultural Studies.

Williams's decision to begin *Culture is Ordinary* with a bus journey is not literary decoration but methodological insistence: it is the founding gesture of British Cultural Studies precisely because it refuses the detached-observer position that elite literary criticism and structuralist Marxism both presupposed. The "I" of the essay is doing sociological work.

The opening is concrete and slow. Williams describes the Mappa Mundi at Hereford cathedral, the chained library where he "cajoled a verger" for an hour, the cinema advertising the *Six-Five Special*, the bus arriving "with a driver and a conductress deeply absorbed in each other" (Williams, 1958: 10). He goes "out of the city, over the old bridge, and on through the orchards and the green meadows, and the fields red under the plough" (Williams, 1958: 10). He climbs into the Black Mountains, past Norman castles and iron workings, into the valley where his grandfather was a farm labourer turned roadman and his father a railway signalman. He himself went to the village school where "a curtain divided the two classes," then grammar school, then Cambridge.

Why does this matter for BCS? First, it locates the analyst inside the culture under analysis. Williams is not a Cambridge don describing the curious folkways of the working class from outside; he is the signalman's son returning. When he says "culture is ordinary: that is where we must start" (Williams, 1958: 11), the "we" is not the academy but includes the bus driver and the conductress. This commits cultural sociology to what would later be called positionality: the analyst is part of the social formation she analyses, and that situation is a resource, not a contaminant. Stuart Hall's later writing on race and Caribbean diaspora inherits exactly this move.

Second, it commits BCS to lived experience as a primary category. Thompson's history from below and Hoggart's "uses of literacy" share the gesture. Against Althusserian structuralism, which will arrive claiming culture is the operation of impersonal ideological apparatuses, Williams's bus journey insists that culture is *lived*: by his grandfather, who "wept while he spoke, finely and excitedly, at the parish meeting, of being turned out of his cottage" (Williams, 1958: 11); by his father, who "spoke quietly and happily of when he had started a trade-union branch and a Labour Party group in the village" (Williams, 1958: 11). The unit of analysis is the man at the parish meeting, not the apparatus.

Third, it refuses elite literary culture's geography. Williams was "not, by the way, oppressed by Cambridge" (Williams, 1958: 12); he had come "from a country with twenty centuries of history written visibly into the earth." Learning was not exotic in his family: "Learning was ordinary; we learned where we could." This refuses Leavis's geography (culture preserved in a Cambridge minority), Bloomsbury's (culture as a London teashop), and Marxist sociology's (workers as object, intellectuals as subject). It claims a different geography: the bus route between cathedral and farming valley as the proper space of British culture. The Open University would later institutionalise the same move on a national scale.

Fourth, and most importantly, it makes the writer accountable. When Williams asks of his own family, "talking, as we were, about our own lives, about people, about feelings, could I in fact find this lack of quality we are discussing? … I found as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19), he is reporting an empirical test on his own people. He could have elegised them (Hoggart's temptation) or pitied them (Leavis's). He does neither. The autobiographical frame keeps him honest.

What follows in BCS, Hoggart's scholarship boy at the friction-point, Hall's Caribbean intellectual, Skeggs's working-class women, McKenzie's estates, is the long continuation of this gesture. Contemporary writers from the Stormzy generation or Lisa McKenzie reading post-Brexit working-class places are doing the same work: writing from somewhere. The "view from nowhere" is refused. That refusal made BCS the first really popular sociology of culture in Britain, and it gives Williams's bus journey its continuing force.

### Essay 5. "There are no masses": Williams against Frankfurt School pessimism.

Williams's "there are in fact no masses, but only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18) is among the sharpest single sentences in twentieth-century sociology, and stages a complete alternative to Frankfurt School pessimism. Where Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* sees the culture industry producing an undifferentiated mass of administered subjects, Williams sees a class projection by those whose money "gave them access to the new communication techniques" (Williams, 1958: 18).

The Frankfurt position is grim: jazz, cinema, popular newspapers and radio are reading instructions for consent; the "doped mass" Williams names on the last page (Williams, 1958: 24) cannot tell because it has been made unable to tell. The political consequence is that intellectuals are the only surviving site of critique. Williams's response is that this picture mistakes a method of seeing for a fact about people.

The argument runs in three steps. First, historical: "the names of our social classes, and the word 'class' itself in this sense, date only from the Industrial Revolution" (Williams, 1958: 18). When industrialisation breaks the old face-to-face village, "it became a matter of difficult personal experience that we were constantly seeing people we did not know, and it was tempting to mass them, as 'the others', in our minds" (Williams, 1958: 18). Mass is what people become when you do not know them.

Second, sociological: new communications technologies amplified the mistake. "The improvement in communications … created unbridgeable divisions between transmitter and audience, which again led to the audience being interpreted as an unknown mass" (Williams, 1958: 18). The transmitter sees a mass because that is all the technique permits him to see; the technical position becomes a sociological theory. This is exactly Adorno's position. Williams names it as a position, not an observation.

Third, empirical: Williams tests the verdict on his own family. Spending weeks "in a house with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" (Williams, 1958: 19), could he find the "lack of quality" culture-industry analysis says must be there? He found instead "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19). The Frankfurt verdict fails the empirical test.

Williams concedes the puzzle. He admits "that people whose quality of personal living is high are apparently satisfied by a low quality of printed feeling and opinion" (Williams, 1958: 19). He does not deny that mass cultural products are often shoddy. What he denies is the inference that the shoddy product reveals the shoddy consumer. "Clearly there is something in the psychology of print and image that none of us has yet quite grasped" (Williams, 1958: 19). The puzzle is real; the Frankfurt solution is wrong.

The father's reading practice makes the point. The "satisfied reader of the *Daily Herald*" got "simply from reading the company reports a clear idea, based on names, of the rapid development of combine and interlocking ownership in British industry" (Williams, 1958: 19). A Frankfurt-style verdict would have to read this man as ideologically captured. Williams looks at the actual practice, the company reports being mined for political content, and refuses to mass this reader. The same point will be formalised by Hall (1980): the same encoded text can be read in dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, or oppositional ways, with the audience as active decoder. Hall's model is impossible without Williams's prior refusal of the masses thesis.

The argument works on contemporary materials. *Coronation Street* and *EastEnders* viewers, working-class consumers of Stormzy, Oasis or the Beatles, and TikTok users in cottage-core or manosphere subcultures are decoders, not a doped mass. Even pessimistic readings of Andrew Tate's audiences or of Brexit voters must start from Williams's refusal: these are not masses but crowded people making meanings out of their conditions. The sociologist's task is to listen, not to declare in advance what the listener will find.

The pay-off is methodological. Frankfurt pessimism produces a sociology that cannot listen. Williams's refusal of "masses" permits the listening BCS and its inheritors (Willis, 1977; Mirza, 1992; Skeggs, 1997; McKenzie, 2015) have done since. The sentence does the work of a method. The Frankfurt School wanted to save the masses. Williams says the masses do not exist; the people do; and they govern themselves better than anyone is saving them (Williams, 1958: 24).

### Essay 6. Williams on flow as the cultural form of television.

Williams's concept of flow, set out in *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974), is the single most useful concept BCS produced for analysing how broadcast media produce meaning. It is inseparable from his broader refusal of technological determinism and his demand that the political economy of communication be analysed alongside the texts.

Williams's 1974 argument targeted two dominant ways of thinking about television. The first was technological determinism: the view that media technologies themselves dictate social outcomes. Williams insisted instead that technologies are developed and used within specific social and political contexts. Television's flow is not a property of cathode-ray tubes but a programming decision made by broadcasters embedded in particular political economies, public-service BBC, commercial ITV, US network television each producing different flows. The second target was the programme-by-programme analysis inherited from film and literature. Television is experienced not as separable objects but as a flow, a continuous sequence in which the news bulletin, the trailer, the advert, and the next programme run into one another. The viewer turns on the box and is delivered into a sequence she did not choose. The unit of analysis is not the programme; it is the evening.

The conceptual pay-off is the claim about meaning. Because the viewer experiences sequence, meanings emerge cumulatively rather than in isolation (Williams, 1974). A racist joke in a sitcom is not just that programme; it is a meaning produced by the joke *plus* the news bulletin half an hour earlier *plus* the advert *plus* tomorrow night's programming. This is the cumulative form Hall would analyse in "The Whites of Their Eyes": the "grammar of race" is not a single image but a structure built across the flow, with comedy "protect[ing] / defend[ing] viewer from acknowledging racism" because the comic frame is followed and surrounded by materials that license it (Hall, 1981a). Hall's argument is a direct application of Williams's flow concept to racial ideology on television.

Three further consequences matter. First, flow makes media political-economic. *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television* (Williams, 1974) both insist that the financing of broadcasting, the £20,000,000 on the Arts Council against £365,000,000 on advertising (Williams, 1958: 22), is the determining structural fact about how flow is built. The advert is not an interruption of the flow; in commercial television, it is what the flow is for.

Second, flow refuses the transmission model. The viewer is not a passive recipient of discrete messages; she is the inhabitant of a sequence whose cumulative effect on her common meanings is the proper object of analysis. This is the same move Williams makes against the "masses" thesis: the audience is not a mass to be administered with ideology, it is a "crowded people in the course of an extraordinarily rapid and confusing expansion of their lives" (Williams, 1958: 24).

Third, flow lets the analyst read media as embedded in everyday life. Media are neither autonomous machines administering ideology nor neutral conduits of information; they are cultural forms whose flow is a programming decision with social and economic conditions. BBC popular culture today, *EastEnders*, *Doctor Who*, *Strictly Come Dancing*, the *Great British Bake Off*, is precisely such a flow, its meaning emerging cumulatively across the evening rather than from any single episode.

The limit is that Williams in 1974 was analysing scarce-channel broadcasting in which one flow was imposed on all viewers within a national schedule. Streaming platforms, *Black Mirror* and *Adolescence* on Netflix, autoplay sequences and algorithmically curated TikTok feeds, individualise the flow. Each viewer now inhabits a personalised sequence rather than a shared evening. The Williams question still bites, "what is the flow doing to the cumulative production of meaning?", but the unit has to be reconstructed: not the evening but the algorithmically programmed personal feed, whose political economy (advertising, data extraction, platform capture) is exactly what Williams said the analyst must read.

Look at the sequence, not the unit; look at who programmed the sequence and on what economic basis; refuse both technological determinism and the discrete-programme picture. That is the cultural form of media analysis BCS gave us, and it is still the right starting point in the streaming era.

### Essay 7. Using Hall's encoding/decoding model to read *Love Thy Neighbour*.

Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1980) remains the indispensable analytic instrument for British cultural sociology of media. The ITV sitcom *Love Thy Neighbour*, which Hall himself analyses in "The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media" (Hall, 1981a), is the textbook case both because Hall picks it and because its operations are transferable to other television texts.

Hall's model rejects the linear-transmission picture. Media messages are not parcels of meaning that producers pack and audiences unpack intact. Producers *encode* messages using "frameworks of knowledge," "relations of production," and "technical infrastructure" (lecture slide 13); the message circulates as "programme as 'meaningful' discourse"; and audiences *decode* using their own frameworks. Encoding and decoding are not symmetrical: there is no guarantee that the meaning encoded is the meaning decoded. Hall identifies three positions: dominant-hegemonic (the viewer takes the preferred meaning straight); negotiated (the viewer broadly accepts but inflects); and oppositional (the viewer reads within an alternative framework) (Hall, 1980). The model is underwritten by Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971): dominant readings are not neutral but ideological, secured by common sense, contestable.

*Love Thy Neighbour* is built around a white working-class couple, Eddie and Joan Booth, in Twickenham, and a Black couple, Bill and Barbie Reynolds, next door (Hall, 1981a). Hall argues that the show encodes a "grammar of race": negative racial stereotypes, slave figure, native, clown / entertainer, circulating as traces "even if not so apparent," and race itself framed as "a problem" (lecture slide 14). Frameworks of knowledge: post-imperial Britain's commonsense racial categories. Relations of production: ITV as commercial popular broadcasting. Technical infrastructure: studio-sitcom form with live laughter track.

A dominant-hegemonic reading accepts the preferred meaning as "a comedy about two ordinary couples whose racial banter is good-natured English fun." The viewer reads Eddie Booth's slurs as harmless jokes, hears the laughter track as confirmation, and treats the grammar of race as comic rather than ideological. The slave figure, native, clown / entertainer are encoded as traces through which racial stereotypes can be reproduced under cover of comic licence.

A negotiated reading accepts the show is "just a comedy" while inflecting it. A working-class viewer in Twickenham, recognising her own street, might find the Booths sympathetic and the Reynoldses funny while laughing at Eddie's stupidity rather than at the slur, or take the show as a chance to think about her own neighbours.

An oppositional reading detotalises the preferred meaning. *Love Thy Neighbour* is itself an exhibit in the racial ideology Hall is naming: the comic frame is precisely what does the ideological work, because "comedy protects / defends viewer from acknowledging racism" (Hall, 1981a). The encoding produces the grammar of race even where viewers report not noticing it. Hall calls this "contradictions and ambivalence": readings that might contradict the encoding yet still do its ideological work.

Hall's argument extends to *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978), where press-and-state encoding of "mugging" produced a racialised criminal-on-the-streets common sense that secured authoritarian state response to the 1970s organic crisis. The same machinery, encoding, dominant / negotiated / oppositional decoding, hegemony, does the work.

The model transfers cleanly to contemporary television. *Adolescence* (Netflix) encodes a framework about online radicalisation and young men; dominant readings take its moral framing as obvious truth, negotiated readings inflect by class and place, oppositional readings (from inside the manosphere, or from media-literate Andrew Tate critics) read the encoding itself. *Black Mirror* episodes invite dominant readings of technological pessimism, negotiated readings that affirm individual platforms, oppositional readings of capitalist political economy. BBC programmes such as *Doctor Who* and *EastEnders* encode British national popular meanings that streaming-era audiences variously accept, negotiate or oppose.

The pay-off is that all three readings are real audience responses, occurring simultaneously, and the sociologist's job is not to declare one correct but to map the field. The preferred encoding is *not* guaranteed to land. A teenage Black British viewer of *Love Thy Neighbour* in 1972 was not obliged to receive the encoded grammar of race; her oppositional decoding was exactly the empirical fact the model was built to handle. Encoding/decoding remains the right tool, and *Love Thy Neighbour* is the model case-study because encoding, decoding, contradiction and ambivalence are visible in a single half-hour cultural form.

### Essay 8. Hall's grammar of race and racial ideology on television.

Stuart Hall's "The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media" (Hall, 1981a) provides the foundational BCS framework for analysing race in popular television. Its strength lies in three moves: identification of a "grammar of race" in the encoded image, demonstration that ideological work proceeds through *traces* rather than overt slurs, and the insight that comedy structurally licenses the racist content of the comic text.

Hall identified a "grammar of race" in 1970s British television (lecture slide 14). Negative racial stereotypes were encoded through three figures: the slave figure, the native, and the clown / entertainer. These were often present as "traces" even when not overt; race was framed as "a problem"; and decoding produced "contradictions and ambivalence", with a comic frame that "protects / defends viewer from acknowledging racism" (Hall, 1981a). The hegemonic work could proceed precisely because comic framing licensed a "we don't really mean it" decoding. The grammar of race did not require crude vilification; it required structural positioning of Black characters in the slave / native / entertainer slots.

The textbook case is *Love Thy Neighbour*: the ITV sitcom built around Eddie and Joan Booth in Twickenham and their Black neighbours Bill and Barbie Reynolds (Hall, 1981a). Hall's reading is structural rather than content-focused. The grammar of race is not located in any single slur but in the way the Booth-Reynolds pairing positions the Reynoldses as comic objects whose blackness is the *occasion* for the joke. The encoding produces traces of the entertainer position even where individual scenes look "balanced." The decoding splits: a dominant reading takes the show as harmless; a negotiated reading laughs at Eddie's stupidity; an oppositional reading, especially from Black British viewers, names the comic frame as the ideological mechanism.

A second case is *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978), where the grammar of race operates not in comedy but in the press construction of "mugging" as a racialised street-crime moral panic. The study shows how a relatively limited phenomenon became a major political issue in 1970s Britain, with media, policing and state power interacting to produce ideological responses to wider social and economic crises. The slide-16 image grid, petrol shortage forecourt, National Front rally, Winter-of-Discontent refuse, "Britain on Half Time" headline, the policing of a Black man, NUR pickets, Powell's "stop this flow of coloured immigrants" cutting, an anti-police-violence protest, assembles the visual conditions in which the grammar of race operates across the organic crisis. Race is framed as a problem, encoded with traces of older imperial stereotypes, and read in contradiction and ambivalence by audiences who may not fully recognise what they receive.

Contemporary applications are immediate. The grammar of race still encodes Black British figures in 1970s-inherited slots, but contemporary television also encodes new versions: the Andrew Tate / manosphere figure as a racialised-and-gendered moral panic, *Adolescence* (Netflix) and *Black Mirror* episodes encoding race and class together in cumulative flow, the post-Brexit press encoding of immigration and asylum that recycles the "problem" framing Hall identified. The slave / native / entertainer grammar is residual; new grammars are emergent; the analytical engine is the same.

The conceptual sharpness of Hall's framework links three pieces. First, encoding/decoding (Hall, 1980) supplies the model of media meaning. Second, Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971) supplies the political theory: dominant groups secure consent by making meanings feel like common sense. Third, the grammar of race (Hall, 1981a) supplies the specific cultural mechanism. Together they let the sociologist analyse race in popular television without reducing it to overt content or evaporating it into discourse.

The limit, acknowledged inside BCS, is that Hall is writing about a particular British televisual moment. The framework asks to be extended, and the slide-17 sociological legacy, Mirza's *Young, Female and Black* (1992) and the rest, is where the extension was done. But the analytical engine is Hall's, and it still works: the grammar of race is encoded, decoded in contradictions and ambivalence, and comedy still protects viewers from acknowledging racism. Hall is not yet historical; he is the toolkit.

### Essay 9. Thompson on history from below and the sociology of culture.

E. P. Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* (Thompson, 1963) supplies British Cultural Studies with its most important historiographical commitment, history from below. The move matters for the contemporary sociology of culture because it refuses to let either economic structures or elite ideologies do the explanatory work that lived experience and cultural agency must do.

Thompson's preface contains the most quoted sentence in British social history: he wanted to rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the hand-loom weaver, the utopian artisan, even the deluded follower of Joanna Southcott, from "the enormous condescension of posterity." The English working class did not appear as the deposit of an economic structure; it *made itself*, in chapels, friendly societies, Corresponding Societies, food riots, radical pamphlets, hymns and trade-union banners. Class is "a relationship, not a thing", something people do, in struggle, with one another and against others.

Three concepts matter for L14. First, the lived experience of class: class is felt before it is structured. Thompson's artisans experience exploitation as a moral as well as economic outrage and respond in idioms, religious, customary, traditional, that do not look "modern" but are the work of cultural self-making. Second, history from below: the historian writes from the standpoint of those who did not leave parliamentary speeches but appear in criminal records, parish reports, magistrates' notes and seditious pamphlets. Third, the moral economy: Thompson's 1971 essay showed eighteenth-century food riots were not blind hunger eruptions but principled enforcement of customary fair-price norms against new market behaviour. The crowd had a theory; the historian has to take it seriously.

The pay-off is fourfold. First, against structuralist Marxism, Thompson supplied an account of class in which agency and culture were not afterthoughts to be "explained" by the relations of production but the medium in which class came into being. The famous Thompson-Althusser exchange in *The Poverty of Theory* (1978) ran on exactly this point.

Second, against Frankfurt School pessimism, Thompson's working class was not a "mass" administered by ideology but a self-organising cultural formation. Williams's "no masses" thesis and Thompson's "the class made itself" are the same move from different disciplinary angles.

Third, Thompson supplied a method of reading sources from below. The minute book of a Friendly Society, the chapel hymn-book, the penny pamphlet, the union banner are legitimate documents of cultural analysis. The slide-17 inheritors, Willis (1977), Mirza (1992), Skeggs (1997), McKenzie (2015), all draw on this licence.

Fourth, the moral economy concept travels into the rest of the lecture. Williams's working-class institutions, trade-union branch, Co-op, friendly society, the Labour movement, the "kept men" his father remarked on (Williams, 1958: 11, 15), are moral-economy formations: customary norms about how working people should be treated, defended in idioms that exceed pure economic calculation. Hoggart's first half is a portrait of 1930s working-class culture organised around precisely such customary norms (Hoggart, 1957).

Cases inside the lecture materials show the framework operating. The slide-16 image grid, NUR pickets, Winter-of-Discontent refuse, anti-police-violence protests, makes the moral-economy claim: customary norms of fair pay and decent policing being defended through collective action. *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) reads the criminalisation of the "mugger" as the breaking of an older moral-economic settlement between communities and the state.

Contemporary applications travel cleanly. The cost-of-living crisis, food banks, and Brexit can all be read in Thompson's idiom: as moments when older moral-economic settlements (fair wages, mutual obligation, public provision) break and provoke responses in customary as well as new idioms. Rugby league as a working-class sport, working-class musicians from the Beatles and Oasis to Stormzy, the *Great British Bake Off* as a popular ritual of mutual sociability, all carry the moral-economy traces Thompson taught us to read. Lisa McKenzie's *Getting By* (2015) is explicitly Thompson on austerity-Britain estates.

The limits are real. Thompson wrote about white English men in the early industrial revolution; gender and race are largely absent. The slide-17 corrections, Mirza on young, female and Black experience; Skeggs on class and gender; McKenzie on estates under austerity, make clear what Thompson's "below" excluded. But these are corrections from within his method, not refutations of it. History from below remains the right commitment. It tells us that the proper unit of analysis is not the apparatus, the ideology, or the structure, but the lived activity by which working people make themselves into something they were not before.

### Essay 10. "Culture is a site of struggle." Discuss with reference to two BCS thinkers.

The BCS slogan "culture is a site of struggle" is best understood by reading Williams and Hall together: Williams supplies the *ontology* (culture is the whole way of life in which struggles over meaning happen), Hall supplies the *mechanics* (encoding, decoding, hegemony, common sense, moral panic). The phrase is not a vague claim that "culture is political"; it is a specific claim about how power operates in late capitalism.

Williams refuses the two positions that would deny culture is a site of struggle. The first is the elite literary view, culture as a finished inheritance to be transmitted to those capable of receiving it. Williams's Cambridge teashop is full of "cultivated people" who "showed you they had it" (Williams, 1958: 12). If culture is already settled in the teashop, it is not struggle but curatorship. Williams refuses: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12). The second is reflectionist Marxism, which treats culture as the secondary expression of an economic base. Williams accepts that "a culture is a whole way of life, and the arts are part of a social organisation which economic change clearly radically affects" (Williams, 1958: 14) but refuses the "directive element." Culture is *both* shaped by production *and* a domain in which production relations are contested, naturalised, refused, lived.

The struggle is fought through common meanings. "A culture is common meanings, the product of a whole people" (Williams, 1958: 15). Common meanings are "made and remade." The struggle is over which meanings become common and on whose terms. Williams's own argument with the highbrow argot, with Bell's "park wall," with Leavis's dying-culture diagnosis and with the Marxist directive on prescribed forms is itself a series of struggles over what "culture" will be allowed to mean. Residual / dominant / emergent and the long revolution name a dynamic field, not a static base/superstructure schema but a culture in motion.

Hall gives Williams's ontology Gramscian mechanics. Hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), the process by which dominant groups secure consent rather than relying on force, names the form of the struggle. The dominant order wins by making its meanings feel natural, "common sense." The encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1980) gives the struggle a precise location: in the encoding moment, dominant frameworks of knowledge install a preferred reading; in the decoding moment, audiences may take it whole, negotiate it, or oppose it. The struggle happens in living rooms, in the press, in classrooms, in the encoded text and the decoding moment.

*Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) is the textbook case. Hall and colleagues show how street robbery became, through media labelling and policing intensification, the "mugging" moral panic of the mid-1970s. The struggle is over what counts as the public problem: racial criminality (dominant) or youth unemployment, deindustrialisation, and a state in crisis (oppositional). The hegemonic outcome was the former; the cost was the closing-down of alternative political readings of the organic crisis. Culture is the site at which the crisis is *resolved* into one ideological story rather than another.

Two applications make the model concrete. *Love Thy Neighbour* at sitcom scale: the encoded preferred reading installs the grammar of race in the comic frame; negotiated and oppositional decodings contest the meaning in the living room (Hall, 1981a). The hegemonic reading wins because comic licence makes racial encoding feel like common sense. Contemporary materials extend the model: Brexit was a cultural struggle over what "Britain" would mean; the cost-of-living crisis is a struggle over what counts as the public problem; *Adolescence* (Netflix) and *Black Mirror* are sitcom-scale sites in the streaming era; Andrew Tate / manosphere and TikTok cottage-core are sites of emergent ideological struggle.

The cost of treating culture as a site of struggle is that the sociology of culture becomes harder. Reflection theory was easier, find the base, derive the superstructure. Williams-Hall analysis requires close reading of texts, attention to encoding regimes and decoding practices, ethnography of audiences, and historical placement in residual / dominant / emergent fields. It also runs a political risk: if culture is the site at which power is contested, cultural intervention (a TV drama, a song, a meme) is taken seriously as politics, which can flatter the producer into thinking he is the revolution. Williams and Hall both warned against this. The risk is the price of taking culture, and ordinary people, seriously.

### Essay 11. "There is a distinct working-class way of life." The politics of British Cultural Studies.

Williams's sentence "There is a distinct working-class way of life, which I for one value" (Williams, 1958: 15) is the most precise single statement of the politics of British Cultural Studies. It does three things: it asserts a class culture exists; it values that culture without sentimentalising it; and it makes the analyst declare himself politically. All three moves are constitutive of BCS.

The first move refuses two erasures. The elite literary erasure says working people have no culture worth the name; they consume what their betters make for them. The determinist Marxist erasure says working-class culture is just bourgeois culture imposed on the workers; there is no autonomous form. Williams insists the working-class way of life exists in its own right, "with its emphases of neighbourhood, mutual obligation, and common betterment, as expressed in the great working-class political and industrial institutions" (Williams, 1958: 15). The claim points to actual social formations, trade unions, friendly societies, the Co-op, the Labour movement, the Nonconformist chapel network, working-men's adult-education institutions, which were not bourgeois inventions and which had distinct internal cultures.

The second move, valuing without sentimentalising, is harder. Hoggart slides into elegy: the 1930s as self-made culture killed by 1950s candy-floss. Thompson slides at moments into heroic identification. Williams declines both. He values the working-class way of life "not only because I was bred in it, for I now, in certain respects, live differently. I think this way of life … is in fact the best basis for any future English society" (Williams, 1958: 15). The valuation is forward-looking, not nostalgic. He is clear-eyed about what was hard: "dirty water, an earth bucket, a fourmile walk each way to work, headaches, broken women, hunger and monotony of diet" (Williams, 1958: 17). The working-class way of life is not romanticised.

The third move, the analyst declares himself, is the constitutive BCS gesture. "I for one value" makes the writer accountable. He is taking a side. The political position is socialist-democratic, committed to the proposition that "the ordinary people should govern" (Williams, 1958: 24). The socialist intellectual's job is "to attack the clamps on that energy … in industrial relations, public administration, education, for a start" (Williams, 1958: 24).

This declared politics distinguishes BCS from later, more academic Cultural Studies. By the 1980s and 1990s the field had become methodologically sophisticated but politically thinner: discourse analysis without a labour movement, semiotic readings of consumer goods without political economy. Williams's sentence is a reminder of the original commitment. The analyst is on the side of the working people whose culture she analyses.

The costs are real. The "we" of Williams's Welsh valley is gendered male, white, and Christian-secular; women, racialised workers and migrants are not adequately included. Skeggs (1997) and Mirza (1992) corrected the gender and race blindness; Hall did similar work from within. The working-class movement Williams "valued" has fragmented under deindustrialisation. McKenzie's *Getting By* (2015) documents what the working-class way of life looks like under austerity: more atomised and precarious, but still distinct and worth analysing on its own terms.

Contemporary materials still answer to Williams's sentence. Working-class musicians from the Beatles and Oasis to Stormzy, rugby league as a working-class sport, food banks under the cost-of-living crisis as sites of mutual obligation, the *Great British Bake Off* and *Strictly Come Dancing*, and *Coronation Street* and *EastEnders* as archives of neighbourhood meaning are all "distinct working-class ways of life" a Williams analyst would value. The Open University remains a working-class adult-education institution in Williams's third-wish sense. Brexit has made the political question of working-class culture more visible.

The empirical force rests on the institutions Williams names: the trade-union branch and Labour Party group his father started in the village (Williams, 1958: 11), the working men's institutions, the Friendly Societies and chapels Thompson (1963) traced as the site at which the working class "made itself," the 1930s neighbourhood culture Hoggart (1957) documents.

Williams names the risk as sentimentality. "I would not expect the working people of England to support works which, after proper and patient preparation, they could not accept" (Williams, 1958: 22). He is not flattering working-class taste; he is committing to extending cultural provision in a way the working people themselves will help shape. The politics of BCS, at its best, is exactly this commitment: distinct culture, valued, by an analyst on its side, working to extend it.

### Essay 12. What does British Cultural Studies put in place of high culture and reflection theory, and at what cost?

BCS replaces two earlier orthodoxies, elite "high culture" and the Marxist base-superstructure reflection thesis, with a unified alternative: culture as ordinary, lived, contested, hegemonic, encoded and decoded. The replacement is a real advance but has costs: a tendency to overstate cultural agency, relative neglect of political economy, and difficulty telling significant cultural struggles from trivial ones.

The displaced orthodoxies are the elite literary position (Clive Bell, the Cambridge teashop, the Leavisite reading) and the Marxist reflectionist position (Frankfurt School pessimism, crude communist cultural orthodoxy). The elite position says culture is the preserve of cultivated people who possess it as "the outward and emphatically visible sign" of cultivation (Williams, 1958: 12). The reflectionist position says culture is the secondary expression of an economic base. Williams refuses both: "We use the word culture in these two senses: to mean a whole way of life, the common meanings; to mean the arts and learning … I insist on both, and on the significance of their conjunction" (Williams, 1958: 11).

BCS puts in place a four-part alternative. First, an ontology: culture is the whole way of life. Hoggart's "uses of literacy," Thompson's "lived experience of class," Hall's "hegemony" all sit inside Williams's "whole way of life." Second, a method: history from below (Thompson, 1963), close reading of working-class materials (Hoggart, 1957), structure-of-feeling analysis (Williams, 1961; 1977), encoding/decoding analysis (Hall, 1980). Third, a politics: socialist-democratic, on the side of ordinary people. Fourth, an institutional location: the CCCS at Birmingham, founded 1964.

This achieves three things the displaced orthodoxies could not. It makes working-class and popular cultural materials sociologically legible without elegy. It accounts for media without technological determinism (Williams) or transmission-of-ideology determinism (Hall). And it permits political reading of culture without prescribing correct content. The intellectual gain is large.

The costs are real. First, *over-extension of cultural agency*. Once "everything is culture" and "audiences negotiate or oppose," it becomes tempting to read every consumption choice as resistance and every popular text as a site of struggle. Hall (1980) established that audiences decode actively; this does not entail that every act of decoding is politically meaningful. The strongest BCS work, including Hall's own *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978), was sharply disciplined about which decodings matter politically.

Second, *relative neglect of political economy*. Williams's *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television* (Williams, 1974) do attend to who owns the media (Williams's £20,000,000 vs £365,000,000 figures, Williams, 1958: 22, are the right kind of accounting). But the broader tradition was less consistent, with text analysis sometimes detached from the relations of production Williams insisted were determining. The cost-of-living crisis, food banks, streaming-platform economies, and the political economy of TikTok are the contemporary terrain on which this gap shows.

Third, *significant versus trivial struggles*. If every text encodes and is decoded, how do we tell *Policing the Crisis* mugging panic, a struggle of national-historical weight, from a comic exchange on a forgotten sitcom? Williams's residual / dominant / emergent helps; Thompson's moral economy helps; Hall's hegemony helps. But the analyst still has to judge, and BCS gives only partial guidance.

Fourth, the *category of class itself*. BCS was built on a robust account of British class culture, Williams's signalman father, Hoggart's 1930s neighbourhood, Thompson's hand-loom weavers, transformed by deindustrialisation and neoliberalism. The slide-17 inheritors (Willis, 1977; Mirza, 1992; Skeggs, 1997; McKenzie, 2015) have updated the analysis, but the framework must keep adapting: Brexit, the manosphere, *Adolescence* (Netflix) and *Black Mirror*, TikTok cottage-core as emergent forms.

A case shows both the achievement and the cost. *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) can be analysed with real power as a moral panic articulated through press encoding and decoded in multiple ways, under hegemonic conditions in which racialised-street-crime common sense was built across the organic crisis. But a fuller analysis needs the political economy of post-war Britain and the institutional analysis of state-press-police interaction the authors themselves provide. The strongest BCS work always integrated cultural and political-economic analysis.

BCS replaced two exhausted orthodoxies with a tradition that has itself become orthodox. They remain the right starting point. The cost of replacing them would be enormous, back to the teashop, back to the reflection, back to the masses. But each founder would have insisted the tradition keep moving. Culture is ordinary, and so is its analysis: made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance.
