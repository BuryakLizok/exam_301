# L14. Culture is Ordinary (Raymond Williams, British Cultural Studies)

## §1. One-line thesis

Raymond Williams's argument that culture is ordinary refounds the study of culture as the analysis of a whole way of life, common meanings made and remade by working people themselves, and, together with Hoggart's *Uses of Literacy*, Thompson on history from below, and Hall's encoding/decoding model, launches British Cultural Studies as a tradition that rejects both elite "high culture" and the Marxist base-superstructure reflection thesis, treating culture instead as a political site of struggle where hegemony is contested in the lived experience of class.

## §2. Why this lecture matters in SC301

L14 is the spine of the Spring term's "culture, class, media" arc and it sits at the joint between two earlier moves on the SC301 syllabus: the critique of mass-society pessimism (Adorno, Frankfurt School) and the Marxist account of ideology (base/superstructure). British Cultural Studies (BCS) is the moment when sociology stops asking "what does high culture mean?" and starts asking "what do working-class lives mean to those who live them?", and decides that the answer is not "they are dupes of capitalism," but that ordinary people make culture and remake themselves through it. This matters for SC301 because the lecture supplies a vocabulary, structure of feeling, the long revolution, flow (in TV), residual/dominant/emergent, encoding/decoding, hegemony, common sense, history from below, the moral economy, the working-class autodidact, that you will need across the media, class, race, and modernity essays. It also sets up L17 (Stuart Hall on race and representation), the gender lectures (Skeggs, McRobbie), and any question about whether modernity homogenises culture. Williams's biographical claim, "I was born and grew up halfway along that bus journey" (Williams, 1958: 10), is doing methodological work: he insists that the analyst belongs to the culture under analysis, that the sociologist of culture is not Clive Bell in his Cambridge teashop but the railway signalman's son going back home. The lecture is, finally, a teachable counter to cultural pessimism: it refuses the people-without-history thesis and insists that "there are no masses; there are only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18) [VERIFY p.?].

## §4. Williams's argument in *Culture is Ordinary*, step by step

1. **Biography as method.** Williams opens with a bus journey from Hereford cathedral up the Black Mountains to a Welsh farming valley. His grandfather a farm labourer turned roadman; his father a railway signalman; he himself the grammar-school boy who goes to Cambridge. "Culture is ordinary: that is where we must start" (Williams, 1958: 11).
2. **Two senses of culture, both insisted on.** "We use the word culture in these two senses: to mean a whole way of life, the common meanings; to mean the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort. Some writers reserve the word for one or other of these senses; I insist on both, and on the significance of their conjunction" (Williams, 1958: 11).
3. **Against the teashop.** At Cambridge Williams encounters "culture" as "the outward and emphatically visible sign of a special kind of people, cultivated people" (Williams, 1958: 12). He refuses this: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12).
4. **Against Clive Bell.** Bell's *Civilisation* divides "ordinary people and ordinary work" from culture as with "a park wall" (Williams, 1958: 12). Williams: at home "we met and made music, listened to it, recited and listened to poems, valued fine language" (Williams, 1958: 12).
5. **Against the new "argot" of the culture-vultures.** A new petty class of "do-gooders and highbrows and superior prigs" has "invented and disseminated the argot, in an attempt to influence ordinary people" (Williams, 1958: 13).
6. **Two serious influences he keeps in dialogue with.** "When I got to Cambridge I encountered two serious influences which have left a very deep impression on my mind. The first was Marxism, the second the teaching of Leavis" (Williams, 1958: 14).
7. **What he takes from Marxism, what he refuses.** He accepts that "a culture is a whole way of life, and the arts are part of a social organisation which economic change clearly radically affects" (Williams, 1958: 14). He refuses the "directive element", the idea "that if you honestly want socialism you must write, think, learn in certain prescribed ways" (Williams, 1958: 15).
8. **What he takes from Leavis, what he refuses.** He accepts that an "old, mainly agricultural England" has been replaced by "a modern, organised, industrial state" (Williams, 1958: 16). He refuses Leavis's verdict that the new commercial culture is total and that the masses are lost. Of mass-trinket nostalgia he says: "not in a million years would you make us give up this power", plumbing, tractors, medicines, contraceptives (Williams, 1958: 17).
9. **Four legacies to be cleared away.** Williams identifies "a legacy of two false equations, one false analogy, and one false proposition" (Williams, 1958: 17): (i) ugliness as price of power, false; (ii) popular education breeds commercial culture, false; (iii) bad popular culture proves the inner badness of its consumers, false; (iv) Gresham's Law in culture, bad driving out good, false.
10. **The "masses" formula.** "There are in fact no masses; there are only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18) [VERIFY p.?]. Mass is a class projection.
11. **Three wishes.** Williams ends with three: that education be recognised as ordinary; that public provision for arts and adult learning be increased ("we now spend £20,000,000 annually on … the Arts Council … At the same time we spend £365,000,000 annually on advertising", Williams, 1958: 22); and that mass culture's keystone, advertising revenue, be addressed.
12. **The closing image.** The bus journey, again: "where the language changes but the voice is the same" (Williams, 1958: 24).

## §5. The wider BCS field (Hoggart, Thompson, Hall) and the contemporary relevance

**Hoggart.** *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957) is a book of two halves: the 1930s "lived," "self-made" working-class culture of his Leeds childhood, and the 1950s "world where things are done for the people." Hoggart's scholarship boy is "at the friction-point of two cultures," "sad and also solitary." The "Juke Box Boy" passage condemns the commercial cafe culture as "a peculiarly thin and pallid form of dissipation, a sort of spiritual dry-rot amid the odour of boiled milk." Hoggart's loss is more cautious than Adorno's; he insists the moral failure lies in "the culture provided for the working class," not in working-class people themselves.

**Thompson.** *The Making of the English Working Class* (Thompson, 1963): class made itself, in struggle, in chapels and pubs and Corresponding Societies and food riots; it was not the deposit of an economic structure. Thompson supplies the slogan "history from below" and the concept of the moral economy. His insistence on lived experience and human agency is the BCS counter to structuralist Marxism.

**Hall.** Drawing on Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), Hall's 1973 essay "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Hall, 1980) demolishes the transmission model of media: producers encode, audiences decode, with dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, and oppositional positions available. "The Whites of Their Eyes" (Hall, 1981a) reads the grammar of race in 1970s sitcoms like *Love Thy Neighbour*: the comedy "protects/defends viewer from acknowledging racism." *Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) turns the moral panic over "mugging" into a case study of how state, media and policing manufacture ideological responses to the 1970s organic crisis.

**Contemporary anchors for revision**, keep these ready as worked examples: post-war working men's clubs vs Spotify and bedroom producers; *Coronation Street* as Williams "ordinary" culture; *The Great British Bake Off* as managed common-meaning television; YouTubers as new working-class cultural producers; TikTok cottage-core as residual rural longing; Brexit voting as the cultural expression of structure of feeling, not just economic interest; *Adolescence* (Netflix 2025) and the online manosphere as a structure of feeling now articulated in mass moral panic; Andrew Tate and the manosphere as residual masculinity reasserting itself in emergent platforms; rugby league (northern, working-class) vs rugby union (southern, middle-class) as Williams's "whole way of life"; The Beatles as Liverpool working-class culture going global; Stormzy at Glastonbury 2019 as a Hall-style negotiated reading of British national festival space; reading groups in working-class communities and the Open University as the surviving institutions of Williams's third wish; food banks as moral-economy practices revived under austerity; the cost-of-living crisis as a contemporary structure of feeling not yet stabilised in formal political ideology.

## §8. Full essays

### Essay 1. "Culture is ordinary." Critically assess Raymond Williams's claim.

I want to argue that Williams's claim that culture is ordinary is the founding move of British Cultural Studies because it does two things at once: it democratises the *object* of cultural analysis, and it democratises the *position* of the analyst. The slogan is not a celebration of pop culture and it is not an attack on Shakespeare. It is a demand that we hold both senses of culture together, "a whole way of life, the common meanings" and "the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort" (Williams, 1958: 11), and refuse the class division that puts them in different rooms.

Williams's argument is staged biographically. He opens the essay on a bus from Hereford cathedral up into the Black Mountains, past Norman castles and the line of early iron workings, into a farming valley where his grandfather worked as a farm labourer until he was "turned out of his cottage and, in his fifties, became a roadman" (Williams, 1958: 11). His father became a railway signalman; he himself went to the village school, the grammar school, and Cambridge. The opening matters because it puts the sociologist of culture inside the culture under analysis. When he arrives in the Cambridge teashop, he finds "culture, not in any sense I knew, but in a special sense: the outward and emphatically visible sign of a special kind of people, cultivated people" (Williams, 1958: 12). His verdict is famous: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12). He has been on the other end of the bus route.

The argument then proceeds by clearing two rival positions. The first is the cultural pessimism Williams calls "Leavis": the diagnosis that "an old, mainly agricultural England" has been replaced by "a modern, organised, industrial state" whose institutions "deliberately cheapen our natural human responses" (Williams, 1958: 16). Williams accepts the diagnosis as a "personal crisis" but rejects its conclusion. The working people he knows are not the cultural casualties of industrial mechanisation; the Industrial Revolution brought "the gift of power that is everything to men who have worked with their hands" (Williams, 1958: 16), plumbing, electricity, contraceptives, canned food. "Not in a million years would you make us give up this power" (Williams, 1958: 17). The second target is Marxist economism: the view that culture is a reflection of the economic base, with prescribed correct forms. Williams agrees that culture and production are related, but refuses the "directive element, this insistence that if you honestly want socialism you must write, think, learn in certain prescribed ways" (Williams, 1958: 15).

What is left, once both rivals are cleared, is the positive thesis: culture is made by living, "by living, made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance" (Williams, 1958: 15). This makes culture political without being prescribed. It also makes the "masses" a category mistake. Williams's most quoted line, "there are in fact no masses; there are only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18) [VERIFY p.?], is a sociological knife. Mass is a name imposed by those whose money "gave them access to the new communication techniques" (Williams, 1958: 18) on the people they did not know.

I would test the thesis against a contemporary case. Think about rugby league in the north of England and rugby union in the south. To call rugby league working-class culture is not to refer to its content (oval ball, similar rules) but to a whole way of life: Friday-night pints, mining-town clubs, the BBC commentary that names former pit villages, the family ritual of the Challenge Cup. The Beatles, similarly, were Liverpool working-class culture going global, and a Williams analysis would refuse the choice between celebrating them as art and dismissing them as commerce. Stormzy at Glastonbury 2019 is Williams's "whole way of life" updated for grime and Black British class culture, and his refusal to perform for free for the BBC was a small reassertion of the "long revolution" in who controls cultural production. The thesis still works.

The critical limits are real. Williams writes before the cultural turn that took race and gender seriously; the "we" of his Welsh valley is a particular kind of common. He also assumes a stable national frame ("England", "Englishness") that does not survive multicultural Britain or globalisation intact. But the underlying move, refuse the teashop, refuse the dying-culture verdict, refuse to mass the people you do not know, is still, I think, the best opening sentence British sociology of culture ever wrote. Culture is ordinary; that is where we must still start.

### Essay 2. Compare Williams's "whole way of life" with Hoggart on working-class culture.

I want to argue that although Williams and Hoggart are usually shelved together as the founders of British Cultural Studies, their accounts of working-class culture diverge in tone and politics in ways that matter for revision. Williams's "whole way of life" is generous, expansive, and democratic; Hoggart's portrait in *The Uses of Literacy* (Hoggart, 1957) is elegiac and at moments moralistic. Both refuse the Adorno-style verdict that the masses are dupes, but they refuse it differently, and Williams's version travels better into the twenty-first century.

Hoggart's book is famously a book of two halves. The first half describes the "lived," "self-made" working-class culture of his Leeds boyhood in the 1930s: its proverbs, its parlour singing, its co-operative women's networks, its quiet moralism. The second half describes the same culture under threat from the new commercial entertainments of the 1950s, "a world where things are done for the people." On the "Juke Box Boy" page Hoggart turns sharp: the milk-bar boy is "living to a large extent in a myth world … perhaps most of them are rather less intelligent than the average … directionless and tamed helots of a machine-minding class." Hoggart wants to defend working-class people from a commercial culture that he thinks is corroding them. He is careful: "Not so much a moral decline in the working class as it is a decline in the moral seriousness of the culture provided for the working class." But the structure of feeling of the second half is loss.

Williams, in *Culture is Ordinary*, will not have it. The Welsh valley he describes is not a museum being lost. It is a living formation that took the Industrial Revolution as a gift: "I know, from the most ordinary experience, that the interest is there, the capacity is there" (Williams, 1958: 12). When he sits in a house "with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" and listens to them read, watch, talk, he reports finding "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience" as he found anywhere else (Williams, 1958: 19). The empirical claim is identical to Hoggart's first half. The verdict on the new mass-cultural material is much less alarmed.

The deepest difference is conceptual. Hoggart's framework is literary, "uses of literacy", and his sociological imagination is tied to the cultural form of the printed page being supplanted by jukeboxes and tabloids. Williams's framework is anthropological-political, "a whole way of life", and so it is far more elastic. It can include the steam engine and the trade-union branch his father started, "without bitterness, of the 'kept men' of the new politics" (Williams, 1958: 11). It can include the Labour Party group in the village. It does not need a golden age of self-made culture to anchor it. Hoggart has Stuart Hall's "common sense" but only as folk wisdom; Williams already, here, has structure of feeling.

The contemporary test cuts in Williams's favour. Imagine reading Hoggart's "Juke Box Boy" alongside an interview with a young grime fan in Croydon, or a TikTok user posting cottage-core fantasies of an England she has never lived in. Hoggart's instinct would be to read these as symptoms of a "candy floss world," "anti-life," "corrupt brightness." Williams's instinct is to ask what the structure of feeling is that the cottage-core video articulates, what longing for rootedness in a post-2008 precarious labour market is being lived but not yet politically named, and how the practice itself is making meaning in a way that may be both compromised and creative. Williams can hold the contradictions; Hoggart's literary aesthetics keeps pulling him towards loss.

Williams himself is generous about this. He records that Leavis (and behind Leavis, the sensibility Hoggart shares) "knows more than any Marxist I have met about the real relations between art and experience. We have all learned from him in this" (Williams, 1958: 16). But the politics differ: Hoggart wants to defend the people; Williams wants the people to govern. His third wish in the essay is exactly that "the ordinary people should govern; that culture and education are ordinary; that there are no masses to save, to capture, or to direct" (Williams, 1958: 24).

For analysing British culture now, food banks, working-class TikTok producers, the Open University, *Coronation Street*'s decline, Stormzy's headlining, Williams's "whole way of life" is the better tool because it can register working-class cultural agency without elegy. Hoggart gave us the scholarship boy at the "friction-point of two cultures"; Williams gave us the bus journey on which the friction is reconciled.

### Essay 3. Williams on structure of feeling, applied to a twenty-first-century moment.

I want to argue that Williams's notion of structure of feeling is one of the most useful, and most underused, tools in contemporary cultural sociology, and that the cost-of-living crisis in 2020s Britain is a textbook case of its operation. The concept names something specific that other concepts cannot quite reach: a shared, emergent way of sensing and understanding the world that has not yet hardened into formal ideology, institutions, or political programme.

Williams develops structure of feeling across *Culture and Society* (Williams, 1958), *The Long Revolution* (Williams, 1961), and *Marxism and Literature* (Williams, 1977), but the seed is here in *Culture is Ordinary*: culture is "common meanings, the product of a whole people, and offered individual meanings, the product of a man's whole committed personal and social experience" (Williams, 1958: 15). Such meanings, he insists, "cannot be prescribed; they are made by living, made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance" (Williams, 1958: 15). Structure of feeling is the name he later gives to the in-between zone where new common meanings are being made but are not yet expressed in dominant ideology.

The slide-deck definition is sharp: "pre-emergent cultural experiences of a class not yet articulated in formal ideology." Three features matter. First, it is collective, not individual. It is not a mood; it is a structure. Second, it is felt before it is theorised, present in tone, vocabulary, gesture, popular culture, before it appears in manifestos. Third, it is emergent in Williams's later residual/dominant/emergent triad: it belongs to what is coming into being, not what is established (dominant) or surviving (residual).

The cost-of-living crisis in Britain since 2022 is, I want to argue, a contemporary structure of feeling. The objective facts, energy bills, food inflation, mortgage rates, are not yet a politics. Neither main party has a programme that names the experience as such; the trade-union revival, the rise of mutual-aid networks, the proliferation of "warm banks" in libraries and churches, the food banks (Williams would have called them moral-economy practices), these are felt responses, not yet a coherent ideology. The "feeling" includes a particular tone: exhaustion, low-grade dread, a sense that decent ordinary life is being foreclosed by people elsewhere. It produces cultural objects, TikTok videos of weekly food shops; *Bake Off* contestants joking about microwaves; *Adolescence* (Netflix 2025) reading male loneliness back into the family, that articulate the feeling without yet politicising it. This is structure of feeling exactly: lived, shared, sensed, but not yet "articulated in formal ideology."

A second example: the manosphere and the Andrew Tate phenomenon. Here a residual masculinity (Williams's residual category, a form left over from a previous dominant order) is being reactivated through new media, YouTube, TikTok, Telegram, to produce a structure of feeling among young men that is not yet a political party but is already shaping behaviour, language, and relationships. *Adolescence* dramatises the gap between the structure of feeling (the boy's online world) and the institutions (school, family, police) that cannot yet articulate it. The success of the series, water-cooler shows are not what they used to be, depends on the audience already half-recognising the feeling. That is what Williams means by "shared, emergent ways of sensing."

A third example would be Brexit. The 2016 vote was an articulation, in the formal politics of a referendum, of a structure of feeling that had been building for at least a decade in deindustrial English towns: a feeling of being left behind, of cultural loss, of "we used to make things here," of unease about freedom of movement that was not always racism but was sometimes class resentment in a national-cultural register. The structure of feeling pre-existed UKIP and the Vote Leave campaign; the campaign articulated it. After 2016 the feeling did not disappear; it has migrated into "small boats" rhetoric and culture-war flashpoints. Williams's concept can hold this without reducing it either to false consciousness (a leftist temptation) or to the people's authentic voice (a populist temptation).

The methodological pay-off is large. Structure of feeling tells the cultural sociologist to look first at the texts and practices, soaps, music, jokes, fashion, the comments under TikTok videos, *before* the formal political articulation arrives, because that is where you will see the emergent meanings being lived. It also keeps Williams's politics in view: he wants the analyst to register what ordinary people are making of their conditions, not to score them against an ideology in advance. "The making of a society is the finding of common meanings and directions, and its growth is an active debate and amendment under the pressures of experience, contact, and discovery, writing themselves into the land" (Williams, 1958: 11). Structure of feeling is the name for the moment when those meanings have begun to be written but have not yet been read aloud.

### Essay 4. Why the autobiographical frame matters for British Cultural Studies.

I want to argue that Williams's decision to begin *Culture is Ordinary* with a bus journey is not literary decoration but methodological insistence: it is the founding gesture of British Cultural Studies precisely because it refuses the position of detached observer that elite literary criticism and structuralist Marxism both presupposed. The "I" of the essay is doing sociological work.

The opening is concrete and slow. Williams describes the Mappa Mundi at Hereford cathedral, the chained library where he had to "cajole a verger" for an hour, the cinema across the street advertising the *Six-Five Special*, the bus arriving "with a driver and a conductress deeply absorbed in each other" (Williams, 1958: 10). He goes "out of the city, over the old bridge, and on through the orchards and the green meadows, and the fields red under the plough" (Williams, 1958: 10). He climbs into the Black Mountains; the rock changes under the bus; he passes Norman castles, the iron workings, the farming valleys, and arrives in the valley where he grew up. The grandfather is a farm labourer turned roadman. The father is a railway signalman. He himself went to the village school where "a curtain divided the two classes," then to the local grammar school, then to Cambridge.

Why does this matter for BCS? First, it locates the analyst inside the culture under analysis. Williams is not a Cambridge don describing the curious folkways of the working class from outside; he is the signalman's son returning. When he says "culture is ordinary: that is where we must start" (Williams, 1958: 11), the "we" is not the academy but a "we" that includes the bus driver and the conductress. This is methodologically loaded. It commits cultural sociology to what would later be called positionality: the analyst is part of the social formation she analyses, and that situation is a resource, not a contaminant. Stuart Hall's later writing on race and Caribbean diaspora will inherit exactly this move.

Second, it commits BCS to lived experience as a primary category. Thompson's history from below and Hoggart's "uses of literacy" share the gesture. Against Althusserian structuralism, which will arrive in the 1960s and claim that culture is the operation of impersonal ideological apparatuses, Williams's bus journey insists that culture is *lived*: by his grandfather, who "wept while he spoke, finely and excitedly, at the parish meeting, of being turned out of his cottage" (Williams, 1958: 11); by his father, who, "not long before he died, spoke quietly and happily of when he had started a trade-union branch and a Labour Party group in the village" (Williams, 1958: 11). The unit of analysis is the man at the parish meeting, not the apparatus.

Third, it refuses elite literary culture's geography. Williams notes that he was "not, by the way, oppressed by Cambridge" (Williams, 1958: 12); he had come "from a country with twenty centuries of history written visibly into the earth." Cambridge's "bookcases I now sit to work at in Oxford are of the same design as those in the chained library." Learning was not exotic in his family: "Learning was ordinary; we learned where we could." This refuses Leavis's geography (true culture preserved in a Cambridge minority) and refuses Bloomsbury's (culture as a London teashop) and refuses Marxist sociology's (workers as the object, intellectuals as the subject). It claims a different geography: the bus route between cathedral and farming valley as the proper space of British culture.

Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, it makes the writer accountable. When Williams confesses that he had to "look, my training has done that for me" at his own family, "talking, as we were, about our own lives, about people, about feelings, could I in fact find this lack of quality we are discussing? … I can only say that I found as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19), he is reporting an empirical test conducted on his own people. He could have lied. He could have either elegised them (Hoggart's temptation) or pitied them (Leavis's). He does neither. The autobiographical frame is what keeps him honest.

What follows in British Cultural Studies, Hoggart's scholarship boy, Hall's Caribbean intellectual, Beverley Skeggs's working-class women, Lisa McKenzie's Nottingham estates, is the long continuation of this gesture. The sociologist of culture is permitted, and indeed required, to write from somewhere. The "view from nowhere" is refused. That refusal is what made BCS the first really popular sociology of culture in Britain, and it is what gives Williams's bus journey, sixty-eight years on, its continuing force.

### Essay 5. "There are no masses": Williams against Frankfurt School pessimism.

I want to argue that Williams's "there are in fact no masses; there are only ways of seeing people as masses" (Williams, 1958: 18) [VERIFY p.?] is one of the sharpest single sentences in twentieth-century sociology, and that it stages a complete alternative to the cultural pessimism of the Frankfurt School. Where Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* sees the culture industry producing an undifferentiated mass of administered subjects, Williams sees a class projection by those whose money "gave them access to the new communication techniques" (Williams, 1958: 18).

The Frankfurt position, as the SC301 module has staged it earlier in the year, is grim: jazz, cinema, popular newspapers and radio are reading instructions for consent; the masses produced by the culture industry are the "doped mass" Williams himself names on the last page (Williams, 1958: 24). Adorno's verdict is that the masses cannot tell because they have been made unable to tell. The political consequence is that intellectuals are the only surviving site of critique. Williams's response is, exactly, that this picture mistakes a method of seeing for a fact about people.

His argument runs in three steps. First, historical: the word "mass" enters English only with the Industrial Revolution. "The names of our social classes, and the word 'class' itself in this sense, date only from the Industrial Revolution" (Williams, 1958: 18). When industrialisation breaks the old face-to-face village social organisation, "it became a matter of difficult personal experience that we were constantly seeing people we did not know, and it was tempting to mass them, as 'the others', in our minds" (Williams, 1958: 18). Mass, in other words, is what people become when you do not know them.

Second, sociological: the new communications technologies amplified this mistake. "The improvement in communications, in particular the development of new forms of multiple transmission of news and entertainment, created unbridgeable divisions between transmitter and audience, which again led to the audience being interpreted as an unknown mass" (Williams, 1958: 18). The transmitter sees a mass because that is the only thing the transmission technique allows him to see; the technical position becomes a sociological theory. This is exactly Adorno's position. Williams names it as a position, not as an observation.

Third, empirical: Williams tests the verdict on his own family. He spent a few weeks "in a house with a commercial traveller, a lorry driver, a bricklayer, a shopgirl, a fitter, a signalman, a nylon operative, a domestic help" (Williams, 1958: 19). Could he find, in conversation with them, the "lack of quality" the culture-industry analysis says must be there? He found instead "as much natural fineness of feeling, as much quick discrimination, as much clear grasp of ideas within the range of experience as I have found anywhere" (Williams, 1958: 19). The Frankfurt verdict fails the empirical test.

Williams does concede the problem Adorno was naming. He admits the surprising fact "that people whose quality of personal living is high are apparently satisfied by a low quality of printed feeling and opinion" (Williams, 1958: 19). He does not deny that mass cultural products are often shoddy. What he denies is the inference that the shoddy product reveals the shoddy consumer. "Clearly there is something in the psychology of print and image that none of us has yet quite grasped" (Williams, 1958: 19). The puzzle is real; the Frankfurt solution to the puzzle is wrong.

A contemporary test makes the point. Take the British tabloid press of the 2010s, *The Sun*, the *Daily Mail*, read on the bus by people we might call working-class. A Frankfurt-style verdict reads the readers as ideologically captured by the property pages and the moral panics about immigration. A Williams-style verdict, by contrast, looks at the actual practice of reading, the laughs, the skipping, the talk in the staff canteen, the way the back-page sports section is taken seriously while the editorial is half-mocked, and refuses to mass these readers as "the doped mass." This is exactly what Stuart Hall will formalise as the encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1980): the same encoded tabloid text can be read in dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, or oppositional ways, and most actual readers float between them. Hall's model is impossible without Williams's prior refusal of the masses thesis.

The pay-off for SC301 is methodological. Frankfurt pessimism produces a sociology that cannot listen, because it has already decided what the listener is going to find. Williams's refusal of "masses" is what permits the listening that British Cultural Studies, ethnography of media reception (Morley, Ang, Radway), and the contemporary sociology of class (Skeggs, McKenzie) have done since. The sentence does the work of a method.

The closing of *Culture is Ordinary* makes the political stakes explicit. "The biggest difficulty is in accepting, deep in our minds, the values on which they depend: that the ordinary people should govern; that culture and education are ordinary; that there are no masses to save, to capture, or to direct, but rather this crowded people in the course of an extraordinarily rapid and confusing expansion of their lives" (Williams, 1958: 24). The Frankfurt School wanted to save the masses. Williams says the masses do not exist; the people do; and they govern themselves better than anyone is saving them.

### Essay 6. Williams on flow: does the concept still illuminate streaming and TikTok?

I want to argue that Williams's concept of flow (in TV), developed in *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974), is still the single most useful concept for analysing how contemporary screen media produce meaning, and that it survives the move from broadcast television to streaming and TikTok with surprisingly little damage, but it survives only if we update what "the flow" is a flow *of*.

Williams's argument in 1974 was directed against two then-dominant ways of thinking about television: as a transparent technology (technological determinism) and as a sequence of discrete programmes (the programme-by-programme analysis inherited from film and literature). He insisted, against both, that television is experienced not as a series of separable objects but as a flow, a continuous sequence in which the news bulletin, the trailer for next week's drama, the advert for a washing machine, the moment of music, and the next programme run into one another and produce meaning *cumulatively*. The viewer turns on the box and is delivered into a sequence she did not choose. The unit of cultural analysis, Williams said, is not the programme; it is the evening. As the teacher-summary puts it: "broadcasting operates as a continuous sequence rather than a series of discrete programmes, shaping how audiences experience media in everyday life."

Two further moves from the same book matter. First, technologies "are developed and used within specific social and political contexts"; they do not "dictate social outcomes." Television's flow is not a technical property of cathode-ray tubes but a programming decision made by broadcasters embedded in particular political economies, public-service BBC, commercial ITV, US network television each producing different flows. Second, because the viewer experiences sequence, "meanings emerge cumulatively rather than in isolation." A racist joke in a sitcom is not just that programme; it is a meaning produced by the joke *plus* the news bulletin half an hour earlier *plus* the advert *plus* tomorrow night's programming. The cumulative effect is the cultural form.

Does this still illuminate streaming and TikTok? In one important respect, broadcast flow has been eroded. Netflix, Disney+, BBC iPlayer offer a *library* model: the viewer searches, picks, watches, returns. The continuous unchosen sequence Williams analysed has weakened. The "evening" as a unit is no longer the unit; the binge is.

But, and this is the analytical pay-off, the binge is itself a *new kind* of flow, and the autoplay function is the engineering that produces it. Netflix's "playing next episode in 5… 4… 3…" countdown is flow re-engineered: the technical default delivers the viewer into the next episode unless she acts to stop. The cumulative production of meaning Williams analysed is preserved, and intensified. Bingeing *Adolescence* (Netflix 2025) in one night, four episodes, each shot in continuous take, is closer to Williams's flow than to old-style appointment television: the cumulative dread, the rhythm, the inability to step out, are produced by the sequence not the episode. Streaming has not killed flow; it has personalised it.

TikTok is even more clearly a Williams-style flow. The For You Page is precisely a continuous sequence the user did not select; the algorithm decides; the cumulative effect is a structure of feeling assembled from fifteen-second segments. A teenager's afternoon scroll might run: dance, food, dance, manosphere clip, dance, advert, cottage-core, dance, news clip, dance. No single video is the unit of analysis. The political economy is, as Williams said it would be, decisive: the algorithm is not a neutral technology, it is a commercial sorting designed to maximise time-on-platform, embedded in a Chinese-owned company in a UK regulatory environment that has not yet decided what it thinks. Williams's insistence that "technologies are developed and used within specific social and political contexts" reads now like a description of contemporary platform debates.

A second update is needed. Williams was analysing scarce-channel broadcasting in which one flow was imposed on all viewers. Streaming and TikTok deliver *individualised* flows: my For You Page is not yours. This makes the cultural-sociological problem harder, there is no common flow to point to, but it does not make Williams's concept wrong. It makes the unit of analysis the algorithmic regime rather than the broadcast schedule. The question "what is the flow doing to the cumulative production of meaning?" still has to be asked, and Williams's answer, look at the sequence, not the unit, still tells you where to look.

There is a residual limit. Williams's flow assumes a relatively passive viewer; TikTok users *produce* content as well as consume it. The producer-consumer distinction Williams worked with has weakened, and the encoding/decoding model that Stuart Hall built on it (where producers encode and audiences decode) needs supplementing. But this is an extension of Williams, not a refutation. He himself, in *Communications* (Williams, 1962), argued for more democratic ownership of cultural production. TikTok would have horrified him as an advertising machine and delighted him as a working-class production tool, both true.

I would conclude: flow is still the right concept, because it focuses cultural sociology on sequence, cumulative meaning, and political economy of broadcasting decisions. It needs updating on the unit of analysis (binge, scroll, For You Page) and on producer-consumer collapse. But the move that mattered, refusing the discrete-programme model, refusing technological determinism, looking at what the technology is being used to do, is still the move you need.

### Essay 7. Using Hall's encoding/decoding model to read *Adolescence* (Netflix 2025).

I want to argue that Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model, set out in his 1973 essay "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse" (Hall, 1980), remains the indispensable analytic instrument for British cultural sociology of media, and that *Adolescence* (Netflix, 2025) is a particularly rich case because it is both encoded with a clear preferred reading and structured to invite negotiated and oppositional readings simultaneously.

Hall's model rejects the linear-transmission picture of communication. Media messages are not parcels of meaning that producers pack and audiences unpack intact. Instead, producers *encode* messages using frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, and technical infrastructure; the message circulates as "programme as 'meaningful' discourse"; and audiences *decode* using their own frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, and technical infrastructure. Encoding and decoding are not symmetrical: there is no guarantee that the meaning encoded is the meaning decoded. Hall then identifies three positions a viewer can take: the dominant-hegemonic reading, in which the viewer "takes the connoted meaning from, say, a television newscast or current affairs programme full and straight"; the negotiated reading, in which the viewer broadly accepts the dominant frame but inflects it with her own situation; and the oppositional reading, in which the viewer "detotalizes" the message and reads it within an alternative framework. The model is underwritten by Gramsci's hegemony (Gramsci, 1971): dominant readings are not neutral but ideological, secured by common sense, contestable.

*Adolescence* is the four-episode Netflix drama in which a thirteen-year-old white English boy is arrested for the murder of a female classmate, and each of the four episodes is shot in a single unbroken take. The encoded preferred reading is signposted explicitly in public statements by the writer Jack Thorne and director Philip Barantini and in the post-broadcast UK Parliament discussion of the series: the show is a cultural intervention into the online manosphere and the radicalisation of teenage boys by misogynist influencers. The encoding draws on what Hall (1981a) called "the grammar of race" but applied here to gender, a grammar of masculinity, online toxicity, parental absence, and digital ignorance. Frameworks of knowledge: a post-#MeToo discourse on male violence. Relations of production: Netflix as a global streaming platform with a UK production arm and a brief to produce "prestige" socially-engaged drama. Technical infrastructure: the single-take aesthetic, which produces a feeling of unbroken complicity.

A dominant-hegemonic reading of the series accepts this preferred meaning. Watching from this position, the viewer reads Jamie as a victim of unsupervised internet access; his parents as well-meaning but uncomprehending; the murdered girl as a casualty of online radicalised masculinity; and the policy implication as a need for stronger digital safeguarding, school intervention, and parental responsibility. The Westminster political response, Keir Starmer reportedly hosted a screening, is exactly the dominant-hegemonic reception. The hegemonic frame is anti-misogyny, pro-safeguarding, broadly liberal.

A negotiated reading accepts much of this but inflects it. A working-class English viewer in a former industrial town might agree that the boy was radicalised online but read the show as also being *about* the abandonment of working-class boys by an education system, a labour market, and a community that no longer offers them anything else. She might add Williams's structure of feeling to the script: the boy's online world is filling a vacuum that has its own social-historical name. She might note, as a Hoggart reader would, that the show's middle-class production team does not quite *get* working-class fatherhood, the father is sympathetic but slightly caricatured. The negotiated reading agrees that the boy is responsible and that the manosphere is dangerous, but resists the implication that the cause is purely cultural-individual.

An oppositional reading detotalises the message. From an oppositional position, *Adolescence* is itself part of a moral panic, a Hall-and-colleagues *Policing the Crisis* moral panic, in fact, about young men, the internet, and order (Hall et al., 1978). The single-take aesthetic produces a sense of urgency that exceeds the empirical incidence of teenage-male-on-female murder; the script's emphasis on the manosphere encodes a cause that flatters middle-class anxieties about digital culture while distracting from material drivers of male violence (alcohol, household poverty, austerity-era youth services). On this reading, the series performs the ideological work of converting an organic social crisis into a containable cultural-criminal story. An oppositional viewer might note that the show was, predictably, mobilised in Parliament within weeks to argue for new platform regulation, exactly the kind of state response Hall analysed in *Policing the Crisis*.

The pay-off of Hall's model is that all three readings are real audience responses, occurring simultaneously, and the sociologist's job is not to declare one correct but to map the field of decoding. The model also implies that the preferred encoding is *not* guaranteed to land. *Adolescence* may produce, in some teenage male viewers, an *aberrant* decoding, sympathy with Jamie, identification with his anger, that the encoders explicitly did not intend. This is the central insight of Hall's "The Whites of Their Eyes": that the same encoded text can be decoded "in ways that might contradict the encoding," and that "comedy protects/defends viewer from acknowledging racism" (Hall, 1981a). Drama, here, can do the same.

What Hall lets us see, that a simple political-content analysis cannot, is that *Adolescence* is doing several kinds of cultural work at once: producing a moral panic in Hall's sense, articulating a structure of feeling in Williams's sense, and offering an encoded preferred reading that competes with negotiated and oppositional readings on TikTok, in school staff rooms, in Parliament, and in the texts the viewer's parents send her at 11pm. Encoding/decoding remains, fifty-three years after the essay, the right tool.

### Essay 8. Hall's grammar of race and the racial politics of British popular culture today.

I want to argue that Stuart Hall's "The Whites of Their Eyes: Racist Ideologies and the Media" (Hall, 1981a) provides a still-powerful framework for analysing race in British popular culture, but that the framework needs supplementing in the streaming era because the grammar of race has migrated from sitcom comedy to algorithmic content recommendation and to "diversity" as managed corporate aesthetic.

Hall's 1981 argument identified what he called a "grammar of race" in 1970s and 1980s British television. Negative racial stereotypes were "encoded in film and television" through three principal figures, the slave figure, the native, and the clown / entertainer. These stereotypes were often present as "traces" even when not overt; race was framed as "a problem"; and viewers could decode in ways that produced "contradictions and ambivalence." The key insight, sharpened by Hall in close reading of the ITV sitcom *Love Thy Neighbour* (a white working-class couple in Twickenham and their Black neighbours), was that "comedy protects / defends viewer from acknowledging racism." The hegemonic work of racist representation could proceed precisely because the comic framing licensed a "we don't really mean it" decoding. The grammar of race did not require crude vilification; it required structural positioning.

Three contemporary cases test the framework. First, the British sitcom canon today. *Love Thy Neighbour* is dead; *Black Earth Rising*, *I May Destroy You*, *Top Boy*, *Riches* and others have arrived. The sitcom-grammar Hall analysed has been partially displaced by Black-British-led drama that refuses the "native" and "entertainer" positions. But the grammar has also migrated. *Top Boy*'s representation of London estate crime can be read in dominant-hegemonic, negotiated and oppositional ways: the encoded preferred reading is sympathetic-realist, but the dominant decoding by white middle-class audiences may still reproduce the "Black-male-as-problem" grammar Hall named. A Williams-style structure-of-feeling reading would notice that *Top Boy*'s emotional texture, grief, mothers, exhaustion, exceeds the genre and articulates something not yet politically named about Black British working-class life under austerity.

Second, Stormzy at Glastonbury 2019. The headline performance, Stormzy in a Union Jack stab vest designed by Banksy, performing grime on the Pyramid Stage, is a textbook Hall case. Encoded: a Black grime artist claiming the central platform of British national culture, refusing the entertainer position. Dominant-hegemonic decoding: a successful diversity moment; multicultural Britain works. Negotiated decoding: a meaningful but contained inclusion; Stormzy got the stage once, the structures remained. Oppositional decoding: the Union Jack stab vest itself names knife-crime moral panic about Black men, and the symbolic incorporation of Stormzy may operate as Hall's hegemony, securing consent rather than producing change. All three readings are simultaneously available; the sociologist's job is to map their distribution.

Third, the *Adolescence* and "small boats" media nexus. Here race is encoded through proxies: the manosphere is presented as predominantly white-male; the Channel-crossing migrant is presented as predominantly racialised. Hall's grammar names the racial work being done without ever saying "race." The "problem" frame Hall identified, race as a problem to be managed, has been preserved by being relocated: it is now "small boats," "Channel crossings," "asylum hotels." The encoding is policy-laundered, but a Hall reading detects the grammar.

The supplementations needed are two. First, the streaming era spreads the encoded text across multiple platforms simultaneously. The grammar of race on a Netflix drama is decoded by a viewer who is also scrolling X and TikTok, where the same material is being re-encoded in second-order memes. The decoding moment Hall analysed in the living room is now a networked moment. The dominant-hegemonic position is harder to secure because oppositional decodings can be communicated instantly to other oppositional decoders.

Second, the "diversity" aesthetic has become a hegemonic strategy in itself. Hall in 1981 was analysing texts where Black people were minor characters or comic foils. Now, BBC and Netflix dramas often foreground Black characters in central, sympathetic roles, and yet, as Hall would predict, this can perform hegemonic work: it secures consent for a multicultural narrative while leaving the political-economic structures of British racial capitalism (Brixton housing, Hostile Environment, deaths in custody) untouched. Williams's structure of feeling captures the gap: an emergent Black British cultural confidence is being articulated *and* a residual racism is being managed by representational inclusion, and the formal ideology has not yet caught up.

The framework still works. The grammar of race is still encoded, still decodes in contradictions and ambivalence, and comedy, now combined with prestige drama and platform algorithm, still protects viewers from acknowledging racism. Hall is not yet historical; he is just operating on bigger screens.

### Essay 9. Thompson on history from below and the sociology of culture.

I want to argue that E. P. Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* (Thompson, 1963) supplies British Cultural Studies with its most important historiographical commitment, history from below, and that the move matters for the contemporary sociology of culture because it refuses to let either economic structures or elite ideologies do the explanatory work that lived experience and cultural agency must do.

Thompson's preface to *The Making* contains the most quoted sentence in British social history: he wanted to rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the "obsolete" hand-loom weaver, the "utopian" artisan, even the deluded follower of Joanna Southcott, from "the enormous condescension of posterity." The methodological commitment is then unmistakable. The English working class did not appear as the deposit of an economic structure; it *made itself*, in active cultural and political work, in chapels, friendly societies, Corresponding Societies, food riots, radical pamphlets, hymns and trade-union banners. Class is, in Thompson's formula, "a relationship, not a thing", something people do, in struggle, with one another and against others.

Three concepts in Thompson matter for L14. First, the lived experience of class: class is felt before it is structured. Thompson's working-class artisans experience exploitation as a moral as well as economic outrage and respond in idiom, religious, customary, traditional, that does not look "modern" but is in fact the work of cultural self-making. Second, history from below: the historian's responsibility is to write from the standpoint of those who did not leave parliamentary speeches behind, who appear in the archive as criminal records, parish reports, magistrates' notes, and seditious pamphlets. Third, the moral economy: in his 1971 essay "The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century," Thompson showed that eighteenth-century food riots were not blind hunger eruptions but principled enforcement of customary fair-price norms against new market behaviour. The crowd had a theory; the historian has to take it seriously.

The pay-off for the sociology of culture is fourfold. First, against structuralist Marxism (Althusser arrived in Britain in the late 1960s), Thompson supplied an account of class in which agency and culture were not afterthoughts to be "explained" by the relations of production but the very medium in which class came into being. The famous Thompson-Althusser exchange in *The Poverty of Theory* (1978) ran on exactly this point.

Second, against Frankfurt School pessimism, Thompson's working class was not a "mass" administered by ideology but a self-organising cultural formation. Williams's "no masses" thesis and Thompson's "the class made itself" are the same move from different disciplinary angles, sociology of culture from one side, social history from the other.

Third, Thompson supplied a method of reading sources from below. The minute book of a Friendly Society, the chapel hymn-book, the pamphlet sold for a penny, the inscriptions on a union banner, these are now legitimate documents of cultural analysis. Stuart Hall's work on Caribbean diaspora culture, Paul Gilroy's *There Ain't No Black in the Union Jack*, and Beverley Skeggs's *Formations of Class and Gender* all draw on Thompson's licence to read non-canonical cultural materials seriously.

Fourth, the moral economy concept travels astonishingly well into contemporary cultural sociology. The food-bank network in austerity Britain is a textbook moral-economy practice: a popular enforcement of customary norms of "no one should starve here," supplied by a mix of churches, mutual aid groups, and unpaid labour, in a way that does not look like trade-union militancy but is doing moral-economy work. The cost-of-living crisis has produced a moral-economic structure of feeling, the sense that energy companies' profits are wrong in a deeper-than-economic way, that there is a customary norm being violated. Thompson would have recognised the sentiment immediately.

A contemporary case: the 2024 farming protests across Europe, and the smaller-scale British farmer protests over inheritance tax in late 2024. A reductionist Marxist analysis reads these as petty-bourgeois reaction. A Thompsonian analysis notes the moral-economy element: customary norms about land, family succession, and rural community that exceed and predate the tax-policy specifics, and that articulate a structure of feeling not yet politically housed. The protest's idiom, tractors blocking Whitehall, signs reading "no farmers, no food", is moral-economy language, not GDP-growth language. To analyse it well, the sociologist of culture needs Thompson.

A second case: contemporary mutual-aid networks built during Covid-19. These were Thompsonian creatures: street-level WhatsApp groups, drop-off bags of shopping, informal coordination outside the state. Williams's "ordinary people" and Thompson's "history from below" name the same thing, the ongoing cultural self-organisation of working people, recoverable to a sociology that knows how to look.

The limits are real. Thompson wrote about white English men in the early industrial revolution; gender and race are largely absent in *The Making*. Joan Scott's feminist critique and Catherine Hall's later post-colonial work have made clear what Thompson's "below" excluded. But these are corrections from within his method, not refutations of it. History from below remains the right commitment for the sociology of culture. It tells us that the proper unit of analysis is not the apparatus, the ideology, or the structure, but the lived activity by which working people make themselves into something they were not before.

### Essay 10. "Culture is a site of struggle." Discuss with reference to two BCS thinkers.

I want to argue that the BCS slogan "culture is a site of struggle" is best understood by reading Williams and Hall together: Williams supplies the *ontology* (culture is the whole way of life in which struggles over meaning happen), Hall supplies the *mechanics* (encoding, decoding, hegemony, common sense, moral panic). The phrase is not a vague claim that "culture is political"; it is a specific claim about how power operates in late capitalism.

Williams's contribution is to refuse the two positions that would deny that culture is a site of struggle. The first is the elite literary view that treats culture as a finished inheritance to be transmitted to those capable of receiving it. Williams's Cambridge teashop is full of "cultivated people" who "showed you they had it" (Williams, 1958: 12). If culture is what is already settled in the teashop, then it is not a site of struggle, it is a site of curatorship. Williams refuses: "if that is culture, we don't want it; we have seen other people living" (Williams, 1958: 12). The second position is the reflectionist Marxism which treats culture as the secondary expression of an economic base. If culture is a reflection of production relations, then it is not a site of struggle in its own right; it is at best a symptom. Williams accepts that "a culture is a whole way of life, and the arts are part of a social organisation which economic change clearly radically affects" (Williams, 1958: 14). He refuses the "directive element" that follows. Culture is *both* shaped by production *and* a domain of struggle in which production relations are contested, naturalised, refused, lived.

The struggle, in Williams's account, is fought through common meanings. "A culture is common meanings, the product of a whole people, and offered individual meanings, the product of a man's whole committed personal and social experience" (Williams, 1958: 15). Common meanings are not given; they are "made and remade." The struggle is over which meanings become common and on whose terms. His own argument with the "do-gooder" / "highbrow" argot, with Bell's *Civilisation*, with the Cambridge teashop, with Leavis's dying-culture diagnosis and with the Marxist directive on prescribed forms is itself a series of struggles over what "culture" will be allowed to mean. Concepts like residual / dominant / emergent and the long revolution name a dynamic field, not a static base/superstructure schema but a culture in motion in which struggle is ongoing.

Hall picks up Williams's ontology and gives it Gramscian mechanics. Hegemony (Gramsci, 1971), the process by which dominant groups secure consent rather than relying on force, by which power operates through common sense, names the form the struggle takes. The dominant order does not have to win by police repression; it wins by making its meanings feel natural, obvious, "common sense." The site of that struggle is exactly the cultural domain Williams named. The encoding/decoding model (Hall, 1980) gives the struggle a precise location: in the encoding moment, dominant frameworks of knowledge work to install a preferred reading; in the decoding moment, audiences may take the preferred reading whole, negotiate it, or oppose it. The struggle is not metaphorical; it is happening in living rooms, on phones, in cinemas, in bus stops where teenagers read TikTok captions.

*Policing the Crisis* (Hall et al., 1978) is the textbook case. Hall and his colleagues show how a relatively limited social phenomenon, street robbery, became, through media labelling, policing intensification, and political signal-sending, the "mugging" moral panic of the mid-1970s. The struggle is over what counts as the public problem: is it racial criminality (the dominant frame) or is it youth unemployment, deindustrialisation, and a state in crisis (an oppositional frame)? The hegemonic outcome was the former; the cost of that outcome was the closing-down of alternative political readings of the 1970s organic crisis. Culture, here, is not a reflection of the political-economic crisis; it is the site at which the crisis is *resolved* into one ideological story rather than another. This is what "culture is a site of struggle" actually means.

Two contemporary applications. First, Brexit (already discussed in essay 3) is a clear site-of-struggle case: a structure of feeling in deindustrial England gets articulated, by encoding work in the press and the Leave campaign, into a particular preferred reading (immigration, sovereignty); negotiated and oppositional readings continued in remain-voting cities but did not become dominant. Hall would have recognised the architecture immediately. Williams would have insisted on listening to the structure of feeling before condemning the vote.

Second, the post-2024 debate over Andrew Tate and the manosphere. The struggle is over what young male anger means: is it a digital-pathology problem (the dominant *Adolescence*-style framing), is it an economic-precarity problem (a negotiated reading found in some left commentary), is it a misogynist-anti-feminist counter-movement (an oppositional feminist reading), or is it (still) a residual masculinity reasserting itself under emergent platform conditions (the Williams residual/dominant/emergent reading)? All four readings coexist; none has yet become hegemonic; the cultural struggle is live.

The cost of treating culture as a site of struggle is that the sociology of culture becomes harder. Reflection theory was easier, find the base, derive the superstructure. Williams-Hall analysis requires close reading of cultural texts, attention to encoding regimes and decoding practices, ethnography of audiences, and historical placement in residual / dominant / emergent fields. It also runs a political risk: if culture is the site at which power is contested, then cultural intervention (a TV drama, a song, a meme) is taken seriously as politics, which can flatter the cultural producer into believing he is the revolution. Williams and Hall both knew this; both warned against it. But the risk is the price of taking culture, and ordinary people, seriously.

### Essay 11. "There is a distinct working-class way of life." The politics of British Cultural Studies.

I want to argue that Williams's sentence "There is a distinct working-class way of life, which I for one value" (Williams, 1958: 15) is the most precise single statement of the politics of British Cultural Studies. The sentence does three things at once: it asserts the existence of a class culture; it values that culture without sentimentalising it; and it makes the analyst declare himself politically. All three moves are constitutive of BCS as a project.

The first move, assertion of distinct working-class culture, refuses two erasures. It refuses the elite literary erasure (working people have no culture worth the name; they consume what their betters make for them). It also refuses the determinist Marxist erasure (working-class culture is just bourgeois culture imposed on the workers; there is no autonomous form). Williams insists that the working-class way of life exists in its own right, "with its emphases of neighbourhood, mutual obligation, and common betterment, as expressed in the great working-class political and industrial institutions" (Williams, 1958: 15). The empirical claim is contestable but at minimum points to actual social formations, trade unions, friendly societies, working-men's clubs, the Co-op, the Labour movement, the Methodist chapel network, the brass band, the rugby league club, which were not bourgeois inventions and which had distinct internal cultures.

The second move, valuing without sentimentalising, is harder. Hoggart, working three years earlier than Williams, slides easily into elegy: the 1930s as a lived self-made culture being killed by 1950s candy-floss. Thompson, working five years later, slides at moments into heroic identification with the early working class as the agent of history. Williams declines both. He values the working-class way of life "not only because I was bred in it, for I now, in certain respects, live differently. I think this way of life … is in fact the best basis for any future English society" (Williams, 1958: 15). The valuation is forward-looking, not nostalgic: it is the basis for the future, not a relic to be preserved. He is also clear-eyed about what was hard: "dirty water, an earth bucket, a fourmile walk each way to work, headaches, broken women, hunger and monotony of diet" (Williams, 1958: 17). The working-class way of life is not romanticised.

The third move, the analyst declares himself, is the constitutive BCS gesture. "I for one value" makes the writer accountable. He is not reporting a neutral fact; he is taking a side. The political position is socialist-democratic, attached to the institutions and ethos of the labour movement, and committed to the proposition that "the ordinary people should govern" (Williams, 1958: 24). The "socialist intellectual" has a job: "to attack the clamps on that energy", the energy of the ordinary people, "in industrial relations, public administration, education, for a start" (Williams, 1958: 24).

This declared politics is what distinguishes BCS from later, more academic Cultural Studies. By the 1980s and 1990s the field had become more methodologically sophisticated but in places less politically anchored: discourse analysis without a labour movement, semiotic readings of consumer goods without an account of the production relations that made them. Williams's sentence is a reminder of the original commitment. The cultural analyst is on the side of the working people whose culture she analyses.

There are real costs to this politics. The "we" of Williams's Welsh valley is gendered male, white, and Christian-secular; women, racialised workers, and non-British migrants are not adequately included. Beverley Skeggs's *Formations of Class and Gender* (1997) and Heidi Safia Mirza's *Young, Female and Black* (1992) corrected the gender and race blindness; Stuart Hall's writing on race and the New Left did similar work from within. The working-class movement Williams "valued" has also fragmented since 1958: deindustrialisation, the destruction of the mining communities in the 1980s, the decline of the labour-movement institutions he named (trade unions, Co-op, friendly societies, working men's clubs). Lisa McKenzie's *Getting By: Estates, Class and Culture in Austerity Britain* (2015) documents what the working-class way of life looks like after thirty years of neoliberalism: more atomised, more precarious, less institutionally rooted, but still, McKenzie insists, in Williams's voice, distinct, valuable, and worth analysing on its own terms.

A contemporary test. Stormzy headlining Glastonbury in 2019 is a Williams moment: a working-class (and Black) cultural form (grime) taking its place at the centre of the national festival, refusing the entertainer position, paying for free Cambridge tuition for Black students, Williams's third wish (more public provision for the arts and adult learning) being met by a working-class artist rather than by the state. Or: the rugby league Challenge Cup as a continuing working-class cultural institution that still organises Yorkshire and Lancashire weekends in a way that is not bourgeois, not commercialised beyond recognition, and not nostalgic. Or: the rise of working-class podcasts, YouTubers and TikTok producers as new versions of Williams's autodidact, making cultural production without the BBC's permission. Williams's sentence still finds the right cases.

The risk Williams himself names is sentimentality. "I would not expect the working people of England to support works which, after proper and patient preparation, they could not accept" (Williams, 1958: 22). He is not flattering working-class taste; he is committing to extending and changing cultural provision in a way that the working people themselves will help to shape. The politics of British Cultural Studies, at its best, is exactly this commitment: distinct culture, valued, by an analyst on its side, working to extend it.

### Essay 12. What does British Cultural Studies put in place of high culture and reflection theory, and at what cost?

I want to argue that British Cultural Studies replaces two earlier orthodoxies, elite literary "high culture" and the Marxist base-superstructure reflection thesis, with a unified alternative: culture as ordinary, lived, contested, hegemonic, encoded and decoded. The replacement is a real intellectual advance but has costs that are still being paid: a tendency to overstate cultural agency, a relative neglect of political economy, and a difficulty in distinguishing analytically meaningful cultural struggles from sociologically trivial ones.

The two orthodoxies that BCS displaces are the elite literary position (associated in Williams's text with Clive Bell, the Cambridge teashop, and a softer Leavisite reading) and the Marxist reflectionist position (associated with the Frankfurt School in the SC301 module and with crude British communist cultural orthodoxy in Williams's text). The elite position says that culture is the special preserve of cultivated people who possess it as "the outward and emphatically visible sign" of their cultivation (Williams, 1958: 12). The reflectionist position says that culture is the secondary expression of an economic base. Williams refuses both with a single move: "We use the word culture in these two senses: to mean a whole way of life, the common meanings; to mean the arts and learning, the special processes of discovery and creative effort. … I insist on both, and on the significance of their conjunction" (Williams, 1958: 11).

What BCS puts in place is a four-part alternative. First, an ontology: culture is the whole way of life of a people, in which both common meanings and special processes of discovery happen. Hoggart's "uses of literacy," Thompson's "lived experience of class," Hall's "hegemony" all sit inside Williams's "whole way of life." Second, a method: history from below (Thompson, 1963), close reading of working-class cultural materials (Hoggart, 1957), structure-of-feeling analysis (Williams, 1961; 1977), encoding/decoding analysis of media texts (Hall, 1980). Third, a politics: socialist-democratic, on the side of ordinary people, committed to the proposition that the ordinary people should govern. Fourth, an institutional location: the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS) at Birmingham, founded in 1964, which becomes the laboratory for the new approach.

This alternative achieves three things the displaced orthodoxies could not. It makes working-class and popular cultural materials sociologically legible without elegy. It accounts for media without technological determinism (Williams on TV) or transmission-of-ideology determinism (Hall on encoding/decoding). And it permits political reading of culture without prescribing correct cultural content, Williams refuses the "directive element" in Marxism, Hall refuses to declare any audience reading "false." The intellectual gain is large.

The costs are also real. The first cost is the *over-extension of cultural agency*. Once "everything is culture" and "audiences negotiate or oppose," it becomes tempting to read every consumption choice as resistance: shopping at H&M as a feminist statement, watching reality TV as a critical reading, choosing a Spotify playlist as cultural production. The empirical fact that audiences decode actively does not entail that every act of decoding is politically meaningful, and BCS in its weaker variants slid towards that conclusion. Stuart Hall himself was sharply critical of this slide.

The second cost is the *relative neglect of political economy*. BCS analysed media texts brilliantly but spent less time on who owned the media, who paid for the cultural production, and how the labour markets of cultural workers operated. Williams's own *Communications* (Williams, 1962) and *Television: Technology and Cultural Form* (Williams, 1974) do attend to political economy, but the broader tradition was less consistent. The cost of this neglect is visible in current debates about platform capitalism: BCS has the tools to analyse TikTok's preferred encoded readings of teenage femininity, but it has fewer tools to analyse TikTok's ownership structure, its labour conditions for content moderators in the Philippines, or its algorithmic political economy.

The third cost is the *difficulty of distinguishing significant from trivial cultural struggles*. If culture is a site of struggle and every text encodes and is decoded, then the field is flat: how do we tell the *Adolescence*-and-manosphere struggle from a struggle over which contestant wins *The Great British Bake Off*? Williams's residual / dominant / emergent triad helps; Thompson's moral economy helps; Hall's hegemony helps. But the analyst still has to make a judgement, and BCS gives only partial guidance on how to make it.

The fourth, and most discussed, cost is the *category of class itself*. BCS was built on a robust account of British class culture, Williams's signalman father, Hoggart's Leeds 1930s, Thompson's hand-loom weavers, Hall's London Caribbean migrants. That class culture has been deeply transformed by deindustrialisation, neoliberalism, and the destruction of the labour-movement institutions Williams "valued." Skeggs, McKenzie, and others have updated the class analysis; but BCS in 2026 needs a class theory that can talk about Deliveroo riders, Amazon warehouse workers, OnlyFans producers, and care workers in a way that the original framework did not have to.

A contemporary case shows both the achievement and the cost. The Brexit vote can be analysed by a BCS framework with real power: as a structure of feeling articulated through tabloid encoding and decoded in multiple ways (dominant Leave, negotiated Leave, oppositional Remain), under hegemonic conditions in which "common sense" about immigration had been built over decades. This is intellectually serious sociology of culture. But a fuller analysis also needs the political economy of regional deindustrialisation, EU agricultural policy, and the funding of the Leave campaign, which BCS in its purer forms is not as well equipped to deliver.

I would conclude: BCS replaced two intellectually exhausted orthodoxies with a tradition that has now itself become orthodox. Williams's "culture is ordinary" is sixty-eight years old; Hall's encoding/decoding is fifty-three years old; Thompson's *Making* is sixty-three years old. They remain the right starting point, and the cost of replacing them with anything else would be enormous, back to the teashop, back to the reflection, back to the masses. But each of the three founders would have been the first to insist that the tradition keep moving. Culture is ordinary, and so is its analysis: made and remade, in ways we cannot know in advance.
