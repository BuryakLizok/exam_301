## 1. Key Concepts and Thinkers

- **Modernity:** "a way of thinking, acting and living which had and still has enormous and unequal consequences for the world and the peoples of the world" (lecture slides). Tied to industrialisation, urbanisation, individualisation, rationality and the break with tradition.
- **The colonial attitude:** a habit of thought, surviving political independence, that ranks societies as superior/inferior, modern/backward.
- **Eurocentrism:** the assumption that modernity is an exclusively European phenomenon and that European development is the universal model.
- **The myth of modernity (Dussel):** modernity packages emancipation with a concealed irrational myth that justifies genocidal violence on a "civilising" mission.
- **Fallacy of developmentalism (Dussel):** the idea that every culture must follow Europe's ontological path of development.
- **The West and the Rest (Hall):** a discourse, not a geography. A "system of representation" producing binary oppositions: western/non-western = developed/under-developed = good/bad.
- **Bifurcated thinking:** the colonial attitude sorts the world into two camps.
- **Multiple modernities / trans-modernity (Dussel):** alternative routes that refuse Europe as the sole template.
- **Classical-sociology trio:** Marx (commodification, alienation, slavery–cotton–industry chain), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment), Durkheim (mechanical → organic solidarity).
- **Hegel:** world-historical Spirit moves East to West; Africa is "outside history"; Europe holds an "absolute right".
- **Kant:** Enlightenment as exodus from "guilty immaturity"; racialised remarks on "the Negroes of Africa".
- **Du Bois:** before the 15th century there was "no great disparity" between Negroland and Europe; slavery built world trade.

## 3. Information from the Readings

### Enrique Dussel — *Eurocentrism and Modernity*

**Central thesis.** Modernity is a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity. Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the "centre" of a World History it inaugurates; the "periphery" is part of its self-definition. By occluding the periphery — and the role of Spain and Portugal from the late 15th to mid-17th centuries — the major thinkers of the centre commit a Eurocentric fallacy.

**1492 as the birth date.** Dussel dates modernity to 1492: the conquest of the Kingdom of Granada and Columbus's voyage. Modernity is "born" when Europe is in a position to pose itself against an other. The other is not "dis-covered" but "covered-up" — concealed as the same as what Europe assumed it had always been.

**The myth of modernity.** Modernity contains a rational concept of emancipation that Dussel affirms and subsumes, but it also develops an irrational myth — a justification for genocidal violence. Postmodernists criticise modern reason as terror; Dussel criticises modern reason because of the irrational myth it conceals.

**Fallacy of developmentalism.** The fallacy consists in thinking that Europe's path of development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture. Development is taken as an ontological (not merely sociological) category. For Hegel, an "underdeveloped" country is "not-modern", pre-Aufklärung.

**Kant.** "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." Dussel asks: should the African slave, the Mexican Indian, the Latin American mestizo be considered guilty for their immaturity?

**Hegel.** World History is the self-realisation of Spirit, Reason, and Freedom. It moves East to West. Latin America and Africa are "outside" history. Asia is the dawn but not the culmination. Northern Europe — Germany, France, Denmark, Scandinavia — is the heart. The Germanic Spirit, refracted through the Lutheran Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution, is the culmination.

**The right of empire.** For Hegel, the people that bears the Spirit at its current moment has an "absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights". Dussel sees this as the philosophical sacralisation of imperial power over the periphery — a "limitless cynicism" masked as the development of reason.

**Colonies and civil society.** Hegel argues that civil society's internal contradictions push it to seek new consumers and colonise peoples "inferior" with respect to its industry. Colonisation allows surplus poor to become property owners abroad. Hegel does not register that this requires seizing land from others.

**Habermas continues the occlusion.** Habermas locates modernity's principle in the Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution. The discovery of America is mentioned but assigned no constitutive role. Habermas inherits Hegel's discounting of Spain and Portugal.

**The seven elements of the myth of modernity.**
1. European civilisation understands itself as the most developed and superior.
2. This obliges it, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the "primitive".
3. The path of development is Europe's own out of antiquity and the Middle Ages.
4. Where the "barbarian" resists, modernity must, in the last instance, recourse to violence.
5. The violence is ritualistic — victims (colonised, slave, woman, the earth) are cast as participants in redemptive sacrifice.
6. The barbarian is in a state of guilt for opposing civilisation; modernity is innocent and emancipatory.
7. The sufferings and sacrifices of modernisation imposed on immature peoples are inevitable and necessary.

**Trans-modernity.** Dussel proposes neither postmodern relativism nor a reaffirmation of European reason but a "trans-modernity": a co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (the colonial periphery, the Indian, the slave, the woman, the child, subalternised popular cultures). They must first "discover themselves as innocent" of the originary violence.

### Stuart Hall — *Formations of Modernity* (West and the Rest)

**The West as historical, not geographical, construct.** "Western" denotes a type of society — developed, industrialised, urbanised, capitalist, secular, modern. Its meaning is "virtually identical" to the word "modern". Japan can be "western"; Latin America, in the western hemisphere, is not.

**The West as concept** — four functions:
1. Characterises and classifies societies (western vs non-western) — a tool to think with.
2. Condenses a set of images: a system of representation. Western = urban = developed; non-western = rural = under-developed.
3. Provides a standard of comparison — how close to or far from the West?
4. Provides criteria of evaluation: the West = good = desirable; non-West = bad = undesirable. It functions as an ideology.

**Mutual constitution.** "The West and the Rest became two sides of a single coin." The uniqueness of the West was produced through Europe's contact and self-comparison with non-western societies. Identity is formed by difference (Saussure on night/day; psychoanalysis on the infant and the mother).

**Discourse and binary opposition.** Hall reads "West and Rest" as a discourse that simplifies — representing differentiated Europe as homogeneous (the West) and grouping equally diverse societies (Middle East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous America, Australasia) as one Rest. That over-simplification is precisely what makes the discourse destructive.

**Internal others.** Hall notes the West also had its internal others — Jews, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", women. The dichotomy is not only outward-facing.

**Periodisation.** Expansion is dated from Portuguese coastal exploration (1430–1498) and Columbus (1492–1502). Michael Mann's claim that Europe "overtook" Asia around 1450 is cited. The five phases (exploration; early contact and conquest; settlement; imperial consolidation; decolonisation) frame the West–Rest relationship as a long history of asymmetric encounter.

### Howard W. French — *Born in Blackness*

**Restoring Africa to modernity's origin story.** French argues that the conventional story of modernity — Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution — leaves out the constitutive role of Africa and Africans. The Portuguese push down the West African coast in the 15th century was driven by the search for gold and African slave labour, not just spices or a route to Asia. African gold (Mali, Ghana) helped underwrite European mercantilism.

**Slavery and the making of the Atlantic world.** Sugar plantations in São Tomé, the Caribbean and Brazil — staffed by enslaved Africans — generated unprecedented surplus and demand. The capital, commodities and consumer markets of the early modern economy were forged on this base. The cotton mills of Lancashire, the docks of Liverpool, the financial instruments of London and Amsterdam, the banks that survived to the present — all draw lines back to the slave economy.

**Africa as agent, not victim.** French insists Africa is not merely passive raw material. Kongo, Benin, Asante, Songhai were sophisticated polities whose engagements with Europeans shaped the trade. The argument supports Dussel's claim that the periphery is constitutive of the centre, and Hall's claim that the West is the product of relations with the Rest.

**Erasure as method.** Like Dussel's "covered-up" other and Hall's discourse of simplification, French highlights the active forgetting in European historiography — Africa "born in blackness" written out of modernity's account of itself.

## 4. Critical Analysis with Embedded Key Quotes

The central provocation of L2 is that modernity is not innocent. It is sociology's founding object, but the discipline that names it has also concealed its dependence on colonial extraction. Three moves are crucial.

First, Dussel reframes 1492 as modernity's birthdate. He writes:

> "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity… modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other." — Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66

This relocates modernity's origin from the Reformation and Enlightenment to colonial conquest. Habermas and Hegel both fail this test:

> "For Habermas, as for Hegel, the discovery of America is not a constitutive fact of modernity." — Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 74

Second, the lecture and Dussel converge on the "myth of modernity": rational emancipation is real but inseparable from sacrificial violence:

> "Modernity includes a rational 'concept' of emancipation that we affirm and subsume. But, at the same time, it develops an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence." — Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66

The seven-step "myth" — superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault — gives me a checklist for reading any modernising rhetoric, from nineteenth-century missions to twenty-first-century "regime change". The lecture asks pointedly: Iraq, the Falklands, Saudi Arabia?

Third, the violence has explicit textual sources in canonical European philosophy. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* sounds emancipatory until paired with his racial remarks:

> "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." — Kant, cited in Dussel, p. 68

> "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous." — Kant, *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, p. 59

Hegel goes further, treating Africa as outside history altogether:

> "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again." — Hegel, *Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, cited in Dussel, p. 70

> "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning." — Hegel, *Lectures*, p. 243, cited in Dussel, p. 69

The right of empire follows directly:

> "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights." — Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73

Stuart Hall completes the picture by showing that even when we are not citing Hegel, we are speaking through his categories. The discourse of "the West and the Rest":

> "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy — the West/the Rest. That is what makes the discourse of 'the West and the Rest' so destructive — it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'." — Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280

Marx is the partial exception within classical sociology — he saw the chain:

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." — Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203

And Du Bois pushed against the colonial attitude well before postcolonial theory:

> "In the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe." — Du Bois, 1970, p. 86, cited in the lecture slides

Critically: the colonial attitude is not an embarrassing relic. The lecturer's prompt — to look at sport, music, fashion, university league tables — shows that the same hierarchies still shape evaluation. We file Bach as universal and gamelan as "world music". We assume "developing" countries should follow our path. The intellectual task of L2 is to denaturalise that assumption and recover the constitutive role of the colonised in making the modern world.

## 8. Essay Answers

### 2024 (Q1): In what ways is modernity a colonial project?

To call modernity a colonial project is not to deny its rational and emancipatory ambitions; it is to argue that those ambitions cannot be separated from colonial conquest, racial hierarchy, and the violent imposition of one cultural template on the rest of the world. I want to defend this thesis in four moves: modernity's economic base, its philosophical self-image, its sociological vocabulary, and its surviving "attitude" today.

The economic base is the hardest to evade. Even Marx, normally an exemplar of Eurocentric sociology, conceded the point: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire ran on slave-grown cotton from the American South. The sugar that sweetened London and Amsterdam came from plantations staffed by enslaved Africans. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* extends this argument: Portugal's push down the West African coast in the 15th century, driven by gold and slaves, was a pre-condition not just of European wealth but of the financial instruments and consumer markets we now call "modern". Modernity, then, did not happen first in Europe and then spread; it was already a colonial relation in its formation.

The second move is philosophical. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* shows that the canonical European philosophers of reason wrote the colonial relation into the concept of modernity itself. He dates modernity's birth to 1492: "modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66). Kant's exhortation to leave "guilty immaturity" is paired with his judgement that "the Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, p. 59). Hegel writes the global South out of history altogether — "Africa… does not have history as such" — and grants the people that "bears" Spirit at its moment an "absolute right" against which other peoples "have no rights" (Hegel, cited in Dussel, pp. 70, 73). Dussel summarises this as the "myth of modernity": modernity packages rational emancipation with an irrational, sacrificial myth that licenses violence against those who resist the civilising project. The seven-step structure he draws out — superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault — gives modernity its colonial logic, not merely its colonial side-effects.

The third move is sociological. The lecture is explicit that sociology is "a creature of modernity": it arose to explain industrialisation, urbanisation and individualisation, and in doing so it inscribed the West-Rest hierarchy into its core vocabulary. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic transition treats non-European societies as pre-modern, behind, backward. Weber's rationalisation and disenchantment narrative makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Even the lecturer's question — does the rest of the world have history, in the sense modernity demands? — exposes the temporal hierarchy built into the discipline. Stuart Hall calls this "the West and the Rest": a discourse that "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" and that produces destructive over-simplifications of difference (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). The "West" is not a place; it is a model of comparison, an evaluative grid, an ideology. Sociology has often supplied the data for that grid.

The fourth move is contemporary. The lecture asks us to test whether "we" still think this way. Modern Western Classical Music is taught as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music". University league tables crown Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard and MIT as the world's best on criteria that take English-language journals and Anglo-American institutional forms as the measure. International development still imagines a single ladder up which every state must climb. The British Museum still holds the Benin bronzes and the Parthenon marbles. The Boris Johnson clip cited in the lecture is not an aberration; it is a transparent instance of a habit of thought the discipline calls the colonial attitude.

A counter-position argues that modernity also gave us human rights, scientific medicine, mass literacy and democratic accountability — gains that have benefited the colonised too. This is fair. Dussel himself "affirms and subsumes" modernity's emancipatory concept (p. 66). The point is not to discard modernity but to recognise that its rational kernel has always travelled with an irrational myth, and that the rights and goods we want to keep can only be honestly defended once their colonial history is acknowledged. Modernity is a colonial project not as an insult but as a description: at its economic base, in its philosophical self-image, in its sociological vocabulary and in its surviving everyday attitudes, the modern world is unintelligible without the colonies that helped make it.

### 2025 (Q1): Is it possible to fully understand modernity without understanding the role of colonialism in its development?

No — and arguing otherwise requires a curiously selective view of history. To "fully" understand modernity is to understand both its conceptual self-image (reason, progress, autonomy, the secular state) and its material conditions of possibility (industrial capitalism, world trade, mass consumption, the modern state). Colonialism is not an external accident attached to either of these. It is constitutive of both. I will defend this through three lenses — Dussel's philosophical genealogy, Hall's discursive analysis, and Howard French's economic history — before answering the obvious counter-argument that Europe's internal transformations (Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) suffice as an explanation.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* makes the strongest philosophical case. He argues that modernity "appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (p. 65). The European ego that thinks itself modern only becomes thinkable when it can pose itself against an other. Cortés in Mexico, not Luther in Wittenberg, is the prototypical modern subject; the conquest of the New World, not the French Revolution, marks the birth of the "Will to Power specific to [Europe's] self-consciousness" (p. 74). To "fully" understand modernity without colonialism is therefore to misunderstand the constitution of modern subjectivity itself. Habermas's mainstream genealogy — Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution — is, on Dussel's reading, "partial and provincial" (p. 65).

Stuart Hall's "West and the Rest" essay translates this into a discursive register. The West is not a geography but a "system of representation" with four functions: classifying societies, condensing images, providing a model of comparison, and supplying criteria of evaluation (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Crucially, "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (p. 278). Hall is borrowing from Saussure on linguistic difference: "night" only means what it means because of "day". By analogy, "modernity" only means what it means because of "non-modernity" — and that means colonised territories function as the conceptual condition of the modern. Anyone who claims to understand modernity by looking only inside Europe is mistaking the picture for the canvas.

The economic argument is the most concrete. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* traces how Portugal's mid-15th-century push down the West African coast — for gold from Mali and Ghana, then for enslaved labour for São Tomé and later the Atlantic — supplied the capital that funded Europe's early modern boom. Sugar, tobacco and cotton, all plantation crops worked by enslaved Africans, financed the docks of Liverpool, the warehouses of Bristol, the financial houses of London and Amsterdam, and ultimately the steam-powered mills of Manchester. Even Marx — usually a Eurocentric witness — conceded that "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, p. 203). To "fully" understand the Industrial Revolution without slavery is to study the engine and ignore the fuel. Du Bois made the same point a century earlier: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit form the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970, pp. 107–108, cited in the lecture slides).

The conventional answer — that modernity is the internal achievement of European reason — runs into three problems. First, it has to suppress the texts. Kant's "Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59) and Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70) are not stray asides; they are part of the architecture of the philosophical projects in which Enlightenment is celebrated. Second, it has to suppress the money. As soon as one traces the Lancashire cotton mill backwards or the Amsterdam bank backwards, the colonial relation surfaces. Third, it has to suppress the discourse. Hall's point is that even sympathetic European thinkers wrote inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The internalist story is not just incomplete — it is positively misleading because it naturalises the European outcome.

A more honest counter-position concedes that colonialism was constitutive but worries that emphasising it threatens the universal goods modernity also produced — human rights, scientific medicine, democratic governance. Dussel's reply is that we do not have to choose: he "affirms and subsumes" modernity's rational emancipatory concept while exposing the irrational myth that travelled with it (p. 66). His proposal of "trans-modernity" — the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (p. 76) — is precisely a way to retain the goods while refusing the myth.

So the answer is no. We cannot fully understand modernity without colonialism because the economic base, the philosophical self-image and the discursive grammar of the modern were all forged in the encounter between Europe and the worlds it conquered. To set colonialism aside is not to simplify the story; it is to tell a different one — the one Hegel told, and the one Habermas still tells. Both are too provincial to count as full understanding.

### 2023 (Q1): Can we understand modernity without understanding its relationship to colonialism?

This question and the 2025 paper press the same point with different verbs. The 2023 wording — "can we understand" — invites a slightly different answer. We can produce a partial, internalist account of modernity that ignores colonialism; people do it every term. But we cannot produce an account that survives the texts, the money, and the discourse once they are placed on the table. In what follows I sketch what that partial account looks like, then show why it fails three tests.

The standard partial account treats modernity as a European achievement with three internal pillars. Politically, the consolidation of centralised nation-states out of feudal kingdoms (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Religiously and intellectually, the Reformation and the Enlightenment, which Habermas takes as "the historical events that are decisive for the implantation of the principle of [modern] subjectivity" (cited in Dussel, p. 74). Economically, the Industrial Revolution. Sociologically, the discipline rests on Marx (commodification, alienation), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment) and Durkheim (mechanical to organic solidarity). On this account, the colonies feature, if at all, as a market for surplus production or a recipient of the civilising mission — secondary, not constitutive.

The first reason this account fails is textual. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* — "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, p. 68) — sounds universal until you read the same author's *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59). Hegel's *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* state that "the movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West" (p. 243) and that "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). The "absolute right" of the people bearing Spirit against peoples who "have no rights" (Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73) is not a footnote; it is the legitimation of empire by the philosopher whom Habermas continues to cite as the architect of modern subjectivity. To bracket colonialism is to bracket the architecture of the Enlightenment.

The second reason is economic. Marx himself — usually the Eurocentric exhibit — conceded: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). The cotton mills of Manchester were not autonomous European achievements; they were nodes in a circuit that ran through enslaved plantations in the Americas, gold extracted from Africa, silver mined in Potosí, and consumers buying sugar, tobacco and cotton goods. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows that the financial instruments of early modern Amsterdam and London were tooled to manage slave-trading and slave-grown commodities. Take that out and the Industrial Revolution looks unmotivated.

The third reason is discursive. Hall's contribution is to show that even when modern thinkers were not explicitly racist, they were speaking inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The West is "a tool to think with… a system of representation" that classifies, condenses, compares and evaluates (p. 277). It functions as an ideology in which "western = developed = good = desirable" and "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (p. 277). Until you see this, you cannot see why "developing country" is a value-laden category, why "the international community" is so often a code for the OECD, or why the BBC files Senegalese rap as "world music" while it files Bach as universal heritage. The discourse is so naturalised that it can be reproduced without ever mentioning colonialism — but it makes no sense without it.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* knits these together. His "myth of modernity" — superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault (p. 75) — is a single structure underwriting Kant's racial hierarchy, Hegel's philosophy of history, the East India Company, the conquest of the Americas, Belgian rule in the Congo, and the current rhetoric of "regime change". To understand modernity without colonialism, you would have to detach a structure from its instances — a strange thing for a sociologist to do.

A counter-position might say that the colonial element is real but is one factor among many; we can isolate other factors (technology, secularisation, the printing press) and study them on their own terms. This is true at the level of any particular question. The pebble can be examined without the riverbed. But the broader claim — that we can understand modernity as such without colonialism — fails because the riverbed is part of what makes the pebble visible as that kind of pebble. Modernity is not a discrete property; it is a relation. And the relation includes the colonised. So we can produce internalist accounts of modernity, but we cannot understand modernity in any deep sense without taking colonialism seriously.

### 2022 (Q3): Is modernity undermined by its roots in colonialism?

"Undermined" is the live word here. It could mean discredited — modernity is unworthy of allegiance because of its colonial origin; or destabilised — modernity is internally contradictory because the freedom it claims for some rests on the unfreedom of others; or unmasked — modernity is shown to be something other than it claims to be. I will argue that the second reading is correct: modernity is destabilised but not discredited by its colonial roots. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* is the clearest articulation of this position, and I will use Hall and Marx to support it.

Begin with the strong "discredited" reading. On this view, the modern world was built on slavery, conquest and theft. Therefore everything modernity values — human rights, science, democratic government, secular reason — is contaminated and should be rejected. This is sometimes attributed to Dussel or to the postmodernists Dussel cites — Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo. But it is not Dussel's position. He is explicit: "we do not propose a critique of reason as such" and "we do not deny the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment, only its irrational moment as sacrificial myth" (p. 75). Modernity is not all bad. It is internally split.

That split is what is being asked about. Dussel argues that modernity carries a rational concept of emancipation, which he "affirms and subsumes", and an irrational myth, which he negates (p. 66). The rational concept is genuine — autonomy, equality, the rule of law, scientific method, public reason. The irrational myth is the seven-step structure: superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; the victim is in a state of guilt; modernity emancipates by destroying (p. 75). Both have been present from 1492 onwards. The Enlightenment writes the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* in 1789 while the French state runs slave plantations in Saint-Domingue. Kant writes *What is Enlightenment?* and *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* — "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59). The undermining is internal; the same hand wrote both.

Hall's analysis sharpens this. The discourse of "the West and the Rest" is not a side-effect of European modernity; it is part of how modernity thinks itself: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (p. 278). The West can only know itself by negation. Its universalism is structurally provincial. That is undermining in the second sense: modernity claims one thing (universal reason) and depends on another (a binary opposition between West and Rest) — a performative contradiction.

The economic version of the same problem is Marx's. "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). Modern industrial capitalism — its productivity, its mass markets, its working-class consumer — could not have happened without the slave-grown raw materials of the Atlantic system. Manchester is unintelligible without Mississippi. Liverpool is unintelligible without Lagos. So modernity's economic dynamism, often celebrated as the engine of human progress, rests on coerced and racialised labour. This does not "undermine" the productivity gains; the cotton was real; the wages did rise; but it undermines any account of those gains that treats them as the unaided fruit of European industriousness.

What kind of conclusion does this support? Three things follow. First, intellectual honesty: it is not possible to teach the Enlightenment without teaching its racial archive. Second, political reckoning: the contemporary debate over reparations, the return of looted artefacts (the Benin bronzes, the Parthenon marbles), and the structural debts of the Atlantic economy is the unfinished political work that modernity's colonial roots imposed on us. Third — and this is Dussel's distinctive move — the response is not less modernity but a "trans-modernity", a co-realisation of modernity and its alterity (p. 76). The way out of the contradiction is not nostalgic anti-modernism nor postmodern relativism but a deeper modernity that finally takes seriously the universal claims it once made only for Europeans.

A counter-argument might insist that colonial cruelty was contingent; one could have had modernity without slavery; Mesopotamia, China and the Islamic world produced earlier scientific and bureaucratic modernities without trans-Atlantic slavery. This is partly true, but it concedes the point: the modernity that actually exists in 2026 — capitalist, racially stratified, with a global division of labour mapped onto colonial geographies — is not the modernity-in-general but a specific configuration whose roots run through the Atlantic system. Black Lives Matter, recent decolonisation-of-curriculum debates, and the persistence of racialised wealth gaps in London, Paris and New York all show that those roots still bear fruit.

So yes — modernity is undermined by its colonial roots, in the sense that it is internally contradicted, performatively self-betraying, and historically unable to deliver its universalism without exclusions. It is not for that reason worthless. But it cannot any longer be defended in the form it has handed itself down to us.

### 2021 (Q3): Does modernity have its roots in colonialism? If so, what are the implications for how we think of modernity?

This question is two questions. The first is descriptive: does modernity have colonial roots? The second is conceptual: what follows for how we use the word "modernity"? I will answer "yes" to the first and outline three implications for the second.

On the descriptive question, the case is now well established across the readings. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* dates the birth of modernity to 1492 rather than to the Reformation or the French Revolution. "Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (p. 65). The European subject that we recognise as modern — confident, rational, world-historical — only becomes thinkable once Europe can pose itself against a conquered other. Cortés in Mexico is closer to the origin of modern subjectivity than Descartes in his stove. The conquest of America, not the *cogito*, is the founding event.

Hall's "West and the Rest" essay supports the same conclusion through discourse analysis: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (*Formations of Modernity*, p. 278). The West is not a primary fact about which we then ask how it relates to the Rest. It is a relational fact constituted by that comparison. The category "modern" inherits the structure.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* completes the picture with economic history: Portuguese expansion into West Africa from the 1430s, then the trans-Atlantic slave trade, supplied the gold, sugar, tobacco and cotton that financed Europe's long boom. Marx, whom the lecture quotes as a partial exception within classical sociology, recognised this: "It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, p. 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire are not autonomous European achievements; they are nodes in an Atlantic system.

So yes, modernity has colonial roots — philosophically (Dussel), discursively (Hall) and economically (French, Marx).

What follows? Three implications.

First, the periodisation of modernity has to change. The standard textbook places modernity's origin at the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment, with the Industrial Revolution as its consolidation. On the colonial reading, the origin is 1492 — the conquest of Granada, Columbus's voyage, and the Iberian conquest of the New World. Spain and Portugal — discounted by both Hegel and Habermas (Dussel, p. 71) — are restored to the centre of the story. The Lutheran Reformation and the French Revolution are downstream consequences of an earlier world-historical reorganisation rather than its founding moments. The Industrial Revolution is the late phase of a long Atlantic economy that begins with sugar in São Tomé and ends with steam in Manchester.

Second, the canon has to change. If Kant's racial remarks — "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59) — are part of the architecture of Enlightenment, not stray asides, then teaching the Enlightenment without teaching Kant's race theory is teaching a falsified version. The same applies to Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). And it applies to the absent voices: Du Bois, C.L.R. James, Fanon, Aimé Césaire, Dussel himself. They are not optional addenda; they are part of the canon of modern thought because they are part of the story modernity has to tell about itself.

Third, the politics has to change. Dussel's proposal of "trans-modernity" — the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (p. 76) — points to a politics that retains modernity's rational kernel (rights, science, democratic accountability) but refuses its irrational myth (the civilising mission, the developmental ladder, the racialised hierarchy of value). In practical terms this means treating reparations, the repatriation of looted artefacts, decolonising university curricula, and global redistribution as the unfinished political work of modernity itself, not as anti-modern reaction.

A counter-position would say: yes, the colonial story is real, but it is descriptively interesting rather than normatively destabilising. We can keep using "modernity" as a value-neutral term and address colonial wrongs as a separate political matter. The trouble with this is that "modernity" was never value-neutral. As Hall shows, it has always been an evaluative grid — "western = developed = good = desirable; non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (p. 277). To keep using the word as if it were neutral is to keep underwriting the discourse. Better to name what the word is doing and decide whether we want to keep doing it.

So yes — modernity has colonial roots, and the implication is that periodisation, canon and politics all have to be redrawn so that the modernity we choose to defend is not the one Hegel and Habermas inherited but the one Dussel, Hall, Du Bois and Marx pushed against.

### 2024 (Q9): How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to the work of two theorists.

The question asks how European modernity benefited from unequal race relations — note "benefited", not "involved" or "depended on", though both are also true. I will answer with reference to Karl Marx and Enrique Dussel. Marx supplies the economic mechanism by which racial inequality fed into the wealth of modern Europe; Dussel supplies the philosophical mechanism by which European modernity legitimated racial inequality to itself. Together they show that the benefit was both material and ideological, and that the two reinforced each other.

Marx is the right place to start because, despite his Eurocentric framings of the proletariat as the agent of history, he was unusually clear about the slave-trade foundation of industrial capitalism. The lecture cites the canonical line: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). What is at stake here is not a moralising aside but an analytical claim about the structure of value-creation. Modern industry needs raw materials in bulk at low prices; that requirement was met by plantation slavery in the Caribbean and the American South, where racial categorisation legitimised the coercion of enslaved Africans to work without wages. The cheapness of slave-grown cotton allowed Manchester mill-owners to undercut Indian and Chinese textile producers, ruining their industries and redirecting demand to Britain. The cheapness of slave-grown sugar created the mass European consumer market — coffee with sugar, tea with sugar — that sustained the early modern world economy. The cheapness of slave-grown tobacco enriched Virginia planters who funded the American revolution. Each of these chains converts unequal race relations into modern industrial wealth.

Du Bois, cited in the lecture, generalises Marx's point: "The new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970, pp. 107–108). The European working class was, on Du Bois's reading, "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" — flattered by appeals to its racial superiority. Modern Europe benefited not only as a continent but as a racialised bloc that could be sold the story of its own elevation. The British working-class gain in real wages between 1750 and 1850 cannot be separated from the racialised redistribution of value out of the Atlantic system.

Dussel completes the picture by showing how this economic benefit was philosophically legitimated. His *Eurocentrism and Modernity* argues that modern European philosophy delivered a justifying myth for racial inequality at exactly the moment it was being economically institutionalised. Kant — the philosopher of Enlightenment — supplies the racial hierarchy: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, p. 59). Hegel — the philosopher of Spirit — writes Africa out of history: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). And, crucially, Hegel gives empire its philosophical charter: "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73). The slave colony is not just a piece of profitable real estate; it is a stage in the self-realisation of Reason. That is a powerful legitimation. It tells the European subject that the unequal race relation she profits from is also a service to the universal.

Dussel's "myth of modernity" generalises this into a seven-step structure: European superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault; modernity is innocent and emancipatory (Dussel, p. 75). The benefit, on this account, is double. Materially, Europe gets cheap labour, cheap raw materials and captive markets. Ideologically, Europe gets to think of itself as the bearer of universal reason while doing so. The combination is uniquely powerful because each side reinforces the other: the wealth funds the universities that produce the philosophy, and the philosophy legitimises the trade that produces the wealth.

Putting Marx and Dussel together yields a sharper account than either alone. Marx without Dussel risks reading slavery as an unfortunate historical accident attached to a structurally distinct capitalism. Dussel without Marx risks reading the myth of modernity as a purely cultural construct floating free of its economic stakes. Together, they show that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in a tightly integrated way: race made the labour cheap, the trade legal, and the conscience clean. Black Lives Matter, the contemporary reparations debate and the campaign to return the Benin bronzes are not random political flare-ups but the long-deferred bill for a modernity that bought itself with the bodies and goods of racialised peoples. To understand modern Europe's wealth is to understand the racial inequality that produced it. Marx tells us how. Dussel tells us why no one in Europe felt obliged to notice.

### M1: How convincing do you find Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity?

Enrique Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993) compresses an enormous theoretical claim into seven numbered steps. I find the structure convincing on three of its standard tests — fit with canonical texts, portability across cases, and explanatory yield — but vulnerable on a fourth: historical mechanism. I'll work through each step, score it, and reach a verdict.

The seven steps, as Dussel sets them out on p. 75. (1) Modern European civilisation understands itself as most developed and superior. (2) This superiority obliges Europe, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the more primitive. (3) The path of development is the one Europe took. (4) Where the barbarian resists, modernity uses violence "in the last instance". (5) The violence is ritualised as sacrifice — victims participate in their redemption. (6) The barbarian is guilty for resisting; modernity is innocent and emancipatory. (7) The sufferings imposed on the "immature" are inevitable.

Convincing on textual fit. Step 1 is Hegel: "Europe is the absolute end of Universal History" (Hegel, *Lectures*, p. 243, cited in Dussel, p. 69). Step 2 is Kant's *What is Enlightenment?*: humanity should leave "guilty immaturity" behind (Dussel, p. 68). Step 3 is the developmental schema embedded across Marx, Weber and Durkheim. Step 6 is Kant again: "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, p. 68) — the African or American "subject in guilty immaturity" is at fault. Step 4 is Hegel's *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347: the people that bears Spirit has "an absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (cited in Dussel, p. 73). The myth is not imposed on the canon; it is read out of it. That is a convincing piece of philological work.

Convincing on portability. The seven steps map cleanly onto cases separated by four centuries. Cortés in Mexico (1519–1521): the Aztec is barbarian (step 1), Christianity obliges conversion (step 2), the Spanish way is the universal way (step 3), Tenochtitlán's resistance authorises destruction (step 4), the massacre redeems pagan souls (step 5), the Aztec is guilty of human sacrifice (step 6), the thirty million indigenous dead from disease and forced labour are unfortunate but necessary (step 7). Belgian Congo (1885–1908): Leopold's rubber regime as humanitarian intervention against the Arab slave trade, ten million dead, hands and feet severed as "ritual" enforcement, the Congolese coded as guilty primitives. Iraq 2003: shock-and-awe as the price of democracy, half a million Iraqi dead, Saddam coded as resisting an inevitable freedom. The fit is uncanny. A theoretical structure that captures three radically different cases is doing real explanatory work.

Convincing on explanatory yield. The myth explains why critics of modernity often misfire. Postmodernists (Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo) discard the rational kernel and so cannot defend the universal claims the colonised need to articulate their innocence (Dussel, p. 75). Habermasian defenders refuse to see the irrational myth and so reproduce the Eurocentric occlusion. Dussel's two-layer reading — rational concept of emancipation affirmed, irrational myth negated (p. 66) — explains why reasonable people disagree about modernity by tracking which layer they attend to. Few other postcolonial frameworks deliver that kind of meta-explanation.

Less convincing on historical mechanism. This is where I would push back. The seven steps are a logical structure, not a causal story. Dussel does not show, in this short essay, *how* the myth was produced — what discursive practices, what institutional pressures, what economic interests assembled it. Nor does he show how it travelled from sixteenth-century Spain to nineteenth-century Belgium to twenty-first-century Washington. The structure is portable, but Dussel does not theorise the conduits. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* — Lancashire cotton mills, the Royal African Company, the Bank of England's slave-cargo balance sheets — supplies some of the missing mechanism. Hall's discourse analysis in *Formations of Modernity* supplies more (the West as "system of representation" with four functions, p. 277). Dussel's seven steps need this empirical infrastructure to do their work.

Less convincing on internal differentiation. The myth lumps Spanish *encomienda*, Dutch joint-stock plantations, the British Raj, French *mission civilisatrice* and US "freedom agenda" into one structure. The family resemblance is real; the differences also matter. The Mughal Empire's eighteenth-century encounter with the British is not the same as the Aztec encounter with the Spanish; Indian railways were built with extracted Indian capital, not Spanish silver. A more granular account would distinguish phases.

Less convincing on the redemptive register. Dussel's vocabulary — innocent victim, redemptive sacrifice, guilt — is theological (he was a liberation theologian) and may grate on secular readers. The analytical content survives translation, but the rhetoric is sectarian.

My verdict: convincing as a diagnostic, less convincing as a complete theory. The seven steps give a student a portable instrument for reading any rhetoric of civilising mission — IMF structural adjustment, the Sustainable Development Goals, "the international community", the British Museum's defence of holding the Benin bronzes — and finding the same logic at work. That is more than most short philosophical essays deliver. The myth needs French's economic history and Hall's discourse theory to fill in mechanism, but as a diagnostic it has aged well. Highly convincing on what it sets out to do; under-developed on what it sets aside.

### M2: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry." Discuss with reference to Marx.

Marx writes, in the passage the lecture quotes: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). The sentence is the most consequential admission in classical sociology that capitalist modernity is constitutively colonial. I want to take Marx at his word, trace the causal chain he sets out, and then assess where Marx's own framework remains Eurocentric despite the admission.

The chain runs in four moves: slavery → colonial value → world trade → large-scale industry. Read it forward. Enslaved Africans, transported across the Atlantic in twelve million bodies between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries (with perhaps two million dying on the Middle Passage), produced sugar, tobacco and cotton on plantations from São Tomé to Saint-Domingue to Mississippi. That cheapness gave the colonies their value: a Caribbean island like Saint-Domingue was, by the 1780s, more lucrative to France than all of metropolitan French agriculture combined. That value generated world trade: Liverpool and Bristol expanded as slave-trading ports; Amsterdam, Lisbon and Nantes financed the cargoes; Lloyd's of London grew up to insure them. That trade created the pre-condition of large-scale industry: the Lancashire cotton mills processed slave-grown American cotton on machines funded by Atlantic profits, sold cloth into colonial markets opened by colonial conquest, and undercut the older Indian and Chinese textile producers whose collapse Marx documented in his journalism. The Industrial Revolution, on Marx's account, is unintelligible without the Atlantic system that fed it.

What Marx delivers, then, is structural integration. The slavery–cotton chain is not a moralising aside; it is an analytical claim about the relations of production. Modern industrial capitalism requires cheap raw materials at scale; the scale and cheapness are supplied by racialised plantation labour. The reordering matters: the Eurocentric story places European industrial dynamism at the start and treats slavery as a regrettable side-chapter. Marx's ordering puts slavery first. Manchester is unintelligible without Mississippi; Lancashire mills are unintelligible without the Bight of Biafra.

The implication for class analysis follows. If slave-grown cotton funded the wages and consumer goods of the British industrial proletariat, then the European working class is partly a beneficiary of slavery, not just a victim of capitalism. Du Bois made this explicit: "the European and white American working class was practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970, pp. 107–108, cited in the lecture). Marx's quoted sentence sets up Du Bois's elaboration. The class analysis becomes a global racial division of labour, not just a national capital–labour division.

So far, so anti-Eurocentric. But three things temper the picture.

First, Marx's broader theory remains developmentalist. His sequence of historical stages — primitive communism, slave society, feudalism, capitalism, socialism — treats the European trajectory as the universal sequence. India, China, the Ottoman lands and pre-conquest Africa are slotted into "the Asiatic mode of production" or other residual boxes. Marx's journalism on British India sometimes welcomed colonial railways (Indian railways, in his 1853 articles) for breaking what he called the "static" Asiatic forms and pushing them onto the universal road. The cotton sentence does not undo this. Marx is anti-Eurocentric in his account of how capitalism functions, but Eurocentric in his account of how history unfolds.

Second, Marx does not theorise race. He names slavery but does not develop an account of racial hierarchy as an internal feature of capitalism. Why was the plantation labour racially marked? Why did African slavery in particular consolidate when other coercive arrangements were also available? Why does racial division persist in the wage-labour markets of twentieth- and twenty-first-century London, Paris and Detroit? Marx's tools — relations of production, accumulation, class struggle — are insufficient. Cedric Robinson's *Black Marxism* and Stuart Hall's account of race as "the modality through which class is lived" do the work Marx left undone.

Third, Marx does not theorise colonial agency. The agency of the colonised — the Haitian Revolution that overthrew slavery in Saint-Domingue in 1804, the anti-colonial movements of the twentieth century — is not theorised in the quoted sentence. The colonies appear as supply zones for European industry, not as political subjects. Du Bois, Fanon, James and the dependency theorists fill in what Marx leaves empty.

What survives, then, is a serious critique of Eurocentric sociology embedded in a still-Eurocentric historical schema. The lecture is right to call Marx "a possible exception". Three lessons follow. Methodologically, sociology that takes modernity seriously cannot stop at European borders; the world-system is the unit of analysis. Pedagogically, teaching Marx without the cotton-slavery sentence produces a clean European Marx whose insight is invisible; teaching the sentence alongside the abstract analysis recovers a more honest Marx. Politically, contemporary debates about reparations, sovereign debt and the unequal exchange between Global North and Global South are continuous with class struggle in the metropole, not separate from it. The cotton sentence is the moment classical sociology gestures toward what it could have been if the colonial constitution of modernity had been its founding question.

### M3: Eurocentrism in classical sociology: blind spot or constitutive premise?

A blind spot is a defect: something missed that, once seen, can be corrected. A constitutive premise is a foundation: something built into the structure that cannot be removed without rebuilding the whole. The question is which describes the relationship between classical sociology and Eurocentrism. My answer is that the disjunction is sharper than it looks. For Durkheim and most of Weber, Eurocentrism is constitutive: it organises the founding questions. For Marx and parts of late Weber, it is closer to a blind spot — incompletely seen but not architectural. The verdict matters because the two diagnoses imply different remedies.

The case for constitutive premise begins with Durkheim. His distinction between mechanical solidarity (homogeneous, repressive law, primitive) and organic solidarity (differentiated, restitutive law, modern), set out in *The Division of Labour in Society*, is the foundation of the discipline. The schema is unimaginable without the assumption that European societies have moved from one to the other and that non-European societies remain in the first phase. The lecturer puts the colonial implication plainly: "the shift to modernity as going from Mechanical to Organic Societies… suggests that other societies around the world are still 'pre-modern' / behind / backward". Africa, indigenous America and Aboriginal Australia become living museums of European prehistory. Remove the developmental schema and there is no Durkheimian sociology. The Eurocentrism is not a blind spot Durkheim might have noticed; it is the architecture of the question.

Weber is the harder case. On the constitutive side: the central question of *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* — why did rational capitalism develop in northwestern Europe and not elsewhere? — already presupposes that the European outcome is the standard. The comparative works on China, India and Ancient Judaism are studies of what those civilisations failed to produce. The rationalisation-disenchantment narrative makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Dussel calls this "the fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 67–68). Weber's whole comparative apparatus is built on this premise.

But Weber is also the most resourceful of the three. He took non-European religious and economic traditions seriously enough to learn them. He registered the historical contingency of Western rationalisation rather than treating it as inevitable. He warned that the "iron cage" of bureaucratic rationality might be a curse rather than a triumph. The Eurocentrism is constitutive in the framing but not unalloyed in the substance.

Marx is the strongest case for blind spot. The cotton-slavery sentence — "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203) — places the colonial relation at the heart of modern industry. The Lancashire mills are unintelligible without Mississippi slavery. This is structurally anti-Eurocentric. But Marx's broader stage theory — primitive communism, slave society, feudalism, capitalism, socialism — reads as a developmental ladder with European destinations. His journalism on the Mughal Empire and Indian railways sometimes welcomed British colonialism for breaking what he called "static" Asiatic forms. Marx saw the colonial relation when he looked directly at cotton; he reverted to a Eurocentric schema when he looked at history as a whole. Blind spot rather than constitutive premise — but a blind spot the size of a continent.

So the answer disaggregates. Durkheim: constitutive. Weber: constitutive in framing, partially correctable in practice. Marx: a structural insight wrapped in a Eurocentric schema, closer to blind spot. The general claim that "classical sociology is Eurocentric" hides these differences and so blunts the remedies.

The remedies depend on the diagnosis. If Eurocentrism is constitutive, the discipline must rebuild. Dussel's redating of modernity to 1492, his recovery of Iberian sources (Las Casas, Vitoria, Guamán Poma), Howard French's restoration of African gold and slave-trade institutions in *Born in Blackness* — these are not additions to the syllabus but reorganisations of the founding question. The unit of analysis becomes the Atlantic system rather than the European nation-state. If Eurocentrism is a blind spot, the discipline can be extended: keep the analytic categories (alienation, rationalisation, anomie, division of labour) and supplement them with the missing case studies. Add Du Bois, Fanon, Cedric Robinson, Gurminder Bhambra and the dependency theorists to the canon. Teach the Atlantic slave trade alongside the Lancashire mills. Read the *Communist Manifesto* alongside *The Souls of Black Folk*.

A counter-position says the binary is forced: every discipline has its founding assumptions and Eurocentrism is just sociology's. The reply is Hall's: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 278). The founding assumption is not arbitrary; it is a discursive operation with effects. Stuart Hall's four functions of the West-Rest discourse — classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating (p. 277) — describe what sociology textbooks have done. The colonial attitude is not external to the discipline; it is part of how the discipline learned to talk about modernity.

My verdict: the disjunction is too clean for any one founder. Durkheim's Eurocentrism is constitutive; Weber's is constitutive but self-aware; Marx's is a blind spot wrapped around a structural insight. A decolonised sociology has to do different work on each. That is harder than the question's binary suggests — and more honest than picking a side.

### M4: How does the concept of the colonial attitude help us understand contemporary university league tables and the British Museum?

The colonial attitude, as the lecture uses the term, is the habit of bifurcated thinking that ranks the world into a developed European/Western core and a backward non-European periphery, and that takes the core's standards as the universal measure. The colonies are gone; the attitude continues to organise institutions that present themselves as neutral or scholarly. Two test cases — the QS/Times Higher Education league tables and the British Museum's continued holding of the Benin bronzes — show how the concept does explanatory work that other vocabularies cannot.

Take the league tables first. QS, THE and the Shanghai ARWU rank the world's universities every year. The published top ten has been almost stable for two decades: Harvard, MIT, Oxford, Cambridge, Stanford, Caltech, ETH Zurich, Imperial, Chicago, Princeton. Universities in Africa, South Asia and Latin America — including some, like Al-Azhar (970 CE) or the University of Bologna's older Indian and Arab counterparts, that pre-date much of the Western canon — appear far below. The colonial attitude helps us see why. First, the criteria are loaded: heavy weighting on English-language publications, citation counts in journals based in the US and UK, research funding from Anglo-American sources, and reputation surveys whose respondents are drawn from the same networks. Second, the criteria are presented as neutral metrics. The rankings claim to measure "excellence" rather than to enforce a geography. Third, the effects are global: students in Lagos, Mumbai, Cairo and São Paulo grow up knowing that "the best" universities are in the same five countries that ran the empires of the nineteenth century.

Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse names exactly this combination. The discourse "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" through four operations: classification (which universities are world-class?), condensation (a top-ten image), comparison (where does my university rank?), evaluation (Western = good) (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277, p. 280). The rankings perform each operation in metric form. Without the colonial-attitude framing, one might say the rankings are imperfect; with it, one sees that the imperfection is structured — it reproduces the geography of empire under the appearance of methodology.

Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 75) sharpens the diagnosis. Step 1: the West is most developed (here: Harvard and Oxford as the standard). Step 3: the path of development is the path the West took (publish in English, hire on Anglo-American norms, structure the PhD on the US-German model). Step 6: lower-ranked universities are guilty of their own underdevelopment ("weak research culture", "poor governance", "insufficient internationalisation"). The vocabulary of "world-class" universities, "research-intensive" institutions and "globally competitive" departments is the technical face of Dussel's civilising imperative.

The British Museum is a sharper case because the colonial attitude has to be defended against an articulate counter-demand for repatriation. The Benin bronzes, looted by British troops in 1897 during the punitive expedition against the Kingdom of Benin, sit in Bloomsbury alongside the Parthenon marbles, the Rosetta Stone and the Maqdala treasures from Ethiopia. The Museum's defence is well-rehearsed: the objects are preserved better in London; they are visible to more visitors; they belong to a "universal museum" that holds the heritage of humankind. The colonial attitude shows why these arguments are not technical claims but moves within a structure.

Hall's discourse of the West-Rest grounds the assumption that London is a more appropriate custodian than Benin City. Dussel's seven-step myth supplies the legitimating story: the West is most developed (step 1), this obliges it to preserve the heritage of less developed peoples (step 2), the standard of preservation is the British one (step 3), demands for return are unreasonable (step 6, the demander is guilty of misunderstanding universal heritage), and the continuing holding is regrettable but necessary (step 7). Each argument the Museum makes can be located on the structure. The "universal museum" claim is particularly revealing: a museum is "universal" when its collection is global, but the universal viewing position is reserved for London — the visitors who can see "all of humanity" are those who can travel to Bloomsbury.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* supplies the empirical depth. The Benin bronzes were made by the Edo kingdom over five centuries, drawing on copper and brass acquired through trans-Saharan and Atlantic trade networks. The bronzes are themselves evidence of African modernity in the centuries when Hegel was claiming that "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, cited in Dussel, p. 70). The 1897 looting was the colonial attitude armed with Maxim guns; the continued holding is the colonial attitude armed with vitrines.

Why does the concept do work that other vocabularies miss? Three reasons. First, it links rankings and museums and many other practices to a single structure rather than treating them as unrelated. Second, it explains why apparently technical defences (metrics, preservation) replicate hierarchies; the technique is the inheritance. Third, it points to remedies — co-curation, repatriation, alternative ranking systems, decolonising the curriculum — that are continuous with the project Dussel calls trans-modernity (p. 76): retaining the rational goods (scholarship, public access) while extending them past the colonial boundary. The colonial attitude is not an old habit waiting to die. It is alive in spreadsheets and vitrines, and naming it is the first step to undoing it.

### M5: Critically assess Howard French's argument in *Born in Blackness*.

Howard W. French's *Born in Blackness: Africa, Africans, and the Making of the Modern World, 1471 to the Second World War* (2021) argues a single thesis with great empirical density: Africa, not Europe and not the Americas, is the constitutive ground of the modern world. I want to set out the argument in its three strongest claims, evaluate its evidence, and identify two limitations that a sociologist (rather than a journalist-historian) needs to register.

French's first claim is that Portuguese expansion down the West African coast from the 1430s onwards was the genuine origin event of European modernity. The standard textbook foregrounds 1492 and Columbus, but Columbus is downstream: he was looking for a sea route to Asian commodities because the trans-Saharan gold trade was running through Mali under Mansa Musa (whose 1324 Mecca pilgrimage destabilised Mediterranean bullion markets) and the Portuguese were already buying that gold at coastal forts like Elmina (1482). African gold, not American silver, funded the first phase of European mercantilism and the maritime technology that made the Atlantic crossing possible. French's reordering matters: it relocates modernity's birth from the Caribbean to the Gulf of Guinea, and it makes the West African polities — Mali, Songhai, Kongo, Benin, Asante — central rather than incidental.

The second claim is that the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the plantation economies were constitutive of modern capitalism, not external to it. Twelve million Africans were transported between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries; perhaps two million died on the Middle Passage. The slave-grown sugar of São Tomé and the Caribbean, the slave-grown tobacco of Virginia, and the slave-grown cotton of the American South generated the consumer markets, financial instruments and industrial raw materials of the modern Atlantic economy. French traces specific institutions back to slave-trade origins: Liverpool's banks to slave-trading houses, Lloyd's insurance market to slave-cargo insurance, the Bank of England's early balance sheet to Royal African Company dividends, the Lancashire cotton mills to American plantations. This is the empirical body for Marx's compressed claim: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203).

The third claim — and the most distinctive — is African agency. Kongo, Benin, Dahomey, Asante and Songhai were sophisticated polities whose internal politics, military capacities and commercial ambitions shaped what Europeans could and could not do on the coast. The trans-Atlantic slave trade was not a one-sided European action on a passive continent; it was a negotiated and contested system in which African elites participated, profited and sometimes resisted. French is careful — this is not exoneration of European responsibility; it is restoration of African historical agency against the Hegelian erasure ("Africa… does not have history as such", cited in Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 70).

How convincing is the argument?

On evidence, very. French is a long-time *New York Times* correspondent in Africa and the Caribbean; he reads Portuguese, French and Spanish archives; he visits Elmina, Cape Coast, São Tomé, Brazilian and Caribbean sites. The empirical density makes it difficult to dismiss the argument as polemical. Where Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* operates at the level of philosophical structure, French operates at the level of ports, ships, account books and dynasties. The two together do work neither does alone.

On the historiographical move, convincing with caveats. French is part of a larger reorientation — alongside Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972), Eric Williams's *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), Joseph Inikori, John Thornton, and the recent "new history of capitalism" school in the US (Sven Beckert, Edward Baptist, Walter Johnson). French synthesises a generation of African and African-diasporic scholarship for a popular audience. The synthesis is well done but not entirely original; specialists were saying much of this already. What French adds is reach and narrative force.

Two limitations matter for a sociologist.

First, French is empirically rich but theoretically thin. He shows that Africa was constitutive of modernity; he does not theorise the structure of European self-understanding that erased that constitution. There is no "myth of modernity" framework in French. Dussel's seven steps (Dussel, p. 75), Hall's analysis of "the West and the Rest" as a discourse with four functions (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277), and Cedric Robinson's "racial capitalism" all do theoretical work that French's narrative gestures at without performing. A sociologist needs the theoretical scaffolding to do explanatory work; French supplies the materials.

Second, French's emphasis on African agency, while necessary, sits awkwardly with his emphasis on the violence of the trade. The same African elites who participated were also victims of European-imposed market structures; the Kongo kingdom's involvement in the slave trade was partly defensive against neighbours who were also drawn into it. French handles this with care but the tension is real, and critics (Walter Johnson among them) have pressed it. Restoring agency without minimising coercion is a difficult balance.

My verdict: *Born in Blackness* is an important book that does decisively what synthesising works can do — make a specialist consensus available to a general readership. It belongs on any L2 reading list. Its argument is more convincing taken alongside Dussel and Hall than read in isolation, because what French shows empirically the theorists explain structurally. Together they make the case that modernity, the West and capitalism are unintelligible without the African continent that helped make them. Read French for the bodies, the ports and the ships; read Dussel and Hall for why Europe forgot.

### M6: What does Hall mean by "the West and the Rest", and why does he treat it as a discourse of power rather than a geographical description?

Two questions, one analytic. What Hall means by "the West and the Rest" cannot be detached from why he treats it as a discourse. A geographical description records what is there; a discourse produces what counts as there. Hall's argument is that "the West" was never primarily a region — it is a position from which the world has been classified, ranked and acted upon, and the position has effects whether or not the geography supports it.

What Hall means. "The West", in *Formations of Modernity*, is not Europe-plus-its-settler-extensions on a map. It is "a system of representation" with four functions (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). First, it *classifies* societies into western and non-western — "a tool to think with" that sorts the world into two boxes. Second, it *condenses* a composite image: the West is urban, industrial, secular, democratic, individualist, scientific; the Rest is rural, agrarian, religious, despotic, collectivist, traditional. Third, it *provides a standard of comparison* so that other societies are read as "close to" or "far from" the West, "catching up" or "falling behind". Fourth, it *provides criteria of evaluation*: "western = developed = good = desirable" against "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (p. 277). The four functions interlock. The classification supplies the binary; the condensation gives it content; the comparison makes it operational; the evaluation makes it ideological.

The Saussurean argument behind the four functions. Hall borrows from Saussure on linguistic difference: "night" and "day" do not name primary things; they signify by their difference from each other. By analogy, "the West" does not name a primary set of properties; it signifies by its difference from "the Rest". "The so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest), very different in their histories, ecologies, patterns of development and cultures from the European model" (p. 278). The constitutive relation is at the heart of what Hall means.

Why is this a discourse rather than a geographical description? Three reasons mark the difference.

First, Hall's category does not match the geography. The "West" includes the United States, Australia and New Zealand — none of which is European in any straightforward sense. It excludes Latin America, despite being culturally and religiously closer to Iberian Europe than (say) the eastern fringes of "the West" in Poland or Greece. It excludes Russia, whose imperial Tsars Europeanised their court at St Petersburg in self-conscious imitation. And it lumps "the Rest" — the Mughal Empire, the Ottoman Empire, the Qing, the African polities — into a single residual category despite their being more different from each other than from "the West". A descriptive geography would not produce this map. A discourse can.

Second, the discourse is productive in the Foucauldian sense. "The idea of 'the West'… had real effects: it enabled people to know or speak of certain things in certain ways. It produced knowledge. It became both the organising factor in a system of global power relations and the organising concept or term in a whole way of thinking and speaking" (p. 278). The categorisation does not just describe a world; it shapes what counts as a question worth asking, a society worth studying, a problem worth solving. The colonial attitude that Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" diagnoses (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 75) is one product of the discourse; the IMF's developmental conditionalities are another; the British Museum's defence of holding the Benin bronzes is a third.

Third, the discourse simplifies in ways that a description should not. Hall is explicit: "It draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (p. 280). Simplification is what the discourse does: it represents the internally differentiated West (Germanic, Latin, Iberian, Nordic, Mediterranean) as one bloc and the more differentiated Rest as another. A descriptive geography would resist that simplification. A discourse of power requires it: without simplification, the binary cannot do its sorting work.

To call this a discourse of power, three further things follow. The discourse has internal others as well as external ones: Jews, Eastern Europeans and women within the West are positioned analogously to the colonised outside (p. 280). The discourse organises material institutions — universities, museums, news geographies, development banks — that operationalise the binary in concrete decisions. And the discourse is constitutive of modern subjectivity itself, in Dussel's complementary register: "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, p. 65).

Why does Hall insist on this framing? Because treating "the West" as a geographical description would naturalise it — make it look like a fact about the world rather than an operation on the world. Treating it as a discourse of power denaturalises it: it makes visible the work the category does, the simplifications it requires, the institutions it sustains. The framing is the politics. Once you can see the discourse as a discourse, you can analyse a Lancashire cotton mill, the IMF, the British Museum, Iraq 2003 or a QS ranking as performances of the same structure. That is what Hall's "West and the Rest" is for: not a description but a diagnostic.

### M7: Compare Kant's account of non-European peoples with Hegel's.

Kant and Hegel are the two pillars Dussel uses, in *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, to show that the colonial relation is built into the architecture of canonical modern philosophy. They make the same kind of move but through different mechanisms. Comparing them precisely matters because the differences determine which forms of recuperation are available today.

Kant's mechanism is universal-in-form, racially-restrictive-in-content. The famous Enlightenment slogan reads as inclusive: "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (Kant, *What is Enlightenment?*, cited in Dussel, p. 68). The word is "humanity". Any reader could be flattered into thinking themselves included. But Kant's empirical anthropology, set out in *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, narrows the universal sharply: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, p. 59). The universal is qualified by a racial hierarchy that excludes most of the species from the rational vocation it claims to extend to everyone.

Dussel's interrogation gets the contradiction exactly. "An African in Africa or as a slave in the United States in the eighteenth century; an Indian in Mexico or a Latin American mestizo: Should all of these subjects be considered to reside in a state of guilty immaturity?" (Dussel, p. 68). The Kantian answer is yes — but the immaturity, in their case, is not curable by individual effort because the racial constitution prevents it. The "guilty immaturity" coding does double work: it blames the colonised for their condition (Dussel's step 6) while denying them the rational equipment to leave it.

Hegel's mechanism is different: hierarchical-in-form, with non-Europeans assigned positions in a developmental schema or excluded from the schema altogether. World History, in the *Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, is "the development of the consciousness that the Spirit has of its freedom" (cited in Dussel, p. 68), and the development is geographically directed: "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning" (Hegel, *Lectures*, p. 243, cited in Dussel, p. 69). Latin America is excluded as "land of the future": "as a land of the future Latin America has no interest for us, because the philosopher does not make prophesies" (cited in Dussel, p. 69). Africa is excluded more brutally: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again. It is not part of the historical world" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). And on the African himself: "Among negroes it is the case that consciousness has not attained even the intuition of any sort of objectivity… [The negro] is the man as beast" (p. 70).

The contrasts are sharp.

On argumentative structure: Kant smuggles racial exclusion into a universal frame; Hegel makes hierarchy the frame itself. Kant's reader can be misled by the universal language until she meets the *Observations*; Hegel's reader cannot be misled because the geography of Spirit is announced at the outset.

On the function of non-Europeans: Kant uses them as moral foils — examples of what humans look like before they have done the Enlightenment work. Hegel uses them as historical stages — the African is the dawn of Spirit, the Asian its childhood, the European its mature end. The function is similar (Europeans as standard) but the technique differs: Kant's empirical anthropology vs Hegel's metaphysical historicism.

On political implication: Kant's writing on race underwrites the slave economies of his lifetime — the Atlantic slave trade was running at peak volume while Kant taught in Königsberg. Hegel goes further: he supplies the philosophical charter for empire. "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73). The British in India, the French in Algeria, the Belgians in the Congo — each could find in this sentence the rationalisation that the colonised "have no rights" against the bearer of Spirit.

What does the comparison imply for reading them today? Kant is more deceptive but more recuperable. The universal claim can be turned against the racial qualification: one can argue, with Dussel's "trans-modernity" (p. 76), that Kant fails by his own standard and that the rational kernel survives the negation of the irrational myth. Hegel offers less to recuperate because the hierarchy is the architecture: removing the racial geography of World History leaves a different system, not a corrected one. A "Hegel against himself" reading is therefore more reconstructive than a "Kant against himself" reading.

But both readings are possible. Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (p. 75) supplies the structure: keep the rational concept of emancipation, expose the irrational myth, extend the universals to those the canonical author excluded. The point is not to discard the canon but to teach the Enlightenment with its racial archive — to read *What is Enlightenment?* alongside the *Observations*, the *Phenomenology* alongside the *Lectures*. The student who has done that reading is better equipped to recognise the colonial attitude in its contemporary forms — in IMF conditionalities, in the British Museum's vitrines, in the discourse of "rogue states" — than the student who learned a sanitised Enlightenment.

### M8: Multiple modernities: useful pluralism or empty inclusion?

The phrase "multiple modernities" is most associated with Shmuel Eisenstadt, who argued in the 2000 *Daedalus* special issue that modernity is not one project diffused from Europe but a plural family of self-consciously modern formations — Japanese, Indian, Chinese, Latin American, Iberian, Anglo-American, Russian — each with distinctive institutional and cultural patterns. The phrase has been adopted widely in postcolonial sociology because it appears to give the colonised conceptual room: their modernities are not deficient versions of the European original but parallel achievements. The question is whether the pluralism is useful or empty — whether it does theoretical work, or whether it merely papers over the asymmetries that Dussel, Hall and French analysed.

The case for useful pluralism is real. Eisenstadt's framework breaks the unilinearity of the standard story. It replaces the developmental ladder with a family of paths. It registers that Tokugawa-Meiji Japan, Mughal-then-British India, Soviet Russia and Brazilian modernity have institutional features that do not collapse into "the Western model". It refuses the colonial attitude in its strongest form: the claim that there is one road and the colonised are behind on it. Dussel's "fallacy of developmentalism" — "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 67–68) — is what the multiple-modernities framing is designed to escape.

The pluralism is also analytically productive. It draws attention to specifically non-Western modernising projects: the Mughal Empire's bureaucratic and fiscal sophistication in the seventeenth century (more centralised than any contemporary European state); Japan's Meiji-era institutional borrowing without religious conversion; Atatürk's secular Turkey; Nehru's developmental socialism; Singapore's post-1965 state capitalism. Each requires a vocabulary richer than "modernisation as Westernisation". Eisenstadt's framework supplies the vocabulary.

But the case for empty inclusion is also strong, and pressing it matters.

First, the pluralism can dissolve the colonial relation. If every society has its own modernity, then the asymmetric power relations that Howard French documents in *Born in Blackness* — the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the Lancashire mills running on slave-grown cotton, the City of London financing it all — can recede from view. "Multiple modernities" can become a celebration of variety that elides the fact that some modernities funded themselves by extracting from others. The Mughal Empire's wealth did not extract from Britain; Britain's industrial wealth did extract from India (deindustrialised by tariff and conquest after 1757), from West Africa (drained of twelve million enslaved people) and from the Caribbean (sugar plantations). A pluralism that misses this becomes ideology.

Second, the pluralism can be a Western gift. The category "multiple modernities" is itself articulated within Western academic sociology, evaluated by Western journals, ranked on Western criteria. Indian, Chinese or African scholars who want to claim "their" modernity have to do so in a vocabulary supplied by Princeton. The asymmetry of recognition reproduces the West-Rest discourse that Hall analysed: "the West" remains the position from which classification, comparison and evaluation happen, even when it is generously classifying other modernities as also valid (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277).

Third, the pluralism can be empty in 2026 specifically. The institutions that organise the global economy — the IMF, the World Bank, the WTO, the dollar-based payments system, the SWIFT network, the credit-rating agencies, the SDG indicator framework, the QS university rankings, the *Financial Times* most influential MBAs list — operate on a single template. Whatever theoretical pluralism exists in academic sociology, institutional reality is largely unilinear. "Multiple modernities" describes how scholars classify; "one modernity" describes how the IMF lends. The gap is wide.

How should one adjudicate? Three positions are defensible.

The strong-useful position. Multiple modernities is a regulative ideal worth defending. It does anti-Eurocentric work in the curriculum, in research design, in funding priorities, in how comparative sociology asks its questions. It deserves continuing investment.

The strong-empty position. Multiple modernities is liberal recognition without redistribution. It pluralises the cultural register while leaving the material structure (capital flows, debt regimes, military hierarchies) untouched. It is the academic version of the British Museum's "universal museum" defence: the keeper is generous, but the keeper is still the keeper.

The position I find most defensible. Multiple modernities is useful as a starting point and empty as a stopping point. It is the necessary first move against the unilinear narrative. But on its own it does not name the asymmetric relations between modernities — the colonial extraction, the racialised labour, the Atlantic foundations of European wealth — that French, Hall and Dussel together analyse. It needs to be supplemented by Dussel's trans-modernity (Dussel, p. 76), which insists not merely on plural recognition but on the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity, with redistribution following recognition.

Three contemporary cases test this. The reparations debate is what multiple-modernities cannot resolve on its own: recognising that Caribbean slavery built British modernity is not enough; the financial settlement is the modernity-internal question. The decolonising-the-curriculum movement is the same pattern: recognising plural modernities is necessary but insufficient; institutional reform of hiring, citation and assessment is the substantive work. The climate crisis is the third: the recognition that Indian and African modernities decarbonise differently from European ones is true but cheap; the redistribution of climate liability, by historical responsibility, is the live question.

So: useful pluralism, yes — but only if it knows it is unfinished. Empty inclusion if it congratulates itself. In 2026 the gap between recognised pluralism and redistributive politics is the real test, and "multiple modernities" passes only when it does not stop where its name does.

### M9: Does the climate crisis vindicate or refute Dussel's myth of modernity?

The climate crisis is the largest test currently underway of how modernity governs itself. If Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (*Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 75) describes a real structure, the climate crisis should map onto it. If the crisis instead shows modernity self-correcting through rational institutions, the myth thesis would be weakened. My argument is that the crisis vindicates Dussel's diagnosis more than it refutes it — but that the vindication is uncomfortable enough to push the framework somewhere Dussel did not take it.

Begin with the empirical structure of the crisis. Cumulative carbon emissions are massively unequal by historical responsibility. The United States and the European Union together account for roughly 47% of all CO₂ emitted since 1850; the United Kingdom alone, by historical share per capita, leads the world. India and most of Africa have contributed minimally. Yet the impacts — sea-level rise on the Bay of Bengal coast, Sahelian drought, Pacific island submergence, glacier loss in the Andes — fall disproportionately on the territories Dussel's framework calls the periphery. The pattern is colonial extraction continued by atmospheric means: the Global North has used the atmosphere as a free dump while industrialising, and the bill is being delivered to those who did not.

How does this map onto Dussel's seven steps?

Step 1 (Europe most developed): the global climate negotiations are conducted from positions of "developed" / "developing" status enshrined in the 1992 UN Framework Convention. The vocabulary is identical to the developmental fallacy Dussel critiques (p. 67–68).

Step 2 (obligation to civilise): "decarbonisation assistance" is offered by Northern donors to Southern recipients on Northern terms — technology transfers, climate finance conditionalities, carbon markets designed in London and Geneva. The civilising imperative now wears green clothes.

Step 3 (the path is Europe's path): the assumption that all economies should decarbonise on the same trajectory ignores that European industrialisation used cheap atmospheric space that is no longer available to later industrialisers. Indian coal expansion is criticised in vocabulary that elides the colonial extraction (Lancashire cotton mills, Indian railways built with extracted Indian capital) that funded Britain's earlier carbon-heavy growth.

Step 4 (violence in the last instance): not military violence so far, but structural — climate adaptation costs forced onto Bangladesh, Sahel, small island states; climate refugees pushed back at European and US borders; carbon-border-adjustment taxes that penalise late industrialisers. The 2003 Iraq war, often framed as an oil war, can be read as the prototype of climate violence to come.

Step 5 (sacrificial ritualisation): the Sustainable Development Goals frame Southern climate vulnerability as an opportunity for redemption through Northern investment. The 1.5°C target is conducted as a collective effort in which the South must "do its part" alongside the North that produced the crisis.

Step 6 (the victim is guilty): Indian and Chinese emissions are foregrounded in Northern climate discourse; Western per-capita emissions and historical cumulative responsibility recede. Sahelian drought is coded as "resource conflict" or "population pressure" — the African as guilty primitive who failed to manage his environment.

Step 7 (sufferings are inevitable): climate damages in the Global South are treated as facts to adapt to rather than debts to settle. The UN's Loss and Damage Fund, agreed at COP27 in 2022, was capitalised at a tiny fraction of estimated needs.

This is heavy vindication. The crisis is being managed inside Dussel's structure.

But two things push past Dussel.

First, the climate crisis is the first modernity-internal contradiction that threatens the Global North materially. European heatwaves, North American wildfires and Pacific atmospheric rivers are not symbolic damages. The climate crisis is the moment at which the colonial attitude's costs come home. Dussel did not anticipate this; his framework treats the violence as exported. The Northern realisation that some of the violence is now imported should, in principle, undo the myth's psychological hold. Whether it will is the live political question.

Second, the climate crisis cannot be solved inside Dussel's "trans-modernity" frame as he sketched it (p. 76). Trans-modernity is articulated as a co-realisation between center and periphery, with the periphery's "innocence" recognised and modernity's rational kernel preserved. But the climate crisis requires more than recognition: it requires redistribution at a scale equivalent to a Marshall Plan run in reverse. The institutional architecture for that does not exist. Reparations for cumulative carbon would dwarf any historical settlement. Dussel's seven-step diagnosis fits; his constructive proposal does not yet meet the scale of the problem.

So my answer: vindicates more than refutes, but vindication is incomplete praise. The climate crisis confirms that the myth of modernity structures contemporary global governance — the colonial attitude updated for the atmospheric era. It also shows that Dussel's framework needs a sequel: a politics of redistribution adequate to the modernity-internal scale of the crisis. The seven steps name the structure; the next decade will tell whether the structure can be unbuilt. Howard French's restoration of Africa to modernity (in *Born in Blackness*) and Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse (*Formations of Modernity*, p. 277) supply the historical and discursive equipment; what is still missing is the institutional equipment. Dussel's myth diagnosis holds. His remedy needs reinforcing. The climate crisis is the case that demands it.

### M10: Using the work of at least two authors, assess the case for reparations as a modernity-internal demand.

A reparations demand is usually framed as extra-modern: a moral claim against modernity for what it did to the colonised. I want to argue, using Marx and Dussel together (with Howard French in support), that reparations is better understood as a modernity-internal demand — a debt that modernity's own rational kernel makes payable. The argument is not that modernity should be repaired by something outside itself, but that modernity's universal claims (property, contract, equal moral worth, the rule of law) generate the obligation when extended to the people from whom they were originally withheld.

The case rests on three steps.

First, the economic foundation. Marx supplies it: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). The Lancashire cotton mills processed slave-grown American cotton; British financial institutions grew up to fund the trade; the early balance sheet of the Bank of England carried Royal African Company dividends. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* fills in the empirical body: twelve million Africans transported across the Atlantic, two million dying on the Middle Passage, Liverpool's banks and Lloyd's insurance market built on slave-cargo finance. The wealth that funded British modernity — the steam-powered Lancashire mills, the City of London, the railways including the Indian railways built with extracted Indian capital — was substantially generated by slave labour and colonial extraction. This is not contested by serious economic historians; the disputed questions are about magnitudes, not directions.

Second, the philosophical move. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* shows that modernity contains both a rational concept of emancipation and an irrational myth that legitimated genocidal violence (p. 66). The rational concept includes property rights, contractual obligation, restitution for wrongful taking, equal moral worth under law. These are the categories the standard British and US legal systems use to settle ordinary disputes between citizens: when one party takes another's labour or property without consent, the law requires restitution with appropriate adjustments for time. Apply these same categories to the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the colonial extractions. The taking was without consent (the enslaved person did not contract). The labour was unpaid. The receipts went to identifiable beneficiaries (the Royal African Company, the planters, the financial intermediaries, the British state that taxed them). The descendants of those beneficiaries are still identifiable (the institutions are mostly still operating). The categories of modern law generate an obligation to repay. The obligation has not been honoured because the categories were never extended to those modernity called barbarians — but the obligation exists inside modernity's own logic, not outside it.

This is what makes the demand modernity-internal. Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity (p. 75) explains why the obligation has been ignored: step 6 codes the victim as guilty (the African as guilty primitive), step 7 codes the costs as inevitable, the rational kernel covers the irrational myth. The reparations demand is a request to remove the myth and let the rational kernel do its work. It does not require importing new moral categories. It requires applying the existing ones consistently.

Third, the contemporary cases. Three are visible.

CARICOM, the Caribbean Community organisation, has issued a 10-point plan since 2013 demanding reparations from the European colonial powers for slavery and indigenous genocide. The demand is articulated in the vocabulary of international law, development finance and human rights — modernity's own vocabularies. CARICOM is not asking for charity; it is asking for restitution under existing legal categories.

The University of Glasgow's 2018 reparations programme — a £20 million commitment over twenty years, in partnership with the University of the West Indies, after a study identified that the university had received financial bequests from slave-owners worth perhaps £200 million in current values. Glasgow's settlement is small but precedent-setting: an institution acknowledging an existing debt and beginning to honour it.

The 2024 Dutch state apology and €200 million slavery legacy fund, the 2023 Royal Dutch apology, the 2020 Belgian Commission of inquiry on colonial past — these are partial, slow, contested, but they show the demand percolating through European political institutions. Black Lives Matter sharpened the case in 2020; the toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol made the point visually: the wealth that funded eighteenth-century Bristol is still extractable as a public liability.

Two objections need to be answered.

Objection one: temporal distance. The transactions are old; the descendants are far removed. The reply is that the institutional beneficiaries — universities, banks, insurance markets, museums, the British state — are continuous. The Bank of England, Lloyd's, the Church of England, the City of London Corporation, Oxford and Cambridge colleges are the same legal persons that held slave-trade balances. Continuity of institution is sufficient for legal continuity of obligation.

Objection two: collective guilt. Individual contemporary Britons did not enslave anyone. The reply is that reparations is not about individual guilt but about unjust enrichment. The British state collected slave-trade revenues, used them in fiscal terms, and compounded them; the institutions that benefited are still solvent. The demand is for redistribution from those institutions, not for personal contrition from their current employees.

So: reparations is modernity-internal because it uses modernity's own legal and moral categories, extended to those modernity originally excluded. Marx supplies the economic ground; Dussel supplies the philosophical structure; Howard French supplies the empirical case. The demand is not anti-modern reaction; it is what Dussel calls "trans-modernity" (p. 76): modernity finally honouring the universals it once articulated only for some. The British Museum, the IMF, Lancashire's banks and the City of London are the addressees. The bill is overdue.

### M11: Can sociology be decolonised without abandoning its classical canon?

The decolonising-the-curriculum movement has put this question to every department in the Russell Group and beyond. The strong version says no: the canonical founders — Marx, Weber, Durkheim — are so deeply Eurocentric that decolonisation requires displacing them. The weak version says yes: the founders can be read critically, supplemented and reframed, without abandonment. I want to argue for an intermediate position. Sociology can be decolonised without abandoning the canon, but only on three conditions: that the canon is read with its colonial archive, that the canon is supplemented with the voices it excluded, and that the discipline's founding question is rewritten.

Begin with the case that the canon must go. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic schema, as the lecture notes, places non-European societies into the "premodern, behind, backward" column by default. Weber's question — why did rational capitalism develop in northwestern Europe and not elsewhere? — already takes the European outcome as the standard. Both presuppose Dussel's "fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 67–68). The Eurocentrism is not a stain on the texts; it is the architecture of the questions. On this strong reading, no amount of supplementation can save them.

But the strong reading has three difficulties.

First, abandonment is empirically expensive. The analytic categories the founders built — alienation, anomie, rationalisation, division of labour, ideology, status, class — are genuinely useful for sociology of any geographical scope. They were derived from European cases but they describe relations that are not unique to Europe. Indian sociologists using "caste" alongside "class" and "status" do not need to import the European cases; they need the analytic vocabulary, which Marx, Weber and Durkheim refined. Abandonment would forfeit these tools without obvious replacement.

Second, the canon contains internal critics. Marx is the lecture's "possible exception": "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203) is one of the most consequential admissions in classical sociology that capitalist modernity is colonial. Du Bois — frequently excluded from the standard canon but increasingly included — develops this into a fully theorised racial capitalism. Reading these voices into the canon rather than out of it is the decolonising move.

Third, abandonment can be performative. Dropping Marx, Weber and Durkheim from the syllabus while teaching Foucault, Bourdieu and Butler — all also European, also Eurocentric in different ways — does not decolonise; it relabels. The structural problem persists.

What does decolonisation look like, then, if the canon stays? Three conditions.

Condition one: read the canon with its colonial archive. Teach Marx with the cotton-slavery sentence, not without it. Teach Weber's *Protestant Ethic* alongside his comparative work on Indian and Chinese religion, with explicit attention to the Eurocentric framing of "why not them". Teach Durkheim's mechanical/organic distinction with explicit attention to its colonial implications. Teach Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* alongside the *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, p. 59). Teach Hegel's *Phenomenology* alongside the *Lectures on the Philosophy of History*: "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). Reading the canon without the racial archive is teaching a sanitised version; reading it with the archive is teaching the canon honestly.

Condition two: supplement the canon with the voices it excluded. Add Du Bois, C.L.R. James, Aimé Césaire, Fanon, Dussel, Stuart Hall, Cedric Robinson, Gurminder Bhambra, Howard French. Add African and Asian sources that the founders treated as objects of study rather than as theorists: Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala on the Spanish conquest of Peru, Bartolomé de las Casas's defence of indigenous rights, twentieth-century African political thought (Nkrumah, Nyerere, Cabral, Sankara). The point is not to fill diversity quotas but to recognise that these thinkers theorised the modernity Marx-Weber-Durkheim were describing from inside Europe — and theorised it with information the founders did not have.

Condition three: rewrite the discipline's founding question. The standard question — "what is modern society and how did it come to be?" — presupposes that modernity is a European phenomenon to be analysed. A decolonised question — "how did the modern world come to be, and how do its colonial relations continue to structure it?" — keeps the analytic categories but reorients the unit of analysis. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows what this looks like in practice: Lancashire, Liverpool and Manchester are studied as nodes in an Atlantic system that includes Elmina, Benin City and the Bight of Biafra. The Mughal Empire and Indian railways are studied alongside British industrialisation. The class struggle in nineteenth-century Manchester is studied alongside the Haitian Revolution and the Maji Maji uprising.

Three contemporary signals suggest the conditions are workable. The publication of Bhambra's *Connected Sociologies* (2014); the BSA's curriculum-decolonisation initiatives; the inclusion of Du Bois in the LSE's undergraduate canon — these are partial victories but real ones. The British Museum has not returned the Benin bronzes, but the sociology curriculum is moving faster than the museum is.

So my answer: yes, sociology can be decolonised without abandoning its classical canon, but only by reading the canon honestly, supplementing it generously, and rewriting the founding question. The transformation is incomplete. But that is true of every academic discipline worth doing, and Dussel's trans-modernity (p. 76) — keeping the rational kernel while including the excluded — is the right description of what the project requires.

### M12: Trans-modernity in 2026 — programme or pious wish?

Dussel ends *Eurocentrism and Modernity* with the proposal of trans-modernity: "a process that will transcend modernity as such, a trans-modernity, in which both modernity and its negated alterity (the victims) co-realize themselves in a process of mutual creative fertilization" (Dussel, p. 76). The vocabulary is ambitious; the proposal is sketched in a single paragraph. The question in 2026 is whether it is being honoured by actual political work, or whether it has become the kind of phrase that decorates academic conferences while changing nothing. My argument is that trans-modernity is a programme where it is being institutionalised and a pious wish where it is not — and that the distinction can be tested on five contemporary sites.

Begin with what the programme would require. Trans-modernity is a co-realisation: keep modernity's rational kernel (universal rights, the rule of law, scientific method, democratic accountability) and reject its irrational myth (Dussel's seven steps, p. 75). The colonised, the slave, the woman, subalternised popular cultures must "discover themselves as innocent" (p. 76) and judge modernity for the violence it concealed. The redistribution implied by recognition is part of the proposal, not separable from it. A trans-modern project is therefore identifiable by two markers: it preserves modern goods that the colonial relation extracted from, and it includes the formerly excluded in their substantive enjoyment.

Five sites in 2026.

Site one: decolonising the curriculum. The movement to revise reading lists, hiring practices and assessment criteria in higher education is the clearest case of trans-modernity as actual practice. It keeps the analytic categories of Marx, Weber, Durkheim and the Enlightenment; it adds Du Bois, Hall, Dussel, Fanon, French; it asks why "great works" in English departments have been so racially narrow. Imperfect, contested, often performative — but real, and visible in the LSE's current sociology syllabus, Glasgow's reparative-justice scholarships, SOAS's curriculum reviews, and the very fact that this exam paper exists. Programme, in operation.

Site two: reparations. CARICOM's 10-point plan (2013–present), the Dutch state's €200 million slavery legacy fund (2023), the University of Glasgow's £20 million reparative partnership with the University of the West Indies (2018), the contested debate over the Bank of England's slave-trade balances. These are partial, slow and morally compromised, but they are also institutional admissions of obligations that modernity's own categories generate. Glasgow's settlement reads exactly as Dussel's "co-realization": the institution honours the goods (university scholarship, public funds, partnership) by extending them to those previously excluded. Programme, in painful first steps.

Site three: repatriation of artefacts. The Benin bronzes are slowly being returned: Germany announced unconditional restitution in 2022; Nigeria received the first batch from the Smithsonian in 2022; the British Museum has not moved. The Maqdala loot from Ethiopia, the Parthenon marbles, the Australian indigenous remains in Edinburgh — partial returns, contested defences. Each return is a small act of trans-modernity: the rational kernel (public scholarship, conservation, access) is preserved; the colonial taking is reversed. Programme on the continent (Germany, France, Belgium); pious wish in Bloomsbury.

Site four: climate reparations. The 2022 COP27 Loss and Damage Fund is the first formal admission by Northern states that climate impacts in the South are a debt rather than a misfortune. The Fund is capitalised at tiny levels relative to estimated needs ($700 million pledged against estimated needs of hundreds of billions annually), but the structural admission has happened. The argument is exactly trans-modern: Northern industrialisation — Lancashire mills, German steel, US cars — produced atmospheric damage borne disproportionately by Bangladesh, the Sahel, Pacific islands. Modernity's own concept of liability for damage generates the obligation. Programme as a structure; pious wish as a sum.

Site five: indigenous-rights movements at the UN. The Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), the increasing recognition of indigenous nations as sovereign actors in plurinational constitutions (Bolivia 2009, Ecuador 2008), the legal personhood granted to rivers and forests in New Zealand and Colombia. These extend modern legal categories (rights, personhood, sovereignty) to subjects modernity had excluded. They are uneven and contested but real. Programme on the rise.

What about the pious-wish side? Two patterns.

The first: trans-modernity as conference vocabulary without institutional follow-through. The phrase circulates in academic sociology, in NGO mission statements, in corporate "decolonial" branding — without translating into curriculum reform, repatriation, financial settlement or political restructuring. Recognition without redistribution. Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277) describes the structure: classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating — and now classifying generously, with "decolonial" as a positive label that does no structural work.

The second: trans-modernity blocked by hard institutions. The IMF and World Bank still operate on the developmental fallacy. The British Museum still holds the Benin bronzes. QS still ranks Oxford ahead of Lagos. The colonial attitude in the structures that organise global wealth, knowledge and heritage has been minimally disturbed. Where the structures have not moved, trans-modernity is rhetoric.

So my verdict: trans-modernity in 2026 is a programme in education, in some repatriations, in incipient reparations, in indigenous-rights law. It is a pious wish at the IMF, at the British Museum, in cumulative climate liability. The disjunction matters because it tells us where the analytical work has translated into political work and where it has not. Dussel's proposal is a regulative ideal that has done real work in some sites and remains a slogan in others. The next decade will widen or close the gap. Trans-modernity is not yet a complete answer to modernity; it is the right direction of travel, with a long way still to go.

### M13: "Modernity is a Janus-faced project: emancipation for some, the colonial wound for others." Discuss.

The Janus image — two faces, opposite directions, one head — is exactly right for modernity. The Roman god of doors looked both ways at once, and any honest account of the modern world has to do the same. Modernity emancipated Europeans (and some others) from arbitrary monarchy, scriptural orthodoxy, hereditary status and material scarcity. Modernity inflicted the colonial wound on Africans, Asians, indigenous Americans and their descendants — chattel slavery, conquest, extraction, racial hierarchy, the genocides of the Americas. The two faces are not separate; they share a head. I want to defend this Janus reading by working through the emancipation face, the colonial-wound face, and the structural reasons the two are inseparable.

The emancipation face is the standard textbook narrative and not to be dismissed. The Reformation broke the monopoly of the Roman church. The Scientific Revolution replaced authority with experiment. The Enlightenment articulated universal human rights: Kant's "exodus from guilty immaturity" (cited in Dussel, p. 68), the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* (1789), the US *Bill of Rights*. The Industrial Revolution lifted European real wages, extended life expectancy, and produced mass literacy. Parliamentary democracy slowly extended the franchise. Modern medicine cut child mortality. None of this is fake; the institutions delivered, within their borders, real goods to real populations. The European working class of 1950 had access to literacy, healthcare, political voice and material consumption unknown to their grandparents.

The colonial-wound face is what modernity also was, simultaneously and inseparably. Walter Mignolo's phrase "the colonial wound" names the trauma inflicted on the colonised by the modern project — and the marks it left on their descendants. The Atlantic slave trade transported twelve million Africans in chains; two million died on the Middle Passage. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* traces how the slave-grown sugar of São Tomé, the tobacco of Virginia and the cotton of Mississippi funded European modernity. The Spanish conquest of the Americas killed perhaps thirty million indigenous people by disease, forced labour and direct violence. The Belgian Congo under Leopold II (1885–1908) killed ten million through rubber quotas enforced by mutilation. The 1876–78 Indian famine, exacerbated by British grain export policies, killed perhaps ten million. The Mughal Empire's textile industries were deliberately destroyed by tariff and conquest after 1757; Indian railways were built with extracted Indian capital. The British Museum still holds the Benin bronzes, looted in 1897. The wound is not metaphorical.

Why are the two faces inseparable? Three reasons.

First, the emancipation was funded by the wound. Marx wrote it bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). The Lancashire cotton mills that lifted British real wages ran on slave-grown American cotton. The Bank of England's early balance sheet carried Royal African Company dividends. Liverpool's banks grew up to finance slave cargoes. The wealth that emancipated Europeans was substantially generated by the labour and lands of the colonised.

Second, the emancipation was conceptually structured by the wound. Stuart Hall shows that "the West" is not a primary fact but a relation: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 278). The modern European subject — Kant's autonomous reasoner, Hegel's bearer of Spirit — became thinkable by being posed against an inferior other. The universal claims of European modernity were articulated, in their canonical formulations, in a vocabulary that excluded most of humanity. Kant: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, p. 59). Hegel: "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). The emancipation and the exclusion came out of the same mouths in the same decades.

Third, the relation continues. The colonial attitude, as the lecture names it, outlives the formal end of colonies. The IMF's developmental conditionalities, the QS university league tables that rank Oxford ahead of Cairo, the British Museum's continued holding of the Benin bronzes, the news geographies that treat a Paris shooting as world news and a million dead in eastern Congo as regional — these are the wound continuing under modern administration. Black Lives Matter, the reparations debates, the decolonising-the-curriculum movement and the climate-loss-and-damage negotiations are the contemporary form of the demand that the wound be acknowledged.

What follows for political action? Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 75) names the legitimating structure of the wound; his proposal of trans-modernity (p. 76) names the way forward — "co-realisation" of modernity and its negated alterity, keeping the rational goods while including the excluded. The Janus image suggests the same: turn the head so both faces are seen at once, and the project becomes one of extending the emancipation to those modernity originally wounded. That is reparations, repatriation, curriculum decolonisation and climate redistribution as continuous with the universalist project, not as its anti-modern enemy.

A counter-position says the wound is overstated or the emancipation undervalued. The reply is simply to look. The Lancashire mills, the Bank of England, the Benin bronzes, Iraq 2003, the IMF, the carbon ledgers, the league tables. The two faces are still both visible. Modernity is Janus-faced because it always was, and any sociology that pretends otherwise is teaching one face while concealing the other. The honest task is to recognise both.

(~750 words)
