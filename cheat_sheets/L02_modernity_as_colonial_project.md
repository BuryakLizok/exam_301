## 1. Key Concepts and Thinkers

- **Modernity:** "a way of thinking, acting and living which had and still has enormous and unequal consequences for the world and the peoples of the world" (lecture slides). Tied to industrialisation, urbanisation, individualisation, rationality and the break with tradition.
- **The colonial attitude:** a habit of thought, surviving political independence, that ranks societies as superior/inferior, modern/backward.
- **Eurocentrism:** the assumption that modernity is an exclusively European phenomenon and that European development is the universal model.
- **The myth of modernity (Dussel):** modernity packages emancipation with a concealed irrational myth that justifies genocidal violence on a "civilising" mission.
- **Fallacy of developmentalism (Dussel):** the idea that every culture must follow Europe's ontological path of development.
- **The West and the Rest (Hall):** a discourse, not a geography. A "system of representation" producing binary oppositions: western/non-western = developed/under-developed = good/bad.
- **Bifurcated thinking:** the colonial attitude sorts the world into two camps.
- **Multiple modernities / trans-modernity (Dussel):** alternative routes that refuse Europe as the sole template.
- **Classical-sociology trio:** Marx (commodification, alienation, slavery–cotton–industry chain), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment), Durkheim (mechanical → organic solidarity).
- **Hegel:** world-historical Spirit moves East to West; Africa is "outside history"; Europe holds an "absolute right".
- **Kant:** Enlightenment as exodus from "guilty immaturity"; racialised remarks on "the Negroes of Africa".
- **Du Bois:** before the 15th century there was "no great disparity" between Negroland and Europe; slavery built world trade.

## 3. Information from the Readings

### Enrique Dussel — *Eurocentrism and Modernity*

**Central thesis.** Modernity is a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity. Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the "centre" of a World History it inaugurates; the "periphery" is part of its self-definition. By occluding the periphery — and the role of Spain and Portugal from the late 15th to mid-17th centuries — the major thinkers of the centre commit a Eurocentric fallacy.

**1492 as the birth date.** Dussel dates modernity to 1492: the conquest of the Kingdom of Granada and Columbus's voyage. Modernity is "born" when Europe is in a position to pose itself against an other. The other is not "dis-covered" but "covered-up" — concealed as the same as what Europe assumed it had always been.

**The myth of modernity.** Modernity contains a rational concept of emancipation that Dussel affirms and subsumes, but it also develops an irrational myth — a justification for genocidal violence. Postmodernists criticise modern reason as terror; Dussel criticises modern reason because of the irrational myth it conceals.

**Fallacy of developmentalism.** The fallacy consists in thinking that Europe's path of development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture. Development is taken as an ontological (not merely sociological) category. For Hegel, an "underdeveloped" country is "not-modern", pre-Aufklärung.

**Kant.** "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." Dussel asks: should the African slave, the Mexican Indian, the Latin American mestizo be considered guilty for their immaturity?

**Hegel.** World History is the self-realisation of Spirit, Reason, and Freedom. It moves East to West. Latin America and Africa are "outside" history. Asia is the dawn but not the culmination. Northern Europe — Germany, France, Denmark, Scandinavia — is the heart. The Germanic Spirit, refracted through the Lutheran Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution, is the culmination.

**The right of empire.** For Hegel, the people that bears the Spirit at its current moment has an "absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights". Dussel sees this as the philosophical sacralisation of imperial power over the periphery — a "limitless cynicism" masked as the development of reason.

**Colonies and civil society.** Hegel argues that civil society's internal contradictions push it to seek new consumers and colonise peoples "inferior" with respect to its industry. Colonisation allows surplus poor to become property owners abroad. Hegel does not register that this requires seizing land from others.

**Habermas continues the occlusion.** Habermas locates modernity's principle in the Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution. The discovery of America is mentioned but assigned no constitutive role. Habermas inherits Hegel's discounting of Spain and Portugal.

**The seven elements of the myth of modernity.**
1. European civilisation understands itself as the most developed and superior.
2. This obliges it, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the "primitive".
3. The path of development is Europe's own out of antiquity and the Middle Ages.
4. Where the "barbarian" resists, modernity must, in the last instance, recourse to violence.
5. The violence is ritualistic — victims (colonised, slave, woman, the earth) are cast as participants in redemptive sacrifice.
6. The barbarian is in a state of guilt for opposing civilisation; modernity is innocent and emancipatory.
7. The sufferings and sacrifices of modernisation imposed on immature peoples are inevitable and necessary.

**Trans-modernity.** Dussel proposes neither postmodern relativism nor a reaffirmation of European reason but a "trans-modernity": a co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (the colonial periphery, the Indian, the slave, the woman, the child, subalternised popular cultures). They must first "discover themselves as innocent" of the originary violence.

### Stuart Hall — *Formations of Modernity* (West and the Rest)

**The West as historical, not geographical, construct.** "Western" denotes a type of society — developed, industrialised, urbanised, capitalist, secular, modern. Its meaning is "virtually identical" to the word "modern". Japan can be "western"; Latin America, in the western hemisphere, is not.

**The West as concept** — four functions:
1. Characterises and classifies societies (western vs non-western) — a tool to think with.
2. Condenses a set of images: a system of representation. Western = urban = developed; non-western = rural = under-developed.
3. Provides a standard of comparison — how close to or far from the West?
4. Provides criteria of evaluation: the West = good = desirable; non-West = bad = undesirable. It functions as an ideology.

**Mutual constitution.** "The West and the Rest became two sides of a single coin." The uniqueness of the West was produced through Europe's contact and self-comparison with non-western societies. Identity is formed by difference (Saussure on night/day; psychoanalysis on the infant and the mother).

**Discourse and binary opposition.** Hall reads "West and Rest" as a discourse that simplifies — representing differentiated Europe as homogeneous (the West) and grouping equally diverse societies (Middle East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous America, Australasia) as one Rest. That over-simplification is precisely what makes the discourse destructive.

**Internal others.** Hall notes the West also had its internal others — Jews, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", women. The dichotomy is not only outward-facing.

**Periodisation.** Expansion is dated from Portuguese coastal exploration (1430–1498) and Columbus (1492–1502). Michael Mann's claim that Europe "overtook" Asia around 1450 is cited. The five phases (exploration; early contact and conquest; settlement; imperial consolidation; decolonisation) frame the West–Rest relationship as a long history of asymmetric encounter.

### Howard W. French — *Born in Blackness*

**Restoring Africa to modernity's origin story.** French argues that the conventional story of modernity — Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution — leaves out the constitutive role of Africa and Africans. The Portuguese push down the West African coast in the 15th century was driven by the search for gold and African slave labour, not just spices or a route to Asia. African gold (Mali, Ghana) helped underwrite European mercantilism.

**Slavery and the making of the Atlantic world.** Sugar plantations in São Tomé, the Caribbean and Brazil — staffed by enslaved Africans — generated unprecedented surplus and demand. The capital, commodities and consumer markets of the early modern economy were forged on this base. The cotton mills of Lancashire, the docks of Liverpool, the financial instruments of London and Amsterdam, the banks that survived to the present — all draw lines back to the slave economy.

**Africa as agent, not victim.** French insists Africa is not merely passive raw material. Kongo, Benin, Asante, Songhai were sophisticated polities whose engagements with Europeans shaped the trade. The argument supports Dussel's claim that the periphery is constitutive of the centre, and Hall's claim that the West is the product of relations with the Rest.

**Erasure as method.** Like Dussel's "covered-up" other and Hall's discourse of simplification, French highlights the active forgetting in European historiography — Africa "born in blackness" written out of modernity's account of itself.

## 4. Critical Analysis with Embedded Key Quotes

The central provocation of L2 is that modernity is not innocent. It is sociology's founding object, but the discipline that names it has also concealed its dependence on colonial extraction. Three moves are crucial.

First, Dussel reframes 1492 as modernity's birthdate. He writes:

> "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity… modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other." — Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66

This relocates modernity's origin from the Reformation and Enlightenment to colonial conquest. Habermas and Hegel both fail this test:

> "For Habermas, as for Hegel, the discovery of America is not a constitutive fact of modernity." — Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 74

Second, the lecture and Dussel converge on the "myth of modernity": rational emancipation is real but inseparable from sacrificial violence:

> "Modernity includes a rational 'concept' of emancipation that we affirm and subsume. But, at the same time, it develops an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence." — Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66

The seven-step "myth" — superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault — gives me a checklist for reading any modernising rhetoric, from nineteenth-century missions to twenty-first-century "regime change". The lecture asks pointedly: Iraq, the Falklands, Saudi Arabia?

Third, the violence has explicit textual sources in canonical European philosophy. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* sounds emancipatory until paired with his racial remarks:

> "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." — Kant, cited in Dussel, p. 68

> "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous." — Kant, *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, p. 59

Hegel goes further, treating Africa as outside history altogether:

> "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again." — Hegel, *Lectures on the Philosophy of History*, cited in Dussel, p. 70

> "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning." — Hegel, *Lectures*, p. 243, cited in Dussel, p. 69

The right of empire follows directly:

> "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights." — Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73

Stuart Hall completes the picture by showing that even when we are not citing Hegel, we are speaking through his categories. The discourse of "the West and the Rest":

> "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy — the West/the Rest. That is what makes the discourse of 'the West and the Rest' so destructive — it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'." — Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280

Marx is the partial exception within classical sociology — he saw the chain:

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." — Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203

And Du Bois pushed against the colonial attitude well before postcolonial theory:

> "In the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe." — Du Bois, 1970, p. 86, cited in the lecture slides

Critically: the colonial attitude is not an embarrassing relic. The lecturer's prompt — to look at sport, music, fashion, university league tables — shows that the same hierarchies still shape evaluation. We file Bach as universal and gamelan as "world music". We assume "developing" countries should follow our path. The intellectual task of L2 is to denaturalise that assumption and recover the constitutive role of the colonised in making the modern world.

## 8. Essay Answers

### 2024 (Q1): In what ways is modernity a colonial project?

To call modernity a colonial project is not to deny its rational and emancipatory ambitions; it is to argue that those ambitions cannot be separated from colonial conquest, racial hierarchy, and the violent imposition of one cultural template on the rest of the world. I want to defend this thesis in four moves: modernity's economic base, its philosophical self-image, its sociological vocabulary, and its surviving "attitude" today.

The economic base is the hardest to evade. Even Marx, normally an exemplar of Eurocentric sociology, conceded the point: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire ran on slave-grown cotton from the American South. The sugar that sweetened London and Amsterdam came from plantations staffed by enslaved Africans. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* extends this argument: Portugal's push down the West African coast in the 15th century, driven by gold and slaves, was a pre-condition not just of European wealth but of the financial instruments and consumer markets we now call "modern". Modernity, then, did not happen first in Europe and then spread; it was already a colonial relation in its formation.

The second move is philosophical. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* shows that the canonical European philosophers of reason wrote the colonial relation into the concept of modernity itself. He dates modernity's birth to 1492: "modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66). Kant's exhortation to leave "guilty immaturity" is paired with his judgement that "the Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, p. 59). Hegel writes the global South out of history altogether — "Africa… does not have history as such" — and grants the people that "bears" Spirit at its moment an "absolute right" against which other peoples "have no rights" (Hegel, cited in Dussel, pp. 70, 73). Dussel summarises this as the "myth of modernity": modernity packages rational emancipation with an irrational, sacrificial myth that licenses violence against those who resist the civilising project. The seven-step structure he draws out — superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault — gives modernity its colonial logic, not merely its colonial side-effects.

The third move is sociological. The lecture is explicit that sociology is "a creature of modernity": it arose to explain industrialisation, urbanisation and individualisation, and in doing so it inscribed the West-Rest hierarchy into its core vocabulary. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic transition treats non-European societies as pre-modern, behind, backward. Weber's rationalisation and disenchantment narrative makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Even the lecturer's question — does the rest of the world have history, in the sense modernity demands? — exposes the temporal hierarchy built into the discipline. Stuart Hall calls this "the West and the Rest": a discourse that "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" and that produces destructive over-simplifications of difference (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). The "West" is not a place; it is a model of comparison, an evaluative grid, an ideology. Sociology has often supplied the data for that grid.

The fourth move is contemporary. The lecture asks us to test whether "we" still think this way. Modern Western Classical Music is taught as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music". University league tables crown Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard and MIT as the world's best on criteria that take English-language journals and Anglo-American institutional forms as the measure. International development still imagines a single ladder up which every state must climb. The British Museum still holds the Benin bronzes and the Parthenon marbles. The Boris Johnson clip cited in the lecture is not an aberration; it is a transparent instance of a habit of thought the discipline calls the colonial attitude.

A counter-position argues that modernity also gave us human rights, scientific medicine, mass literacy and democratic accountability — gains that have benefited the colonised too. This is fair. Dussel himself "affirms and subsumes" modernity's emancipatory concept (p. 66). The point is not to discard modernity but to recognise that its rational kernel has always travelled with an irrational myth, and that the rights and goods we want to keep can only be honestly defended once their colonial history is acknowledged. Modernity is a colonial project not as an insult but as a description: at its economic base, in its philosophical self-image, in its sociological vocabulary and in its surviving everyday attitudes, the modern world is unintelligible without the colonies that helped make it.

### 2025 (Q1): Is it possible to fully understand modernity without understanding the role of colonialism in its development?

No — and arguing otherwise requires a curiously selective view of history. To "fully" understand modernity is to understand both its conceptual self-image (reason, progress, autonomy, the secular state) and its material conditions of possibility (industrial capitalism, world trade, mass consumption, the modern state). Colonialism is not an external accident attached to either of these. It is constitutive of both. I will defend this through three lenses — Dussel's philosophical genealogy, Hall's discursive analysis, and Howard French's economic history — before answering the obvious counter-argument that Europe's internal transformations (Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) suffice as an explanation.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* makes the strongest philosophical case. He argues that modernity "appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (p. 65). The European ego that thinks itself modern only becomes thinkable when it can pose itself against an other. Cortés in Mexico, not Luther in Wittenberg, is the prototypical modern subject; the conquest of the New World, not the French Revolution, marks the birth of the "Will to Power specific to [Europe's] self-consciousness" (p. 74). To "fully" understand modernity without colonialism is therefore to misunderstand the constitution of modern subjectivity itself. Habermas's mainstream genealogy — Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution — is, on Dussel's reading, "partial and provincial" (p. 65).

Stuart Hall's "West and the Rest" essay translates this into a discursive register. The West is not a geography but a "system of representation" with four functions: classifying societies, condensing images, providing a model of comparison, and supplying criteria of evaluation (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Crucially, "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (p. 278). Hall is borrowing from Saussure on linguistic difference: "night" only means what it means because of "day". By analogy, "modernity" only means what it means because of "non-modernity" — and that means colonised territories function as the conceptual condition of the modern. Anyone who claims to understand modernity by looking only inside Europe is mistaking the picture for the canvas.

The economic argument is the most concrete. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* traces how Portugal's mid-15th-century push down the West African coast — for gold from Mali and Ghana, then for enslaved labour for São Tomé and later the Atlantic — supplied the capital that funded Europe's early modern boom. Sugar, tobacco and cotton, all plantation crops worked by enslaved Africans, financed the docks of Liverpool, the warehouses of Bristol, the financial houses of London and Amsterdam, and ultimately the steam-powered mills of Manchester. Even Marx — usually a Eurocentric witness — conceded that "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, p. 203). To "fully" understand the Industrial Revolution without slavery is to study the engine and ignore the fuel. Du Bois made the same point a century earlier: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit form the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970, pp. 107–108, cited in the lecture slides).

The conventional answer — that modernity is the internal achievement of European reason — runs into three problems. First, it has to suppress the texts. Kant's "Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59) and Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70) are not stray asides; they are part of the architecture of the philosophical projects in which Enlightenment is celebrated. Second, it has to suppress the money. As soon as one traces the Lancashire cotton mill backwards or the Amsterdam bank backwards, the colonial relation surfaces. Third, it has to suppress the discourse. Hall's point is that even sympathetic European thinkers wrote inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The internalist story is not just incomplete — it is positively misleading because it naturalises the European outcome.

A more honest counter-position concedes that colonialism was constitutive but worries that emphasising it threatens the universal goods modernity also produced — human rights, scientific medicine, democratic governance. Dussel's reply is that we do not have to choose: he "affirms and subsumes" modernity's rational emancipatory concept while exposing the irrational myth that travelled with it (p. 66). His proposal of "trans-modernity" — the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (p. 76) — is precisely a way to retain the goods while refusing the myth.

So the answer is no. We cannot fully understand modernity without colonialism because the economic base, the philosophical self-image and the discursive grammar of the modern were all forged in the encounter between Europe and the worlds it conquered. To set colonialism aside is not to simplify the story; it is to tell a different one — the one Hegel told, and the one Habermas still tells. Both are too provincial to count as full understanding.

### 2023 (Q1): Can we understand modernity without understanding its relationship to colonialism?

This question and the 2025 paper press the same point with different verbs. The 2023 wording — "can we understand" — invites a slightly different answer. We can produce a partial, internalist account of modernity that ignores colonialism; people do it every term. But we cannot produce an account that survives the texts, the money, and the discourse once they are placed on the table. In what follows I sketch what that partial account looks like, then show why it fails three tests.

The standard partial account treats modernity as a European achievement with three internal pillars. Politically, the consolidation of centralised nation-states out of feudal kingdoms (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Religiously and intellectually, the Reformation and the Enlightenment, which Habermas takes as "the historical events that are decisive for the implantation of the principle of [modern] subjectivity" (cited in Dussel, p. 74). Economically, the Industrial Revolution. Sociologically, the discipline rests on Marx (commodification, alienation), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment) and Durkheim (mechanical to organic solidarity). On this account, the colonies feature, if at all, as a market for surplus production or a recipient of the civilising mission — secondary, not constitutive.

The first reason this account fails is textual. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* — "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, p. 68) — sounds universal until you read the same author's *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59). Hegel's *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* state that "the movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West" (p. 243) and that "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). The "absolute right" of the people bearing Spirit against peoples who "have no rights" (Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73) is not a footnote; it is the legitimation of empire by the philosopher whom Habermas continues to cite as the architect of modern subjectivity. To bracket colonialism is to bracket the architecture of the Enlightenment.

The second reason is economic. Marx himself — usually the Eurocentric exhibit — conceded: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). The cotton mills of Manchester were not autonomous European achievements; they were nodes in a circuit that ran through enslaved plantations in the Americas, gold extracted from Africa, silver mined in Potosí, and consumers buying sugar, tobacco and cotton goods. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows that the financial instruments of early modern Amsterdam and London were tooled to manage slave-trading and slave-grown commodities. Take that out and the Industrial Revolution looks unmotivated.

The third reason is discursive. Hall's contribution is to show that even when modern thinkers were not explicitly racist, they were speaking inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The West is "a tool to think with… a system of representation" that classifies, condenses, compares and evaluates (p. 277). It functions as an ideology in which "western = developed = good = desirable" and "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (p. 277). Until you see this, you cannot see why "developing country" is a value-laden category, why "the international community" is so often a code for the OECD, or why the BBC files Senegalese rap as "world music" while it files Bach as universal heritage. The discourse is so naturalised that it can be reproduced without ever mentioning colonialism — but it makes no sense without it.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* knits these together. His "myth of modernity" — superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault (p. 75) — is a single structure underwriting Kant's racial hierarchy, Hegel's philosophy of history, the East India Company, the conquest of the Americas, Belgian rule in the Congo, and the current rhetoric of "regime change". To understand modernity without colonialism, you would have to detach a structure from its instances — a strange thing for a sociologist to do.

A counter-position might say that the colonial element is real but is one factor among many; we can isolate other factors (technology, secularisation, the printing press) and study them on their own terms. This is true at the level of any particular question. The pebble can be examined without the riverbed. But the broader claim — that we can understand modernity as such without colonialism — fails because the riverbed is part of what makes the pebble visible as that kind of pebble. Modernity is not a discrete property; it is a relation. And the relation includes the colonised. So we can produce internalist accounts of modernity, but we cannot understand modernity in any deep sense without taking colonialism seriously.

### 2022 (Q3): Is modernity undermined by its roots in colonialism?

"Undermined" is the live word here. It could mean discredited — modernity is unworthy of allegiance because of its colonial origin; or destabilised — modernity is internally contradictory because the freedom it claims for some rests on the unfreedom of others; or unmasked — modernity is shown to be something other than it claims to be. I will argue that the second reading is correct: modernity is destabilised but not discredited by its colonial roots. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* is the clearest articulation of this position, and I will use Hall and Marx to support it.

Begin with the strong "discredited" reading. On this view, the modern world was built on slavery, conquest and theft. Therefore everything modernity values — human rights, science, democratic government, secular reason — is contaminated and should be rejected. This is sometimes attributed to Dussel or to the postmodernists Dussel cites — Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo. But it is not Dussel's position. He is explicit: "we do not propose a critique of reason as such" and "we do not deny the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment, only its irrational moment as sacrificial myth" (p. 75). Modernity is not all bad. It is internally split.

That split is what is being asked about. Dussel argues that modernity carries a rational concept of emancipation, which he "affirms and subsumes", and an irrational myth, which he negates (p. 66). The rational concept is genuine — autonomy, equality, the rule of law, scientific method, public reason. The irrational myth is the seven-step structure: superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; the victim is in a state of guilt; modernity emancipates by destroying (p. 75). Both have been present from 1492 onwards. The Enlightenment writes the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* in 1789 while the French state runs slave plantations in Saint-Domingue. Kant writes *What is Enlightenment?* and *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* — "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59). The undermining is internal; the same hand wrote both.

Hall's analysis sharpens this. The discourse of "the West and the Rest" is not a side-effect of European modernity; it is part of how modernity thinks itself: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (p. 278). The West can only know itself by negation. Its universalism is structurally provincial. That is undermining in the second sense: modernity claims one thing (universal reason) and depends on another (a binary opposition between West and Rest) — a performative contradiction.

The economic version of the same problem is Marx's. "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). Modern industrial capitalism — its productivity, its mass markets, its working-class consumer — could not have happened without the slave-grown raw materials of the Atlantic system. Manchester is unintelligible without Mississippi. Liverpool is unintelligible without Lagos. So modernity's economic dynamism, often celebrated as the engine of human progress, rests on coerced and racialised labour. This does not "undermine" the productivity gains; the cotton was real; the wages did rise; but it undermines any account of those gains that treats them as the unaided fruit of European industriousness.

What kind of conclusion does this support? Three things follow. First, intellectual honesty: it is not possible to teach the Enlightenment without teaching its racial archive. Second, political reckoning: the contemporary debate over reparations, the return of looted artefacts (the Benin bronzes, the Parthenon marbles), and the structural debts of the Atlantic economy is the unfinished political work that modernity's colonial roots imposed on us. Third — and this is Dussel's distinctive move — the response is not less modernity but a "trans-modernity", a co-realisation of modernity and its alterity (p. 76). The way out of the contradiction is not nostalgic anti-modernism nor postmodern relativism but a deeper modernity that finally takes seriously the universal claims it once made only for Europeans.

A counter-argument might insist that colonial cruelty was contingent; one could have had modernity without slavery; Mesopotamia, China and the Islamic world produced earlier scientific and bureaucratic modernities without trans-Atlantic slavery. This is partly true, but it concedes the point: the modernity that actually exists in 2026 — capitalist, racially stratified, with a global division of labour mapped onto colonial geographies — is not the modernity-in-general but a specific configuration whose roots run through the Atlantic system. Black Lives Matter, recent decolonisation-of-curriculum debates, and the persistence of racialised wealth gaps in London, Paris and New York all show that those roots still bear fruit.

So yes — modernity is undermined by its colonial roots, in the sense that it is internally contradicted, performatively self-betraying, and historically unable to deliver its universalism without exclusions. It is not for that reason worthless. But it cannot any longer be defended in the form it has handed itself down to us.

### 2021 (Q3): Does modernity have its roots in colonialism? If so, what are the implications for how we think of modernity?

This question is two questions. The first is descriptive: does modernity have colonial roots? The second is conceptual: what follows for how we use the word "modernity"? I will answer "yes" to the first and outline three implications for the second.

On the descriptive question, the case is now well established across the readings. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* dates the birth of modernity to 1492 rather than to the Reformation or the French Revolution. "Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (p. 65). The European subject that we recognise as modern — confident, rational, world-historical — only becomes thinkable once Europe can pose itself against a conquered other. Cortés in Mexico is closer to the origin of modern subjectivity than Descartes in his stove. The conquest of America, not the *cogito*, is the founding event.

Hall's "West and the Rest" essay supports the same conclusion through discourse analysis: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (*Formations of Modernity*, p. 278). The West is not a primary fact about which we then ask how it relates to the Rest. It is a relational fact constituted by that comparison. The category "modern" inherits the structure.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* completes the picture with economic history: Portuguese expansion into West Africa from the 1430s, then the trans-Atlantic slave trade, supplied the gold, sugar, tobacco and cotton that financed Europe's long boom. Marx, whom the lecture quotes as a partial exception within classical sociology, recognised this: "It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, p. 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire are not autonomous European achievements; they are nodes in an Atlantic system.

So yes, modernity has colonial roots — philosophically (Dussel), discursively (Hall) and economically (French, Marx).

What follows? Three implications.

First, the periodisation of modernity has to change. The standard textbook places modernity's origin at the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment, with the Industrial Revolution as its consolidation. On the colonial reading, the origin is 1492 — the conquest of Granada, Columbus's voyage, and the Iberian conquest of the New World. Spain and Portugal — discounted by both Hegel and Habermas (Dussel, p. 71) — are restored to the centre of the story. The Lutheran Reformation and the French Revolution are downstream consequences of an earlier world-historical reorganisation rather than its founding moments. The Industrial Revolution is the late phase of a long Atlantic economy that begins with sugar in São Tomé and ends with steam in Manchester.

Second, the canon has to change. If Kant's racial remarks — "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (p. 59) — are part of the architecture of Enlightenment, not stray asides, then teaching the Enlightenment without teaching Kant's race theory is teaching a falsified version. The same applies to Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). And it applies to the absent voices: Du Bois, C.L.R. James, Fanon, Aimé Césaire, Dussel himself. They are not optional addenda; they are part of the canon of modern thought because they are part of the story modernity has to tell about itself.

Third, the politics has to change. Dussel's proposal of "trans-modernity" — the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (p. 76) — points to a politics that retains modernity's rational kernel (rights, science, democratic accountability) but refuses its irrational myth (the civilising mission, the developmental ladder, the racialised hierarchy of value). In practical terms this means treating reparations, the repatriation of looted artefacts, decolonising university curricula, and global redistribution as the unfinished political work of modernity itself, not as anti-modern reaction.

A counter-position would say: yes, the colonial story is real, but it is descriptively interesting rather than normatively destabilising. We can keep using "modernity" as a value-neutral term and address colonial wrongs as a separate political matter. The trouble with this is that "modernity" was never value-neutral. As Hall shows, it has always been an evaluative grid — "western = developed = good = desirable; non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (p. 277). To keep using the word as if it were neutral is to keep underwriting the discourse. Better to name what the word is doing and decide whether we want to keep doing it.

So yes — modernity has colonial roots, and the implication is that periodisation, canon and politics all have to be redrawn so that the modernity we choose to defend is not the one Hegel and Habermas inherited but the one Dussel, Hall, Du Bois and Marx pushed against.

### 2024 (Q9): How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to the work of two theorists.

The question asks how European modernity benefited from unequal race relations — note "benefited", not "involved" or "depended on", though both are also true. I will answer with reference to Karl Marx and Enrique Dussel. Marx supplies the economic mechanism by which racial inequality fed into the wealth of modern Europe; Dussel supplies the philosophical mechanism by which European modernity legitimated racial inequality to itself. Together they show that the benefit was both material and ideological, and that the two reinforced each other.

Marx is the right place to start because, despite his Eurocentric framings of the proletariat as the agent of history, he was unusually clear about the slave-trade foundation of industrial capitalism. The lecture cites the canonical line: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). What is at stake here is not a moralising aside but an analytical claim about the structure of value-creation. Modern industry needs raw materials in bulk at low prices; that requirement was met by plantation slavery in the Caribbean and the American South, where racial categorisation legitimised the coercion of enslaved Africans to work without wages. The cheapness of slave-grown cotton allowed Manchester mill-owners to undercut Indian and Chinese textile producers, ruining their industries and redirecting demand to Britain. The cheapness of slave-grown sugar created the mass European consumer market — coffee with sugar, tea with sugar — that sustained the early modern world economy. The cheapness of slave-grown tobacco enriched Virginia planters who funded the American revolution. Each of these chains converts unequal race relations into modern industrial wealth.

Du Bois, cited in the lecture, generalises Marx's point: "The new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970, pp. 107–108). The European working class was, on Du Bois's reading, "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" — flattered by appeals to its racial superiority. Modern Europe benefited not only as a continent but as a racialised bloc that could be sold the story of its own elevation. The British working-class gain in real wages between 1750 and 1850 cannot be separated from the racialised redistribution of value out of the Atlantic system.

Dussel completes the picture by showing how this economic benefit was philosophically legitimated. His *Eurocentrism and Modernity* argues that modern European philosophy delivered a justifying myth for racial inequality at exactly the moment it was being economically institutionalised. Kant — the philosopher of Enlightenment — supplies the racial hierarchy: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, p. 59). Hegel — the philosopher of Spirit — writes Africa out of history: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again" (cited in Dussel, p. 70). And, crucially, Hegel gives empire its philosophical charter: "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73). The slave colony is not just a piece of profitable real estate; it is a stage in the self-realisation of Reason. That is a powerful legitimation. It tells the European subject that the unequal race relation she profits from is also a service to the universal.

Dussel's "myth of modernity" generalises this into a seven-step structure: European superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault; modernity is innocent and emancipatory (Dussel, p. 75). The benefit, on this account, is double. Materially, Europe gets cheap labour, cheap raw materials and captive markets. Ideologically, Europe gets to think of itself as the bearer of universal reason while doing so. The combination is uniquely powerful because each side reinforces the other: the wealth funds the universities that produce the philosophy, and the philosophy legitimises the trade that produces the wealth.

Putting Marx and Dussel together yields a sharper account than either alone. Marx without Dussel risks reading slavery as an unfortunate historical accident attached to a structurally distinct capitalism. Dussel without Marx risks reading the myth of modernity as a purely cultural construct floating free of its economic stakes. Together, they show that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in a tightly integrated way: race made the labour cheap, the trade legal, and the conscience clean. Black Lives Matter, the contemporary reparations debate and the campaign to return the Benin bronzes are not random political flare-ups but the long-deferred bill for a modernity that bought itself with the bodies and goods of racialised peoples. To understand modern Europe's wealth is to understand the racial inequality that produced it. Marx tells us how. Dussel tells us why no one in Europe felt obliged to notice.

### M1: To what extent is sociology itself a colonial project?

Sociology presents itself as the science of modern society. The lecture's strongest claim is that the discipline is also "a creature of modernity" that helped produce the colonial attitude it now studies. I want to argue that sociology is partially but not wholly a colonial project: its founding categories carry colonial assumptions, but its methods can be turned against those assumptions, and the discipline contains its own internal critics from Marx onwards.

Begin with the case for "yes". The lecture summarises it cleanly: sociology arose to explain industrialisation, urbanisation and individualisation, and in doing so it built a hierarchy of societies into its core vocabulary. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic transition treats non-European societies as "premodern, behind, backward". Weber's rationalisation narrative makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Even the most reflexive question of the discipline — what does it mean to be modern? — is asked from inside the West-Rest binary. The lecturer makes the point sharply: when sociologists try to define modernity, they describe it as a break with tradition, religion and custom — and that description automatically casts non-modern societies as backward.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* deepens the indictment. He names the assumption — the "fallacy of developmentalism" — by which sociology takes Europe's path as the universal template (p. 67). The whole field of "development sociology" is constructed on this fallacy. The categories "developed" / "developing" / "underdeveloped" are not neutral descriptors; they map the world onto a single ladder up which every country is asked to climb. Dussel reads this back to Hegel: "an 'underdeveloped' country is, for Hegel, 'not-modern,' pre-Aufklärung" (p. 68 fn). Modern sociology has inherited this without acknowledging the inheritance.

Stuart Hall's contribution sharpens it further. The discourse of "the West and the Rest" "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" — and it does so with all the authority of a discipline that claims to describe rather than evaluate (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). Hall's four functions of "the West" as concept — classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating (p. 277) — read like a description of how sociology textbooks actually work. The case for "yes" is strong.

But the case is not total. Three qualifications matter.

First, classical sociology contains internal exceptions. Marx, on the lecture's own account, is "a possible exception": his observation that "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203) places the colonial relation at the heart of modern capitalism. Du Bois, who is part of the canon if anyone is, argues that the new colonial theory "transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races" (Du Bois, 1970, p. 107). Even when the mainstream of the discipline was Eurocentric, the discipline contained voices that contested it.

Second, sociology has reflexive resources. The same methods that produce a Durkheimian narrative of progress can be turned on that narrative. Hall's essay is itself a piece of sociology. Dussel's myth-of-modernity argument is a sociology of knowledge in everything but name. The discipline contains the tools by which to expose its own provincialism — historical comparison, discourse analysis, ideology critique. To call sociology a colonial project is therefore to use sociological tools to make a sociological argument, which is harder to do in a discipline that is wholly captured.

Third, the colonial attitude is not unique to sociology. Anthropology, history, economics, political science, area studies and even biology have their colonial archives. The point is not to single sociology out for unique blame but to recognise that the human sciences as a whole emerged in the imperial era and inherited its categories. The remedy is not to abandon sociology but to decolonise it.

So my answer is: sociology is substantially but not totally a colonial project. The colonial attitude is built into its vocabulary, its canon, its periodisations and its sense of what counts as a "modern" society. But the colonial attitude is challengeable from inside the discipline, and the present generation of sociologists — including the lecturer — is doing that challenging.

What would a less colonial sociology look like? First, it would teach the Atlantic system as part of the origin of modern industrial capitalism rather than as a side-chapter on slavery. Second, it would treat Africa, Latin America and Asia as sites of theoretical innovation rather than as fields for European theory to be applied. Third, it would name "modernisation" as one trajectory among many rather than the universal direction. Fourth, it would acknowledge that the "we" who do sociology has historically been a Western "we", and would open the category accordingly. Dussel's "trans-modernity" — the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (p. 76) — is the philosophical name for the same project.

To the extent that sociology takes these steps, it ceases to be (only) a colonial project. The transformation is incomplete. But that is true of every academic discipline worth doing.

### M2: Discuss Dussel's account of the "myth of modernity".

Enrique Dussel's "myth of modernity" is one of the most economical and powerful tools in postcolonial theory. In *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993) he sets out, in seven numbered steps, the structure of self-justification by which European modernity legitimates the violence it inflicts. I want to lay out the structure, situate it against the alternative accounts Dussel rejects (postmodernism and Habermasian critical theory), and assess what it can and cannot do.

The structure first. Dussel writes that modernity contains two layers: a rational concept of emancipation, which he affirms, and an irrational myth, which he negates (p. 66). The myth has seven elements (p. 75):

1. Modern European civilisation understands itself as the most developed and superior.
2. This superiority obliges it, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the more primitive cultures.
3. The path of development must be the one Europe took out of antiquity and the Middle Ages.
4. Where the barbarian resists, modernity must in the last instance use violence.
5. The violence is ritual: victims (the colonised, the slave, the woman, the earth) are cast as participants in redemptive sacrifice.
6. The barbarian is in a state of guilt for opposing civilisation; modernity is innocent and emancipatory.
7. The sufferings imposed on immature peoples are inevitable and necessary.

Each step is connected to the next. Steps 1–3 supply the ontology: there is a single ladder of development with Europe on top. Step 4 turns ontology into practical politics. Step 5 sacralises the violence by recoding it as redemption. Steps 6–7 close the circle: the victim is at fault, the violence is inevitable, the perpetrator stays innocent. Read backwards, the myth allows any modernising violence — from the Spanish conquest of Mexico to the U.S. invasion of Iraq — to be conducted in good conscience.

Dussel's textual evidence comes from Kant and Hegel. Kant supplies step 6: "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, p. 68). The non-modern is guilty of his own non-modernity. Hegel supplies steps 1–3: World History moves East to West, with Europe as "the absolute end" (p. 69). Africa "does not have history as such" (p. 70). The people that bears Spirit at its current moment has an "absolute right" against which other peoples "have no rights" (p. 73). Dussel pairs these with Hegel's account of colonisation in the *Philosophy of Right*: civil society's contradictions push it outward to "other peoples that are inferior to it with respect to the resources that it has in abundance" (p. 73). The myth is not a stray asseveration; it is structured into the architecture of modern philosophy.

Dussel positions his account against two alternatives. Against postmodernism (Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo), he refuses to discard reason as such: "we do not propose a critique of reason as such; but we do accept their critique of a violent, coercive, genocidal reason" (p. 75). Against Habermasian critical theory, which finds modernity's resources of self-correction in the Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution (Habermas, *Der philosophische Diskurs der Moderne*, cited in Dussel, p. 74), he insists that Habermas's genealogy itself reproduces the Eurocentric occlusion of 1492. Dussel's positive proposal is "trans-modernity": a co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (p. 76). The colonised, the slave, the woman, the child, subalternised popular cultures — the "other-face" of modernity — must first "discover themselves as innocent", and then judge modernity for the originary violence it has concealed.

What can the account do? Three things. First, it gives a portable diagnostic. Any rhetoric of civilising mission, regime change, or developmental ladder can be tested against the seven steps. The fit is usually close. Second, it explains why critics of modernity often misfire. Postmodernists discard the rational kernel; defenders of modernity refuse to see the myth; both speak past each other. Dussel's two-layer account explains how reasonable people can disagree about modernity by tracking which layer they are attending to. Third, it gives a political programme — trans-modernity — that is neither nostalgic anti-modernism nor postmodern relativism. It points to a future in which the universal claims of modernity are honoured by being extended.

What can it not do? Three things. First, it is short on historical mechanism. The seven-step myth is a logical structure rather than a causal story; Dussel does not show, in this introductory essay, how the myth was produced or how it travels. Second, it risks lumping. Spanish *encomienda*, British indirect rule, French *mission civilisatrice*, American "freedom agenda" and Chinese "Belt and Road" all fit the seven steps to varying degrees; the differences may matter as much as the similarities. Third, the path from diagnosis to political action is sketched rather than elaborated. What would it mean institutionally for the colonised to "discover themselves as innocent" and to "judge modernity"? Dussel signals it but does not work it out.

Even so, the "myth of modernity" remains one of the sharpest tools available for reading modernity's self-presentation against the grain. It does what good critical concepts do: it lets us recognise the same structure across very different historical instances, and it does so with seven numbered steps that a student can carry into the exam room.

### M3: Compare Dussel and Hall on the relation between Europe and its outside.

Enrique Dussel and Stuart Hall both argue that modern Europe constituted itself through its relation with a non-European outside. They disagree, however, on how that constitution worked and what kind of critique it warrants. Dussel writes as a Latin American philosopher in the liberation-theology tradition; Hall writes as a Caribbean-British cultural theorist in the Marxist-Foucauldian tradition. Their accounts converge on the conclusion that the West and the Rest are mutually constituted, but they diverge on whether the right level of analysis is philosophical (Dussel) or discursive (Hall), and whether the political response is "trans-modernity" (Dussel) or the ongoing work of cultural critique (Hall).

The agreement first. Both reject the view that Europe was made internally and then exported itself outward. For Dussel, "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (*Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 65). For Hall, "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (*Formations of Modernity*, p. 278). Both deny that 1492 or the early Atlantic encounters were merely incidents in a story Europe was writing on its own. Both argue, instead, that the encounter with an outside was constitutive of what Europe became.

Both also identify the same canonical philosophers as carriers of the Eurocentric narrative. Dussel works systematically through Hegel — "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History" (Hegel, *Lectures*, p. 243, cited in Dussel, p. 69) — and through Kant — "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, p. 59). Hall, more briefly, locates the same hierarchy in the Enlightenment: "European society, it assumed, was the most advanced type of society on earth, European man (sic) the pinnacle of human achievement" (p. 278). The targets overlap.

Now the differences. The first is methodological. Dussel works philosophically: he reads the great texts of Western thought (Hegel, Kant, Habermas) and reconstructs the implicit ontology that holds them together. The output is a logical structure — the seven-step "myth of modernity" — that can be tested against any particular instance of modernising rhetoric (p. 75). Hall works discursively: he treats "the West" not as a set of doctrines but as a system of representation, "a tool to think with" with four functions — classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating (p. 277). Hall's output is an analysis of how the discourse works in everyday cultural production — how a TV documentary, a news report or a textbook reproduces the binary West/Rest without ever explicitly defending it.

The second difference is in the unit of analysis. For Dussel, the constitutive other is concrete and historical: it is the Aztec, the slave, the gaucho, the *teixtli* ("other-face" in Aztec, p. 74) of the Latin American conquest. The conquest of Mexico is for him "the first extra-European space in which it can carry out a prototypic 'development'" (p. 74). For Hall, the constitutive other is more abstract: it is "the Rest" as a discursive position, into which a wide variety of geographical and cultural others can be slotted. Hall is explicit about the simplification — "the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (p. 280) — but his analytic stays at the level of the simplification rather than disaggregating it. Dussel's approach reads better as a story about specific encounters; Hall's reads better as a story about the cultural grammar that registers them.

The third difference is political. Dussel's response is "trans-modernity": a co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity, "an incorporative solidarity… between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (p. 76). It is a programmatic, philosophical proposal with redemptive overtones (his liberation-theology background shows). Hall's response is more modest: ongoing critical analysis of how the discourse of West and Rest continues to operate in mass media, in academic disciplines, in everyday speech. Hall does not promise transcendence; he promises clarification, denaturalisation, and the production of better descriptions.

Which is more useful? It depends on the question. If you want to test contemporary geopolitical rhetoric — the "international community", "regime change", "the developed world" — Dussel's seven-step diagnostic is sharper. If you want to analyse how a particular newspaper article or museum exhibition organises difference, Hall's discourse analysis is more tractable. The two approaches are complementary. Together they show that the West-Rest relation operates at every level — philosophical ontology, political legitimation, economic structure and everyday cultural production — and that no single critique exhausts it.

A unified position, which I think the readings invite, would say this. Europe was made by relations with its outside — economically through the Atlantic system (Marx, French), philosophically through the discounting of the periphery (Dussel), and discursively through the binary West/Rest (Hall). The same relation continues to operate today in different registers — university league tables, sovereign-debt regimes, news geographies, music classification. The political project is therefore plural: trans-modern political imagination of the kind Dussel sketches, plus the constant labour of discourse critique of the kind Hall practises. Neither alone is enough; together they constitute the postcolonial sociology of modernity.

### M4: "The colonial attitude has outlived the colonies." Discuss with examples.

The phrase "colonial attitude" comes from the lecture itself, which "traces the development of a colonial attitude" through definitions of modernity in classical sociology and through canonical European philosophy. The lecturer is careful that an attitude is not the whole of colonialism: "of course there is much more to colonialism than just an attitude". But the claim that this attitude lives on, "even though most countries have attained independence", is testable and important. I want to defend it with five examples — music, university league tables, international development, news geographies and the British Museum — before considering counter-examples.

The first example is cultural. The lecture asks us to consider Western Classical Music. Beethoven, Mozart and Bach are taught as universal masters; gamelan, samba and Hindustani classical are filed as "World Music" in record shops, festival programmes and Spotify categories. The lecturer's joke — that Beethoven and Bach are themselves "local, ethnic musicians" — exposes the asymmetry. A universal is what the West produces; what the rest produces is interesting but particular. The same asymmetry shows up in art history (the canon vs "non-Western art"), in literature (English literature vs "post-colonial writing"), and even in food (French vs "ethnic cuisine"). The colonial attitude operates here as a habit of evaluative categorisation that survives the end of formal empire.

The second example is academic. University league tables — QS, Times Higher Education, ARWU — rank the world's universities according to criteria heavily weighted towards English-language publications, Anglo-American research funding norms and citation networks dominated by Western journals. The result is a top-ten that has not changed in twenty years: Harvard, MIT, Oxford, Cambridge, Stanford, Caltech, ETH Zurich, Imperial, Chicago, Princeton. Universities in Africa, South Asia, Latin America and the Middle East — many older than their European competitors — appear far below. Students in Lagos and Mumbai grow up being told that "the best" universities are in the same five countries that ran the global empires of the nineteenth century. The lecturer notes this directly: "Education (which are the best universities in the world, and why?)". The ranking technology reproduces the colonial attitude with the apparent neutrality of metrics.

The third example is economic. The international development industry — World Bank, IMF, OECD, USAID, DFID, the UN's Sustainable Development Goals — still operates inside Dussel's "fallacy of developmentalism": the assumption that every country must follow a single ladder up which Europe has already climbed. The categories "developed", "developing", "least developed" are inscribed in the institutional vocabulary. Aid is administered on the assumption that the donor knows the destination. Conditionalities on structural adjustment loans require recipients to adopt market reforms, public-sector privatisations, currency convertibility and foreign-investment regimes that mirror the donor's own institutions. Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity (p. 75) maps almost too neatly: Europe is most developed (step 1), this obliges it to civilise (step 2), the path is Europe's path (step 3), opposition triggers sanction (step 4), the costs are inevitable (step 7).

The fourth example is media. Western news geographies treat European and North American events as world news (a Paris shooting leads the BBC) and African or Asian events as regional news of limited interest. A million deaths in eastern Congo over twenty years receive less coverage than a single terror attack in Brussels. The "international community" usually means the OECD plus a few clients. The category "the developing world" sweeps four billion people into one analytic bucket. Hall's analysis of "the West and the Rest" — "they are all different from the West" (p. 280) — is reproduced nightly on television news.

The fifth example is institutional. The British Museum still holds the Benin bronzes, looted by British troops in 1897 during the punitive expedition against the Kingdom of Benin. The Louvre still holds artefacts from Algeria, the Netherlands' Rijksmuseum from Indonesia, Berlin's collections from German East Africa. These institutions defend their possession with arguments about preservation, scholarship and universal access — arguments that map onto Dussel's "civilising" categorical imperative (step 2). The objects were taken from contexts where they had meaning; they are displayed in contexts that frame them as exotic; the framing reproduces the colonial attitude in the architecture of the institution.

Counter-examples? Three. First, decolonisation is partial but real: Black Lives Matter has shifted curricula and museum policy; the Belgian and Dutch states have begun repatriations; Mexican and Greek demands for the return of artefacts are increasingly successful. Second, the South-South economic axis (China, India, Brazil, Turkey, the Gulf) is now significant enough that the West-Rest binary increasingly looks creaky as a description of global flows of capital and influence. Third, within Western academia, decolonising-the-curriculum movements have made significant inroads, including this very module. The colonial attitude is contestable, and is being contested.

But contestable is not the same as defeated. The lecturer's challenge — "think of other examples of how a 'colonial attitude' plays out today" — is one we should keep asking. The colonies are gone; the attitude that organised them is still organising us. Music categories, league tables, development discourse, news priorities and museum collections all show the same structure that Kant, Hegel and Habermas built into the architecture of modern thought. Dismantling it is the work the lecture is asking us to do.

### M5: To what extent does classical sociology (Marx, Weber, Durkheim) reproduce the colonial attitude it claims to analyse?

Classical sociology — Marx, Weber, Durkheim — was the discipline's foundational generation, and its founding question was: what is modern society and how did it come to be? In trying to answer that question, the three founders produced explanatory frameworks that have shaped sociology ever since. The lecture argues that, in producing those frameworks, they also reproduced the colonial attitude — bifurcated thinking that ranks societies as modern/backward and treats the European trajectory as universal. I want to assess each in turn and then offer a verdict.

Durkheim is the easiest case. His distinction between mechanical solidarity (simple, homogeneous, repressive-law societies) and organic solidarity (complex, heterogeneous, restitutive-law societies based on the division of labour) is foundational to the discipline. The lecture spells out the colonial implication: "He sees the shift to modernity as going from Mechanical to Organic Societies. This suggests that other societies around the world are still 'pre-modern' / behind / backward". Durkheim does not say this in so many words; he draws his examples of mechanical solidarity from European pre-history and from anthropological reports of "primitive" societies. But the schema places contemporary non-European societies into the mechanical column by default. Africa, Aboriginal Australia and indigenous America become living museums of European prehistory. The colonial attitude is built into the developmental schema.

Weber is more ambivalent. On one hand, his comparative sociology of religion — *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, *The Religion of China*, *The Religion of India*, *Ancient Judaism* — engages seriously with non-European traditions. He does not dismiss Confucianism or Hinduism as primitive; he asks what makes their economic ethics different from Calvinism. On the other hand, the question he is asking is constructed around the European outcome: why did rational capitalism develop here and not elsewhere? Confucianism and Hinduism are studied for what they failed to produce. The narrative of rationalisation and disenchantment treats Western bureaucratic rationality as the endpoint of an evolutionary process that other civilisations either did or did not begin. Dussel might call this a sophisticated form of the developmental fallacy. Weber does not insult non-Europeans, but he also does not let go of the assumption that European modernity is the standard.

Marx is the most complex case, and the lecture explicitly names him as "one possible exception". His insight is structural: the modern capitalist economy is unintelligible without slavery. "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203). This is the strongest acknowledgement in classical sociology of the colonial constitution of modernity. Marx also wrote with horror about British rule in India and about the Highland clearances. But Marx is not free of the colonial attitude. His vocabulary of "stages" — primitive communism, slave society, feudalism, capitalism, socialism — is itself a developmental ladder. His remarks on India sometimes welcome British colonialism for breaking the "static" Asiatic mode of production. The exception, on closer inspection, is partial.

Putting the three together: classical sociology gives us the conceptual vocabulary of modernity — division of labour, rationalisation, alienation, anomie, commodification — which is genuinely indispensable. It also gives us, often in the same texts, a developmental schema that treats Europe as the destination of human history. The lecture's claim that sociology "helped create this colonial attitude" is not an exaggeration. The colonial attitude is not a residue smuggled in from outside; it is part of how the discipline asked its founding question.

But classical sociology also contains the resources to undo this. Marx's slavery-cotton chain is one. Weber's serious engagement with non-European religious traditions, even when his framing was teleological, opened a comparative method that later sociologists could use to dethrone the European baseline. Durkheim's insistence that morality is socially produced — that "the social" is real and shapes consciousness — was later turned against Durkheim by Mauss, Lévi-Strauss and the French sociology of knowledge to argue that European modernity is itself a particular social-symbolic construction, not a universal endpoint.

The verdict, then, is mixed. Classical sociology reproduces the colonial attitude in the developmental schema that organises its central questions and in the canonical examples it chooses. It does not reproduce it uniformly: Marx exposes the slavery-capitalism link; Weber refuses easy supremacism in his comparative work. The discipline can be critiqued from within — and has been, by Du Bois, by Cedric Robinson's *Black Marxism*, by Gurminder Bhambra's *Connected Sociologies*, by Dussel himself. The path forward is not to discard Marx, Weber and Durkheim but to read them critically: to keep the analytic tools they built (division of labour, rationalisation, alienation) and to discard the developmental ladder they often used those tools to climb.

A genuinely decolonised sociology would do four things: teach the Atlantic system as part of the origin of modern capitalism, not a side-chapter; treat non-European societies as sites of theoretical innovation, not just fields for European theory; periodise modernity from 1492, not 1789; and recognise the canon as wider than Marx-Weber-Durkheim. Some of that is already happening. The classical founders are partially complicit in the colonial attitude but also partially indispensable to the discipline that is now trying to undo it.

### M6: Discuss Hall's account of "the West and the Rest" as a discourse of power.

Stuart Hall's "The West and the Rest" is a deceptively short piece that does heavy theoretical work. It argues that "the West" is not a geography but a discourse — a system of representation that organises how the world is known, classified, valued and acted upon. I want to lay out the four functions Hall attributes to the discourse, situate his Saussurean argument about meaning and difference, examine what makes the discourse a discourse of power, and finally note the limits of his analysis.

First, the four functions. Hall lists them on p. 277. The discourse of "the West" (1) classifies societies into western and non-western categories — "a tool to think with"; (2) condenses a set of images into a composite picture of what western societies look like — "a system of representation" working alongside other images (western = urban = developed; non-western = rural = under-developed); (3) provides a standard of comparison so that other societies can be ranked as "close to" or "far from" or "catching up with" the West; (4) provides criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked, with positive feelings clustering around the West and negative feelings around the non-West. The fourth function is the most important: it converts the discourse into an ideology.

The four functions interlock. Classification (1) presupposes a binary; the binary needs content, supplied by the images (2); the content needs to be operationalisable, supplied by the comparison (3); and the comparison is loaded with value, supplied by evaluation (4). The discourse is therefore a coherent apparatus, not just a vocabulary.

Second, the Saussurean argument. Hall borrows from Saussure on linguistic difference: the words "night" and "day" do not refer to a primary thing each; they signify by their difference from each other (p. 278). Identity is relational. By analogy, "the West" does not refer to a primary set of properties; it signifies by its difference from "the Rest". "The so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest), very different in their histories, ecologies, patterns of development and cultures from the European model" (p. 278). The corollary, which Hall borrows from Foucault and psychoanalysis, is that this self-knowledge is constitutive: it is not a description after the fact; it is part of what makes the West the West.

Third, why is this a discourse of power? Three reasons. The first is that the discourse simplifies. Hall is explicit: he is forced to simplify the West (which is internally differentiated — Germanic, Latin, Iberian, Mediterranean, Nordic — p. 279) and the Rest (Middle East, Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous America, Australasia, p. 280). But "simplification is precisely what this discourse itself does. It represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)" (p. 280). The discourse is destructive because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (p. 280). Simplification is not innocent: it erases the internal diversity of both sides and licenses lumping.

The second reason is that the discourse becomes productive. Once produced, "the idea of 'the West'… had real effects: it enabled people to know or speak of certain things in certain ways. It produced knowledge. It became both the organising factor in a system of global power relations and the organising concept or term in a whole way of thinking and speaking" (p. 278). The discourse does not just describe the world; it shapes how the world can be known. Foucault's power/knowledge formulation is in the background. The West-Rest binary determines what counts as a question worth asking, what counts as a society worth studying, what counts as a problem worth solving.

The third reason is that the discourse has internal others as well as external ones. "As well as treating non-European cultures as different and inferior, the West had its own internal 'others'. Jews, in particular, though close to western religious traditions, were frequently excluded and ostracised. West Europeans often regarded Eastern Europeans as 'barbaric', and, throughout the West, western women were represented as inferior to western men" (p. 280). The same binary technology that organises the world also organises the inside. The discourse of power produces hierarchies of value within Europe as well as between Europe and elsewhere.

Fourth, the limits. Hall's analysis has been critiqued on three grounds. First, it can flatten the differences within the discourse: the British, French, Spanish and German versions of the West-Rest binary were significantly different in their categories and applications, and lumping them obscures that. Hall notes this — "attitudes towards other cultures within the West varied widely" (p. 280) — but his analysis stays at the level of the unified discourse. Second, it can underplay the agency of the Rest. The peoples slotted into "the Rest" were not passive recipients of the discourse; they negotiated, resisted, reappropriated. Dussel's "trans-modernity" and Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* fill out what Hall leaves implicit. Third, the discourse is not the only thing going on. Material structures of trade, military force and political institutions sit alongside the discourse and partly constrain it.

But these limits are limits of emphasis, not of substance. Hall's "West and the Rest" remains one of the most teachable pieces in postcolonial cultural theory because it gives students a portable model for analysing any text — a news report, a school curriculum, a museum label, a tourist brochure — in which the binary is doing work. The discourse of power is not a vague accusation; it is a structure with four functions, and once you can see the structure, you can see it everywhere.

### M7: Compare Kant and Hegel on the place of non-European peoples in the history of reason.

Kant and Hegel are central to the standard story of European modernity. Both wrote racially and historically about non-European peoples; both did so in works that are still in the philosophical canon. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* uses both as evidence for the "myth of modernity". I want to compare them on three axes — the structure of the argument, the function of non-Europeans within it, and the implications for how we read each thinker today.

Begin with Kant. His most quoted Enlightenment slogan is universal: "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, p. 68). Read in isolation, this sounds emancipatory: every human being can and should think for themselves; immaturity is a moral failure that can be overcome. The universal "humanity" seems inclusive. But Kant's *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* makes clear that he did not think all of humanity was in fact capable: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, p. 59). The reading of "humanity" in *What is Enlightenment?* turns out to be narrower than the word suggests.

Dussel's question is exactly the right one to ask: "An African in Africa or as a slave in the United States in the eighteenth century; an Indian in Mexico or a Latin American mestizo: Should all of these subjects be considered to reside in a state of guilty immaturity?" (p. 68). Kant's textual evidence suggests his answer would have been yes — though the immaturity is, in their case, not curable by individual effort because their racial constitution prevents it. The universal opening becomes a hierarchical closing once we add the empirical anthropology.

Hegel is structurally different. Where Kant is essentially moralising — humans should leave immaturity behind — Hegel is essentially historicising: humans actually do leave immaturity behind, and we can chart the path. The *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* present "World History" as the self-realisation of Spirit, "the development of the consciousness that the Spirit has of its freedom" (cited in Dussel, p. 68). The path is geographically directed: "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning" (Lectures, p. 243, cited in Dussel, p. 69). Latin America and Africa are excluded from the movement entirely.

The exclusions are explicit. On Latin America: "America is, consequently, the land of the future… But as a land of the future Latin America has no interest for us, because the philosopher does not make prophesies" (p. 69). On Africa: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again. It is not part of the historical world; it does not evidence historical movement or development" (p. 70). On the place of "the negro": "Among negroes it is the case that consciousness has not attained even the intuition of any sort of objectivity… [The negro] is the man as beast" (p. 70). And on the right of empire: the people that bears Spirit at its moment has an "absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, *Encyclopedia*, sections 346–347, cited in Dussel, p. 73).

So how do they compare?

On the structure of the argument, Kant is universal in form and racially restrictive in content. The universal "every human being should reason" is qualified by an empirical anthropology that excludes most of the species from full rationality. Hegel is hierarchical in form: World History is structured into stages, with non-Europeans assigned to lower stages or excluded from the schema altogether. Kant smuggles racial exclusion into an apparently universal frame; Hegel makes the hierarchy the frame itself. Kant is therefore more deceptive but also more recuperable: one can argue that he failed by his own universal standard. Hegel offers less to recuperate because the hierarchy is the architecture.

On the function of non-Europeans, both make Europeans the standard of reason. For Kant, non-Europeans (the African in particular) are foils — examples of what humans look like before they have done the Enlightenment work. For Hegel, non-Europeans are stages — the African is the dawn of Spirit's history, the Asian its childhood, the European its maturity and end. The function is similar (Europeans are the destination) but the technique is different (Kant's empirical anthropology vs Hegel's metaphysical historicism).

On the implications for how we read them today, three positions are available. The first — sometimes called "the strategic separation" — argues that we should keep the philosophical core (Kant on autonomy, Hegel on dialectic) and discard the racial periphery. The trouble is that the periphery is not peripheral: Kant's *Observations* is not a stray essay; Hegel's *Lectures* is not a stray course. The racial exclusion is woven into the architecture. The second position — "the wholesale rejection" — says we should stop teaching them. This loses too much; the philosophical insights are real, and other traditions have developed in dialogue with them. The third position — which Dussel sketches — is to read Kant and Hegel against themselves: keep their universal claims, expose the irrational myth that travelled with them, and use that exposure to extend the universals to the peoples they originally excluded. This is the trans-modern reading: not less Kant and Hegel, but a more honest and more universal Kant and Hegel.

The takeaway: both Kant and Hegel placed non-European peoples below European reason, but through different argumentative mechanisms. Kant by qualifying a universal with an empirical racial hierarchy; Hegel by writing the hierarchy into the architecture of World History. Reading them today requires registering both moves — and refusing to let the canon teach Enlightenment without teaching its racial archive.

### M8: "Modernity is rational on the surface and violent underneath." Discuss with examples.

The quotation paraphrases Dussel's "myth of modernity": modernity contains "a rational 'concept' of emancipation… and an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence" (*Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66). The slogan is sharp but invites three questions: in what sense rational, in what sense violent, and how are the two related? I will work through each with examples — the Spanish conquest of Mexico, the Atlantic slave trade, the Belgian Congo, and the 2003 invasion of Iraq — and then assess the slogan's limits.

The rational surface first. Modernity's rationality is real and many-sided. It includes the rule of law (no arbitrary punishment, due process, written codes), scientific method (hypothesis, experiment, peer review), bureaucratic administration (impersonal rules, written records, qualifications by examination — Weber's rationalisation), market exchange (calculable prices, contracts, double-entry book-keeping), and political institutions of consent (representation, periodic elections, free speech). The Enlightenment slogans — Kant's "exodus from guilty immaturity", the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen*, the U.S. *Bill of Rights* — articulate this rational surface in its most appealing form. Modernity, on this view, is the slow extension of reason against superstition, of consent against arbitrariness, of progress against stagnation.

The violent underneath comes next. Dussel's argument is that the same modern Europe that produced the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* in 1789 ran slave plantations in Saint-Domingue, where the *Code Noir* (1685) authorised the lash, the iron collar and the death penalty for runaways. The same Britain that produced parliamentary democracy enclosed common lands, sent convicts to Australia, killed in the Indian Famine of 1876–78, and built its industrial base on slave-grown cotton from the American South. The same Belgium that hosted the Brussels Conference against slavery in 1890 ran the Congo Free State as a personal possession of King Leopold II from 1885 to 1908, with a death toll estimated by Adam Hochschild at ten million. The rational surface is not fake — the institutions of due process were real for those they covered — but it was not extended to those modernity called barbarians.

The relation between surface and underneath is the heart of Dussel's argument. The seven-step "myth of modernity" (p. 75) explains how the two coexist without contradiction in the European conscience. Step 1: Europe is the most developed. Step 2: this obliges Europe to civilise. Step 3: the path of civilisation is Europe's path. Step 4: where the barbarian resists, violence is necessary. Step 5: the violence is ritualised as sacrifice — the victim is participating in his own redemption. Step 6: the victim is in a state of guilt for resisting. Step 7: the costs are inevitable and necessary. Each step converts a violent act into a rational duty. The conscience stays clean.

The examples test the structure. The Spanish conquest of Mexico (1519–1521): Cortés framed the destruction of Tenochtitlán as the lifting of pagan tyranny and the bringing of Christianity. The conquistadores carried law codes and chaplains. The Aztecs, in Cortés's narrative, were ruled by demonic priests and welcomed liberation. The thirty million indigenous deaths over the next century — from smallpox, forced labour and direct killing — were, in Dussel's terms, "inevitable" costs of redemption.

The Atlantic slave trade (1500s–1800s): philosophers and economists wrote in defence of the trade not as a regrettable necessity but as a positive duty. Africans were lifted from heathen darkness; they would learn Christianity; their labour produced wealth for the world economy. Adam Smith disliked slavery on economic grounds but did not call it murder. Locke owned shares in the Royal African Company. The rational surface — property rights, contract, free trade — covered a violent underneath in which twelve million Africans were transported in chains and perhaps two million died on the Middle Passage.

Belgian Congo (1885–1908): Leopold II's Congo Free State was branded as a humanitarian project — the suppression of the Arab slave trade in East Africa, the bringing of Western civilisation to the Congo Basin. The Belgian state, the British Foreign Office, the Vatican and the U.S. Senate all endorsed Leopold's stewardship in the 1880s. Underneath the philanthropic rhetoric, rubber quotas were enforced by mutilation: villages that failed to deliver had hands and feet cut off, and ten million died. The exposure by E.D. Morel and Roger Casement is itself a modern story — modern investigative journalism shining light on modern violence — but the violence had to be exposed; it was not legible from the rational surface.

Iraq 2003: the rhetoric was emancipatory — democracy, weapons-of-mass-destruction abolition, women's rights, freedom. The means were violent — shock-and-awe bombing, Abu Ghraib torture, an occupation that killed perhaps half a million Iraqis. Dussel's seven steps fit: the U.S. as most developed; the obligation to civilise; the Iraqi regime as barbarian; the resistance requires violence; the costs are unfortunate but inevitable. The slogan "rational on the surface, violent underneath" describes the U.S. invasion almost exactly.

Limits of the slogan. Three. First, not all of modernity is violent; many modern institutions deliver on their rational promises within their borders. Second, not all violence is uniquely modern; pre-modern societies were also violent. Third, the slogan can flatten differences between historical periods and instances. Spanish conquest and U.S. invasion share a structure but differ in technologies, justifications and aftermaths.

But the slogan still holds. Modernity is rational on the surface and violent underneath because the rational surface was produced for some by extracting from others, and the universal claim — equal moral worth, due process, consent — was never extended to those modernity called barbarians. Dussel's call to "trans-modernity" (p. 76) is the call to make the universal universal by finally including the people from whom it was originally withheld.

### M9: To what extent does the date 1492 reframe what we mean by modernity?

Dating modernity is not just an antiquarian dispute. The date you pick determines which events count as constitutive, which thinkers belong to the canon, and which problems count as central. The standard textbook dates modernity from the Reformation (1517), the Scientific Revolution (1543), the Treaty of Westphalia (1648), the Enlightenment (eighteenth century), or the French and Industrial Revolutions (1789, 1760–1840). Dussel proposes 1492 — the conquest of the Kingdom of Granada and Columbus's first voyage. I want to assess how much of modernity is reframed by this shift and where the reframing reaches its limits.

Three things change immediately when you accept 1492.

First, the geography of modernity's origin moves south. Spain and Portugal — the two powers Hegel and Habermas both dismiss as peripheral to modernity (Dussel, p. 71, p. 74 fn) — return to the centre of the story. Iberian conquest, Iberian missions, Iberian slavery and Iberian gold are no longer prelude to the real modernity in Northern Europe; they are the founding events. The Atlantic, not the North Sea, is modernity's first sea. The Catholic monarchies of Castile and Aragon, not the Protestant principalities of Germany, are the first modern states. This reorients the literature: figures like Bartolomé de las Casas, Francisco de Vitoria, José de Acosta, and Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala become central rather than marginal.

Second, the role of conquest in the constitution of modern subjectivity becomes visible. Dussel's central thesis is that "modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other" (*Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66). The modern European subject — confident, rational, world-historical — is constituted not by introspection (Descartes in his stove) but by conquest (Cortés in Tenochtitlán). The *cogito* is preceded by the *conquiro*: I conquer, therefore I am. The reframing is philosophical: not just an addition to the curriculum but a different account of where modern subjectivity comes from.

Third, the violence of modernity becomes legible from the beginning. If modernity is dated from the Reformation or the Enlightenment, slavery and conquest can be treated as regrettable side-effects of an essentially rational project. If modernity is dated from 1492, slavery and conquest are constitutive. The "myth of modernity" — Dussel's seven-step structure (p. 75) — is not an interpretation imposed retrospectively; it describes the practical logic of the original event.

These three changes are major. But the reframing has limits.

The first limit is that 1492 is not the only available "deep" date. Some scholars push earlier — to the Mediterranean revolution of the long thirteenth century (Janet Abu-Lughod), to the Mongol world-system of the fourteenth century (Andre Gunder Frank), to the Crusades. Others date modernity later — to the seventeenth-century scientific revolution, the eighteenth-century financial revolutions, or the nineteenth-century industrial take-off. 1492 is one option among several, and its plausibility depends on what aspect of modernity you want to centre. For the colonial relation, 1492 is hard to beat. For the rise of capitalism strictly defined, the late eighteenth century is better. For the consolidation of the modern state, 1648 has a claim.

The second limit is that 1492 risks collapsing the differences between Iberian, Northern European and Anglo-Atlantic modernities. The Spanish *encomienda* system, the Dutch joint-stock company, the British plantation, the French *mission civilisatrice* and the U.S. settler-colonial state are all different forms of modern colonial power. Dating them all to 1492 captures their family resemblance but elides their specificities. A more careful periodisation would distinguish phases — early Iberian conquest (1492–1600), Dutch and British commercial expansion (1600–1750), high industrial imperialism (1750–1900), formal decolonisation (1945–present) — while keeping 1492 as the founding event.

The third limit is that 1492 does not by itself produce the philosophical canon shifts. To make the reframing analytic, you also need to read Vitoria, Las Casas, Guamán Poma, Du Bois, C.L.R. James, Aimé Césaire, Fanon and Dussel into the canon alongside Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant and Hegel. The dating provides the occasion; the curriculum work is separate.

So how much does 1492 reframe? In my view, substantially but not totally. It reframes:

- The geography of origins (Iberian peninsula, not Northern Europe).
- The role of conquest in modern subjectivity.
- The visibility of modernity's violence.
- The pertinent canon (extending it to Iberian, Latin American, African and Afro-diasporic thinkers).
- The standard sociological periodisation that has Marx-Weber-Durkheim explaining "modernity" as a nineteenth-century European phenomenon.

It does not reframe:

- The technical analytic categories of capitalism, bureaucracy and individuation, which still operate.
- The differences between phases of European colonial power, which 1492 alone cannot capture.
- The internal European story of the Reformation, scientific revolution and political revolutions, which still belong to any history of modernity.

The strongest case for 1492 is that it shifts the centre of gravity. Once you accept it, the question of modernity is no longer "what makes European societies advanced?" but "what makes European societies global?" — and once that question is asked, the colonial relation is no longer optional. Dussel summarises it in one sentence: "Latin America is thus the 'other-face' (teixtli, in Aztec), the essential alterity of modernity" (p. 74). Modernity is not a European achievement first and a world-historical project second. It is a world-historical project from the beginning, with Europe at the centre and the periphery as its constitutive outside. That is what 1492 buys you.

### M10: Discuss Marx's claim that "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" as a critique of Eurocentric sociology.

The lecture cites Marx's *Selected Writings*, ed. McLellan, p. 203: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." The lecturer presents this as a partial exception within classical sociology — the one founder who saw the colonial constitution of modern capitalism. I want to take that exception seriously: what does Marx's claim deliver as a critique of Eurocentric sociology, and what does it leave undone?

What Marx delivers, in three steps.

First, structural integration. The slavery–cotton–industry chain is not a moralising aside; it is an analytical claim about the relations of production. Modern industrial capitalism, on Marx's account, requires cheap raw materials at scale. The scale is supplied by the plantation system; the cheapness is supplied by slave labour. The relation between Manchester and Mississippi is therefore not metaphorical or contingent; it is structural. The cotton mill that defines modern industry could not have operated on the volumes and prices it did without the slave plantation that supplied it. Marx delivers what Dussel and Hall later theorised: modernity is constitutively colonial.

Second, the line of historical causation. "It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." Read carefully, this is a four-step causation: slavery → colonial value → world trade → large-scale industry. Slavery is at the start; large-scale industry is at the end. The Eurocentric narrative inverts this, placing European industrial dynamism at the start and treating slavery and the colonies as later consequences. Marx's ordering puts slavery first. This single sentence reorganises the standard story.

Third, the implication for class analysis. If the slave-grown cotton funded the wages and working conditions of the British industrial proletariat, then the European working class is partly a beneficiary of slavery, not just a victim of capitalism. Du Bois develops this into the explicit claim that "the European and white American working class was practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970, pp. 107–108, cited in the lecture slides). Marx's quoted sentence sets up Du Bois's elaboration. The result is a more honest class analysis: the European proletariat is positioned within a global racial division of labour, not just a national capital–labour division.

Three things Marx's quoted sentence does, then: integrates colonial slavery into the structure of capitalism, reorders the causal sequence, and complicates the class analysis.

But there are also three things it does not do.

First, it does not fully escape the developmental schema. Marx's broader theory of historical stages — primitive communism, slave society, feudalism, capitalism, socialism — treats the European trajectory as the universal sequence. India, China and pre-conquest Africa are slotted into the "Asiatic mode of production" or other residual categories. His writings on British rule in India sometimes welcomed colonialism for breaking up "static" Asiatic forms and pushing them onto the universal road to capitalism. The quoted sentence about cotton does not undo this; Marx remains Eurocentric in his overall periodisation even while he is anti-Eurocentric in his economic analysis. The exception is partial.

Second, it does not theorise race. Marx names slavery and the colonies but does not develop a theory of racial hierarchy as an internal feature of capitalism. Why was the labour racially marked? Why did African slavery in particular consolidate? Why does racial division persist in the wage-labour markets of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries? Marx's tools — relations of production, class struggle, accumulation — are not sufficient. Later thinkers — Cedric Robinson's "racial capitalism", Stuart Hall on race as the "modality through which class is lived" — have to do the additional work.

Third, it does not propose a politics of decolonisation. Marx's politics is the working-class revolution in the industrial heartland. The colonies, on the standard reading, are addenda that will be liberated when the metropolitan workers seize the means of production. The agency of the colonised — the Haitian Revolution, the anti-colonial movements of the twentieth century, contemporary indigenous resistance — is not theorised in the quoted sentence. Du Bois, Lenin (on imperialism), Fanon, and the dependency theorists fill in what Marx left empty.

Even so, what the sentence does is a serious critique of Eurocentric sociology. The lecture is right to single Marx out as a possible exception. Three lessons follow.

The first is methodological. Sociology that takes modernity seriously cannot stop at the European borders. The unit of analysis has to be the world-system in which European industry, American slavery, African gold and Indian cotton are integrated.

The second is pedagogical. Teaching Marx without the cotton-slavery sentence — teaching only the abstract commodity, alienation and ideology — produces a clean European Marx whose anti-Eurocentric insight is invisible. Teaching the cotton sentence alongside the abstract analysis recovers a more honest Marx and a more accurate Marxism.

The third is political. If modern industry is built on slavery, then contemporary debates about reparations, structural adjustment, sovereign debt and the unequal exchange between Global North and Global South are not separate from class struggle in the metropole; they are continuous with it. Solidarity across the colour line and across the metropolitan border is not a moral demand bolted onto class analysis; it is a structural feature of the analysis itself.

Marx is not Dussel, and his quoted sentence is not the same as the full postcolonial reframing of modernity. But it is more than a footnote. It is the moment at which classical sociology gestures toward what it could have become if it had taken the colonial constitution of modernity as a founding question rather than a side-topic.

### M11: Compare Dussel and Howard French on the relation between Africa and modernity.

Enrique Dussel and Howard W. French both argue that Africa is constitutive of modernity, not external to it. They are unlike in genre: Dussel is a Latin American philosopher writing a short polemical introduction to a series of philosophical lectures; French is an American journalist and historian writing a long popular work of historical reconstruction. They are also unlike in geographical emphasis: Dussel centres Latin America and treats Africa as a parallel case; French centres Africa and tracks how Africans made the modern Atlantic world. Reading them together gives a more complete account than either alone.

Dussel's central thesis, in *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, is that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (p. 65). Africa, in this framework, is one of the constitutive peripheries. Dussel attends to it mainly through his reading of Hegel, who excludes Africa from World History altogether: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again" (Hegel, *Lectures*, cited in Dussel, p. 70). The exclusion is part of how modernity thinks itself, and the work of postcolonial theory is to restore the excluded periphery to view.

Dussel's strength is the philosophical diagnostic. He shows that the exclusion of Africa is not a contingent oversight but is built into the architecture of canonical modern philosophy. Hegel's "Africa is in general a closed land" (p. 70) and "[The negro] is the man as beast" (p. 70) are not stray prejudices but structural premises that make the Hegelian system run. To restore Africa is therefore to reorganise modernity's self-understanding from the ground up.

Dussel's weakness is the empirical specificity. Because he is working philosophically, he treats Africa mostly as the object of European philosophical exclusion rather than as a continent of agents whose actions made modernity. We learn what Hegel said about Africa; we learn less about what Africans did.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* is the complement. French centres African agency in three moves.

First, fifteenth-century gold. The Portuguese push down the West African coast was driven by the search for the gold of Mali, Ghana and the Akan forest. African gold, not American silver, underwrote the first phase of European mercantilism. The Mali Empire under Mansa Musa (early fourteenth century) was already a global power; his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 destabilised Mediterranean gold markets. African gold financed Portuguese expansion, which in turn produced the maritime technology and capital that funded Columbus.

Second, fifteenth- to nineteenth-century slavery. Twelve million Africans were transported in the trans-Atlantic slave trade; another two million died on the Middle Passage. The slave-grown sugar, tobacco and cotton of São Tomé, Brazil, the Caribbean and the American South generated the consumer markets, the financial instruments and the industrial raw materials that defined the modern Atlantic economy. French traces specific institutions back to specific African origins: Liverpool's banks to Liverpool's slave-trading houses, Lloyd's insurance market to slave-cargo insurance, the Bank of England's early balance sheet to Royal African Company dividends.

Third, African agency in shaping the trade. Kongo, Benin, Dahomey, Asante and Songhai were sophisticated polities whose internal politics, military capacities and commercial ambitions shaped what the Europeans could and could not do on the coast. The slave trade was not a one-sided European action on a passive Africa; it was a negotiated and contested system in which African elites participated, profited, and sometimes resisted. This is important because it complicates the narrative of pure European agency without exonerating Europeans of moral responsibility for the system as a whole.

French's strength is the empirical concreteness. He names the gold, the ports, the ships, the institutions, the African polities, the European companies, the financial instruments. The reader finishes the book unable to think of Liverpool, Manchester, Bristol, Amsterdam, Lisbon or Nantes without thinking of Benin, Kongo, Elmina, Senegambia and the Bight of Biafra.

French's weakness — relative to Dussel — is the philosophical generalisation. He shows that Africa was constitutive of modernity; he does not theorise the structure of European self-understanding that erased that constitution. The "myth of modernity" framework that Dussel supplies is largely absent from French.

Read together, the two complete each other. French gives the empirical body — what Africans did, what European institutions extracted, how the Atlantic system worked. Dussel gives the philosophical skeleton — how the exclusion of Africa was built into modern philosophy and how modernity legitimates the violence that produced it. The empirical body without the philosophical skeleton risks reading as a series of facts in search of a structure; the skeleton without the body risks reading as a polemic in search of evidence.

The combined position has political traction. Contemporary debates about the return of African artefacts (the Benin bronzes, looted by British troops in 1897; the Maqdala loot from Ethiopia; the German colonial collections in Berlin) are unintelligible without French's restoration of African agency. The reluctance of European museums and governments to engage with these claims is unintelligible without Dussel's analysis of how modern European thought legitimises its own past. The reparations debate is the empirical-philosophical hybrid that both readings point towards: a reckoning that is both about specific extractions (French) and about the architecture of self-justification that allowed them (Dussel).

Africa is not modernity's exception. It is modernity's foundation, both economically (French) and as the negated other whose negation made European self-understanding possible (Dussel). Reading the two together is the first step towards a sociology of modernity that does not repeat the Hegelian erasure.

### M12: Discuss how the language of "developed" and "developing" countries continues the colonial project today, with examples.

The vocabulary of "developed", "developing", "least developed" and "emerging" countries is so naturalised in policy documents, news reports and academic writing that it is easy to forget how recent and how loaded it is. Dussel calls it the "fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture. Development is taken here as an ontological, and not simply a sociological or economic, category" (*Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 67–68). I want to argue that this language continues the colonial project today, and to do so with concrete examples from the World Bank, the UN, the OECD, the media and the academy.

The argument first. Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (p. 75) is encoded into the development vocabulary:

- Step 1: Europe (now generalised to "the West", "advanced economies", "the OECD") is the most developed.
- Step 2: this obliges the developed to develop the underdeveloped.
- Step 3: the path of development is the one Europe took.
- Step 4: where the underdeveloped resist, conditionalities, sanctions and in extreme cases military intervention are necessary.
- Step 5: the costs are ritualised as the unfortunate but inevitable price of catching up.
- Step 6: the underdeveloped are in a state of guilt for their underdevelopment ("corruption", "bad institutions", "rent-seeking elites").
- Step 7: the costs are inevitable.

Each step is visible in the institutional vocabulary, sometimes literally. The World Bank's classification — high-income, upper-middle-income, lower-middle-income, low-income — is a ranking that maps directly onto step 1 and step 3. The UN's Human Development Index ranks countries on a single scale anchored at Norway and Switzerland and descending to the Central African Republic and South Sudan. The IMF's structural adjustment conditionalities — privatisation, currency convertibility, fiscal discipline, trade liberalisation — operationalise step 3: do as we did. The "good governance" agenda operationalises step 6: your problems are your fault. And humanitarian intervention — Libya 2011, Iraq 2003 — operationalises step 4.

Five examples make this concrete.

First, the World Bank. Its World Development Report each year ranks countries by GDP per capita and assesses progress on a battery of indicators standardised on the OECD experience. The vocabulary is technical but the structure is hierarchical: countries are evaluated by how closely they approximate the high-income reference group. The implicit message: there is one path, and the further you are from it, the further behind you are. Dussel's step 3 is institutionalised in the indicator set.

Second, the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Adopted in 2015 to cover 2015–2030, the SDGs are universal in form: every country reports on every goal. But the assessment is structured around the high-income outcome — life expectancy at OECD levels, full primary enrolment, gender parity benchmarks set by Northern European norms, carbon emissions reductions calibrated to industrial-economy baselines. The SDG vocabulary is more inclusive than the older Millennium Development Goals it replaced, but the hierarchy is still encoded.

Third, the IMF and structural adjustment. In the 1980s and 1990s, the IMF and World Bank attached conditions to loans for poor countries: open your markets, privatise your state-owned enterprises, devalue your currency, cut your public spending. The results in sub-Saharan Africa included declining real wages, deteriorating health systems, and stalled industrialisation. The structural-adjustment vocabulary — "reform", "discipline", "transparency" — is the technical face of Dussel's "civilising" imperative. Step 6 is the explanatory move: when the reforms failed, the failure was attributed to local corruption, bad governance, or insufficient commitment to reform, not to the reforms themselves.

Fourth, the media. The BBC, CNN, Reuters and AFP file stories from "the developing world" with a different visual and narrative grammar than stories from "advanced economies". A drought in Ethiopia is framed as a humanitarian crisis; the same level of agricultural disruption in Spain or Australia is framed as a policy challenge. Conflicts in Africa are coded as "tribal" or "ethnic"; conflicts in Europe are coded as "political". The same vocabulary that Stuart Hall analysed in "the West and the Rest" — west = developed = good, non-west = under-developed = bad (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277) — is reproduced in news taxonomies.

Fifth, the academy. Development studies, comparative politics and economic history routinely use the "developed/developing" binary as a baseline analytic. Comparative tables list OECD countries first and "developing countries" second. Theoretical models derived from European or U.S. data are tested in "developing-country contexts" with adjustments for "institutional weakness". Dependency theorists, world-systems theorists, and postcolonial scholars have been critiquing this for decades; the critique has made headway but has not yet displaced the standard vocabulary.

What are the alternatives? Three are visible.

First, geographical and regional descriptors that avoid the developmental ranking — "Global South", "Latin America", "sub-Saharan Africa". These are not free of difficulties (the "Global South" is itself a category with its own simplifications) but they at least do not encode a ladder.

Second, structural descriptors — "core", "periphery", "semi-periphery" (Wallerstein's world-systems vocabulary). These name a position in a global division of labour without implying that the periphery is at fault or that the core is the destination.

Third, plural-modernity descriptors that recognise multiple paths — Dussel's "trans-modernity" (p. 76), Eisenstadt's "multiple modernities", Bhambra's "connected sociologies". These resist the unilinear assumption that there is one modernity to which all are ascending.

The lecturer's prompt — "think of other examples of how a 'colonial attitude' plays out today" — is what this essay is responding to. The developed/developing vocabulary is not a neutral technical taxonomy; it is the contemporary face of the colonial attitude. To dismantle it requires not just terminological substitution but the substantive analysis Dussel, Hall and the dependency tradition supply. The colonies are gone; their attitude lives on in the metric.

### M13: To what extent is "trans-modernity" (Dussel) a plausible alternative to both modernity and postmodernism?

Dussel ends *Eurocentrism and Modernity* with the proposal of "trans-modernity": "a process that will transcend modernity as such, a trans-modernity, in which both modernity and its negated alterity (the victims) co-realize themselves in a process of mutual creative fertilization" (p. 76). The proposal is ambitious — it claims to overcome both modernity's irrational myth and postmodernism's relativist scepticism. I want to assess how plausible this is by laying out the position, comparing it with its rivals, and considering three lines of objection.

First, the position. Trans-modernity rests on Dussel's two-layer reading of modernity. Layer one: a rational concept of emancipation, which Dussel "affirms and subsumes" (p. 66). Layer two: an irrational myth, which legitimates genocidal violence, which Dussel negates (p. 66). Modernity is internally split. The way out is not to discard modernity entirely (the postmodern move) nor to defend it without revision (the Habermasian move) but to keep the rational kernel while exposing and rejecting the myth, and to do so by bringing the negated alterity — the colonised, the slave, the woman, the child, subalternised popular cultures — back into the dialogue. Trans-modernity is "a project of political, economic, ecological, erotic, pedagogical, and religious liberation… an incorporative solidarity… between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (p. 76).

Second, the rivals.

Postmodernism — Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo — proposes to discard the universal claims of modern reason. There are only local language games, partial perspectives, ironic conversations. Dussel rejects this. He notes Rorty's "we liberal Americans" starting point and argues that for the colonised who cannot "intervene effectively" in such a conversation, "not even conversation is possible" (p. 76 fn). Postmodern relativism, on Dussel's reading, is a luxury available only to those already inside the West, and it disarms critique by denying the universals that the colonised need to articulate their innocence.

Habermasian modernity — extended in the recent literature by, among others, Seyla Benhabib and Axel Honneth — proposes to defend modernity's rational kernel by repairing its internal pathologies through more inclusive deliberation. Dussel respects the project but argues that it stays inside the Eurocentric frame. Habermas's genealogy still excludes 1492; his interlocutors are still European; the colonised are still objects of solidarity rather than subjects of critique. Habermasian modernity, on Dussel's reading, is not wrong but too narrow.

Trans-modernity claims to take what is right from both. Like Habermas, it keeps the rational universal. Like the postmodernists, it exposes the violence of modern reason. But it goes further than either by demanding that the negated alterity speak in the universal. The point is not to give up on universalism but to make it actually universal.

Third, three objections.

Objection one: vagueness. Trans-modernity is sketched in a single paragraph at the end of a short essay. It is named as "incorporative solidarity" and "mutual creative fertilization" but not institutionally specified. What would a trans-modern university look like? A trans-modern legal system? A trans-modern global economic order? Dussel's later books — *The Invention of the Americas*, *Ethics of Liberation*, *Twenty Theses on Politics* — flesh some of this out, but the introductory essay leaves the proposal at the level of programmatic gesture. The reply: programmatic gestures are not nothing. They name a direction. The institutional work follows.

Objection two: agency. Who is the agent of trans-modernity? Dussel says the "negated and victimized 'other-face' of modernity… must, in the first place, discover itself as innocent" (p. 76). But how? Through what political organisation, what intellectual production, what cultural transformation? The Latin American liberation movements of the 1970s and 1980s, in dialogue with which Dussel wrote, supplied one answer. The Bolivian *Movimiento al Socialismo* and the Ecuadorian indigenous movement supplied another in the 2000s. Black Lives Matter, Standing Rock and contemporary anti-colonial movements supply versions today. Trans-modernity has agents, but they are plural and contested. The reply: this is a feature, not a bug. Postcolonial liberation is not a single global movement with a vanguard; it is a constellation of struggles whose convergence is itself the project.

Objection three: redemption. Dussel's vocabulary — "co-realization", "innocent victim", "irrational myth", "redemptive sacrifice" — comes from Christian theology, specifically from his liberation-theology background. Critics worry that this Christian framing limits the proposal's appeal across traditions and reproduces a salvific structure that Dussel claims to critique. The reply: Dussel is candid about the genealogy. He does not require his readers to accept the theology; the analytical claims can be detached. But the language carries a normative texture — innocence, guilt, sacrifice, redemption — that may be unwelcome to secular and non-Christian readers.

How plausible, then, is trans-modernity? In my view, plausible as a regulative ideal, less plausible as a fully worked-out programme. As a regulative ideal — keep the universal, reject the myth, include the excluded — it is more defensible than either postmodernism or Habermasian modernity because it does not require the colonised to give up on universal claims (the postmodern price) and does not require Europeans to maintain the colonial occlusion (the Habermasian price). As a programme, it needs the institutional and political work Dussel does not, in this essay, undertake.

Three contemporary developments suggest the regulative ideal is doing real work. First, the decolonising-the-curriculum movement is a trans-modern educational project: keep the canon, expose its exclusions, include the excluded voices. Second, the reparations debate is a trans-modern economic project: keep the rule of law, recognise the unrepaid debts of colonial extraction, build redistribution into the legal order. Third, the indigenous-rights movements at the UN are trans-modern political projects: keep universal human rights, extend them to indigenous nations, reorganise the state on a plurinational basis.

Each of these is partial and contested. None is uniquely Dusselian. But each operates inside the structure Dussel named in 1993: keep the rational kernel, expose the myth, include the excluded. That structure has aged well. Trans-modernity is not a complete answer to the question of what comes after modernity, but it is a workable framework for what should come next. As a regulative ideal it is plausible. As a programme it is unfinished. That is a reasonable place for a critical project to be.

(~745 words)
