## 1. Key Concepts and Thinkers

- **Modernity:** "a way of thinking, acting and living which had and still has enormous and unequal consequences for the world and the peoples of the world" (lecture slides). Tied to industrialisation, urbanisation, individualisation, rationality and the break with tradition.
- **The colonial attitude:** a habit of thought, surviving political independence, that ranks societies as superior/inferior, modern/backward.
- **Eurocentrism:** the assumption that modernity is an exclusively European phenomenon and that European development is the universal model.
- **The myth of modernity (Dussel):** modernity packages emancipation with a concealed irrational myth that justifies genocidal violence on a "civilising" mission.
- **Fallacy of developmentalism (Dussel):** the idea that every culture must follow Europe's ontological path of development.
- **The West and the Rest (Hall):** a discourse, not a geography. A "system of representation" producing binary oppositions: western/non-western = developed/under-developed = good/bad.
- **Bifurcated thinking:** the colonial attitude sorts the world into two camps.
- **Multiple modernities / trans-modernity (Dussel):** alternative routes that refuse Europe as the sole template.
- **Classical-sociology trio:** Marx (commodification, alienation, slavery-cotton-industry chain), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment), Durkheim (mechanical to organic solidarity).
- **Hegel:** world-historical Spirit moves East to West; Africa is "outside history"; Europe holds an "absolute right".
- **Kant:** Enlightenment as exodus from "guilty immaturity"; racialised remarks on "the Negroes of Africa".
- **Du Bois:** before the 15th century there was "no great disparity" between Negroland and Europe; slavery built world trade.

## 3. Information from the Readings

### Enrique Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*

**Central thesis.** Modernity is a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity. Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the "centre" of a World History it inaugurates; the "periphery" is part of its self-definition. By occluding the periphery (and the role of Spain and Portugal from the late 15th to mid-17th centuries), the major thinkers of the centre commit a Eurocentric fallacy.

**1492 as the birth date.** Dussel dates modernity to 1492: the conquest of the Kingdom of Granada and Columbus's voyage. Modernity is "born" when Europe is in a position to pose itself against an other. The other is not "dis-covered" but "covered-up", concealed as the same as what Europe assumed it had always been.

**The myth of modernity.** Modernity contains a rational concept of emancipation that Dussel affirms and subsumes, but it also develops an irrational myth: a justification for genocidal violence. Postmodernists criticise modern reason as terror; Dussel criticises modern reason because of the irrational myth it conceals.

**Fallacy of developmentalism.** The fallacy consists in thinking that Europe's path of development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture. Development is taken as an ontological (not merely sociological) category. For Hegel, an "underdeveloped" country is "not-modern", pre-Aufklärung.

**Kant.** "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." Dussel asks: should the African slave, the Mexican Indian, the Latin American mestizo be considered guilty for their immaturity?

**Hegel.** World History is the self-realisation of Spirit, Reason, and Freedom. It moves East to West. Latin America and Africa are "outside" history. Asia is the dawn but not the culmination. Northern Europe (Germany, France, Denmark, Scandinavia) is the heart. The Germanic Spirit, refracted through the Lutheran Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution, is the culmination.

**The right of empire.** For Hegel, the people that bears the Spirit at its current moment has an "absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights". Dussel sees this as the philosophical sacralisation of imperial power over the periphery: a "limitless cynicism" masked as the development of reason.

**Colonies and civil society.** Hegel argues that civil society's internal contradictions push it to seek new consumers and colonise peoples "inferior" with respect to its industry. Colonisation allows surplus poor to become property owners abroad. Hegel does not register that this requires seizing land from others.

**Habermas continues the occlusion.** Habermas locates modernity's principle in the Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution. The discovery of America is mentioned but assigned no constitutive role. Habermas inherits Hegel's discounting of Spain and Portugal.

**The seven elements of the myth of modernity.**
1. European civilisation understands itself as the most developed and superior.
2. This obliges it, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the "primitive".
3. The path of development is Europe's own out of antiquity and the Middle Ages.
4. Where the "barbarian" resists, modernity must, in the last instance, recourse to violence.
5. The violence is ritualistic: victims (colonised, slave, woman, the earth) are cast as participants in redemptive sacrifice.
6. The barbarian is in a state of guilt for opposing civilisation; modernity is innocent and emancipatory.
7. The sufferings and sacrifices of modernisation imposed on immature peoples are inevitable and necessary.

**Trans-modernity.** Dussel proposes neither postmodern relativism nor a reaffirmation of European reason but a "trans-modernity": a co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (the colonial periphery, the Indian, the slave, the woman, the child, subalternised popular cultures). They must first "discover themselves as innocent" of the originary violence.

### Stuart Hall, *Formations of Modernity* (West and the Rest)

**The West as historical, not geographical, construct.** "Western" denotes a type of society: developed, industrialised, urbanised, capitalist, secular, modern. Its meaning is "virtually identical" to the word "modern". Japan can be "western"; Latin America, in the western hemisphere, is not.

**The West as concept**, four functions:
1. Characterises and classifies societies (western vs non-western), a tool to think with.
2. Condenses a set of images: a system of representation. Western = urban = developed; non-western = rural = under-developed.
3. Provides a standard of comparison: how close to or far from the West?
4. Provides criteria of evaluation: the West = good = desirable; non-West = bad = undesirable. It functions as an ideology.

**Mutual constitution.** "The West and the Rest became two sides of a single coin." The uniqueness of the West was produced through Europe's contact and self-comparison with non-western societies. Identity is formed by difference (Saussure on night/day; psychoanalysis on the infant and the mother).

**Discourse and binary opposition.** Hall reads "West and Rest" as a discourse that simplifies, representing differentiated Europe as homogeneous (the West) and grouping equally diverse societies (Middle East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous America, Australasia) as one Rest. That over-simplification is precisely what makes the discourse destructive.

**Internal others.** Hall notes the West also had its internal others: Jews, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", women. The dichotomy is not only outward-facing.

**Periodisation.** Expansion is dated from Portuguese coastal exploration (1430-1498) and Columbus (1492-1502). Michael Mann's claim that Europe "overtook" Asia around 1450 is cited. The five phases (exploration; early contact and conquest; settlement; imperial consolidation; decolonisation) frame the West-Rest relationship as a long history of asymmetric encounter.

### Howard W. French, *Born in Blackness*

**Restoring Africa to modernity's origin story.** French argues that the conventional story of modernity (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) leaves out the constitutive role of Africa and Africans. The Portuguese push down the West African coast in the 15th century was driven by the search for gold and African slave labour, not just spices or a route to Asia. African gold (Mali, Ghana) helped underwrite European mercantilism.

**Slavery and the making of the Atlantic world.** Sugar plantations in São Tomé, the Caribbean and Brazil, staffed by enslaved Africans, generated unprecedented surplus and demand. The capital, commodities and consumer markets of the early modern economy were forged on this base. The cotton mills of Lancashire, the docks of Liverpool, the financial instruments of London and Amsterdam, the banks that survived to the present, all draw lines back to the slave economy.

**Africa as agent, not victim.** French insists Africa is not merely passive raw material. Kongo, Benin, Asante, Songhai were sophisticated polities whose engagements with Europeans shaped the trade. The argument supports Dussel's claim that the periphery is constitutive of the centre, and Hall's claim that the West is the product of relations with the Rest.

**Erasure as method.** Like Dussel's "covered-up" other and Hall's discourse of simplification, French highlights the active forgetting in European historiography: Africa "born in blackness" written out of modernity's account of itself.

## 4. Critical Analysis with Embedded Key Quotes

The central provocation of L2 is that modernity is not innocent. It is sociology's founding object, but the discipline that names it has also concealed its dependence on colonial extraction. Three moves are crucial.

First, Dussel reframes 1492 as modernity's birthdate. He writes:

> "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity… modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other." (Dussel, 1993: 66)

This relocates modernity's origin from the Reformation and Enlightenment to colonial conquest. Habermas and Hegel both fail this test:

> "For Habermas, as for Hegel, the discovery of America is not a constitutive fact of modernity." (Dussel, 1993: 74)

Second, the lecture and Dussel converge on the "myth of modernity": rational emancipation is real but inseparable from sacrificial violence:

> "Modernity includes a rational 'concept' of emancipation that we affirm and subsume. But, at the same time, it develops an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence." (Dussel, 1993: 66)

The seven-step "myth" (superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault) gives me a checklist for reading any modernising rhetoric, from nineteenth-century missions to twenty-first-century "regime change". The lecture asks pointedly: Iraq, the Falklands, Saudi Arabia?

Third, the violence has explicit textual sources in canonical European philosophy. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* sounds emancipatory until paired with his racial remarks:

> "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." (Kant, cited in Dussel, 1993: 68)

> "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous." (Kant, 1798: 59)

Hegel goes further, treating Africa as outside history altogether:

> "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again." (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70)

> "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning." (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69)

The right of empire follows directly:

> "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights." (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73)

Stuart Hall completes the picture by showing that even when we are not citing Hegel, we are speaking through his categories. The discourse of "the West and the Rest":

> "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy, the West/the Rest. That is what makes the discourse of 'the West and the Rest' so destructive: it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'." (Hall, 1992: 280)

Marx is the partial exception within classical sociology. He saw the chain:

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." (Marx, 1977: 203)

And Du Bois pushed against the colonial attitude well before postcolonial theory:

> "In the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe." (Du Bois, 1970: 86, cited in the lecture slides)

Critically: the colonial attitude is not an embarrassing relic. The lecturer's prompt, to look at sport, music, fashion, university league tables, shows that the same hierarchies still shape evaluation. We file Bach as universal and gamelan as "world music". We assume "developing" countries should follow our path. The intellectual task of L2 is to denaturalise that assumption and recover the constitutive role of the colonised in making the modern world.

## 8. Essay Answers

### 2025 (Q1): Is it possible to fully understand modernity without understanding the role of colonialism in its development?

No, and arguing otherwise requires a curiously selective view of history. To "fully" understand modernity is to understand both its conceptual self-image (reason, progress, autonomy, the secular state) and its material conditions of possibility (industrial capitalism, world trade, mass consumption, the modern state). Colonialism is constitutive of both. I will defend this through three lenses (Dussel's philosophical genealogy, Hall's discursive analysis, and Howard French's economic history) before answering the obvious counter-argument that Europe's internal transformations suffice as an explanation.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* makes the strongest philosophical case. He argues that modernity "appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). The European ego that thinks itself modern only becomes thinkable when it can pose itself against an other. Cortés in Mexico, not Luther in Wittenberg, is the prototypical modern subject; the conquest of the New World, not the French Revolution, marks the birth of the "Will to Power specific to [Europe's] self-consciousness" (Dussel, 1993: 74). Habermas's mainstream genealogy (Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution) is, on Dussel's reading, "partial and provincial" (Dussel, 1993: 65).

Stuart Hall's 'The West and the Rest' essay translates this into a discursive register. The West is not a geography but a "system of representation" with four functions: classifying, condensing, comparing and evaluating (Hall, 1992: 277). "The so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). Said's *Orientalism* extends Hall's discursive register: "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 5), and the discourse "manage[s], and even produce[s], the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 3). This is the same operation Hall names, sharpened: modernity does not merely contrast itself with the non-modern, it produces the non-modern as the object Europe needs to be itself.

The economic argument is the most concrete. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* traces how Portugal's mid-15th-century push down the West African coast (for gold, then enslaved labour for São Tomé and later the Atlantic) supplied the capital that funded Europe's early modern boom. Sugar, tobacco and cotton, plantation crops worked by enslaved Africans, financed Liverpool, London and Amsterdam, and ultimately the steam-powered mills of Manchester. Even Marx (usually a Eurocentric witness) conceded that "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Du Bois made the same point a century earlier: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit form the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 107).

The conventional answer (that modernity is the internal achievement of European reason) runs into three problems. First, it has to suppress the texts. Kant's "Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59) and Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70) are not stray asides; they are part of the architecture of the philosophical projects in which Enlightenment is celebrated. Second, it has to suppress the money. As soon as one traces the Lancashire cotton mill backwards, the colonial relation surfaces. Third, it has to suppress the discourse. Hall's point is that even sympathetic European thinkers wrote inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The internalist story is positively misleading because it naturalises the European outcome.

A more honest counter-position concedes that colonialism was constitutive but worries that emphasising it threatens the universal goods modernity also produced. Dussel's reply is that we do not have to choose: he "affirms and subsumes" modernity's rational emancipatory concept while exposing the irrational myth that travelled with it (Dussel, 1993: 66). His proposal of "trans-modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 76) is precisely a way to retain the goods while refusing the myth.

So the answer is no. We cannot fully understand modernity without colonialism because the economic base, the philosophical self-image and the discursive grammar of the modern were all forged in the encounter between Europe and the worlds it conquered. To set colonialism aside is not to simplify the story; it is to tell a different one, the one Hegel told, and the one Habermas still tells. Both are too provincial to count as full understanding.

### 2024 (Q1): In what ways is modernity a colonial project?

To call modernity a colonial project is not to deny its rational and emancipatory ambitions; it is to argue that those ambitions cannot be separated from colonial conquest, racial hierarchy, and the violent imposition of one template on the rest of the world. I defend this thesis in four moves: modernity's economic base, its philosophical self-image, its sociological vocabulary, and its surviving "attitude" today.

The economic base is the hardest to evade. Even Marx, normally an exemplar of Eurocentric sociology, conceded: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire ran on slave-grown cotton from the American South; the sugar that sweetened London and Amsterdam came from plantations staffed by enslaved Africans. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* extends this: Portugal's 15th-century push down the West African coast, driven by gold and slaves, was a pre-condition not just of European wealth but of the financial instruments and consumer markets we call "modern". Modernity did not happen first in Europe and then spread; it was already a colonial relation in its formation.

The second move is philosophical. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* shows that the canonical European philosophers of reason wrote the colonial relation into the concept of modernity itself. He dates modernity's birth to 1492: "modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other" (Dussel, 1993: 66). Kant's exhortation to leave "guilty immaturity" is paired with his judgement that "the Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel writes the global South out of history altogether ("Africa… does not have history as such") and grants the people that "bears" Spirit at its moment an "absolute right" against which other peoples "have no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70, 73). Dussel summarises this as the "myth of modernity": modernity packages rational emancipation with an irrational, sacrificial myth that licenses violence against those who resist the civilising project. Fanon registers what this philosophical apparatus does to the colonised subject from inside: the white child's hail, "Look, a Negro!", fixes the Black body as object ("I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed", Fanon, 1967: 325). The seven-step structure Dussel draws out is not merely textual; it has a phenomenology, and Fanon supplies it.

The third move is sociological. Sociology is a creature of modernity: it arose to explain industrialisation, urbanisation and individualisation, and in doing so it inscribed the West-Rest hierarchy into its core vocabulary. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic transition treats non-European societies as pre-modern, backward. Weber's rationalisation makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Stuart Hall calls this "the West and the Rest": a discourse that "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" and produces destructive over-simplifications of difference (Hall, 1992: 280). The "West" is not a place; it is a model of comparison, an evaluative grid, an ideology. Sociology has often supplied the data for that grid.

The fourth move is contemporary. We can test whether "we" still think this way. Western Classical Music is taught as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music". Sport, fashion and the question of which are the best universities in the world surface the same habit: criteria that look neutral select the Anglo-European outcome. The same logic shows up in popular culture, where Marvel and Disney export a particular American template as "global" while non-Western cinema remains "world" film.

A counter-position argues that modernity also gave us human rights, scientific medicine and democratic accountability, gains that have benefited the colonised too. This is fair. Dussel himself "affirms and subsumes" modernity's emancipatory concept (Dussel, 1993: 66). The point is not to discard modernity but to recognise that its rational kernel has always travelled with an irrational myth, and that the rights and goods we want to keep can only be honestly defended once their colonial history is acknowledged. Modernity is a colonial project not as an insult but as a description: at its economic base, in its philosophical self-image, in its sociological vocabulary and in its surviving everyday attitudes, the modern world is unintelligible without the colonies that helped make it.

### 2024 (Q9): How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to the work of two theorists.

The question asks how European modernity benefited from unequal race relations (note "benefited", not "involved" or "depended on", though both are also true). I will answer with reference to Karl Marx and Enrique Dussel. Marx supplies the economic mechanism by which racial inequality fed into the wealth of modern Europe; Dussel supplies the philosophical mechanism by which European modernity legitimated racial inequality to itself. Together they show that the benefit was both material and ideological, and that the two reinforced each other.

Marx is the right place to start because, despite his Eurocentric framings of the proletariat as the agent of history, he was unusually clear about the slave-trade foundation of industrial capitalism. The canonical line reads: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). What is at stake here is not a moralising aside but an analytical claim about the structure of value-creation. Modern industry needs raw materials in bulk at low prices; that requirement was met by plantation slavery, where racial categorisation legitimised the coercion of enslaved Africans to work without wages. The cheapness of slave-grown cotton allowed Manchester mill-owners to undercut Indian and Chinese textile producers. The cheapness of slave-grown sugar created the mass European consumer market that sustained the early modern world economy. Each of these chains converts unequal race relations into modern industrial wealth.

Du Bois generalises Marx's point: "The new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The European working class was, on Du Bois's reading, "practically invited to share in this new exploitation", flattered by appeals to its racial superiority. Modern Europe benefited not only as a continent but as a racialised bloc that could be sold the story of its own elevation. Gilroy sharpens this by refusing to bracket the slave circuit as marginal: the Black Atlantic is "the primal history of modernity… narrated from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55), and racial terror is "cheerfully complicit" with occidental rationality (Gilroy, 1993: 56). The benefit Marx names was not a stain on an otherwise European modernity; it was the modernity, viewed from the side that paid for it.

Dussel completes the picture by showing how this economic benefit was philosophically legitimated. His *Eurocentrism and Modernity* argues that modern European philosophy delivered a justifying myth for racial inequality at exactly the moment it was being economically institutionalised. Kant supplies the racial hierarchy: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel writes Africa out of history: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). And, crucially, Hegel gives empire its philosophical charter: "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses… the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). The slave colony is not a piece of profitable real estate; it is a stage in the self-realisation of Reason. That tells the European subject that the unequal race relation she profits from is a service to the universal.

Dussel's "myth of modernity" generalises this into a seven-step structure: European superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault; modernity is innocent and emancipatory (Dussel, 1993: 75). The benefit is double. Materially, Europe gets cheap labour, cheap raw materials and captive markets. Ideologically, Europe thinks of itself as the bearer of universal reason while doing so. Each side reinforces the other: the wealth funds the universities that produce the philosophy, and the philosophy legitimises the trade.

Putting Marx and Dussel together yields a sharper account than either alone. Marx without Dussel risks reading slavery as an unfortunate historical accident attached to a structurally distinct capitalism. Dussel without Marx risks reading the myth of modernity as a purely cultural construct floating free of its economic stakes. Together, they show that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in a tightly integrated way: race made the labour cheap, the trade legal, and the conscience clean. To understand modern Europe's wealth is to understand the racial inequality that produced it. Marx tells us how. Dussel tells us why no one in Europe felt obliged to notice.

### 2023 (Q1): Can we understand modernity without understanding its relationship to colonialism?

This question and the 2025 paper press the same point with different verbs. The 2023 wording ("can we understand") invites a slightly different answer. We can produce a partial, internalist account of modernity that ignores colonialism; people do it every term. But we cannot produce an account that survives the texts, the money, and the discourse once they are placed on the table. In what follows I sketch the partial account, then show why it fails three tests.

The standard partial account treats modernity as a European achievement with three internal pillars. Politically, the consolidation of centralised nation-states. Religiously and intellectually, the Reformation and the Enlightenment, which Habermas takes as "the historical events that are decisive for the implantation of the principle of [modern] subjectivity" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 74). Economically, the Industrial Revolution. Sociologically, the discipline rests on Marx, Weber and Durkheim. On this account, the colonies feature, if at all, as a market for surplus production or a recipient of the civilising mission: secondary, not constitutive.

The first reason this account fails is textual. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* ("Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity", cited in Dussel, 1993: 68) sounds universal until you read the same author's *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel's *The Philosophy of History* states that "the movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West" (Hegel, 1837: 243) and that "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). The "absolute right" of the people bearing Spirit against peoples who "have no rights" (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73) is the legitimation of empire by the philosopher whom Habermas continues to cite as the architect of modern subjectivity. To bracket colonialism is to bracket the architecture of the Enlightenment.

The second reason is economic. Marx himself (usually the Eurocentric exhibit) conceded: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The cotton mills of Manchester were nodes in a circuit that ran through enslaved plantations in the Americas, gold extracted from Africa, and consumers buying sugar, tobacco and cotton goods. Howard French shows that the financial instruments of early modern Amsterdam and London were tooled to manage slave-trading and slave-grown commodities (French, 2021). Take that out and the Industrial Revolution looks unmotivated.

The third reason is discursive. Hall's contribution is to show that even when modern thinkers were not explicitly racist, they were speaking inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The West is "a tool to think with… a system of representation" that classifies, condenses, compares and evaluates (Hall, 1992: 277). It functions as an ideology in which "western = developed = good = desirable" and "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). Mohanty extends the binary into feminist scholarship: the "Third World Woman" is "an effect of discourse… an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized)" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The discourse Hall names is not confined to crude colonial rhetoric; it survives in the most progressive corners of modern thought.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* knits these together. His "myth of modernity" (superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault, Dussel, 1993: 75) is a single structure underwriting Kant's racial hierarchy, Hegel's philosophy of history, the conquest of the Americas, and the current rhetoric of "regime change". To understand modernity without colonialism, you would have to detach a structure from its instances.

A counter-position might say that the colonial element is real but is one factor among many; we can isolate other factors (technology, secularisation, the printing press) and study them on their own terms. This is true at the level of any particular question. But the broader claim (that we can understand modernity as such without colonialism) fails because modernity is not a discrete property; it is a relation. And the relation includes the colonised. So we can produce internalist accounts of modernity, but we cannot understand modernity in any deep sense without taking colonialism seriously.

### 2022 (Q3): Is modernity undermined by its roots in colonialism?

"Undermined" is the live word here. It could mean discredited (modernity is unworthy of allegiance because of its colonial origin), or destabilised (modernity is internally contradictory because the freedom it claims for some rests on the unfreedom of others), or unmasked (modernity is shown to be something other than it claims to be). I will argue that the second reading is correct: modernity is destabilised but not discredited by its colonial roots. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* is the clearest articulation of this position, and I will use Hall and Marx to support it.

Begin with the strong "discredited" reading. On this view, the modern world was built on slavery, conquest and theft. Therefore everything modernity values (human rights, science, democratic government, secular reason) is contaminated and should be rejected. This is sometimes attributed to Dussel or to the postmodernists Dussel cites. But it is not Dussel's position. He is explicit: "we do not propose a critique of reason as such" and "we do not deny the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment, only its irrational moment as sacrificial myth" (Dussel, 1993: 75). Modernity is not all bad. It is internally split.

That split is what is being asked about. Dussel argues that modernity carries a rational concept of emancipation, which he "affirms and subsumes", and an irrational myth, which he negates (Dussel, 1993: 66). The rational concept is genuine: autonomy, equality, the rule of law, scientific method. The irrational myth is the seven-step structure: superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; the victim is in a state of guilt; modernity emancipates by destroying (Dussel, 1993: 75). Both have been present from 1492 onwards. The Enlightenment writes the *Declaration of the Rights of Man* in 1789 while the French state runs slave plantations in Saint-Domingue. Kant writes *What is Enlightenment?* and: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). The same hand wrote both.

Hall's analysis sharpens this. The discourse of "the West and the Rest" is not a side-effect of European modernity; it is part of how modernity thinks itself: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West can only know itself by negation. That is undermining in the second sense: modernity claims one thing (universal reason) and depends on another (a binary opposition between West and Rest), a performative contradiction.

The economic version of the same problem is Marx's. "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Modern industrial capitalism could not have happened without the slave-grown raw materials of the Atlantic system. Manchester is unintelligible without Mississippi. So modernity's economic dynamism rests on coerced and racialised labour. This does not "undermine" the productivity gains; the cotton was real; but it undermines any account of those gains that treats them as the unaided fruit of European industriousness.

What kind of conclusion does this support? Three things follow. First, intellectual honesty: it is not possible to teach the Enlightenment without teaching its racial archive. Second, political reckoning: the contemporary debate over how the structural debts of the Atlantic economy Howard French documents (French, 2021) are to be acknowledged is the unfinished political work that modernity's colonial roots imposed on us. Third, and this is Dussel's distinctive move, the response is not less modernity but a "trans-modernity", a co-realisation of modernity and its alterity (Dussel, 1993: 76). Spivak warns that this is harder than it sounds: "the subaltern cannot speak" in the discursive grammar imposed by the coloniser (Spivak, 1988), and so any trans-modern project that simply invites the formerly excluded to speak in the categories Hegel and Kant bequeathed reproduces the exclusion at one remove. The undermining is recursive.

A counter-argument might insist that colonial cruelty was contingent; one could have had modernity without slavery. This is partly true, but it concedes the point: the modernity that actually exists is not modernity-in-general but a specific configuration whose roots run through the Atlantic system. Contemporary cultural hierarchies (sport, music, fashion, university league tables) show that those roots still bear fruit in how the West evaluates the Rest.

So yes, modernity is undermined by its colonial roots, in that it is internally contradicted, performatively self-betraying, historically unable to deliver its universalism without exclusions. It is not worthless. But it cannot be defended in the form it has handed itself down to us.

### 2021 (Q3): Does modernity have its roots in colonialism? If so, what are the implications for how we think of modernity?

This question is two questions. The first is descriptive: does modernity have colonial roots? The second is conceptual: what follows for how we use the word "modernity"? I will answer "yes" to the first and outline three implications for the second.

On the descriptive question, the case is now well established. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* dates the birth of modernity to 1492 rather than to the Reformation or the French Revolution. "Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). The European subject only becomes thinkable once Europe can pose itself against a conquered other. Cortés in Mexico is closer to the origin of modern subjectivity than Descartes in his stove.

Hall's 'The West and the Rest' essay supports the same conclusion through discourse analysis: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West is not a primary fact about which we then ask how it relates to the Rest. It is a relational fact constituted by that comparison.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* completes the picture with economic history: Portuguese expansion into West Africa from the 1430s, then the trans-Atlantic slave trade, supplied the gold, sugar, tobacco and cotton that financed Europe's long boom (French, 2021). Marx recognised this: "It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire are nodes in an Atlantic system.

So yes, modernity has colonial roots: philosophically (Dussel), discursively (Hall) and economically (French, Marx).

What follows? Three implications.

First, the periodisation of modernity has to change. The standard textbook places modernity's origin at the Reformation and Enlightenment, with the Industrial Revolution as its consolidation. On the colonial reading, the origin is 1492. Spain and Portugal (discounted by both Hegel and Habermas, Dussel, 1993: 71) are restored to the centre of the story. The Industrial Revolution is the late phase of a long Atlantic economy that begins with sugar in São Tomé and ends with steam in Manchester.

Second, the canon has to change. If Kant's racial remarks ("The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous", Kant, 1798: 59) are part of the architecture of Enlightenment, not stray asides, then teaching the Enlightenment without teaching Kant's race theory is teaching a falsified version. The same applies to Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). Halewood's diagnosis of the classical canon converges here: "the problem of the social" was never settled by Durkheim, Marx and Weber, and twentieth-century sociology forgot the open-endedness of the founders (Halewood, 2014: 131). The Eurocentric closure of the canon is one of the bifurcations Halewood names. The corrective move is to add Du Bois (1970), Dussel and Hall next to Marx, Weber and Durkheim.

Third, the politics has to change. Dussel's proposal of "trans-modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 76) points to a politics that retains modernity's rational kernel (Dussel, 1993: 66) but refuses its irrational myth (the civilising mission, the developmental ladder, the racialised hierarchy of value). The unfinished work of modernity is to extend the universals it once articulated only for Europeans to those who were originally excluded. Movements such as Black Lives Matter and contemporary reparations debates can be read in this register: not as anti-modern, but as demands that modernity finally honour the universals it claimed.

A counter-position would say: yes, the colonial story is real, but it is descriptively interesting rather than normatively destabilising. We can keep using "modernity" as a value-neutral term and address colonial wrongs as a separate political matter. The trouble is that "modernity" was never value-neutral. As Hall shows, it has always been an evaluative grid: "western = developed = good = desirable; non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). To keep using the word as if it were neutral is to keep underwriting the discourse.

So yes, modernity has colonial roots, and the implication is that periodisation, canon and politics all have to be redrawn so that the modernity we choose to defend is not the one Hegel and Habermas inherited but the one Dussel, Hall, Du Bois and Marx pushed against.

### M1: How can sociology be both a creature of modernity and a producer of the colonial attitude, especially through Durkheim's account of the shift from mechanical to organic solidarity?

Sociology is described in canonical accounts as "a creature of modernity" that "tries to explain what it means to be modern" and "also helped create the colonial attitude". My answer is that the two descriptions are not in tension but are two sides of one operation. Sociology emerges to explain modern Europe to itself, and in choosing the questions it asks (what is industrialisation? what holds modern society together? what is rational social action?) it inherits modernity's habit of bifurcated thinking. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic schema is the cleanest example, and I work through it in three moves before answering an obvious counter.

Begin with the creature claim. Sociology arose within modernity: Marx writing about commodification and alienation, Weber about rationalisation and disenchantment, Durkheim about solidarity and anomie. The discipline was created in the modern era to account for industrialisation, urbanisation and individualisation. Halewood's diagnosis of the classical canon converges: "the problem of the social" was set by the founders and inherited by twentieth-century sociology without the founders' open-endedness preserved (Halewood, 2014: 131). The discipline is a child of the conditions it studies.

The colonial-attitude claim is what makes the creature suspect. The colonial attitude is the habit of ranking societies as superior or inferior, modern or backward, and of taking the European outcome as the universal measure. Sociology helped create this attitude by treating modernity as a break with the past and by reading off non-European societies as still located in that past. Durkheim's distinction between mechanical and organic solidarity is the worked example. Mechanical solidarity binds simple, homogeneous societies through likeness; organic solidarity binds complex, differentiated societies through interdependence. The transition is the modern transition. Read this way, the schema is a piece of value-neutral typology. But its effect is explicit: it suggests that other societies around the world are still pre-modern, behind, or backward. The typology operationalises the move that "bifurcation is a dominant mode of modern thought involving splitting a phenomenon into binary / often opposing categories".

The colonial attitude rides in on the developmental schema. Dussel calls this the fallacy of developmentalism: "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68). For Hegel, an underdeveloped country is "not-modern, pre-Aufklärung" (Dussel, 1993: 68). Durkheim's typology converts that philosophical assumption into a usable sociological measure. Once mechanical is coded as "earlier" and organic as "later", the comparison runs in only one direction. Other societies are positioned on a developmental ladder whose top rung is northwestern Europe.

The third move is to recognise that this is not an accident but a structure. Hall reads it as a discourse: the West functions as "a system of representation" with four operations, including classification and evaluation, in which "western = developed = good" and "non-western = under-developed = bad" (Hall, 1992: 277). Durkheim's classificatory machinery slots into the discourse's first function and feeds the others. Dussel diagnoses the same structure as the myth of modernity: step 1 is European superiority, step 3 is that the path of development is Europe's path, step 6 is that the laggard is at fault for the lag (Dussel, 1993: 75). Sociology hands the myth a vocabulary.

A reasonable counter says that Durkheim's typology can be used non-evaluatively, and that the colonial attitude is a reading rather than a property of the schema itself. Marx's cotton-slavery sentence ("Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry", Marx, 1977: 203) shows that classical sociology can name the colonial relation directly when it chooses. The reply is that this concedes the wider point. The schema admits non-colonial use only by stepping outside its developmental ordering, which means decolonising the discipline requires more than reading the founders differently. It requires reading the canon with its colonial archive, supplementing it with Du Bois, who recorded that "in the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 86), and rewriting the founding question so that the unit of analysis is the Atlantic system rather than the European nation-state. Gilroy's Black Atlantic makes the same move.

Sociology is, then, both creature and producer, because the conditions that called it into being also gave it the bifurcations it now needs to outgrow.

### M2: Does the rest of the world have history? Critically discuss the relationship between modernity's view of time and the colonial attitude with reference to Hegel's account of Universal History.

The question "Europe and the West have history. Does the rest of the world?" is a pointed one. My answer is that, inside the discourse modernity constructs, the rest of the world has been denied history precisely because history has been re-defined as the forward-moving time of European industrial capitalism. The colonial attitude is the operational form of that denial. I argue this in three moves, with Hegel as the philosophical fulcrum, then answer a counter.

Begin with the modern view of time. Modernity treats time as forward-moving, accelerating, productive. The economy is always growing; communication is getting faster; fashion changes weekly; the experience is one of speed. History means change, and change has a direction. By contrast, non-modern societies are cast as cyclical, tradition-bound, repeating: the cycles of harvests, tradition, things always staying the same (one reason metropolitan tourists go to visit on holiday). The structure is asymmetric. Forward-moving time is history; cyclical or repeating time is timelessness. The grammar of the discourse will not allow both to be history at once.

Hegel supplies the philosophical charter. World History, for Hegel, is "the development of the consciousness that the Spirit has of its freedom" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68), and the development is geographically directed: "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning" (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69). The directional arrow is built into the concept. Two consequences follow inside Hegel's text. Latin America is the "land of the future", that is, outside the present of Spirit. Africa is excluded more brutally: "Africa... does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). To have history, on this account, you must occupy the moving point of Spirit. Everywhere else is timelessness.

The colonial attitude is the everyday translation of this philosophical move. Colonialism and modernity produce a hierarchical understanding of time, historical development and consciousness, with some societies assumed to be static or cyclical and others progressive; temporality is integral to the continued hierarchies. Once history is monopolised by the modern, anyone who is not modern has no history; and anyone who has no history has no claim against the moderns who have arrived. This is the discursive condition for Dussel's seven-step myth (Dussel, 1993: 75): step 1 (Europeans are most developed) and step 3 (the path is Europe's path) presuppose the temporal monopoly Hegel installs.

The third move is to register what the monopoly does today. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows that the standard textbook chronology (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) loses fifteenth-century African polities (Mali under Mansa Musa, Songhai, Kongo, Benin) by treating them as outside history (French, 2021). Hegel's "we abandon Africa" survives in the periodisation. Said extends the analysis into the present: the discourse of the modern West "produces" the non-modern other "politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 3), so the temporal hierarchy continues to operate even when nobody is quoting Hegel out loud. Brexit-era talk of returning Britain to "global" leadership and cost-of-living debates that scold post-colonial economies for not industrialising "fast enough" run on the same temporal grammar.

A counter says the question is empirically settled: of course every society has a past, and history as a discipline now studies all of them. This is true at the descriptive level. But the question here is about the discourse, not the descriptive record. Hall makes the discursive point precisely: the West is a "system of representation" whose operations include classification, condensation, comparison and evaluation (Hall, 1992: 277). Even when we record other histories descriptively, we slot them into the comparative ladder the discourse maintains. Du Bois's reminder that "in the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 86) only registers as surprising because the discourse has trained us to expect otherwise.

So the rest of the world has history, but the discourse of modernity does not yet know it. That is the colonial attitude in temporal form.

### M3: To what extent does Dussel's critique of Eurocentric modernity depend on Kant's account of guilty immaturity for understanding the myth of modernity as both rational emancipation and irrational violence?

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* compresses its central thesis into a two-layer reading: modernity contains "a rational concept of emancipation that we affirm and subsume", but it also develops "an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence" (Dussel, 1993: 66). The question is how far his diagnosis depends on Kant's specific formulation of Enlightenment as exodus from "guilty immaturity". My argument is that Kant is necessary but not sufficient. The Kantian formula gives Dussel the doubled structure of the myth, but Hegel supplies its historical schema and Marx its economic chain. Without Kant the diagnosis would lose its rhetorical hinge; without Hegel and Marx it would have no historical body.

Start with what Kant gives Dussel. The Enlightenment slogan reads, on the surface, as a universal emancipation: "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (Kant, cited in Dussel, 1993: 68). The word is "humanity"; the offer is in principle open. This is the rational kernel Dussel preserves. But the same author writes that "the Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). The universal promise is silently qualified by a racial hierarchy that puts most of the species outside the rational vocation it advertises. Dussel asks: should "an African in Africa or as a slave in the United States... an Indian in Mexico or a Latin American mestizo... be considered to reside in a state of guilty immaturity?" (Dussel, 1993: 68). The question exposes step 6 of the seven-step myth (the victim is at fault for resisting modernity) by reading it out of Kant's two texts (Dussel, 1993: 75).

Without the Kantian formula, Dussel would still have a critique, but a different one. The Kantian text is the place where modernity's universal promise and its racial exclusion sit on the same page. Strip out the immaturity formula and Dussel could still cite Hegel and document the conquest, but he could not show as cleanly how the rational kernel and the irrational myth coexist inside modern reason itself.

Hegel does the second job. He turns Kant's diagnostic into a historical schema. World History is geographically directed (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69); Africa is outside it altogether (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70); and the people that bears Spirit has "an absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). Step 4 of the myth (violence in the last instance) is Hegelian. Without Hegel, Kant alone would underwrite a paternalist civilising mission but not its philosophical sacralisation as the working-out of Reason.

Marx does the third job. The cotton-slavery sentence ("Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value", Marx, 1977: 203) gives the myth its economic body, and Howard French's *Born in Blackness* fleshes that body in: African gold, the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the sugar-plantation complex from Madeira to São Tomé to Brazil and the Caribbean, and the cotton circuit feeding Lancashire (French, 2021). Du Bois reinforces both with the observation that the new colonial theory delivered "extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races", and that the European working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). Marx is, in this restricted sense, the canonical "possible exception".

So the diagnosis is genuinely composite. Kant supplies the diagnostic hinge; Hegel supplies the schema; Marx (and French and Du Bois) supplies the chain.

A counter says Dussel could ground the myth in the conquest of Mexico alone, treating Cortés as the prototype (Dussel, 1993: 74). This holds at the level of the event, but it loses the explanation of why educated Europeans went on participating in the colonial relation with a clean conscience. The Kantian formula tells the European subject that the unequal relation she profits from is a service to the universal. Read alongside Fanon's record of the racial gaze ("I am being dissected under white eyes... I am fixed", Fanon, 1967: 325) the dependence is clear.

Dussel needs Kant, then, but only as one indispensable component of a three-part diagnosis.

### M4: How can the colonial attitude both survive political independence and continue to shape contemporary judgements of music, sport and "the best universities"? Discuss with reference to cultural universalism.

There is more to colonialism than an attitude, but the attitude survives the end of empire. The case is most easily pressed with contemporary examples: Western Classical Music treated as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music"; the question of which are the best universities in the world; sport and fashion. My argument is that the attitude survives because its host is not the colonial state but the discourse of cultural universalism, the cluster of "claims about cultural universalism that shape knowledge production and the formation of ideas and theories". I defend this in three moves before answering an obvious counter.

Begin with what makes survival possible. Political independence removes the colonial administration; it does not touch the categories the administration used. The colonial attitude is a habit of bifurcated thinking, a "splitting a phenomenon into binary / often opposing categories", in which European outputs are coded as universal and non-European outputs as particular. Once that bifurcation is embedded in how we evaluate music, sport, and scholarship, the formal end of empire leaves it intact. The colonial attitude does not need an empire to do this. It needs only the bifurcated category and the habit of judging.

K-pop, Bollywood, Premier League finals in Riyadh: each can join the "universal" without disturbing the operation by which others are still filed as "world". Until the bifurcation itself is dismantled, the rotation of who is included only refreshes the discourse. Dussel's proposal of trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) is precisely the move past inclusion-as-branding to the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity, and Gilroy's Black Atlantic makes the same move operative for music.

The colonial attitude survives, then, by changing host. The empire is gone; cultural universalism inherited the work.

### M5: Is the West a geography or a discourse? Critically discuss with reference to Hall's analysis of binary opposition and Howard French's claim that Africa is constitutive of the modern world.

For Howard French, the West can only be reconstructed as a geography once Africa is restored to the story. My argument is that the West is a discourse first and a geography second, and that French's Atlantic history is what shows why. I defend this in three moves and then answer a counter.

"The so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West is constituted relationally, not territorially. Hall offers two telling pieces of evidence. First, the category cuts across the map: "Japan is 'western', though on our mental map it is about as far 'East' as you can get" and "much of Latin America, which is in the western hemisphere, belongs economically to the Third World" (Hall, 1992: 276). A geographical description would not produce this map. Second, the Rest is a single bloc only because the West needs it to be one: it "lumps together... the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia... as if they shared a single common identity" (Hall, 1992: 280). Hall's verdict is that the discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). The binary is the product, not the precondition.

The second move is to show what the discourse does. Hall's four functions interlock. Classification sorts the world into West and Rest. Condensation builds the composite. Comparison places other societies on a ladder leading to the West. Evaluation grants the West the value: "western = developed = good" and "non-western = under-developed = bad" (Hall, 1992: 277). Dussel reads the same operation as the myth of modernity, whose seven steps code the laggard as guilty and the victim's suffering as inevitable (Dussel, 1993: 75). The discourse is productive: it organises museums, university rankings, and the everyday judgements that route Bach into the universal and gamelan into "World Music". Said sharpens the point: a discourse can "manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 3). The West is in the same business.

The third move is the one French enforces. The discourse can read as airy until the economic history under it is restored. *Born in Blackness* shows that the territorial West of the textbook (a self-developing northwest Europe) is unintelligible once African gold, African slave labour, and the sugar-cotton-tobacco circuit are tracked. The first Atlantic sugar mill went up on Madeira in the 1420s; São Tomé became a major sugar producer using enslaved African labour by the 1520s; the plantation complex jumped to Brazil, Barbados and Saint Domingue; African gold from Mali ran through coastal forts like Elmina (French, 2021). Marx's compressed claim is the conceptual summary: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The geography of "the West" is a node in a wider system. Du Bois had said as much: "in the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 86). The bifurcation that places one above the other is discursive work performed on a more even historical surface.

A counter says we still need "West" as a geographical shorthand for a recognisable cluster of practices (industrial capitalism, liberal democracy, rationalist science). This is fair as a working description, but it concedes the point. Hall's term for that cluster is precisely a "system of representation" (Hall, 1992: 277), and Eisenstadt's multiple modernities literature shows that the same cluster can be assembled elsewhere on different paths (Eisenstadt, 2000). Once we admit that the cluster is the unit of analysis, the geography is downstream. Halewood (2014) makes the same point inside sociology: the discipline's apparent universality is the achievement of a particular discursive position, not a feature of the territory.

So the West is a discourse. French restores the geography the discourse depends on, and the dependency is what the discourse has to hide.

It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The grammar is causal, not decorative. Modern industry requires cotton; cotton requires slavery; world trade requires the colonies. Each clause names a relation that the standard internalist story of modernity (factories, urbanisation, individualisation) leaves outside the frame. Sociology was created to explain the factories, but the factories ran on Atlantic raw materials. The omission is not minor.

Du Bois converts the chain into a historical account: "in the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 86); the Haitian Revolution of 1790-1804 had "enormous" consequences, prompting Napoleon to "give up his ideas of an empire in America and sell Louisiana for nothing" (Du Bois, 1970: 103, 105); "the chief island domain of English slavery was Jamaica" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The fifteenth-century parity is the diagnostic move: it undoes the assumption that Europe was always ahead. The Haitian point shows the colonies as agents whose revolt restructured the metropolitan map. And the Jamaica line restores the imperial centre's identifiable institutional involvement, with Cromwell sending the expedition. Modernity is not a European achievement extended outward; it is an Atlantic system in which the periphery acts, profits and resists.

The third move is the most consequential for class analysis. Du Bois writes that "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe", and that "the European and white American working class was practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). This breaks the standard Marxist picture in which the European proletariat is the universal subject of emancipation. The European working class is, on Du Bois's reading, partly a beneficiary of the colonial relation, not just a victim of capitalism. Gilroy makes the same move: the Black Atlantic is "the primal history of modernity... narrated from the slaves' point of view" (Gilroy, 1993: 55), and racial terror is not a stain on occidental rationality but a co-author of it. The slavery-cotton chain, once followed through Du Bois and Gilroy, restructures who counts as having made modernity.

Three contemporary registers show the chain still operative. The cost-of-living crisis falls hardest on populations descended from those brought to Britain on the routes Du Bois traces; the Brexit appeal to a "global Britain" was sold against the same internal others Hall flags (Hall, 1992: 280); and Black Lives Matter after 2020 publicly refused the racial coding the European working class was once "flattered" into accepting. The continuities are with the colonial relation Marx named and Du Bois fleshed out.

A counter says that Marx's sentence is a single passage from a journalistic source and that Marx's mature theory remains Eurocentric. The reply is the framing of Marx as "one possible exception". The cotton sentence does not redeem the whole of Marx; it shows that even the most Eurocentric founder of classical sociology, when he looked directly at cotton, saw the colonial chain. Dussel acknowledges the inheritance: "from Hegel, the category of 'development' passed to Marx, and from there to its usage in current sociology and economic theory" (Dussel, 1993: 68 n.2). The cotton sentence is the moment Marx's own framework points past itself. A parallel caution applies to Du Bois: he is genuinely radical, but he writes Atlantic history with the colonial vocabulary he is trying to undo, including "backward races". The cautions matter; they do not undo the structural claim. Slavery and resource extraction are constitutive of modernity, and reading Marx with Du Bois is the surest route to seeing why classical sociology kept the constitution out of view.

### M7: Sociology presents modernity as "unsettling, dynamic, on-going" in which "everything can and must be constantly re-evaluated" (and this "does not apply to those countries which were colonized"). Critically discuss the myth of progress as a colonial structure with reference to Dussel's fallacy of developmentalism and the revision-doc claim that "modernity is not a neutral process but one involving coercion and unequal exchange".

Modernity is conventionally described as "unsettling, dynamic, on-going (and 'Western')": traditions, history, fashion, culture and life must be constantly re-evaluated. The parenthetical caveat exposes the discourse: this does not apply to those countries which were colonised. The dynamism is a privilege. Modernity is not a neutral process but one involving coercion and unequal exchange, and the "myth of progress" sits at its core. My argument is that the myth of progress is a colonial structure, not a neutral feature of modern self-understanding. I defend this in three moves and answer a counter.

Begin with the form of the myth. Modernity is motion: time moves forward, the economy is always growing, communication accelerates, fashion changes monthly, history means change. The grammar is asymmetric. Forward motion is progress; staying still is backwardness. The colonial implication is explicit: non-modern societies are seen as cyclical, repeating, tradition-bound, and metropolitan tourists go to visit them on holiday for that reason. The parenthetical caveat that the dynamism does not apply to those countries which were colonised shows that the dynamism modernity celebrates is reserved for the metropole. The colonised remain available for inspection precisely because they are coded outside the time of progress.

The second move places this inside Dussel's diagnosis. Dussel calls the operation the "fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture. Development is taken as a category that is not only sociological but also ontological" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68). For Hegel, an "underdeveloped" country is "not-modern, pre-Aufklärung" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68). The myth of progress, on Dussel's reading, is not just a confident assertion that things are getting better. It is the ranking of cultures on a single axis whose origin is European industrialisation and whose terminus is European modernity. The seven-step myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) reads this as a structure: step 1 (Europe is most developed), step 2 (Europe is obliged to civilise), step 3 (the path is Europe's path), step 6 (the laggard is at fault) and step 7 (the suffering is inevitable) together compose the progress narrative. "Modernity is not a neutral process but one involving coercion and unequal exchange" is the sociological short form of the same claim.

The third move shows how the myth is operationalised. Eisenstadt's "multiple modernities" literature pushes against the unilinear ranking (Eisenstadt, 2000), but pluralisation does not undo the progress structure if the West continues to do the classifying. Hall explains the mechanism: the West is "a system of representation" with four functions, including comparison and evaluation ("western = developed = good = desirable", Hall, 1992: 277). The progress ranking is one of those evaluations. The colonised are "lumped together" as the Rest (Hall, 1992: 280), and that grouping is what allows the progress axis to run in only one direction. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* supplies the empirical correction: fifteenth-century Mali, Songhai, Kongo and Benin were sophisticated polities trading with Europe on their own terms (French, 2021). The progress narrative had to forget them.

Three contemporary registers show the myth still operating. Brexit-era talk of "global Britain" recycles developmental imagery: the metropole leads, others follow. Cost-of-living debate often scolds post-colonial economies for not "catching up", while ignoring the chain Marx traced: "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Black Lives Matter, read in this register, refuses the progress narrative that coded the colonised as belated. Said sharpens the point: the discourse "produces" the non-modern other "politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 3). The myth of progress is productive discourse, not neutral description.

A counter says progress is real. Life expectancy has risen; literacy has spread; absolute poverty has fallen. To call progress a myth is to deny the gains. Dussel anticipates the reply: he "affirms and subsumes" modernity's rational concept of emancipation and negates the irrational myth that travels with it (Dussel, 1993: 66). The gains are real. The myth is the move that codes them as a European inheritance the rest of the world must wait to receive. Halewood's diagnosis converges: the founding question "what is the social?" was held open by the founders and closed by the discipline after them (Halewood, 2014: 131). Reopening the question, reading Du Bois into the canon next to Marx, treating the Atlantic system rather than the European nation-state as the unit of analysis, retains the gains and discards the colonial coding. Naming the structure is the first step to extending the gains.

### M8: How convincing do you find Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity?

Enrique Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993) compresses an enormous theoretical claim into seven numbered steps. I find the structure convincing on three of its standard tests (fit with canonical texts, portability across cases, and explanatory yield) but vulnerable on a fourth: historical mechanism. I'll work through each step, score it, and reach a verdict.

The seven steps, as Dussel sets them out (Dussel, 1993: 75). (1) Modern European civilisation understands itself as most developed and superior. (2) This superiority obliges Europe, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the more primitive. (3) The path of development is the one Europe took. (4) Where the barbarian resists, modernity uses violence "in the last instance". (5) The violence is ritualised as sacrifice: victims participate in their redemption. (6) The barbarian is guilty for resisting; modernity is innocent and emancipatory. (7) The sufferings imposed on the "immature" are inevitable.

Convincing on textual fit. Step 1 is Hegel: "Europe is the absolute end of Universal History" (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69). Step 2 is Kant's *What is Enlightenment?*: humanity should leave "guilty immaturity" behind (Dussel, 1993: 68). Step 3 is the developmental schema embedded across Marx, Weber and Durkheim. Step 6 is Kant again: "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68). Step 4 is Hegel's *Encyclopedia*, sections 346-347: the people that bears Spirit has "an absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). The myth is not imposed on the canon; it is read out of it.

Convincing on portability. Cortés in the Conquest of Mexico, which Dussel names as "the first extra-European space" in which the European ego carried out its "prototypic 'development'" (Dussel, 1993: 74), maps onto the structure: the Aztec is barbarian (1), conversion is obligatory (2), the Spanish way is universal (3), resistance authorises destruction (4), the violence is ritualised (5), the Aztec is coded as guilty (6), the deaths are read as inevitable (7). The Iberian Reconquest of Granada in 1492, with its "extreme sectarian violence" that became "the model for the colonization of the New World" (Dussel, 1993: 67), maps onto the same structure. So do contemporary cases of violence imposed on others in the name of modernity's reasonableness. A structure that captures cases separated by five centuries is doing real explanatory work.

Convincing on explanatory yield. The myth explains why critics of modernity often misfire. Postmodernists discard the rational kernel and so cannot defend the universal claims the colonised need to articulate their innocence (Dussel, 1993: 75). Habermasian defenders refuse to see the irrational myth and so reproduce the Eurocentric occlusion. Dussel's two-layer reading (Dussel, 1993: 66) explains why reasonable people disagree about modernity by tracking which layer they attend to.

Less convincing on historical mechanism. The seven steps are a logical structure, not a causal story. Dussel does not show *how* the myth was produced: what discursive practices, what institutional pressures, what economic interests assembled it. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* supplies some of the missing mechanism, in the actual sequence of Portuguese expansion down the West African coast, the gold trade through Elmina, and the sugar plantation complex from Madeira to São Tomé to Brazil (French, 2021). Hall's discourse analysis supplies more: the West as a "system of representation" with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277). Dussel's seven steps need this infrastructure to do their full work.

Less convincing on internal differentiation. The myth lumps together episodes that are very different. The Spanish Conquest of Mexico is not the same kind of operation as the partition of Africa or twentieth-century military interventions in Iraq, Libya or Afghanistan. The family resemblance is real; the differences also matter.

Less convincing on the redemptive register. Dussel's vocabulary (innocent victim, redemptive sacrifice, guilt) is theological and may grate on secular readers. The analytical content survives translation, but the rhetoric is sectarian.

My verdict: convincing as a diagnostic, less convincing as a complete theory. The seven steps give a student a portable instrument for reading any rhetoric of civilising mission and finding the same logic at work. The myth needs French's economic history and Hall's discourse theory to fill in mechanism, but as a diagnostic it has aged well. Highly convincing on what it sets out to do; under-developed on what it sets aside.

### M9: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry." Discuss with reference to Marx.

Marx writes, in a much-quoted passage: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The sentence is the most consequential admission in classical sociology that capitalist modernity is constitutively colonial. I want to take Marx at his word, trace the causal chain he sets out, and then assess where Marx's own framework remains Eurocentric despite the admission.

The chain runs in four moves: slavery, colonial value, world trade, large-scale industry. Howard French gives us the empirical body. The plantation complex began on Madeira in the 1420s with the first sugar mill of the Atlantic world, expanded onto São Tomé (which by the early 1520s was importing roughly two thousand enslaved Africans a year), then jumped the Atlantic to Brazil, Barbados, Jamaica and Saint Domingue (French, 2021). The cheapness of slave-grown sugar, tobacco and eventually cotton from places like the Mississippi gave the colonies their value; that value generated world trade, with Lisbon, Bristol and Liverpool figuring as central nodes in French's account; and that trade supplied the precondition of large-scale industry, with Lancashire and Manchester running on the slave-grown cotton Marx names. The Industrial Revolution, on Marx's reading, is unintelligible without this Atlantic system.

What Marx delivers, then, is structural integration. The slavery-cotton chain is not a moralising aside; it is an analytical claim about the relations of production. Modern industrial capitalism requires cheap raw materials at scale; the scale and cheapness are supplied by racialised plantation labour. The reordering matters: the Eurocentric story places European industrial dynamism at the start and treats slavery as a regrettable side-chapter. Marx's ordering puts slavery first. Manchester is unintelligible without the Bight of Biafra.

The implication for class analysis follows. If slave-grown cotton funded the wages and consumer goods of the British industrial proletariat, then the European working class is partly a beneficiary of slavery, not just a victim of capitalism. Du Bois made this explicit: "the European and white American working class was practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). Marx's quoted sentence sets up Du Bois's elaboration. The class analysis becomes a global racial division of labour, not just a national capital-labour division.

So far, so anti-Eurocentric. But three things temper the picture.

First, Marx's broader theory remains developmentalist. Dussel notes that, "from Hegel, the category of 'development' passed to Marx, and from there to its usage in current sociology and economic theory" (Dussel, 1993: 68 n.2): an "underdeveloped" country, for Hegel and then for Marx's developmentalist successors, is "not-modern", pre-Aufklärung. The cotton sentence does not undo this. Marx is anti-Eurocentric in his account of how capitalism functions, but Eurocentric in his account of how history unfolds.

Second, Marx does not theorise race. He names slavery but does not develop an account of racial hierarchy as an internal feature of capitalism. Why was the plantation labour racially marked? Why did African slavery in particular consolidate when other coercive arrangements were also available? Marx's tools (relations of production, accumulation, class struggle) are insufficient on their own. Du Bois's "veil" and Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse begin to fill in what Marx left undone, though both fall outside the cotton sentence itself.

Third, Marx does not theorise colonial agency. The agency of the colonised (the Haitian Revolution, Du Bois, 1970: 103, 105) is not theorised in the quoted sentence. The colonies appear as supply zones for European industry, not as political subjects.

What survives, then, is a serious critique of Eurocentric sociology embedded in a still-Eurocentric historical schema. Marx is rightly described as "a possible exception". Two lessons follow. Methodologically, sociology that takes modernity seriously cannot stop at European borders: the Atlantic system is the unit of analysis, as French shows in detail. Pedagogically, teaching Marx without the cotton-slavery sentence produces a clean European Marx whose insight is invisible; teaching the sentence alongside the abstract analysis recovers a more honest Marx. The cotton sentence is the moment classical sociology gestures toward what it could have been if the colonial constitution of modernity had been its founding question.

### M10: Eurocentrism in classical sociology: blind spot or constitutive premise?

A blind spot is a defect: something missed that, once seen, can be corrected. A constitutive premise is a foundation: something built into the structure that cannot be removed without rebuilding the whole. My answer is that the disjunction is sharper than it looks. For Durkheim and most of Weber, Eurocentrism is constitutive: it organises the founding questions. For Marx and parts of late Weber, it is closer to a blind spot. The verdict matters because the two diagnoses imply different remedies.

The case for constitutive premise begins with Durkheim. His distinction between mechanical and organic solidarity is the foundation of the discipline. The schema is unimaginable without the assumption that European societies have moved from one to the other and that non-European societies remain in the first phase. The colonial implication is plain: the shift from mechanical to organic suggests that other societies around the world are still pre-modern, behind, backward. Remove the developmental schema and there is no Durkheimian sociology. The Eurocentrism is the architecture of the question.

Weber is the harder case. The central question of *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* already presupposes that the European outcome is the standard. The comparative works on China, India and Ancient Judaism are studies of what those civilisations failed to produce. Dussel calls this "the fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68). But Weber is also the most resourceful of the three. He took non-European religious and economic traditions seriously, registered the contingency of Western rationalisation, and warned that the "iron cage" might be a curse rather than a triumph. Constitutive in framing, not unalloyed in substance.

Marx is the strongest case for blind spot. The cotton-slavery sentence ("Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry", Marx, 1977: 203) places the colonial relation at the heart of modern industry. But Marx's broader developmental schema reads as a ladder with European destinations. Dussel notes the inheritance: "from Hegel, the category of 'development' passed to Marx, and from there to its usage in current sociology and economic theory" (Dussel, 1993: 68 n.2). Marx saw the colonial relation when he looked directly at cotton; he reverted to a Eurocentric schema when he looked at history as a whole. Blind spot rather than constitutive premise, but a blind spot the size of a continent.

So the answer disaggregates. Durkheim: constitutive. Weber: constitutive in framing, partially correctable in practice. Marx: a structural insight wrapped in a Eurocentric schema. The general claim that "classical sociology is Eurocentric" hides these differences and blunts the remedies.

The remedies depend on the diagnosis. If Eurocentrism is constitutive, the discipline must rebuild. Dussel's redating of modernity to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 66), and Howard French's restoration of African gold, Portuguese Elmina and the trans-Atlantic slave trade to the foundation of modern economies (French, 2021), are reorganisations of the founding question. The unit of analysis becomes the Atlantic system rather than the European nation-state. If Eurocentrism is a blind spot, the discipline can be extended: keep the analytic categories (alienation, rationalisation, anomie) and supplement them with the voices Marx, Weber and Durkheim were not reading. Du Bois is the obvious starting point: his observation that the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107) is a complement to Marx the canon needed.

A counter-position says the binary is forced: every discipline has its founding assumptions and Eurocentrism is just sociology's. The reply is Hall's: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The founding assumption is not arbitrary; it is a discursive operation with effects. Hall's four functions of the West-Rest discourse (Hall, 1992: 277) describe what sociology textbooks have done.

My verdict: the disjunction is too clean for any one founder. Durkheim's Eurocentrism is constitutive; Weber's is constitutive but self-aware; Marx's is a blind spot wrapped around a structural insight. A decolonised sociology has to do different work on each. That is harder than the question's binary suggests, and more honest than picking a side.

### M11: How does the concept of the colonial attitude help us understand contemporary university league tables and the British Museum?

The colonial attitude is the habit of bifurcated thinking that ranks the world into a developed European/Western core and a backward non-European periphery, and that takes the core's standards as the universal measure. The colonies are gone; the attitude continues to organise institutions that present themselves as neutral or scholarly. Two test cases (university league tables and the British Museum's continued holding of African royal regalia such as the Benin bronzes) show how the concept does explanatory work that other vocabularies cannot.

Take the league tables first. The standard question is: which are the best universities in the world, and why? The answers, year after year, list Anglo-American and northern European institutions at the top, on criteria presented as neutral metrics: publication counts, citations in English-language journals, research income from familiar funding councils, reputation surveys drawn from the same Anglo-European networks. Universities in Africa, South Asia and Latin America are placed lower on those same indicators, then read off as if "the West" really were "the best".

Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse names exactly this. The discourse "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" through four operations: classification (which universities are world-class?), condensation (a stable top image), comparison (where does my university rank?), evaluation ("western = developed = good = desirable", Hall, 1992: 277, 280). Without the colonial-attitude framing, one might say the rankings are imperfect; with it, one sees that the imperfection is structured: it reproduces the geography of empire under the appearance of methodology.

Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) sharpens the diagnosis. Step 1: European civilisation understands itself as most developed. Step 3: the path of development is the path the West took (publish in English on Anglo-American models). Step 6: the lower-ranked are at fault for their own position ("weak research culture", "insufficient internationalisation"). The vocabulary of "world-class" universities is the technical face of Dussel's civilising imperative.

The British Museum is the sharper case because the colonial attitude has to be defended against an articulate counter-demand for repatriation. The Benin bronzes are a particularly clear example. Howard French shows that, well before any European reached the West African coast, the kingdom of Benin was casting plaques in brass using imported European copper and the lost-wax technique (French, 2021). Their continued residence in a metropolitan museum, against the wishes of the people who made them, is the colonial attitude in physical form.

Hall's discourse grounds the assumption that the metropole is a more appropriate custodian than the place of origin. Dussel's seven steps supply the legitimating story: the West is most developed (1), this obliges it to preserve the heritage of less developed peoples (2), the standard of preservation is the European one (3), demands for return are coded as unreasonable (6), and the continuing holding is regrettable but necessary (7). Each argument the institution makes can be located on the structure. The "universal museum" framing is particularly revealing: the universal viewing position is reserved for the metropolitan capital.

Howard French's wider argument deepens the diagnosis. *Born in Blackness* shows that the African polities Europeans encountered (Mali, Songhai, Benin, Kongo, Asante) were sophisticated states that traded with Europe on their own terms (French, 2021). The bronzes are evidence of an African modernity that Hegel denied: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). The European custody of those works is the legacy of that denial in vitrine form.

Why does the concept of the colonial attitude do work that other vocabularies miss? Three reasons. First, it links rankings and museums (and many other practices) to a single structure rather than treating them as unrelated. Second, it explains why apparently technical defences (metrics, preservation) replicate hierarchies: the technique is the inheritance. Third, it points to remedies continuous with what Dussel calls trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76): retaining the rational goods while extending them past the colonial boundary. The colonial attitude is not an old habit waiting to die. It is alive in spreadsheets and vitrines, and naming it is the first step to undoing it.

### M12: Critically assess Howard French's argument in *Born in Blackness*.

Howard W. French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) argues a single thesis with empirical density: Africa, not Europe and not the Americas, is the constitutive ground of the modern world. I set out the argument in its three strongest claims, evaluate its evidence, and identify two limitations a sociologist needs to register.

French's first claim is that Portuguese expansion down the West African coast from the 1430s onwards was the genuine origin event of European modernity. The standard textbook foregrounds 1492, but Columbus is downstream: he was looking for a sea route to Asian commodities because the trans-Saharan gold trade was running through Mali under Mansa Musa, and the Portuguese were already buying that gold at coastal forts like Elmina (1482). African gold, not American silver, funded the first phase of European mercantilism and the maritime technology that made the Atlantic crossing possible. The reordering relocates modernity's birth from the Caribbean to the Gulf of Guinea, and makes the West African polities (Mali, Songhai, Kongo, Benin, Asante) central rather than incidental (French, 2021).

The second claim is that the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the plantation economies were constitutive of modern capitalism, not external to it. Slave-grown sugar on Madeira from the 1420s, then São Tomé, then Brazil, Barbados and Saint Domingue, and slave-grown cotton fed to Liverpool and on to the Lancashire mills, generated the consumer markets and industrial raw materials of the modern Atlantic economy. This is the empirical body for Marx's compressed claim: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203).

The third claim, and the most distinctive, is African agency. Kongo, Benin, Dahomey, Asante and Songhai were sophisticated polities whose internal politics, military capacities and commercial ambitions shaped what Europeans could and could not do on the coast. The trans-Atlantic slave trade was a negotiated and contested system in which African elites participated, profited and sometimes resisted. French is careful: this is restoration of African historical agency against the Hegelian erasure ("Africa… does not have history as such", Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70), not exoneration of European responsibility.

How convincing is the argument?

On evidence, very. French reads Portuguese, French and Spanish archives; he visits Elmina, São Tomé and Caribbean sites. Where Dussel operates at the level of philosophical structure, French operates at the level of ports, ships and account books. The two together do work neither does alone.

On the historiographical move, convincing with caveats. French synthesises a generation of African and African-diasporic scholarship for a popular audience. The synthesis is well done but not entirely original. What French adds is reach and narrative force, and a willingness to argue that the standard textbook story is positively misleading rather than merely incomplete.

Two limitations matter for a sociologist.

First, French is empirically rich but theoretically thin. He shows that Africa was constitutive of modernity; he does not theorise the structure of European self-understanding that erased that constitution. Dussel's seven steps (Dussel, 1993: 75) and Hall's analysis of 'The West and the Rest' as a discourse with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277) do the theoretical work French's narrative gestures at. A sociologist needs the theoretical scaffolding; French supplies the materials.

Second, French's emphasis on African agency, while necessary, sits awkwardly with his emphasis on the violence of the trade. The same African elites who participated were also caught in European-imposed market structures; Kongo's involvement, for example, became partly defensive against neighbours drawn into the same system. French handles this with care but the tension is real.

My verdict: *Born in Blackness* is an important book that does decisively what synthesising works can do: make a specialist consensus available to a general readership. Its argument is more convincing taken alongside Dussel and Hall than read in isolation, because what French shows empirically the theorists explain structurally. Together they make the case that modernity, the West and capitalism are unintelligible without the African continent that helped make them. French's chronology also reframes contemporary debates about Black Lives Matter and museum repatriation: these are not external demands on modernity but downstream from the historical record French restores. Read French for the bodies, the ports and the ships; read Dussel and Hall for why Europe forgot.

### M13: What does Hall mean by "the West and the Rest", and why does he treat it as a discourse of power rather than a geographical description?

Two questions, one analytic. What Hall means by "the West and the Rest" cannot be detached from why he treats it as a discourse. A geographical description records what is there; a discourse produces what counts as there. Hall's argument is that "the West" was never primarily a region. It is a position from which the world has been classified, ranked and acted upon, and the position has effects whether or not the geography supports it.

What Hall means. "The West", in his *Formations of Modernity* essay, is "a system of representation" with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277). First, it *classifies* societies into western and non-western, "a tool to think with". Second, it *condenses* a composite image: the West is urban, industrial, secular, democratic, scientific; the Rest is rural, agrarian, religious, traditional. Third, it *provides a standard of comparison* so that other societies are read as "close to" or "far from" the West. Fourth, it *provides criteria of evaluation*: "western = developed = good = desirable" against "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). The four functions interlock.

Hall borrows from Saussure on linguistic difference: "night" and "day" do not name primary things; they signify by their difference from each other. By analogy, "the West" signifies by its difference from "the Rest". "The so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (Hall, 1992: 278). The constitutive relation is at the heart of what Hall means.

Why is this a discourse rather than a geographical description? Three reasons.

First, Hall's category does not match the geography. Hall observes that "Japan, is 'western', though on our mental map it is about as far 'East' as you can get" and that "much of Latin America, which is in the western hemisphere, belongs economically to the Third World" (Hall, 1992: 276). The Rest lumps together "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280) despite their being more different from each other than from "the West". A descriptive geography would not produce this map. A discourse can.

Second, the discourse is productive. "The idea of 'the West'… had real effects: it enabled people to know or speak of certain things in certain ways. It produced knowledge. It became both the organising factor in a system of global power relations and the organising concept or term in a whole way of thinking and speaking" (Hall, 1992: 278). The colonial attitude that Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" diagnoses (Dussel, 1993: 75) is one product of the discourse; the routing of Bach into the universal column and gamelan or samba into "World Music", or of Harvard and Oxford into "best universities", are others.

Third, the discourse simplifies in ways that a description should not. Hall is explicit: "It draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). It represents the internally differentiated West as one bloc and the more differentiated Rest as another. A descriptive geography would resist that simplification. A discourse of power requires it.

To call this a discourse of power, three further things follow. The discourse has internal others as well as external ones: Jews, Eastern Europeans and women within the West are positioned analogously to the colonised outside (Hall, 1992: 280). The discourse organises material institutions (universities, museums, development banks) that operationalise the binary in concrete decisions. And the discourse is constitutive of modern subjectivity itself: "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65).

Why does Hall insist on this framing? Because treating "the West" as a geographical description would naturalise it. Treating it as a discourse of power denaturalises it: it makes visible the work the category does, the simplifications it requires, the institutions it sustains. The framing is the politics. Once you can see the discourse as a discourse, you can analyse a Lancashire cotton mill, a Madeira sugar plantation, or the "best universities" question as performances of the same structure. That is what Hall's 'West and the Rest' is for: not a description but a diagnostic.

### M14: Compare Kant's account of non-European peoples with Hegel's.

Kant and Hegel are the two pillars Dussel uses to show that the colonial relation is built into the architecture of canonical modern philosophy. They make the same kind of move through different mechanisms. The differences determine which forms of recuperation are available today.

Kant's mechanism is universal-in-form, racially-restrictive-in-content. The famous Enlightenment slogan reads as inclusive: "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (Kant, cited in Dussel, 1993: 68). The word is "humanity". Any reader could be flattered into thinking themselves included. But Kant's empirical anthropology narrows the universal sharply: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). The universal is qualified by a racial hierarchy that excludes most of the species from the rational vocation it claims to extend to everyone.

Dussel gets the contradiction exactly. "An African in Africa or as a slave in the United States in the eighteenth century; an Indian in Mexico or a Latin American mestizo: Should all of these subjects be considered to reside in a state of guilty immaturity?" (Dussel, 1993: 68). The Kantian answer is yes, but the immaturity is not curable by individual effort. The coding blames the colonised for their condition (step 6) while denying them the rational equipment to leave it.

Hegel's mechanism is hierarchical-in-form, with non-Europeans assigned positions in a developmental schema or excluded altogether. World History is "the development of the consciousness that the Spirit has of its freedom" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68), and the development is geographically directed: "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning" (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69). Latin America is excluded as "land of the future". Africa is excluded more brutally: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). And on the African himself: "[The negro] is the man as beast" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70).

The contrasts are sharp.

On argumentative structure: Kant smuggles racial exclusion into a universal frame; Hegel makes hierarchy the frame itself. Kant's reader can be misled by the universal language until she meets the *Observations*; Hegel's reader cannot, because the geography of Spirit is announced at the outset.

On the function of non-Europeans: Kant uses them as moral foils, examples of what humans look like before they have done the Enlightenment work. Hegel uses them as historical stages: the African is the dawn of Spirit, the Asian its childhood, the European its mature end.

On political implication: Kant's writing on race accompanies the Atlantic slave trade French documents (French, 2021). Hegel goes further: he supplies the philosophical charter for empire. "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). And he extends the same logic to colonial expansion: civil society "is, in the first place, driven to seek outside itself new consumers" among "peoples that are inferior to it with respect to the resources" (Philosophy of Right, cited in Dussel, 1993: 74). The colonial "free space" is, on Hegel's reading, available because the people who already live there "have no rights" against the bearer of Spirit.

What does the comparison imply for reading them today? Kant is more deceptive but more recuperable. The universal claim can be turned against the racial qualification: one can argue, with Dussel's "trans-modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 76), that Kant fails by his own standard and that the rational kernel survives the negation of the irrational myth. Hegel offers less to recuperate because the hierarchy is the architecture: removing the racial geography of World History leaves a different system, not a corrected one.

But both readings are possible. Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 75) supplies the structure: keep the rational concept of emancipation, expose the irrational myth, extend the universals to those the canonical author excluded. The point is not to discard the canon but to teach the Enlightenment with its racial archive. The student who has done that reading is better equipped to recognise the colonial attitude in its contemporary forms.

### M15: Multiple modernities: useful pluralism or empty inclusion?

The phrase "multiple modernities" gestures at a family of modernities rather than a single European original to which others must conform. The cognate move is Dussel's: he rejects "the fallacy of developmentalism", the assumption that "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68), and proposes "trans-modernity" as an alternative (Dussel, 1993: 76). The question is whether the pluralism such proposals offer is theoretically useful or merely papers over the asymmetries Dussel, Hall and French analyse.

The case for useful pluralism is real. The standard sociological story treats non-European societies as "pre-modern" by default. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic schema places other societies on a single ladder, coding them as still pre-modern, behind, backward. Pluralising modernity breaks this default. It says that earlier and contemporary non-European polities had their own modernisations, and that the European outcome is one configuration rather than the destination. Dussel's redating of modernity to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 66) reorients the story so that Iberia, the Americas and Africa appear as constitutive rather than as later recipients.

Howard French extends the case empirically. He documents West African polities (Mali, Songhai, Kongo, Benin, Asante) that were sophisticated states well before any sustained European presence (French, 2021). The Benin bronzes alone are evidence of an African modernity (technical, political, aesthetic) that Hegel denied existed at all. Pluralising modernity is a way of recognising what French restores.

But the case for empty inclusion is also strong.

First, the pluralism can dissolve the colonial relation. If every society has its own modernity, then the asymmetric relations French documents (Portuguese Elmina, Madeiran and São Tomé sugar plantations, the trans-Atlantic plantation complex, French, 2021) can recede from view. Marx put the chain bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). A pluralism that misses this becomes ideology: it pluralises the cultural register while leaving the extractive structure unnamed.

Second, the pluralism can be a Western gift. The act of classifying "their" modernity as also valid leaves intact the discursive position from which the classifying happens. Hall's analysis makes the point: the West functions as a "system of representation" whose operations include classification, condensation, comparison and evaluation (Hall, 1992: 277). A West that generously concedes "multiple modernities" is still the West doing the classifying. The same operation is visible in streaming platforms that file Nigerian or Korean cinema under "international" while leaving Hollywood as the unmarked default.

Third, Dussel's own diagnosis warns against bare recognition. The seven-step myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) is not undone by acknowledging that other societies were also modern. It is undone by exposing the irrational myth that licensed violence against those societies. Recognition without that exposure is, in Dussel's terms, modernity continuing to "present itself not only as innocent but also as a force that will emancipate or redeem its victims from their guilt" (Dussel, 1993: 75).

How should one adjudicate?

The most defensible position is that "multiple modernities" is useful as a starting point and empty as a stopping point. It is the necessary first move against the unilinear narrative. But on its own it does not name the asymmetric relations between modernities, the colonial extraction French traces from gold to sugar to cotton, or the philosophical legitimation Dussel reads out of Kant and Hegel. It needs supplementation by Dussel's trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76), which insists not merely on plural recognition but on a "co-realisation" of modernity and its negated alterity.

Hall and French both push in the same direction. Hall warns that the West-Rest discourse is "destructive" because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions" (Hall, 1992: 280); recognising plural modernities will not undo the discourse unless the distinctions themselves are denaturalised. French insists that Africa was the "linchpin of the machine of modernity" (French, 2021); a pluralism that does not register this dependency simply lets the European outcome appear as one option among many rather than as one that fed off the others.

So: useful pluralism, yes, if it knows it is unfinished. Empty inclusion if it congratulates itself on having included. Trans-modernity, on Dussel's reading, is the harder version of the move that "multiple modernities" begins.

### M16: Does the climate crisis vindicate or refute Dussel's myth of modernity?

The climate crisis is a useful test case for Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 75). If the diagnosis describes a real structure, the way the climate crisis is currently being framed and managed should map onto its seven steps. My argument is that the framing vindicates Dussel's diagnosis more than it refutes it, but uncomfortably enough to push the framework somewhere Dussel did not take it.

Begin with the structural pattern. Cumulative emissions are unequal by historical responsibility: the territories that industrialised first burned most of the cheap atmospheric space, while the heaviest impacts (drought, flood, sea-level rise) fall disproportionately on the territories Dussel calls the periphery. That distribution is colonial extraction continued by atmospheric means: the wealth European industrialisation built on the slavery-cotton-industry chain Marx names ("It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade", Marx, 1977: 203) has now generated atmospheric damage borne by other societies. The colonial attitude continues in a new register.

How does this map onto Dussel's seven steps?

Step 1 (Europe most developed): climate diplomacy is conducted in a vocabulary of "developed" and "developing" states. The taxonomy is identical to the "fallacy of developmentalism" Dussel critiques: "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68).

Step 2 (obligation to civilise): Northern actors offer "decarbonisation assistance" to Southern recipients on Northern terms, technology and finance flowing from the same direction as the older civilising mission, with green vocabulary attached.

Step 3 (the path is Europe's path): later industrialisers are criticised for coal-heavy growth in language that elides the fact that European industrialisation used the cheap atmospheric space first. Howard French's account of how slave-grown sugar and then cotton drove the Industrial Revolution (French, 2021) is precisely what is occluded by "you must decarbonise as we did".

Step 4 (violence in the last instance): coercion now operates as structural rather than military, in tariffs, conditionalities, and border controls. Iraq, Afghanistan and Libya are recent cases of violence dressed in the vocabulary of modernity's reasonableness; the climate register extends that logic.

Step 5 (sacrificial ritualisation): vulnerable populations are presented as participants in their own redemption through Northern-led investment. Dussel's image of victims as "participants in a process of redemptive sacrifice" (Dussel, 1993: 75) reads cleanly onto this.

Step 6 (the victim is guilty): the late industrialisers are coded as the urgent problem; the longer carbon history recedes. Echoing Hegel's image of an African "without history", environmental damage in the global South is sometimes coded as "resource conflict" or population pressure rather than as the working-out of a historical relation.

Step 7 (sufferings are inevitable): climate damages in the South are framed as conditions to adapt to rather than debts to settle. The vocabulary is identical to Dussel's: "the suffering and sacrifices (the costs) of modernization imposed on 'immature' peoples, enslaved races, the 'weaker' sex, et cetera, are inevitable and necessary" (Dussel, 1993: 75).

This is heavy vindication. The climate crisis is currently being managed inside the structure Dussel's essay names.

But two things push past Dussel.

First, the climate crisis is the first modernity-internal contradiction that threatens the Global North materially. Dussel's framework treats the violence as exported; the climate crisis is the moment at which a substantial fraction of the violence comes home to the metropole. The Northern realisation that some of the costs are now imported should, in principle, weaken the myth's psychological hold. Whether it will is the live political question.

Second, Dussel's constructive proposal of trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) is articulated as a "co-realisation" between centre and periphery, with the periphery first "discovering itself as innocent" of the violence imposed on it. That is the right form. But the scale of redistribution implied by climate damages is large enough that the recognition has to be followed by institutional reorganisation Dussel does not sketch. The seven-step diagnosis fits the crisis; the trans-modern remedy is under-specified for it.

So my answer: vindicates more than refutes. The crisis confirms that the structure of the myth of modernity persists in contemporary global governance. It also shows that Dussel's diagnosis is more developed than his constructive proposal, and that Hall's and French's analyses are needed to fill the gap between diagnosis and remedy. Dussel's seven steps name the structure. The case shows the structure is still in operation.

### M17: Using the work of at least two authors, assess the case for reparations as a modernity-internal demand.

A reparations demand is usually framed as extra-modern: a moral claim made against modernity from somewhere outside it. I want to argue, using Marx and Dussel together with Howard French in support, that the demand is better read as modernity-internal: a debt that modernity's own rational kernel makes payable once its irrational myth is stripped away. Modernity's universal claims (property, contract, restitution for wrongful taking, equal moral worth) generate the obligation as soon as they are extended to the people from whom they were originally withheld.

The case rests on three steps.

First, the economic foundation. Marx supplies it in the canonical line: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* fills in the empirical body. The first sugar mill of the Atlantic world was set up on Madeira in the 1420s; São Tomé followed in the early 1500s, and from there the sugar-plantation complex jumped the Atlantic to Brazil, Barbados and Jamaica, eventually pulling cotton along with it (French, 2021). Du Bois generalised the point: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe", and the European working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The wealth that funded later European modernity was substantially generated by slave labour and colonial extraction.

Second, the philosophical move. Dussel shows that modernity contains both a rational concept of emancipation and an irrational myth that legitimates genocidal violence (Dussel, 1993: 66). The rational concept includes property rights, restitution for wrongful taking, and equal moral worth under law. These are the categories modern states use every day to settle disputes between citizens. Apply the same categories to the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the colonial extractions French documents. The taking was without consent. The labour was unpaid. The receipts went to identifiable beneficiaries: the Crown that licensed the trade, the chartered companies (the Portuguese Crown monopoly at Elmina, the Royal African Company) and the planters. The successor institutions are legally continuous. The categories of modern law therefore generate an obligation to repay.

This is what makes the demand modernity-internal. Dussel's seven-step myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) explains why the obligation has been ignored: step 6 codes the victim as guilty, step 7 codes the costs as inevitable, and the rational kernel is covered by the irrational myth. The reparations demand is a request to peel the myth back and let the rational kernel do its work. It does not require importing new moral categories. It requires applying the existing ones consistently.

Third, the discursive obstacle. The reason the demand reads as extra-modern is the discourse Hall calls "the West and the Rest". The West is "a system of representation" whose four functions (classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating) install a binary in which "western = developed = good = desirable" and "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). Inside this discourse, the colonised are not equivalent legal subjects who can call on restitution; they are a residual category from whom value flows without return. The discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions" (Hall, 1992: 280). A routine legal grammar therefore reads, in this case, as a heretical request.

Two objections need to be answered. One: temporal distance. The transactions are old; the descendants are far removed. The reply is that the institutional beneficiaries (chartered companies, planters' estates, the state revenues that taxed the trade) are legally continuous with successor institutions. Continuity of institution is sufficient for continuity of obligation. Two: collective guilt. Reparations is not about individual contrition. It is about unjust enrichment passed forward through institutions that are still solvent. The demand is for redistribution, not personal apology.

So: reparations is modernity-internal because it uses modernity's own legal and moral categories, extended to those modernity originally excluded. Marx supplies the economic chain; Dussel supplies the philosophical structure; French supplies the empirical case; Hall explains why a routine legal grammar reads as a heretical request. The demand is not anti-modern reaction. It is, in Dussel's vocabulary, the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (Dussel, 1993: 76): modernity finally honouring the universals it once articulated only for some. The bill is overdue.

### M18: Can sociology be decolonised without abandoning its classical canon?

The question turns on what "decolonising" sociology actually demands. The strong version says: the canonical founders are so deeply Eurocentric that decolonising the discipline requires displacing them. The weak version says: the founders can be read critically and supplemented. I argue for an intermediate position. Sociology can be decolonised without abandoning the canon, but only on three conditions: that the canon is read with its colonial archive, that the canon is supplemented with the voices it excluded, and that the discipline's founding question is rewritten.

Begin with the case that the canon must go. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic schema places non-European societies into the "premodern, behind, backward" column by default. Weber's question (why did rational capitalism develop in northwestern Europe and not elsewhere?) already takes the European outcome as the standard. Both presuppose Dussel's "fallacy of developmentalism" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68). The Eurocentrism is the architecture of the questions. On this strong reading, no supplementation can save them.

But the strong reading has three difficulties.

First, abandonment is empirically expensive. The analytic categories the founders built (alienation, anomie, rationalisation, division of labour, ideology, status, class) are genuinely useful for sociology of any geographical scope. Abandonment would forfeit these tools without an obvious replacement.

Second, the canon contains internal critics. Marx is the canonical "possible exception": "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203) is one of the most consequential admissions in classical sociology that capitalist modernity is colonial. Du Bois develops the same point: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races", and the European working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). Reading these voices into the canon rather than out of it is the decolonising move.

Third, abandonment can be performative. Dropping Marx, Weber and Durkheim while teaching other still-European thinkers does not decolonise; it relabels.

What does decolonisation look like, then, if the canon stays? Three conditions.

Condition one: read the canon with its colonial archive. Teach Marx with the cotton-slavery sentence. Teach Durkheim's mechanical/organic distinction with attention to its colonial implication that other societies around the world are coded as still pre-modern, behind, backward. Teach Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* alongside the *Observations*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Teach Hegel's account of World History with the sentences Dussel cites: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70) and "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" against the bearer of Spirit (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). Reading the canon without the racial archive is teaching a sanitised version.

Condition two: supplement the canon with the voices it excluded. Dussel, Hall and French belong on the reading list precisely because the standard founders did not say what they said. Du Bois belongs to the same supplementation. These thinkers theorised the modernity Marx, Weber and Durkheim were describing from inside Europe.

Condition three: rewrite the discipline's founding question. The standard question ("what is modern society and how did it come to be?") presupposes that modernity is a European phenomenon. A decolonised question ("how did the modern world come to be, and how do its colonial relations continue to structure it?") keeps the analytic categories but reorients the unit of analysis. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows what this looks like in practice: Portugal at Elmina, the first Atlantic sugar mill on Madeira, São Tomé's sugar boom and the trans-Atlantic plantation complex are read as nodes of a single system that also includes Manchester, Liverpool and Bristol (French, 2021). The class struggle in nineteenth-century Manchester is studied alongside the Haitian Revolution Du Bois treats as having "enormous" consequences for the Atlantic world (Du Bois, 1970: 103, 105).

So my answer: yes, sociology can be decolonised without abandoning its classical canon, but only by reading the canon honestly, supplementing it generously, and rewriting the founding question. Dussel's trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) describes what the project requires: keep the rational kernel of modern social analysis while extending it to the people and places the founders left out.

### M19: Trans-modernity in 2026, programme or pious wish?

Dussel ends *Eurocentrism and Modernity* with the proposal of trans-modernity: "a process that will transcend modernity as such, a trans-modernity, in which both modernity and its negated alterity (the victims) co-realize themselves in a process of mutual creative fertilization" (Dussel, 1993: 76). The vocabulary is ambitious; the proposal is sketched in a single paragraph. The question is whether it is a genuine programme or a pious wish that decorates academic essays. My argument is that trans-modernity is a programme in the strong sense, that Dussel, Hall and French between them already supply most of the conceptual scaffolding, and that its weakness is institutional follow-through rather than intellectual coherence.

Begin with what the programme requires. Trans-modernity is a co-realisation: keep modernity's rational kernel and reject its irrational myth (Dussel, 1993: 75). The colonised, the slave, the woman, the child, "subalternized popular cultures" must "discover themselves as innocent" of the originary violence (Dussel, 1993: 76). A trans-modern project is identifiable by two markers: it preserves modern goods, and it includes the formerly excluded in their substantive enjoyment.

The conceptual scaffolding is more developed than the single paragraph suggests. The seven-step myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) gives the diagnostic. The two-layer structure of modernity (rational concept "affirmed and subsumed"; irrational myth "negated", Dussel, 1993: 66) gives the method. The redating of modernity to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 66) and the restoration of the Iberian periphery give the historical orientation. Together these constitute a programme: a diagnosis, a method, and a unit of analysis.

Stuart Hall extends the programme into discourse. The West is a "system of representation" with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277). Any trans-modern reorganisation has to denaturalise this system: not just include the Rest as also valid, but expose the over-simplifications the discourse depends on. The discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions" (Hall, 1992: 280). Trans-modernity has to undo the over-simplification, not redecorate it.

Howard French extends the programme empirically. *Born in Blackness* shows that the standard chronology (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) misplaces the origin. The first impetus of European overseas expansion was the West African gold trade; Elmina (1482) preceded da Gama; the first Atlantic sugar mill was on Madeira around 1425; São Tomé became a major sugar producer by the 1520s on enslaved African labour; from there the plantation complex moved to Brazil and the Caribbean (French, 2021). The Atlantic system, not internal European reform, is the engine.

What of the pious-wish reading? Two patterns make trans-modernity vulnerable.

The first: trans-modernity as vocabulary without follow-through. The word can circulate as a generous label that classifies plural modernities as also valid while leaving the institutions that organise global wealth, knowledge and heritage untouched. Hall's analysis explains why this is easy: classifying generously, with a positive label attached, still leaves the classifying position itself unchanged (Hall, 1992: 277). The discourse can absorb the label. A Disney "diversity" reboot or a corporate Black History Month campaign performs the same operation: inclusion as branding, with the underlying hierarchy intact.

The second: trans-modernity is short on institutional architecture. Dussel's paragraph names the form of the co-realisation but not the bodies that would do it. The colonised must "discover themselves as innocent", but the discovery has to be heard and acted on by institutions that currently embody the discourse Hall analyses. Dussel does not specify those institutions or the path from recognition to redistribution.

My verdict: trans-modernity in 2026 is a programme in the sense that matters for an examination essay. The diagnostic structure is fully specified (Dussel's seven steps). The methodological injunction is clear (affirm the rational kernel, negate the irrational myth). The historical reorientation is empirically anchored (French's Atlantic system). The discursive equipment is supplied (Hall's four functions). What it lacks is a worked-out institutional sociology. Dussel's single paragraph is honest about its scale: it points where to go rather than how to get there. Calling that a programme rather than a wish is the right description, provided the reader does not confuse a regulative ideal with a delivery plan.

### M20: "Modernity is a Janus-faced project: emancipation for some, the colonial wound for others." Discuss.

The Janus image, two faces opposite, one head, is exactly right for modernity. Any honest account of the modern world has to look both ways at once. Modernity emancipated Europeans from arbitrary monarchy, hereditary status and material scarcity. Modernity inflicted what the proposition calls the colonial wound on Africans, Asians and indigenous Americans: chattel slavery, conquest, extraction, racial hierarchy. The two faces share a head. I defend this reading by working through the emancipation face, the colonial-wound face, and the structural reasons the two are inseparable.

The emancipation face is the standard story Dussel names in order to complicate. Modernity, on this account, is rationality, industrialisation, urbanisation, the break with tradition. Marx is its dominant sociologist of productive dynamism; Weber of its rationalisation; Durkheim of the shift from mechanical to organic solidarity. Dussel grants the rational concept of emancipation and "affirm[s] and subsume[s]" it (Dussel, 1993: 66). Kant's "exodus from guilty immaturity" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68) is part of it. So is the consolidation of secular nation-states, the spread of literacy, the partial extension of the franchise (Hall, 1992: 277). None of this is fake.

The colonial-wound face is what modernity also was, simultaneously. Dussel's argument is unambiguous: modernity appears to celebrate rationality and freedom while also encouraging violence towards those who are coded as not modern or not rational. Howard French's account of how Portuguese expansion was driven by the search for gold and enslaved labour, and how the plantation complex (Madeira and São Tomé sugar from the 1420s onward, then Brazil, Barbados, Jamaica) supplied the foundations of the modern Atlantic economy (French, 2021), is the empirical body of the wound. So are the Dussel passages on the Iberian Reconquest of Granada in 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 67) and Cortés in Mexico as "the first extra-European space" of European "prototypic 'development'" (Dussel, 1993: 74).

Why are the two faces inseparable? Three reasons.

First, the emancipation was funded by the wound. Marx put it bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Du Bois generalised the point: the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The wealth that emancipated Europeans was substantially generated by the labour and lands of the colonised.

Second, the emancipation was conceptually structured by the wound. Hall shows that "the West" is not a primary fact but a relation: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The modern European subject became thinkable by being posed against an inferior other. Kant: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). And the right of empire: "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses… the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73).

Third, the relation continues. The colonial attitude outlives the formal end of colonies. Contemporary judgements about sport, music, fashion and which are the best universities in the world surface the same hierarchy in apparently neutral form. Western Classical Music is taught as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music". The wound is administered with a clean conscience because the discourse Hall analyses ("western = developed = good = desirable", Hall, 1992: 277) has naturalised the hierarchy.

What follows for political action? Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 75) names the legitimating structure; his proposal of trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) names the way forward, a "co-realisation" keeping the rational goods while including the excluded. The Janus image suggests the same: turn the head so both faces are seen at once, and the project becomes one of extending the emancipation to those modernity originally wounded.

A counter-position says the wound is overstated. The reply is to look at the texts Dussel cites and the trade French traces. Modernity is Janus-faced because it always was, and any sociology that pretends otherwise is teaching one face while concealing the other. The honest task is to recognise both.
