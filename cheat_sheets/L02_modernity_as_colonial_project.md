## 1. Key Concepts and Thinkers

- **Modernity:** "a way of thinking, acting and living which had and still has enormous and unequal consequences for the world and the peoples of the world" (lecture slides). Tied to industrialisation, urbanisation, individualisation, rationality and the break with tradition.
- **The colonial attitude:** a habit of thought, surviving political independence, that ranks societies as superior/inferior, modern/backward.
- **Eurocentrism:** the assumption that modernity is an exclusively European phenomenon and that European development is the universal model.
- **The myth of modernity (Dussel):** modernity packages emancipation with a concealed irrational myth that justifies genocidal violence on a "civilising" mission.
- **Fallacy of developmentalism (Dussel):** the idea that every culture must follow Europe's ontological path of development.
- **The West and the Rest (Hall):** a discourse, not a geography. A "system of representation" producing binary oppositions: western/non-western = developed/under-developed = good/bad.
- **Bifurcated thinking:** the colonial attitude sorts the world into two camps.
- **Multiple modernities / trans-modernity (Dussel):** alternative routes that refuse Europe as the sole template.
- **Classical-sociology trio:** Marx (commodification, alienation, slavery-cotton-industry chain), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment), Durkheim (mechanical to organic solidarity).
- **Hegel:** world-historical Spirit moves East to West; Africa is "outside history"; Europe holds an "absolute right".
- **Kant:** Enlightenment as exodus from "guilty immaturity"; racialised remarks on "the Negroes of Africa".
- **Du Bois:** before the 15th century there was "no great disparity" between Negroland and Europe; slavery built world trade.

## 3. Information from the Readings

### Enrique Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*

**Central thesis.** Modernity is a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity. Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the "centre" of a World History it inaugurates; the "periphery" is part of its self-definition. By occluding the periphery (and the role of Spain and Portugal from the late 15th to mid-17th centuries), the major thinkers of the centre commit a Eurocentric fallacy.

**1492 as the birth date.** Dussel dates modernity to 1492: the conquest of the Kingdom of Granada and Columbus's voyage. Modernity is "born" when Europe is in a position to pose itself against an other. The other is not "dis-covered" but "covered-up", concealed as the same as what Europe assumed it had always been.

**The myth of modernity.** Modernity contains a rational concept of emancipation that Dussel affirms and subsumes, but it also develops an irrational myth: a justification for genocidal violence. Postmodernists criticise modern reason as terror; Dussel criticises modern reason because of the irrational myth it conceals.

**Fallacy of developmentalism.** The fallacy consists in thinking that Europe's path of development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture. Development is taken as an ontological (not merely sociological) category. For Hegel, an "underdeveloped" country is "not-modern", pre-Aufklärung.

**Kant.** "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." Dussel asks: should the African slave, the Mexican Indian, the Latin American mestizo be considered guilty for their immaturity?

**Hegel.** World History is the self-realisation of Spirit, Reason, and Freedom. It moves East to West. Latin America and Africa are "outside" history. Asia is the dawn but not the culmination. Northern Europe (Germany, France, Denmark, Scandinavia) is the heart. The Germanic Spirit, refracted through the Lutheran Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution, is the culmination.

**The right of empire.** For Hegel, the people that bears the Spirit at its current moment has an "absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights". Dussel sees this as the philosophical sacralisation of imperial power over the periphery: a "limitless cynicism" masked as the development of reason.

**Colonies and civil society.** Hegel argues that civil society's internal contradictions push it to seek new consumers and colonise peoples "inferior" with respect to its industry. Colonisation allows surplus poor to become property owners abroad. Hegel does not register that this requires seizing land from others.

**Habermas continues the occlusion.** Habermas locates modernity's principle in the Reformation, Enlightenment and French Revolution. The discovery of America is mentioned but assigned no constitutive role. Habermas inherits Hegel's discounting of Spain and Portugal.

**The seven elements of the myth of modernity.**
1. European civilisation understands itself as the most developed and superior.
2. This obliges it, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the "primitive".
3. The path of development is Europe's own out of antiquity and the Middle Ages.
4. Where the "barbarian" resists, modernity must, in the last instance, recourse to violence.
5. The violence is ritualistic: victims (colonised, slave, woman, the earth) are cast as participants in redemptive sacrifice.
6. The barbarian is in a state of guilt for opposing civilisation; modernity is innocent and emancipatory.
7. The sufferings and sacrifices of modernisation imposed on immature peoples are inevitable and necessary.

**Trans-modernity.** Dussel proposes neither postmodern relativism nor a reaffirmation of European reason but a "trans-modernity": a co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (the colonial periphery, the Indian, the slave, the woman, the child, subalternised popular cultures). They must first "discover themselves as innocent" of the originary violence.

### Stuart Hall, *Formations of Modernity* (West and the Rest)

**The West as historical, not geographical, construct.** "Western" denotes a type of society: developed, industrialised, urbanised, capitalist, secular, modern. Its meaning is "virtually identical" to the word "modern". Japan can be "western"; Latin America, in the western hemisphere, is not.

**The West as concept**, four functions:
1. Characterises and classifies societies (western vs non-western), a tool to think with.
2. Condenses a set of images: a system of representation. Western = urban = developed; non-western = rural = under-developed.
3. Provides a standard of comparison: how close to or far from the West?
4. Provides criteria of evaluation: the West = good = desirable; non-West = bad = undesirable. It functions as an ideology.

**Mutual constitution.** "The West and the Rest became two sides of a single coin." The uniqueness of the West was produced through Europe's contact and self-comparison with non-western societies. Identity is formed by difference (Saussure on night/day; psychoanalysis on the infant and the mother).

**Discourse and binary opposition.** Hall reads "West and Rest" as a discourse that simplifies, representing differentiated Europe as homogeneous (the West) and grouping equally diverse societies (Middle East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous America, Australasia) as one Rest. That over-simplification is precisely what makes the discourse destructive.

**Internal others.** Hall notes the West also had its internal others: Jews, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", women. The dichotomy is not only outward-facing.

**Periodisation.** Expansion is dated from Portuguese coastal exploration (1430-1498) and Columbus (1492-1502). Michael Mann's claim that Europe "overtook" Asia around 1450 is cited. The five phases (exploration; early contact and conquest; settlement; imperial consolidation; decolonisation) frame the West-Rest relationship as a long history of asymmetric encounter.

### Howard W. French, *Born in Blackness*

**Restoring Africa to modernity's origin story.** French argues that the conventional story of modernity (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) leaves out the constitutive role of Africa and Africans. The Portuguese push down the West African coast in the 15th century was driven by the search for gold and African slave labour, not just spices or a route to Asia. African gold (Mali, Ghana) helped underwrite European mercantilism.

**Slavery and the making of the Atlantic world.** Sugar plantations in São Tomé, the Caribbean and Brazil, staffed by enslaved Africans, generated unprecedented surplus and demand. The capital, commodities and consumer markets of the early modern economy were forged on this base. The cotton mills of Lancashire, the docks of Liverpool, the financial instruments of London and Amsterdam, the banks that survived to the present, all draw lines back to the slave economy.

**Africa as agent, not victim.** French insists Africa is not merely passive raw material. Kongo, Benin, Asante, Songhai were sophisticated polities whose engagements with Europeans shaped the trade. The argument supports Dussel's claim that the periphery is constitutive of the centre, and Hall's claim that the West is the product of relations with the Rest.

**Erasure as method.** Like Dussel's "covered-up" other and Hall's discourse of simplification, French highlights the active forgetting in European historiography: Africa "born in blackness" written out of modernity's account of itself.

## 4. Critical Analysis with Embedded Key Quotes

The central provocation of L2 is that modernity is not innocent. It is sociology's founding object, but the discipline that names it has also concealed its dependence on colonial extraction. Three moves are crucial.

First, Dussel reframes 1492 as modernity's birthdate. He writes:

> "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity… modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other." (Dussel, 1993: 66)

This relocates modernity's origin from the Reformation and Enlightenment to colonial conquest. Habermas and Hegel both fail this test:

> "For Habermas, as for Hegel, the discovery of America is not a constitutive fact of modernity." (Dussel, 1993: 74)

Second, the lecture and Dussel converge on the "myth of modernity": rational emancipation is real but inseparable from sacrificial violence:

> "Modernity includes a rational 'concept' of emancipation that we affirm and subsume. But, at the same time, it develops an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence." (Dussel, 1993: 66)

The seven-step "myth" (superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault) gives me a checklist for reading any modernising rhetoric, from nineteenth-century missions to twenty-first-century "regime change". The lecture asks pointedly: Iraq, the Falklands, Saudi Arabia?

Third, the violence has explicit textual sources in canonical European philosophy. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* sounds emancipatory until paired with his racial remarks:

> "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity." (Kant, cited in Dussel, 1993: 68)

> "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous." (Kant, 1798: 59)

Hegel goes further, treating Africa as outside history altogether:

> "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again." (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70)

> "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning." (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69)

The right of empire follows directly:

> "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights." (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73)

Stuart Hall completes the picture by showing that even when we are not citing Hegel, we are speaking through his categories. The discourse of "the West and the Rest":

> "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy, the West/the Rest. That is what makes the discourse of 'the West and the Rest' so destructive: it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'." (Hall, 1992: 280)

Marx is the partial exception within classical sociology. He saw the chain:

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." (Marx, 1977: 203)

And Du Bois pushed against the colonial attitude well before postcolonial theory:

> "In the fifteenth century there was no great disparity between the civilization of Negroland and that of Europe." (Du Bois, 1970: 86, cited in the lecture slides)

Critically: the colonial attitude is not an embarrassing relic. The lecturer's prompt, to look at sport, music, fashion, university league tables, shows that the same hierarchies still shape evaluation. We file Bach as universal and gamelan as "world music". We assume "developing" countries should follow our path. The intellectual task of L2 is to denaturalise that assumption and recover the constitutive role of the colonised in making the modern world.

## 8. Essay Answers

### 2025 (Q1): Is it possible to fully understand modernity without understanding the role of colonialism in its development?

No, and arguing otherwise requires a curiously selective view of history. To "fully" understand modernity is to understand both its conceptual self-image (reason, progress, autonomy, the secular state) and its material conditions of possibility (industrial capitalism, world trade, mass consumption, the modern state). Colonialism is not an external accident attached to either of these. It is constitutive of both. I will defend this through three lenses (Dussel's philosophical genealogy, Hall's discursive analysis, and Howard French's economic history) before answering the obvious counter-argument that Europe's internal transformations (Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) suffice as an explanation.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* makes the strongest philosophical case. He argues that modernity "appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). The European ego that thinks itself modern only becomes thinkable when it can pose itself against an other. Cortés in Mexico, not Luther in Wittenberg, is the prototypical modern subject; the conquest of the New World, not the French Revolution, marks the birth of the "Will to Power specific to [Europe's] self-consciousness" (Dussel, 1993: 74). To "fully" understand modernity without colonialism is therefore to misunderstand the constitution of modern subjectivity itself. Habermas's mainstream genealogy (Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution) is, on Dussel's reading, "partial and provincial" (Dussel, 1993: 65).

Stuart Hall's 'The West and the Rest' essay translates this into a discursive register. The West is not a geography but a "system of representation" with four functions: classifying societies, condensing images, providing a model of comparison, and supplying criteria of evaluation (Hall, 1992: 277). Crucially, "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). Hall is borrowing from Saussure on linguistic difference: "night" only means what it means because of "day". By analogy, "modernity" only means what it means because of "non-modernity", and that means colonised territories function as the conceptual condition of the modern. Anyone who claims to understand modernity by looking only inside Europe is mistaking the picture for the canvas.

The economic argument is the most concrete. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* traces how Portugal's mid-15th-century push down the West African coast (for gold from Mali and Ghana, then for enslaved labour for São Tomé and later the Atlantic) supplied the capital that funded Europe's early modern boom. Sugar, tobacco and cotton, all plantation crops worked by enslaved Africans, financed the docks of Liverpool, the warehouses of Bristol, the financial houses of London and Amsterdam, and ultimately the steam-powered mills of Manchester. Even Marx (usually a Eurocentric witness) conceded that "without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). To "fully" understand the Industrial Revolution without slavery is to study the engine and ignore the fuel. Du Bois made the same point a century earlier: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit form the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 107, cited in the lecture slides).

The conventional answer (that modernity is the internal achievement of European reason) runs into three problems. First, it has to suppress the texts. Kant's "Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59) and Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70) are not stray asides; they are part of the architecture of the philosophical projects in which Enlightenment is celebrated. Second, it has to suppress the money. As soon as one traces the Lancashire cotton mill backwards or the Amsterdam bank backwards, the colonial relation surfaces. Third, it has to suppress the discourse. Hall's point is that even sympathetic European thinkers wrote inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The internalist story is not just incomplete: it is positively misleading because it naturalises the European outcome.

A more honest counter-position concedes that colonialism was constitutive but worries that emphasising it threatens the universal goods modernity also produced (human rights, scientific medicine, democratic governance). Dussel's reply is that we do not have to choose: he "affirms and subsumes" modernity's rational emancipatory concept while exposing the irrational myth that travelled with it (Dussel, 1993: 66). His proposal of "trans-modernity" (the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity, Dussel, 1993: 76) is precisely a way to retain the goods while refusing the myth.

So the answer is no. We cannot fully understand modernity without colonialism because the economic base, the philosophical self-image and the discursive grammar of the modern were all forged in the encounter between Europe and the worlds it conquered. To set colonialism aside is not to simplify the story; it is to tell a different one, the one Hegel told, and the one Habermas still tells. Both are too provincial to count as full understanding.

### 2024 (Q1): In what ways is modernity a colonial project?

To call modernity a colonial project is not to deny its rational and emancipatory ambitions; it is to argue that those ambitions cannot be separated from colonial conquest, racial hierarchy, and the violent imposition of one cultural template on the rest of the world. I want to defend this thesis in four moves: modernity's economic base, its philosophical self-image, its sociological vocabulary, and its surviving "attitude" today.

The economic base is the hardest to evade. Even Marx, normally an exemplar of Eurocentric sociology, conceded the point: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire ran on slave-grown cotton from the American South. The sugar that sweetened London and Amsterdam came from plantations staffed by enslaved Africans. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* extends this argument: Portugal's push down the West African coast in the 15th century, driven by gold and slaves, was a pre-condition not just of European wealth but of the financial instruments and consumer markets we now call "modern". Modernity, then, did not happen first in Europe and then spread; it was already a colonial relation in its formation.

The second move is philosophical. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* shows that the canonical European philosophers of reason wrote the colonial relation into the concept of modernity itself. He dates modernity's birth to 1492: "modernity as such was 'born' when Europe was in a position to pose itself against an other" (Dussel, 1993: 66). Kant's exhortation to leave "guilty immaturity" is paired with his judgement that "the Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel writes the global South out of history altogether ("Africa… does not have history as such") and grants the people that "bears" Spirit at its moment an "absolute right" against which other peoples "have no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70, 73). Dussel summarises this as the "myth of modernity": modernity packages rational emancipation with an irrational, sacrificial myth that licenses violence against those who resist the civilising project. The seven-step structure he draws out (superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault) gives modernity its colonial logic, not merely its colonial side-effects.

The third move is sociological. The lecture is explicit that sociology is "a creature of modernity": it arose to explain industrialisation, urbanisation and individualisation, and in doing so it inscribed the West-Rest hierarchy into its core vocabulary. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic transition treats non-European societies as pre-modern, behind, backward. Weber's rationalisation and disenchantment narrative makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Even the lecturer's question (does the rest of the world have history, in the sense modernity demands?) exposes the temporal hierarchy built into the discipline. Stuart Hall calls this "the West and the Rest": a discourse that "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" and that produces destructive over-simplifications of difference (Hall, 1992: 280). The "West" is not a place; it is a model of comparison, an evaluative grid, an ideology. Sociology has often supplied the data for that grid.

The fourth move is contemporary. The lecture asks us to test whether "we" still think this way. Western Classical Music is taught as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music"; Beethoven, Mozart and Bach are treated as the standard while non-Western musicians become "world musicians" (lecture slides). The lecturer's prompts about sport, fashion and which are "the best universities in the world, and why" surface the same habit: criteria that look neutral select the Anglo-European outcome. The Boris Johnson clip the lecture cites is not an aberration; it is a transparent instance of a habit of thought the discipline calls the colonial attitude.

A counter-position argues that modernity also gave us human rights, scientific medicine, mass literacy and democratic accountability, gains that have benefited the colonised too. This is fair. Dussel himself "affirms and subsumes" modernity's emancipatory concept (1993: 66). The point is not to discard modernity but to recognise that its rational kernel has always travelled with an irrational myth, and that the rights and goods we want to keep can only be honestly defended once their colonial history is acknowledged. Modernity is a colonial project not as an insult but as a description: at its economic base, in its philosophical self-image, in its sociological vocabulary and in its surviving everyday attitudes, the modern world is unintelligible without the colonies that helped make it.

### 2024 (Q9): How did European modernity benefit from unequal race relations? Discuss with reference to the work of two theorists.

The question asks how European modernity benefited from unequal race relations (note "benefited", not "involved" or "depended on", though both are also true). I will answer with reference to Karl Marx and Enrique Dussel. Marx supplies the economic mechanism by which racial inequality fed into the wealth of modern Europe; Dussel supplies the philosophical mechanism by which European modernity legitimated racial inequality to itself. Together they show that the benefit was both material and ideological, and that the two reinforced each other.

Marx is the right place to start because, despite his Eurocentric framings of the proletariat as the agent of history, he was unusually clear about the slave-trade foundation of industrial capitalism. The lecture cites the canonical line: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). What is at stake here is not a moralising aside but an analytical claim about the structure of value-creation. Modern industry needs raw materials in bulk at low prices; that requirement was met by plantation slavery in the Caribbean and the American South, where racial categorisation legitimised the coercion of enslaved Africans to work without wages. The cheapness of slave-grown cotton allowed Manchester mill-owners to undercut Indian and Chinese textile producers, ruining their industries and redirecting demand to Britain. The cheapness of slave-grown sugar created the mass European consumer market (coffee with sugar, tea with sugar) that sustained the early modern world economy. The cheapness of slave-grown tobacco enriched Virginia planters who funded the American revolution. Each of these chains converts unequal race relations into modern industrial wealth.

Du Bois, cited in the lecture, generalises Marx's point: "The new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The European working class was, on Du Bois's reading, "practically invited to share in this new exploitation", flattered by appeals to its racial superiority. Modern Europe benefited not only as a continent but as a racialised bloc that could be sold the story of its own elevation. The British working-class gain in real wages between 1750 and 1850 cannot be separated from the racialised redistribution of value out of the Atlantic system.

Dussel completes the picture by showing how this economic benefit was philosophically legitimated. His *Eurocentrism and Modernity* argues that modern European philosophy delivered a justifying myth for racial inequality at exactly the moment it was being economically institutionalised. Kant, the philosopher of Enlightenment, supplies the racial hierarchy: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel, the philosopher of Spirit, writes Africa out of history: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). And, crucially, Hegel gives empire its philosophical charter: "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). The slave colony is not just a piece of profitable real estate; it is a stage in the self-realisation of Reason. That is a powerful legitimation. It tells the European subject that the unequal race relation she profits from is also a service to the universal.

Dussel's "myth of modernity" generalises this into a seven-step structure: European superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault; modernity is innocent and emancipatory (Dussel, 1993: 75). The benefit, on this account, is double. Materially, Europe gets cheap labour, cheap raw materials and captive markets. Ideologically, Europe gets to think of itself as the bearer of universal reason while doing so. The combination is uniquely powerful because each side reinforces the other: the wealth funds the universities that produce the philosophy, and the philosophy legitimises the trade that produces the wealth.

Putting Marx and Dussel together yields a sharper account than either alone. Marx without Dussel risks reading slavery as an unfortunate historical accident attached to a structurally distinct capitalism. Dussel without Marx risks reading the myth of modernity as a purely cultural construct floating free of its economic stakes. Together, they show that European modernity benefited from unequal race relations in a tightly integrated way: race made the labour cheap, the trade legal, and the conscience clean. Du Bois's observation that the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107) confirms the integration. To understand modern Europe's wealth is to understand the racial inequality that produced it. Marx tells us how. Dussel tells us why no one in Europe felt obliged to notice.

### 2023 (Q1): Can we understand modernity without understanding its relationship to colonialism?

This question and the 2025 paper press the same point with different verbs. The 2023 wording ("can we understand") invites a slightly different answer. We can produce a partial, internalist account of modernity that ignores colonialism; people do it every term. But we cannot produce an account that survives the texts, the money, and the discourse once they are placed on the table. In what follows I sketch what that partial account looks like, then show why it fails three tests.

The standard partial account treats modernity as a European achievement with three internal pillars. Politically, the consolidation of centralised nation-states out of feudal kingdoms (Hall, 1992: 277). Religiously and intellectually, the Reformation and the Enlightenment, which Habermas takes as "the historical events that are decisive for the implantation of the principle of [modern] subjectivity" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 74). Economically, the Industrial Revolution. Sociologically, the discipline rests on Marx (commodification, alienation), Weber (rationalisation, disenchantment) and Durkheim (mechanical to organic solidarity). On this account, the colonies feature, if at all, as a market for surplus production or a recipient of the civilising mission: secondary, not constitutive.

The first reason this account fails is textual. Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* ("Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity", cited in Dussel, 1993: 68) sounds universal until you read the same author's *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel's *The Philosophy of History* states that "the movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West" (Hegel, 1837: 243) and that "Africa… does not have history as such" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). The "absolute right" of the people bearing Spirit against peoples who "have no rights" (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73) is not a footnote; it is the legitimation of empire by the philosopher whom Habermas continues to cite as the architect of modern subjectivity. To bracket colonialism is to bracket the architecture of the Enlightenment.

The second reason is economic. Marx himself (usually the Eurocentric exhibit) conceded: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The cotton mills of Manchester were not autonomous European achievements; they were nodes in a circuit that ran through enslaved plantations in the Americas, gold extracted from Africa, silver mined in Potosí, and consumers buying sugar, tobacco and cotton goods. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows that the financial instruments of early modern Amsterdam and London were tooled to manage slave-trading and slave-grown commodities (French, 2021). Take that out and the Industrial Revolution looks unmotivated.

The third reason is discursive. Hall's contribution is to show that even when modern thinkers were not explicitly racist, they were speaking inside a binary that needed the Rest to make sense of the West. The West is "a tool to think with… a system of representation" that classifies, condenses, compares and evaluates (Hall, 1992: 277). It functions as an ideology in which "western = developed = good = desirable" and "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). Until you see this, you cannot see why "developing country" is a value-laden category, why "the international community" is so often a code for the OECD, or why the BBC files Senegalese rap as "world music" while it files Bach as universal heritage. The discourse is so naturalised that it can be reproduced without ever mentioning colonialism, but it makes no sense without it.

Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* knits these together. His "myth of modernity" (superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; victims are at fault, Dussel, 1993: 75) is a single structure underwriting Kant's racial hierarchy, Hegel's philosophy of history, the East India Company, the conquest of the Americas, Belgian rule in the Congo, and the current rhetoric of "regime change". To understand modernity without colonialism, you would have to detach a structure from its instances: a strange thing for a sociologist to do.

A counter-position might say that the colonial element is real but is one factor among many; we can isolate other factors (technology, secularisation, the printing press) and study them on their own terms. This is true at the level of any particular question. The pebble can be examined without the riverbed. But the broader claim (that we can understand modernity as such without colonialism) fails because the riverbed is part of what makes the pebble visible as that kind of pebble. Modernity is not a discrete property; it is a relation. And the relation includes the colonised. So we can produce internalist accounts of modernity, but we cannot understand modernity in any deep sense without taking colonialism seriously.

### 2022 (Q3): Is modernity undermined by its roots in colonialism?

"Undermined" is the live word here. It could mean discredited (modernity is unworthy of allegiance because of its colonial origin), or destabilised (modernity is internally contradictory because the freedom it claims for some rests on the unfreedom of others), or unmasked (modernity is shown to be something other than it claims to be). I will argue that the second reading is correct: modernity is destabilised but not discredited by its colonial roots. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* is the clearest articulation of this position, and I will use Hall and Marx to support it.

Begin with the strong "discredited" reading. On this view, the modern world was built on slavery, conquest and theft. Therefore everything modernity values (human rights, science, democratic government, secular reason) is contaminated and should be rejected. This is sometimes attributed to Dussel or to the postmodernists Dussel cites: Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo. But it is not Dussel's position. He is explicit: "we do not propose a critique of reason as such" and "we do not deny the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment, only its irrational moment as sacrificial myth" (Dussel, 1993: 75). Modernity is not all bad. It is internally split.

That split is what is being asked about. Dussel argues that modernity carries a rational concept of emancipation, which he "affirms and subsumes", and an irrational myth, which he negates (Dussel, 1993: 66). The rational concept is genuine: autonomy, equality, the rule of law, scientific method, public reason. The irrational myth is the seven-step structure: superiority obliges civilising; resistance authorises violence; the victim is in a state of guilt; modernity emancipates by destroying (Dussel, 1993: 75). Both have been present from 1492 onwards. The Enlightenment writes the *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen* in 1789 while the French state runs slave plantations in Saint-Domingue. Kant writes *What is Enlightenment?* and *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). The undermining is internal; the same hand wrote both.

Hall's analysis sharpens this. The discourse of "the West and the Rest" is not a side-effect of European modernity; it is part of how modernity thinks itself: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West can only know itself by negation. Its universalism is structurally provincial. That is undermining in the second sense: modernity claims one thing (universal reason) and depends on another (a binary opposition between West and Rest), a performative contradiction.

The economic version of the same problem is Marx's. "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Modern industrial capitalism (its productivity, its mass markets, its working-class consumer) could not have happened without the slave-grown raw materials of the Atlantic system. Manchester is unintelligible without Mississippi. Liverpool is unintelligible without Lagos. So modernity's economic dynamism, often celebrated as the engine of human progress, rests on coerced and racialised labour. This does not "undermine" the productivity gains; the cotton was real; the wages did rise; but it undermines any account of those gains that treats them as the unaided fruit of European industriousness.

What kind of conclusion does this support? Three things follow. First, intellectual honesty: it is not possible to teach the Enlightenment without teaching its racial archive. Second, political reckoning: the contemporary debate over how the structural debts of the Atlantic economy Howard French documents (French, 2021) are to be acknowledged is the unfinished political work that modernity's colonial roots imposed on us. Third, and this is Dussel's distinctive move, the response is not less modernity but a "trans-modernity", a co-realisation of modernity and its alterity (Dussel, 1993: 76). The way out of the contradiction is not nostalgic anti-modernism nor postmodern relativism but a deeper modernity that finally takes seriously the universal claims it once made only for Europeans.

A counter-argument might insist that colonial cruelty was contingent; one could have had modernity without slavery. This is partly true, but it concedes the point: the modernity that actually exists is not modernity-in-general but a specific configuration whose roots run through the Atlantic system Howard French traces from Portuguese Elmina onwards (French, 2021). The lecturer's contemporary prompts (sport, music, fashion, university league tables, "best universities in the world") show that those roots still bear fruit in how the West evaluates the Rest.

So yes, modernity is undermined by its colonial roots, in the sense that it is internally contradicted, performatively self-betraying, and historically unable to deliver its universalism without exclusions. It is not for that reason worthless. But it cannot any longer be defended in the form it has handed itself down to us.

### 2021 (Q3): Does modernity have its roots in colonialism? If so, what are the implications for how we think of modernity?

This question is two questions. The first is descriptive: does modernity have colonial roots? The second is conceptual: what follows for how we use the word "modernity"? I will answer "yes" to the first and outline three implications for the second.

On the descriptive question, the case is now well established across the readings. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* dates the birth of modernity to 1492 rather than to the Reformation or the French Revolution. "Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). The European subject that we recognise as modern (confident, rational, world-historical) only becomes thinkable once Europe can pose itself against a conquered other. Cortés in Mexico is closer to the origin of modern subjectivity than Descartes in his stove. The conquest of America, not the *cogito*, is the founding event.

Hall's 'The West and the Rest' essay supports the same conclusion through discourse analysis: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West is not a primary fact about which we then ask how it relates to the Rest. It is a relational fact constituted by that comparison. The category "modern" inherits the structure.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* completes the picture with economic history: Portuguese expansion into West Africa from the 1430s, then the trans-Atlantic slave trade, supplied the gold, sugar, tobacco and cotton that financed Europe's long boom (French, 2021). Marx, whom the lecture quotes as a partial exception within classical sociology, recognised this: "It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The cotton mills of Lancashire are not autonomous European achievements; they are nodes in an Atlantic system.

So yes, modernity has colonial roots: philosophically (Dussel), discursively (Hall) and economically (French, Marx).

What follows? Three implications.

First, the periodisation of modernity has to change. The standard textbook places modernity's origin at the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment, with the Industrial Revolution as its consolidation. On the colonial reading, the origin is 1492: the conquest of Granada, Columbus's voyage, and the Iberian conquest of the New World. Spain and Portugal (discounted by both Hegel and Habermas, Dussel, 1993: 71) are restored to the centre of the story. The Lutheran Reformation and the French Revolution are downstream consequences of an earlier world-historical reorganisation rather than its founding moments. The Industrial Revolution is the late phase of a long Atlantic economy that begins with sugar in São Tomé and ends with steam in Manchester.

Second, the canon has to change. If Kant's racial remarks ("The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous", Kant, 1798: 59) are part of the architecture of Enlightenment, not stray asides, then teaching the Enlightenment without teaching Kant's race theory is teaching a falsified version. The same applies to Hegel's "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). The lecture itself models the corrective move by adding Du Bois (cited extensively from the 1970 collection in the slides), Dussel and Hall to the syllabus next to Marx, Weber and Durkheim. These are not optional addenda; they are part of the canon of modern thought because they are part of the story modernity has to tell about itself.

Third, the politics has to change. Dussel's proposal of "trans-modernity" (the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity, Dussel, 1993: 76) points to a politics that retains modernity's rational kernel (the rational concept of emancipation Dussel "affirms and subsumes", 1993: 66) but refuses its irrational myth (the civilising mission, the developmental ladder, the racialised hierarchy of value). The unfinished work of modernity is to extend the universals it once articulated only for Europeans to those who were originally excluded.

A counter-position would say: yes, the colonial story is real, but it is descriptively interesting rather than normatively destabilising. We can keep using "modernity" as a value-neutral term and address colonial wrongs as a separate political matter. The trouble with this is that "modernity" was never value-neutral. As Hall shows, it has always been an evaluative grid: "western = developed = good = desirable; non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). To keep using the word as if it were neutral is to keep underwriting the discourse. Better to name what the word is doing and decide whether we want to keep doing it.

So yes, modernity has colonial roots, and the implication is that periodisation, canon and politics all have to be redrawn so that the modernity we choose to defend is not the one Hegel and Habermas inherited but the one Dussel, Hall, Du Bois and Marx pushed against.

### M1: How convincing do you find Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity?

Enrique Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993) compresses an enormous theoretical claim into seven numbered steps. I find the structure convincing on three of its standard tests (fit with canonical texts, portability across cases, and explanatory yield) but vulnerable on a fourth: historical mechanism. I'll work through each step, score it, and reach a verdict.

The seven steps, as Dussel sets them out (Dussel, 1993: 75). (1) Modern European civilisation understands itself as most developed and superior. (2) This superiority obliges Europe, as a categorical imperative, to civilise the more primitive. (3) The path of development is the one Europe took. (4) Where the barbarian resists, modernity uses violence "in the last instance". (5) The violence is ritualised as sacrifice: victims participate in their redemption. (6) The barbarian is guilty for resisting; modernity is innocent and emancipatory. (7) The sufferings imposed on the "immature" are inevitable.

Convincing on textual fit. Step 1 is Hegel: "Europe is the absolute end of Universal History" (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69). Step 2 is Kant's *What is Enlightenment?*: humanity should leave "guilty immaturity" behind (Dussel, 1993: 68). Step 3 is the developmental schema embedded across Marx, Weber and Durkheim. Step 6 is Kant again: "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68); the African or American "subject in guilty immaturity" is at fault. Step 4 is Hegel's *Encyclopedia*, sections 346-347: the people that bears Spirit has "an absolute right" against which "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). The myth is not imposed on the canon; it is read out of it. That is a convincing piece of philological work.

Convincing on portability. The cases Dussel and the lecture themselves invoke fit the structure. Cortés in the Conquest of Mexico, which Dussel names as "the first extra-European space" in which the European ego carried out its "prototypic 'development'" (Dussel, 1993: 74): the Aztec is barbarian (step 1), Christian conversion is obligatory (step 2), the Spanish way is the universal way (step 3), Tenochtitlán's resistance authorises destruction (step 4), the violence is ritualised (step 5), the Aztec is coded as guilty (step 6), the deaths are read as inevitable (step 7). The Iberian Reconquest of Granada in 1492, with its "extreme sectarian violence" that became "the model for the colonization of the New World" (Dussel, 1993: 67), maps onto the same structure. So do the contemporary cases the slides cite (Iraq, Falklands) as violence "to impose the 'reasonableness' of modernity". A theoretical structure that captures cases separated by five centuries is doing real explanatory work.

Convincing on explanatory yield. The myth explains why critics of modernity often misfire. Postmodernists (Lyotard, Rorty, Vattimo) discard the rational kernel and so cannot defend the universal claims the colonised need to articulate their innocence (Dussel, 1993: 75). Habermasian defenders refuse to see the irrational myth and so reproduce the Eurocentric occlusion. Dussel's two-layer reading (rational concept of emancipation affirmed, irrational myth negated, Dussel, 1993: 66) explains why reasonable people disagree about modernity by tracking which layer they attend to. Few other postcolonial frameworks deliver that kind of meta-explanation.

Less convincing on historical mechanism. This is where I would push back. The seven steps are a logical structure, not a causal story. Dussel does not show, in this short essay, *how* the myth was produced: what discursive practices, what institutional pressures, what economic interests assembled it. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* supplies some of the missing mechanism, in the actual sequence of Portuguese expansion down the West African coast, the gold trade through Elmina, and the sugar plantation complex from Madeira to São Tomé to Brazil (French, 2021). Hall's discourse analysis supplies more: the West as a "system of representation" with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277). Dussel's seven steps need this empirical and discursive infrastructure to do their full work.

Less convincing on internal differentiation. The myth lumps together episodes that are very different from one another. The Spanish Conquest of Mexico is not the same kind of operation as the partition of Africa or the twentieth-century interventions the lecture cites. The family resemblance is real; the differences also matter, and Dussel's compressed seven-step form does not preserve them.

Less convincing on the redemptive register. Dussel's vocabulary (innocent victim, redemptive sacrifice, guilt) is theological and may grate on secular readers. The analytical content survives translation, but the rhetoric is sectarian.

My verdict: convincing as a diagnostic, less convincing as a complete theory. The seven steps give a student a portable instrument for reading any rhetoric of civilising mission and finding the same logic at work. That is more than most short philosophical essays deliver. The myth needs French's economic history and Hall's discourse theory to fill in mechanism, but as a diagnostic it has aged well. Highly convincing on what it sets out to do; under-developed on what it sets aside.

### M2: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry." Discuss with reference to Marx.

Marx writes, in the passage the lecture quotes: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The sentence is the most consequential admission in classical sociology that capitalist modernity is constitutively colonial. I want to take Marx at his word, trace the causal chain he sets out, and then assess where Marx's own framework remains Eurocentric despite the admission.

The chain runs in four moves: slavery, colonial value, world trade, large-scale industry. Howard French gives us the empirical body. The plantation complex began on Madeira in the 1420s with the first sugar mill of the Atlantic world, expanded onto São Tomé (which by the early 1520s was importing roughly two thousand enslaved Africans a year), then jumped the Atlantic to Brazil, Barbados, Jamaica and Saint Domingue (French, 2021). The cheapness of slave-grown sugar, tobacco and eventually cotton from places like the Mississippi gave the colonies their value; that value generated world trade, with Lisbon, Bristol and Liverpool figuring as central nodes in French's account; and that trade supplied the precondition of large-scale industry, with Lancashire and Manchester running on the slave-grown cotton Marx names. The Industrial Revolution, on Marx's reading, is unintelligible without this Atlantic system.

What Marx delivers, then, is structural integration. The slavery-cotton chain is not a moralising aside; it is an analytical claim about the relations of production. Modern industrial capitalism requires cheap raw materials at scale; the scale and cheapness are supplied by racialised plantation labour. The reordering matters: the Eurocentric story places European industrial dynamism at the start and treats slavery as a regrettable side-chapter. Marx's ordering puts slavery first. Manchester is unintelligible without the Bight of Biafra.

The implication for class analysis follows. If slave-grown cotton funded the wages and consumer goods of the British industrial proletariat, then the European working class is partly a beneficiary of slavery, not just a victim of capitalism. Du Bois made this explicit in the lines the lecture cites: "the European and white American working class was practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). Marx's quoted sentence sets up Du Bois's elaboration. The class analysis becomes a global racial division of labour, not just a national capital-labour division.

So far, so anti-Eurocentric. But three things temper the picture.

First, Marx's broader theory remains developmentalist. Dussel notes that, "from Hegel, the category of 'development' passed to Marx, and from there to its usage in current sociology and economic theory" (Dussel, 1993: 68 n.2): an "underdeveloped" country, for Hegel and then for Marx's developmentalist successors, is "not-modern", pre-Aufklärung. The cotton sentence does not undo this. Marx is anti-Eurocentric in his account of how capitalism functions, but Eurocentric in his account of how history unfolds.

Second, Marx does not theorise race. He names slavery but does not develop an account of racial hierarchy as an internal feature of capitalism. Why was the plantation labour racially marked? Why did African slavery in particular consolidate when other coercive arrangements were also available? Marx's tools (relations of production, accumulation, class struggle) are insufficient on their own. Du Bois's "veil" and Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse begin to fill in what Marx left undone, though both fall outside the cotton sentence itself.

Third, Marx does not theorise colonial agency. The agency of the colonised (the Haitian Revolution that the lecture slides flag, Du Bois, 1970: 103, 105) is not theorised in the quoted sentence. The colonies appear as supply zones for European industry, not as political subjects.

What survives, then, is a serious critique of Eurocentric sociology embedded in a still-Eurocentric historical schema. The lecture is right to call Marx "a possible exception". Two lessons follow. Methodologically, sociology that takes modernity seriously cannot stop at European borders: the Atlantic system is the unit of analysis, as French shows in detail. Pedagogically, teaching Marx without the cotton-slavery sentence produces a clean European Marx whose insight is invisible; teaching the sentence alongside the abstract analysis recovers a more honest Marx. The cotton sentence is the moment classical sociology gestures toward what it could have been if the colonial constitution of modernity had been its founding question.

### M3: Eurocentrism in classical sociology: blind spot or constitutive premise?

A blind spot is a defect: something missed that, once seen, can be corrected. A constitutive premise is a foundation: something built into the structure that cannot be removed without rebuilding the whole. The question is which describes the relationship between classical sociology and Eurocentrism. My answer is that the disjunction is sharper than it looks. For Durkheim and most of Weber, Eurocentrism is constitutive: it organises the founding questions. For Marx and parts of late Weber, it is closer to a blind spot, incompletely seen but not architectural. The verdict matters because the two diagnoses imply different remedies.

The case for constitutive premise begins with Durkheim. His distinction between mechanical solidarity (homogeneous, repressive law, primitive) and organic solidarity (differentiated, restitutive law, modern), set out in *The Division of Labour in Society*, is the foundation of the discipline. The schema is unimaginable without the assumption that European societies have moved from one to the other and that non-European societies remain in the first phase. The lecturer puts the colonial implication plainly: "the shift to modernity as going from Mechanical to Organic Societies… suggests that other societies around the world are still 'pre-modern' / behind / backward". Africa, indigenous America and Aboriginal Australia become living museums of European prehistory. Remove the developmental schema and there is no Durkheimian sociology. The Eurocentrism is not a blind spot Durkheim might have noticed; it is the architecture of the question.

Weber is the harder case. On the constitutive side: the central question of *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (why did rational capitalism develop in northwestern Europe and not elsewhere?) already presupposes that the European outcome is the standard. The comparative works on China, India and Ancient Judaism are studies of what those civilisations failed to produce. The rationalisation-disenchantment narrative makes Western bureaucracy the destination of human reason. Dussel calls this "the fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68). Weber's whole comparative apparatus is built on this premise.

But Weber is also the most resourceful of the three. He took non-European religious and economic traditions seriously enough to learn them. He registered the historical contingency of Western rationalisation rather than treating it as inevitable. He warned that the "iron cage" of bureaucratic rationality might be a curse rather than a triumph. The Eurocentrism is constitutive in the framing but not unalloyed in the substance.

Marx is the strongest case for blind spot. The cotton-slavery sentence ("Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry", Marx, 1977: 203) places the colonial relation at the heart of modern industry. This is structurally anti-Eurocentric. But Marx's broader developmental schema reads as a ladder with European destinations. Dussel notes the inheritance: "from Hegel, the category of 'development' passed to Marx, and from there to its usage in current sociology and economic theory" (Dussel, 1993: 68 n.2). Marx saw the colonial relation when he looked directly at cotton; he reverted to a Eurocentric schema when he looked at history as a whole. Blind spot rather than constitutive premise, but a blind spot the size of a continent.

So the answer disaggregates. Durkheim: constitutive. Weber: constitutive in framing, partially correctable in practice. Marx: a structural insight wrapped in a Eurocentric schema, closer to blind spot. The general claim that "classical sociology is Eurocentric" hides these differences and so blunts the remedies.

The remedies depend on the diagnosis. If Eurocentrism is constitutive, the discipline must rebuild. Dussel's redating of modernity to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 66), and Howard French's restoration of African gold, Portuguese Elmina, Madeiran sugar and the trans-Atlantic slave trade to the foundation of modern economies (French, 2021), are not additions to the syllabus but reorganisations of the founding question. The unit of analysis becomes the Atlantic system rather than the European nation-state. If Eurocentrism is a blind spot, the discipline can be extended: keep the analytic categories (alienation, rationalisation, anomie, division of labour) and supplement them with the voices Marx, Weber and Durkheim were not reading. Du Bois, cited in the lecture slides, is the obvious starting point: his observation that the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107) is a complement to Marx that the classical canon needed and did not produce.

A counter-position says the binary is forced: every discipline has its founding assumptions and Eurocentrism is just sociology's. The reply is Hall's: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The founding assumption is not arbitrary; it is a discursive operation with effects. Stuart Hall's four functions of the West-Rest discourse (classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating, Hall, 1992: 277) describe what sociology textbooks have done. The colonial attitude is not external to the discipline; it is part of how the discipline learned to talk about modernity.

My verdict: the disjunction is too clean for any one founder. Durkheim's Eurocentrism is constitutive; Weber's is constitutive but self-aware; Marx's is a blind spot wrapped around a structural insight. A decolonised sociology has to do different work on each. That is harder than the question's binary suggests, and more honest than picking a side.

### M4: How does the concept of the colonial attitude help us understand contemporary university league tables and the British Museum?

The colonial attitude, as the lecture uses the term, is the habit of bifurcated thinking that ranks the world into a developed European/Western core and a backward non-European periphery, and that takes the core's standards as the universal measure. The colonies are gone; the attitude continues to organise institutions that present themselves as neutral or scholarly. Two test cases (university league tables and the British Museum's continued holding of African royal regalia such as the Benin bronzes) show how the concept does explanatory work that other vocabularies cannot.

Take the league tables first. The lecture slide on the colonial attitude explicitly invites the question: "which are the best universities in the world, and why?" The answers, year after year, list Anglo-American and northern European institutions at the top, on criteria that are presented as neutral metrics: publication counts, citations in English-language journals, research income from familiar funding councils, reputation surveys whose respondents are drawn from the same Anglo-European networks. Universities in Africa, South Asia and Latin America are placed lower on those same indicators, then read off as if "the West" really were "the best".

Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse names exactly this combination. The discourse "represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy" through four operations: classification (which universities are world-class?), condensation (a stable top image), comparison (where does my university rank?), evaluation ("western = developed = good = desirable", "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable", Hall, 1992: 277, 280). The rankings perform each operation in metric form. Without the colonial-attitude framing, one might say the rankings are imperfect; with it, one sees that the imperfection is structured: it reproduces the geography of empire under the appearance of methodology.

Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) sharpens the diagnosis. Step 1: European civilisation understands itself as most developed (here: a Western university as the standard). Step 3: the path of development is the path the West took (publish in English on Anglo-American models). Step 6: the lower-ranked are at fault for their own position ("weak research culture", "insufficient internationalisation"). The vocabulary of "world-class" universities and "globally competitive" departments is the technical face of Dussel's civilising imperative. The lecture slide's prompt is itself the colonial attitude phrased as a question.

The British Museum is the sharper case because the colonial attitude has to be defended against an articulate counter-demand for repatriation. The Benin bronzes are a particularly clear example. Howard French shows that, well before any European reached the West African coast, the kingdom of Benin was casting plaques in brass using imported European copper and the lost-wax technique; the resulting works, "collectively known as the Benin bronzes" (French, 2021), are evidence of an African artistic and political tradition that long pre-dates the late-nineteenth-century moment at which they passed into European custody. Their continued residence in a metropolitan museum, against the wishes of the people who made them, is the colonial attitude in physical form.

Hall's discourse of the West-Rest grounds the assumption that the metropole is a more appropriate custodian than the place of origin. Dussel's seven steps supply the legitimating story: the West is most developed (step 1), this obliges it to preserve the heritage of less developed peoples (step 2), the standard of preservation is the European one (step 3), demands for return are coded as unreasonable (step 6: the demander as guilty of misunderstanding universal heritage), and the continuing holding is regrettable but necessary (step 7). Each argument the institution makes can be located on the structure. The "universal museum" framing is particularly revealing: the universal viewing position is reserved for the metropolitan capital.

Howard French's wider argument deepens the diagnosis. *Born in Blackness* shows that the African polities Europeans encountered (Mali, Songhai, Benin, Kongo, Asante) were sophisticated states that traded with Europe on their own terms, and that the African continent was constitutive of modern wealth, not external to it (French, 2021). The bronzes are evidence of an African modernity that Hegel denied: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). The European custody of those works is the legacy of that denial in vitrine form.

Why does the concept of the colonial attitude do work that other vocabularies miss? Three reasons. First, it links rankings and museums (and many other practices) to a single structure rather than treating them as unrelated. Second, it explains why apparently technical defences (metrics, preservation) replicate hierarchies: the technique is the inheritance. Third, it points to remedies that are continuous with the project Dussel calls trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76): retaining the rational goods (scholarship, public access) while extending them past the colonial boundary. The colonial attitude is not an old habit waiting to die. It is alive in spreadsheets and vitrines, and naming it is the first step to undoing it.

### M5: Critically assess Howard French's argument in *Born in Blackness*.

Howard W. French's *Born in Blackness: Africa, Africans, and the Making of the Modern World, 1471 to the Second World War* (2021) argues a single thesis with great empirical density: Africa, not Europe and not the Americas, is the constitutive ground of the modern world. I want to set out the argument in its three strongest claims, evaluate its evidence, and identify two limitations that a sociologist (rather than a journalist-historian) needs to register.

French's first claim is that Portuguese expansion down the West African coast from the 1430s onwards was the genuine origin event of European modernity. The standard textbook foregrounds 1492 and Columbus, but Columbus is downstream: he was looking for a sea route to Asian commodities because the trans-Saharan gold trade was running through Mali under Mansa Musa (whose 1324 Mecca pilgrimage destabilised Mediterranean bullion markets) and the Portuguese were already buying that gold at coastal forts like Elmina (1482). African gold, not American silver, funded the first phase of European mercantilism and the maritime technology that made the Atlantic crossing possible. French's reordering matters: it relocates modernity's birth from the Caribbean to the Gulf of Guinea, and it makes the West African polities (Mali, Songhai, Kongo, Benin, Asante) central rather than incidental (French, 2021).

The second claim is that the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the plantation economies were constitutive of modern capitalism, not external to it. The slave-grown sugar of Madeira from the 1420s, then São Tomé from the early 1500s, then Brazil, Barbados, Jamaica and Saint Domingue, and the slave-grown cotton fed to Liverpool and on to the Lancashire mills, generated the consumer markets and industrial raw materials of the modern Atlantic economy. French traces the institutions involved: the Portuguese Crown monopoly at Elmina (1482), the Royal African Company and its monopoly on the English side, the slave-trading ports of Bristol and Liverpool, and the plantation complex linking the Bight of Biafra to the Mississippi. This is the empirical body for Marx's compressed claim: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203).

The third claim, and the most distinctive, is African agency. Kongo, Benin, Dahomey, Asante and Songhai were sophisticated polities whose internal politics, military capacities and commercial ambitions shaped what Europeans could and could not do on the coast. The trans-Atlantic slave trade was not a one-sided European action on a passive continent; it was a negotiated and contested system in which African elites participated, profited and sometimes resisted. French is careful: this is not exoneration of European responsibility; it is restoration of African historical agency against the Hegelian erasure ("Africa… does not have history as such", Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70).

How convincing is the argument?

On evidence, very. French is a long-time *New York Times* correspondent in Africa and the Caribbean; he reads Portuguese, French and Spanish archives; he visits Elmina, Cape Coast, São Tomé, Brazilian and Caribbean sites. The empirical density makes it difficult to dismiss the argument as polemical. Where Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* operates at the level of philosophical structure, French operates at the level of ports, ships, account books and dynasties. The two together do work neither does alone.

On the historiographical move, convincing with caveats. French synthesises a generation of African and African-diasporic scholarship for a popular audience. The synthesis is well done but not entirely original; specialists have been saying much of this already. What French adds is reach and narrative force, and a willingness to argue that the standard textbook story (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) is positively misleading rather than merely incomplete.

Two limitations matter for a sociologist.

First, French is empirically rich but theoretically thin. He shows that Africa was constitutive of modernity; he does not theorise the structure of European self-understanding that erased that constitution. There is no "myth of modernity" framework in French. Dussel's seven steps (Dussel, 1993: 75) and Hall's analysis of 'The West and the Rest' as a discourse with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277) do the theoretical work that French's narrative gestures at without performing. A sociologist needs the theoretical scaffolding to do explanatory work; French supplies the materials.

Second, French's emphasis on African agency, while necessary, sits awkwardly with his emphasis on the violence of the trade. The same African elites who participated were also caught in European-imposed market structures; Kongo's involvement, for example, became partly defensive against neighbours who were also drawn into it. French handles this with care but the tension is real, and the balance between restoring agency and not minimising coercion is one a careful reader has to manage essay by essay.

My verdict: *Born in Blackness* is an important book that does decisively what synthesising works can do: make a specialist consensus available to a general readership. It belongs on any L2 reading list. Its argument is more convincing taken alongside Dussel and Hall than read in isolation, because what French shows empirically the theorists explain structurally. Together they make the case that modernity, the West and capitalism are unintelligible without the African continent that helped make them. Read French for the bodies, the ports and the ships; read Dussel and Hall for why Europe forgot.

### M6: What does Hall mean by "the West and the Rest", and why does he treat it as a discourse of power rather than a geographical description?

Two questions, one analytic. What Hall means by "the West and the Rest" cannot be detached from why he treats it as a discourse. A geographical description records what is there; a discourse produces what counts as there. Hall's argument is that "the West" was never primarily a region. It is a position from which the world has been classified, ranked and acted upon, and the position has effects whether or not the geography supports it.

What Hall means. "The West", in *Formations of Modernity*, is not Europe-plus-its-settler-extensions on a map. It is "a system of representation" with four functions (Hall, 1992: 277). First, it *classifies* societies into western and non-western, "a tool to think with" that sorts the world into two boxes. Second, it *condenses* a composite image: the West is urban, industrial, secular, democratic, individualist, scientific; the Rest is rural, agrarian, religious, despotic, collectivist, traditional. Third, it *provides a standard of comparison* so that other societies are read as "close to" or "far from" the West, "catching up" or "falling behind". Fourth, it *provides criteria of evaluation*: "western = developed = good = desirable" against "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). The four functions interlock. The classification supplies the binary; the condensation gives it content; the comparison makes it operational; the evaluation makes it ideological.

The Saussurean argument behind the four functions. Hall borrows from Saussure on linguistic difference: "night" and "day" do not name primary things; they signify by their difference from each other. By analogy, "the West" does not name a primary set of properties; it signifies by its difference from "the Rest". "The so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest), very different in their histories, ecologies, patterns of development and cultures from the European model" (Hall, 1992: 278). The constitutive relation is at the heart of what Hall means.

Why is this a discourse rather than a geographical description? Three reasons mark the difference.

First, Hall's category does not match the geography. The "West" includes the United States. Hall observes that "Japan, is 'western', though on our mental map it is about as far 'East' as you can get" and that "much of Latin America, which is in the western hemisphere, belongs economically to the Third World, which is struggling, not very successfully, to catch up with 'the West'" (Hall, 1992: 276). The Rest, meanwhile, lumps together places Hall lists as "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia", and "the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280), into a single residual category despite their being more different from each other than from "the West". A descriptive geography would not produce this map. A discourse can.

Second, the discourse is productive. "The idea of 'the West'… had real effects: it enabled people to know or speak of certain things in certain ways. It produced knowledge. It became both the organising factor in a system of global power relations and the organising concept or term in a whole way of thinking and speaking" (Hall, 1992: 278). The categorisation does not just describe a world; it shapes what counts as a question worth asking, a society worth studying, a problem worth solving. The colonial attitude that Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" diagnoses (Dussel, 1993: 75) is one product of the discourse; the lecturer's slide examples (Western Classical Music as "universal" while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music"; the question of which are the "best universities in the world, and why?") are others.

Third, the discourse simplifies in ways that a description should not. Hall is explicit: "It draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). Simplification is what the discourse does: it represents the internally differentiated West (Germanic, Latin, Iberian, Nordic, Mediterranean) as one bloc and the more differentiated Rest as another. A descriptive geography would resist that simplification. A discourse of power requires it: without simplification, the binary cannot do its sorting work.

To call this a discourse of power, three further things follow. The discourse has internal others as well as external ones: Jews, Eastern Europeans and women within the West are positioned analogously to the colonised outside (Hall, 1992: 280). The discourse organises material institutions (universities, museums, news geographies, development banks) that operationalise the binary in concrete decisions. And the discourse is constitutive of modern subjectivity itself, in Dussel's complementary register: "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65).

Why does Hall insist on this framing? Because treating "the West" as a geographical description would naturalise it, make it look like a fact about the world rather than an operation on the world. Treating it as a discourse of power denaturalises it: it makes visible the work the category does, the simplifications it requires, the institutions it sustains. The framing is the politics. Once you can see the discourse as a discourse, you can analyse a Lancashire cotton mill, a Madeira sugar plantation, the lecturer's "best universities in the world" question, or the rhetoric Dussel's slides associate with Iraq and the Falklands, as performances of the same structure. That is what Hall's 'West and the Rest' is for: not a description but a diagnostic.

### M7: Compare Kant's account of non-European peoples with Hegel's.

Kant and Hegel are the two pillars Dussel uses, in *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, to show that the colonial relation is built into the architecture of canonical modern philosophy. They make the same kind of move but through different mechanisms. Comparing them precisely matters because the differences determine which forms of recuperation are available today.

Kant's mechanism is universal-in-form, racially-restrictive-in-content. The famous Enlightenment slogan reads as inclusive: "Enlightenment is the exodus of humanity by its own effort from the state of guilty immaturity. Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why the greater part of humanity remains pleasurably in this state of immaturity" (Kant, *What is Enlightenment?*, cited in Dussel, 1993: 68). The word is "humanity". Any reader could be flattered into thinking themselves included. But Kant's empirical anthropology, set out in *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*, narrows the universal sharply: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). The universal is qualified by a racial hierarchy that excludes most of the species from the rational vocation it claims to extend to everyone.

Dussel's interrogation gets the contradiction exactly. "An African in Africa or as a slave in the United States in the eighteenth century; an Indian in Mexico or a Latin American mestizo: Should all of these subjects be considered to reside in a state of guilty immaturity?" (Dussel, 1993: 68). The Kantian answer is yes, but the immaturity, in their case, is not curable by individual effort because the racial constitution prevents it. The "guilty immaturity" coding does double work: it blames the colonised for their condition (Dussel's step 6) while denying them the rational equipment to leave it.

Hegel's mechanism is different: hierarchical-in-form, with non-Europeans assigned positions in a developmental schema or excluded from the schema altogether. World History, in *The Philosophy of History*, is "the development of the consciousness that the Spirit has of its freedom" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68), and the development is geographically directed: "The movement of Universal History goes from the East to the West. Europe is the absolute end of Universal History. Asia is its beginning" (Hegel, 1837: 243, cited in Dussel, 1993: 69). Latin America is excluded as "land of the future": "as a land of the future Latin America has no interest for us, because the philosopher does not make prophesies" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 69). Africa is excluded more brutally: "Africa… does not have history as such. Consequently we abandon Africa, to never mention it again. It is not part of the historical world" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). And on the African himself: "Among negroes it is the case that consciousness has not attained even the intuition of any sort of objectivity… [The negro] is the man as beast" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70).

The contrasts are sharp.

On argumentative structure: Kant smuggles racial exclusion into a universal frame; Hegel makes hierarchy the frame itself. Kant's reader can be misled by the universal language until she meets the *Observations*; Hegel's reader cannot be misled because the geography of Spirit is announced at the outset.

On the function of non-Europeans: Kant uses them as moral foils, examples of what humans look like before they have done the Enlightenment work. Hegel uses them as historical stages: the African is the dawn of Spirit, the Asian its childhood, the European its mature end. The function is similar (Europeans as standard) but the technique differs: Kant's empirical anthropology vs Hegel's metaphysical historicism.

On political implication: Kant's writing on race accompanies the Atlantic slave trade that Howard French documents as central to early-modern European wealth (French, 2021). Hegel goes further: he supplies the philosophical charter for empire. "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). And he extends the same logic to colonial expansion: civil society "is, in the first place, driven to seek outside itself new consumers" and seeks "ways to move about among other peoples that are inferior to it with respect to the resources that it has in abundance" (Philosophy of Right, sections 246 and 248, cited in Dussel, 1993: 74). The colonial "free space" is, on Hegel's reading, available because the people who already live there "have no rights" against the bearer of Spirit.

What does the comparison imply for reading them today? Kant is more deceptive but more recuperable. The universal claim can be turned against the racial qualification: one can argue, with Dussel's "trans-modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 76), that Kant fails by his own standard and that the rational kernel survives the negation of the irrational myth. Hegel offers less to recuperate because the hierarchy is the architecture: removing the racial geography of World History leaves a different system, not a corrected one. A "Hegel against himself" reading is therefore more reconstructive than a "Kant against himself" reading.

But both readings are possible. Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 75) supplies the structure: keep the rational concept of emancipation, expose the irrational myth, extend the universals to those the canonical author excluded. The point is not to discard the canon but to teach the Enlightenment with its racial archive: to read *What is Enlightenment?* alongside the *Observations*, the *Philosophy of History* alongside the *Encyclopedia*. The student who has done that reading is better equipped to recognise the colonial attitude in its contemporary forms (the lecturer's sport, music, fashion, "best universities" examples; the Iraq and Falklands cases Dussel's slide cites) than the student who learned a sanitised Enlightenment.

### M8: Multiple modernities: useful pluralism or empty inclusion?

The phrase "multiple modernities" gestures at a family of modernities rather than a single European original to which others must conform. Within L2's source materials the cognate move is Dussel's: he rejects "the fallacy of developmentalism", the assumption that "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68), and proposes "trans-modernity" as an alternative (Dussel, 1993: 76). The question for this essay is whether the pluralism such proposals offer is theoretically useful or whether it merely papers over the asymmetries Dussel, Hall and French analyse.

The case for useful pluralism is real. The standard sociological story (industrialisation, urbanisation, individualisation in Europe, then diffused) treats non-European societies as "pre-modern" or "backward" by default. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic schema, as the lecture notes, suggests that "other societies around the world are still 'pre-modern' / behind / backward" (lecture slides). Pluralising modernity is the conceptual move that breaks this default. It says that there is more than one trajectory, that earlier and contemporary non-European polities had their own modernisations, and that the European outcome is one configuration rather than the destination. Dussel's whole intervention requires this much: his redating of modernity to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 66) reorients the story so that Iberia, the Americas and Africa appear as constitutive rather than as later recipients.

Howard French's *Born in Blackness* extends the case empirically. He documents West African polities (Mali, Songhai, Kongo, Benin, Asante) that were sophisticated states engaged in long-distance trade, internal administration, religious and artistic production, well before any sustained European presence (French, 2021). The Benin bronzes alone are evidence of an African modernity (technical, political, aesthetic) that Hegel denied existed at all. Pluralising modernity is a way of recognising what French restores.

But the case for empty inclusion is also strong, and pressing it matters.

First, the pluralism can dissolve the colonial relation. If every society has its own modernity, then the asymmetric relations Howard French documents (Portuguese Elmina, Madeiran and São Tomé sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans, the trans-Atlantic plantation complex feeding European wealth, French, 2021) can recede from view. Marx put the chain bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). A pluralism that misses this becomes ideology: it pluralises the cultural register while leaving the extractive structure unnamed.

Second, the pluralism can be a Western gift. The act of classifying "their" modernity as also valid leaves intact the discursive position from which the classifying happens. Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse makes the point: the West functions as a "system of representation" whose operations include classification, condensation, comparison and evaluation (Hall, 1992: 277). A West that generously concedes "multiple modernities" is still the West doing the classifying. The discourse's structure is preserved.

Third, Dussel's own diagnosis warns against bare recognition. The seven-step myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) is not undone by acknowledging that other societies were also modern. It is undone by exposing the irrational myth that licensed violence against those societies in the name of civilisation. Recognition without that exposure is, in Dussel's terms, modernity continuing to "present itself not only as innocent but also as a force that will emancipate or redeem its victims from their guilt" (Dussel, 1993: 75). The pluralist label can do nothing against this.

How should one adjudicate?

The most defensible position is that "multiple modernities" is useful as a starting point and empty as a stopping point. It is the necessary first move against the unilinear narrative the lecture diagnoses (Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic, the West holding history while "the rest of the world" does not, the colonial attitude reproduced in sociology's vocabulary). But on its own it does not name the asymmetric relations between modernities, the colonial extraction Howard French traces from gold to sugar to cotton, or the philosophical legitimation Dussel reads out of Kant and Hegel. It needs to be supplemented by Dussel's trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76), which insists not merely on plural recognition but on a "co-realisation" of modernity and its negated alterity, with the colonised first "discovering [themselves] as innocent" of the originary violence (Dussel, 1993: 76).

Hall and French both push in the same direction. Hall warns that the West-Rest discourse is "destructive" because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions" and produces an over-simplified conception of difference (Hall, 1992: 280); recognising plural modernities will not undo the discourse unless the distinctions themselves are denaturalised. French insists that Africa was the "linchpin of the machine of modernity" (French, 2021); a pluralism that does not register this dependency simply lets the European outcome appear as one option among many rather than as one that fed off the others.

So: useful pluralism, yes, if it knows it is unfinished. Empty inclusion if it congratulates itself on having included. Trans-modernity, on Dussel's reading, is the harder version of the move that "multiple modernities" begins.

### M9: Does the climate crisis vindicate or refute Dussel's myth of modernity?

The climate crisis is a useful test case for Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 75). If the diagnosis describes a real structure, the way the climate crisis is currently being framed and managed should map onto its seven steps. If the crisis shows modernity self-correcting through rational institutions, the myth thesis is weakened. My argument is that the framing vindicates Dussel's diagnosis more than it refutes it, but that vindication is uncomfortable enough to push the framework somewhere Dussel did not take it.

Begin with the structural pattern of the crisis. Cumulative emissions are unequal by historical responsibility: the territories that industrialised first burned most of the cheap atmospheric space, while the heaviest impacts (drought, flood, sea-level rise) fall disproportionately on the territories Dussel's framework calls the periphery. That distribution is, structurally, colonial extraction continued by atmospheric means: the wealth that European industrialisation built on the slavery-cotton-industry chain Marx names ("It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry", Marx, 1977: 203) has now generated atmospheric damage borne by other societies. The colonial attitude continues in a new register.

How does this map onto Dussel's seven steps?

Step 1 (Europe most developed): climate diplomacy is conducted in a vocabulary of "developed" and "developing" states. The taxonomy is identical to the "fallacy of developmentalism" Dussel critiques: "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68).

Step 2 (obligation to civilise): Northern actors offer "decarbonisation assistance" to Southern recipients on Northern terms, technology and finance flowing from the same direction as the older civilising mission, with green vocabulary attached.

Step 3 (the path is Europe's path): later industrialisers are criticised for coal-heavy growth in language that elides the fact that European industrialisation used the cheap atmospheric space first. Howard French's account of how slave-grown sugar and then cotton drove the Industrial Revolution (French, 2021) is precisely what is occluded by "you must decarbonise as we did".

Step 4 (violence in the last instance): coercion now operates as structural rather than military, in tariffs, conditionalities, and border controls. The lecturer's slide on Dussel's main points already lists Iraq and the Falklands as examples of "violence to impose the 'reasonableness' of modernity"; the climate register extends that logic.

Step 5 (sacrificial ritualisation): vulnerable populations are presented as participants in their own redemption through Northern-led investment. The slide's image of victims as "participants in a process of redemptive sacrifice" (Dussel, 1993: 75) reads cleanly onto this.

Step 6 (the victim is guilty): the late industrialisers are coded as the urgent problem; the longer carbon history recedes. Echoing Hegel's image of an African "without history", environmental damage in the global South is sometimes coded as "resource conflict" or population pressure rather than as the working-out of a historical relation.

Step 7 (sufferings are inevitable): climate damages in the South are framed as conditions to adapt to rather than debts to settle. The vocabulary is identical to Dussel's: "the suffering and sacrifices (the costs) of modernization imposed on 'immature' peoples, enslaved races, the 'weaker' sex, et cetera, are inevitable and necessary" (Dussel, 1993: 75).

This is heavy vindication. The climate crisis is currently being managed inside the structure Dussel's essay names.

But two things push past Dussel.

First, the climate crisis is the first modernity-internal contradiction that threatens the Global North materially. Dussel's framework treats the violence as exported; the climate crisis is the moment at which a substantial fraction of the violence comes home to the metropole. The Northern realisation that some of the costs are now imported should, in principle, weaken the myth's psychological hold. Whether it will is the live political question.

Second, Dussel's constructive proposal of trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) is articulated as a "co-realisation" between centre and periphery, with the periphery first "discovering itself as innocent" of the violence imposed on it. That is the right form. But the scale of redistribution implied by climate damages is large enough that the recognition has to be followed by institutional reorganisation Dussel does not sketch. The seven-step diagnosis fits the crisis; the trans-modern remedy is under-specified for it.

So my answer: vindicates more than refutes. The crisis confirms that the structure of the myth of modernity persists in contemporary global governance. It also shows that Dussel's diagnosis is more developed than his constructive proposal, and that Hall's and French's analyses (the West-Rest discourse classifying responsibility, the Atlantic system as the source of European wealth, French, 2021; Hall, 1992: 277-280) are needed to fill the gap between diagnosis and remedy. Dussel's seven steps name the structure. The case shows the structure is still in operation.

### M10: Using the work of at least two authors, assess the case for reparations as a modernity-internal demand.

A reparations demand is usually framed as extra-modern: a moral claim made against modernity from somewhere outside it. I want to argue, using Marx and Dussel together with Howard French in support, that the demand is better read as modernity-internal: a debt that modernity's own rational kernel makes payable once its irrational myth is stripped away. Modernity's universal claims (property, contract, restitution for wrongful taking, equal moral worth) generate the obligation as soon as they are extended to the people from whom they were originally withheld.

The case rests on three steps.

First, the economic foundation. Marx supplies it in the line the lecture quotes: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* fills in the empirical body. The first sugar mill of the Atlantic world was set up on the newly conquered island of Madeira in the 1420s; by mid-century Madeira was producing about seventy tons of sugar a year on enslaved labour, brought first from the Canaries and then from the West African mainland (French, 2021). São Tomé followed, importing roughly two thousand enslaved Africans a year by the early 1520s, and from there the sugar-plantation complex jumped the Atlantic to Brazil, Barbados and Jamaica, eventually pulling cotton along with it. Du Bois, cited in the lecture slides, generalised the point: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe", and the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The wealth that funded later European modernity, including the cotton mills Marx names, was substantially generated by slave labour and colonial extraction.

Second, the philosophical move. Dussel's *Eurocentrism and Modernity* shows that modernity contains both a rational concept of emancipation and an irrational myth that legitimates genocidal violence (Dussel, 1993: 66). The rational concept includes property rights, restitution for wrongful taking, and equal moral worth under law. These are the categories that modern states use every day to settle disputes between citizens: when one party takes another's labour or property without consent, restitution is owed. Apply the same categories to the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the colonial extractions French documents. The taking was without consent. The labour was unpaid. The receipts went to identifiable beneficiaries: the Crown that licensed the trade, the chartered companies (the Portuguese Crown monopoly at the Elmina fort built in 1482, and later the Royal African Company, whose monopoly French shows directing both trade and policy) and the planters who held the slaves. The successor institutions are legally continuous. The categories of modern law therefore generate an obligation to repay. The obligation has not been honoured because the categories were never extended to those modernity called barbarians, but the obligation exists inside modernity's own logic, not outside it.

This is what makes the demand modernity-internal. Dussel's seven-step myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) explains why the obligation has been ignored: step 6 codes the victim as guilty (the African as guilty primitive), step 7 codes the costs of modernisation as inevitable, and the rational kernel is covered by the irrational myth. The reparations demand is, in this register, a request to peel the myth back and let the rational kernel do its work. It does not require importing new moral categories. It requires applying the existing ones consistently.

Third, the discursive obstacle. The reason the demand reads as extra-modern, rather than internal, is the discourse Stuart Hall calls "the West and the Rest". The West is "a system of representation" whose four functions (classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating) install a binary in which "western = developed = good = desirable" and "non-western = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). Inside this discourse, the colonised are not equivalent legal subjects who can call on restitution; they are a residual category from whom value flows without return. Hall's point is exactly that the discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). A routine legal grammar therefore reads, in this case, as a heretical request.

Two objections need to be answered.

Objection one: temporal distance. The transactions are old; the descendants are far removed. The reply is that the institutional beneficiaries (chartered companies, planters' estates, the state revenues that taxed the trade) are legally continuous with successor institutions. Continuity of institution is sufficient for continuity of obligation, on modernity's own conception of legal personality.

Objection two: collective guilt. Reparations is not about individual contrition. It is about unjust enrichment passed forward through institutions that are still solvent. The demand is for redistribution from those institutions, not personal apology from their current members.

So: reparations is modernity-internal because it uses modernity's own legal and moral categories, extended to those modernity originally excluded. Marx supplies the economic chain (slavery, cotton, modern industry); Dussel supplies the philosophical structure (rational kernel under irrational myth); French supplies the empirical case (Madeira and São Tomé sugar, the Atlantic plantation complex as foundation of the modern world economy); Hall explains why a routine legal grammar reads, in this case, as a heretical request. The demand is not anti-modern reaction. It is, in Dussel's vocabulary, the co-realisation of modernity and its negated alterity (Dussel, 1993: 76): modernity finally honouring the universals it once articulated only for some. The bill is overdue.

### M11: Can sociology be decolonised without abandoning its classical canon?

The question turns on what "decolonising" sociology actually demands. The strong version says: the canonical founders (Marx, Weber, Durkheim) are so deeply Eurocentric that decolonising the discipline requires displacing them. The weak version says: the founders can be read critically, supplemented and reframed, without abandonment. I want to argue for an intermediate position. Sociology can be decolonised without abandoning the canon, but only on three conditions: that the canon is read with its colonial archive, that the canon is supplemented with the voices it excluded, and that the discipline's founding question is rewritten.

Begin with the case that the canon must go. Durkheim's mechanical-to-organic schema, as the lecture notes, places non-European societies into the "premodern, behind, backward" column by default. Weber's question (why did rational capitalism develop in northwestern Europe and not elsewhere?) already takes the European outcome as the standard. Both presuppose Dussel's "fallacy of developmentalism": "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67-68). The Eurocentrism is not a stain on the texts; it is the architecture of the questions. On this strong reading, no amount of supplementation can save them.

But the strong reading has three difficulties.

First, abandonment is empirically expensive. The analytic categories the founders built (alienation, anomie, rationalisation, division of labour, ideology, status, class) are genuinely useful for sociology of any geographical scope. They were derived from European cases but they describe relations that are not unique to Europe. Abandonment would forfeit these tools without an obvious replacement.

Second, the canon contains internal critics. Marx is the lecture's "possible exception": "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203) is one of the most consequential admissions in classical sociology that capitalist modernity is colonial. Du Bois, cited in the lecture slides, develops the same point: "the new colonial theory transferred the reign of commercial privilege and extraordinary profit from the exploitation of backward races under the political domination of Europe" (Du Bois, 1970: 107), and the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). Reading these voices into the canon rather than out of it is the decolonising move.

Third, abandonment can be performative. Dropping Marx, Weber and Durkheim from the syllabus while teaching other still-European thinkers does not decolonise; it relabels. The structural problem persists.

What does decolonisation look like, then, if the canon stays? Three conditions.

Condition one: read the canon with its colonial archive. Teach Marx with the cotton-slavery sentence, not without it. Teach Durkheim's mechanical/organic distinction with explicit attention to its colonial implication that "other societies around the world are still 'pre-modern' / behind / backward" (lecture slides). Teach Kant's *What is Enlightenment?* alongside the *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime*: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59, cited in the lecture slides). Teach Hegel's account of World History with the sentences Dussel cites: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70) and "the spirit of other peoples has no rights" against the bearer of Spirit (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). Reading the canon without the racial archive is teaching a sanitised version; reading it with the archive is teaching the canon honestly.

Condition two: supplement the canon with the voices it excluded. The lecture's reading list itself models this: Dussel, Hall and French are added precisely because the standard founders did not say what they said. Du Bois belongs to the same supplementation. The point is not to fill diversity quotas but to recognise that these thinkers theorised the modernity Marx, Weber and Durkheim were describing from inside Europe, and theorised it with information the founders either did not have or chose not to use.

Condition three: rewrite the discipline's founding question. The standard question ("what is modern society and how did it come to be?") presupposes that modernity is a European phenomenon to be analysed. A decolonised question ("how did the modern world come to be, and how do its colonial relations continue to structure it?") keeps the analytic categories but reorients the unit of analysis. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* shows what this looks like in practice: Portugal's mid-fifteenth-century arrival at Elmina, the first Atlantic sugar mill on Madeira around 1425, São Tomé's sugar boom and the trans-Atlantic plantation complex are read as nodes of a single system that also includes Manchester, Liverpool and Bristol (French, 2021). The class struggle in nineteenth-century Manchester is studied alongside the Haitian Revolution that Du Bois treats in the lecture slides as having "enormous" consequences for the Atlantic world (Du Bois, 1970: 103, 105).

So my answer: yes, sociology can be decolonised without abandoning its classical canon, but only by reading the canon honestly, supplementing it generously, and rewriting the founding question. Dussel's trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) is the right description of what the project requires: keep the rational kernel of modern social analysis while extending it to the people and places the founders left out.

### M12: Trans-modernity in 2026, programme or pious wish?

Dussel ends *Eurocentrism and Modernity* with the proposal of trans-modernity: "a process that will transcend modernity as such, a trans-modernity, in which both modernity and its negated alterity (the victims) co-realize themselves in a process of mutual creative fertilization" (Dussel, 1993: 76). The vocabulary is ambitious; the proposal is sketched in a single paragraph. The question is whether it is a genuine programme, with the conceptual resources to be cashed out into concrete change, or a pious wish that decorates academic essays without doing work. My argument is that trans-modernity is a programme in the strong sense, that Dussel himself, Stuart Hall and Howard French between them already supply most of the conceptual scaffolding it requires, and that its weakness is one of institutional follow-through rather than intellectual coherence.

Begin with what the programme requires. Trans-modernity is a co-realisation: keep modernity's rational kernel and reject its irrational myth (Dussel's seven steps, 1993: 75). The colonised, the slave, the woman, the child, "subalternized popular cultures" must "discover themselves as innocent" of the originary violence and from that position judge modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76). A trans-modern project is therefore identifiable by two markers: it preserves modern goods that the colonial relation extracted from, and it includes the formerly excluded in their substantive enjoyment of those goods.

The conceptual scaffolding is more developed than the single paragraph suggests, because Dussel's longer argument builds it. The seven-step myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) gives the diagnostic. The two-layer structure of modernity (rational concept of emancipation "affirmed and subsumed"; irrational myth "negated", Dussel, 1993: 66) gives the method: keep the kernel, expose the myth. The redating of modernity to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 66) and the restoration of the Iberian periphery to its origin story give the historical orientation. Together these constitute a programme in the standard sense: a diagnosis, a method, and a unit of analysis.

Stuart Hall extends the programme into discourse. The West, on Hall's reading, is not a place but a "system of representation" with four functions (classifying, condensing, comparing, evaluating, Hall, 1992: 277). Any trans-modern reorganisation has to denaturalise this system: not just include the Rest as also valid, but expose the over-simplifications the discourse depends on. Hall is explicit: the discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). Trans-modernity has to undo the over-simplification, not redecorate it.

Howard French extends the programme empirically. *Born in Blackness* shows that the standard chronology of modernity (Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, Industrial Revolution) misplaces the origin. The first impetus of European overseas expansion was the West African gold trade; the fort at Elmina (1482) preceded da Gama's voyage to India; the first sugar mill of the Atlantic world was on Madeira around 1425; São Tomé became a major sugar producer by the 1520s on enslaved African labour; from there the plantation complex moved to Brazil, Barbados and Jamaica (French, 2021). The Atlantic system, not internal European reform, is the engine. A trans-modern history would teach this chronology rather than the inherited one. The programme has its substantive content.

What of the pious-wish reading? Two patterns make trans-modernity vulnerable to it.

The first: trans-modernity as vocabulary without follow-through. The word can circulate as a generous label that classifies plural modernities as also valid while leaving the institutions that organise global wealth, knowledge and heritage untouched. Hall's analysis of the West-Rest discourse explains why this is so easy: classifying generously, with a positive label such as "trans-modern" attached, still leaves the classifying position itself unchanged (Hall, 1992: 277). The discourse can absorb the label.

The second: trans-modernity is short on institutional architecture. Dussel's paragraph names the form of the co-realisation but not the bodies that would do it. The colonised must "discover themselves as innocent", but the discovery has to be heard and acted on by institutions that currently embody the discourse Hall analyses. Dussel does not specify those institutions or the path from recognition to redistribution. That is the gap his readers (or his successors) have to fill.

My verdict: trans-modernity in 2026 is a programme in the sense that matters for an examination essay. The diagnostic structure is fully specified (Dussel's seven steps). The methodological injunction is clear (affirm the rational kernel, negate the irrational myth). The historical reorientation is empirically anchored (French's Atlantic system, the Iberian periphery, the slave-and-sugar foundation of modern wealth). The discursive equipment is supplied (Hall's four functions of "West and Rest"). What it lacks, and what makes the "pious wish" charge tempting, is a worked-out institutional sociology: what bodies, what mechanisms, what sequence. Dussel's single paragraph is honest about its scale: it points where to go rather than how to get there. Calling that a programme rather than a wish is the right description, provided the reader does not confuse a regulative ideal with a delivery plan.

### M13: "Modernity is a Janus-faced project: emancipation for some, the colonial wound for others." Discuss.

The Janus image, two faces opposite, one head, is exactly right for modernity. Any honest account of the modern world has to look both ways at once. Modernity emancipated Europeans (and some others) from arbitrary monarchy, hereditary status and material scarcity. Modernity inflicted what the proposition calls the colonial wound on Africans, Asians and indigenous Americans: chattel slavery, conquest, extraction, racial hierarchy. The two faces are not separate; they share a head. I want to defend this Janus reading by working through the emancipation face, the colonial-wound face, and the structural reasons the two are inseparable.

The emancipation face is the standard story Dussel and the lecturer name in order to complicate. Modernity, on this account, is rationality, industrialisation, urbanisation, individualisation, the break with tradition. Marx is the dominant sociologist of its productive dynamism (commodification, alienation); Weber of its rationalisation and disenchantment; Durkheim of the shift from mechanical to organic solidarity. Dussel grants the rational concept of emancipation that travels with this story and "affirm[s] and subsume[s]" it (Dussel, 1993: 66). Kant's "exodus from guilty immaturity" (cited in Dussel, 1993: 68) is part of it. So is the consolidation of secular nation-states, the spread of literacy, the partial extension of the franchise that Hall sketches in the broader chapter (Hall, 1992: 277). None of this is fake.

The colonial-wound face is what modernity also was, simultaneously and inseparably. The lecture's reading of Dussel is unambiguous: modernity "appears to celebrate rationality and freedom" while it "also encourages violence towards those who are not so 'modern' or 'rational'" (lecture slides). Howard French's account of how Portuguese expansion down the West African coast was driven by the search for gold and then for enslaved labour, and how the resulting plantation complex (Madeira and São Tomé sugar from the 1420s onward, then Brazil, Barbados, Jamaica) supplied the foundations of the modern Atlantic economy (French, 2021), is the empirical body of the wound. So are the Dussel passages on the Iberian Reconquest of Granada in 1492, the "extreme sectarian violence" that became "the model for the colonization of the New World" (Dussel, 1993: 67), and Cortés in Mexico as "the first extra-European space" in which the European ego could carry out its "prototypic 'development'" (Dussel, 1993: 74).

Why are the two faces inseparable? Three reasons.

First, the emancipation was funded by the wound. Marx put it bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Du Bois generalised the point in the lines the lecture slides cite: the European and white American working class was "practically invited to share in this new exploitation, and particularly were flattered by popular appeals to their inherent superiority" (Du Bois, 1970: 107). The wealth that emancipated Europeans was substantially generated by the labour and lands of the colonised.

Second, the emancipation was conceptually structured by the wound. Stuart Hall shows that "the West" is not a primary fact but a relation: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The modern European subject (Kant's autonomous reasoner, Hegel's bearer of Spirit) became thinkable by being posed against an inferior other. The universal claims of European modernity were articulated, in their canonical formulations, in a vocabulary that excluded most of humanity. Kant: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous" (Kant, 1798: 59). Hegel: "Africa… does not have history as such" (Hegel, 1837, cited in Dussel, 1993: 70). And the right of empire: "Against the absolute right that such a people possesses by virtue of being the bearer of the development of the world Spirit, the spirit of other peoples has no rights" (Hegel, 1837, sections 346-347, cited in Dussel, 1993: 73). The emancipation and the exclusion came out of the same mouths in the same decades.

Third, the relation continues. The colonial attitude, as the lecture insists, outlives the formal end of colonies. The lecturer's prompts (sport, music, fashion, education, "which are the best universities in the world, and why") are designed to surface the same hierarchy in apparently neutral contemporary judgements. Western Classical Music is taught as universal while gamelan and samba are filed as "World Music" (lecture slides). The wound is administered with a clean conscience because the discourse Hall analyses ("western = developed = good = desirable", Hall, 1992: 277) has naturalised the hierarchy.

What follows for political action? Dussel's seven-step "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993: 75) names the legitimating structure of the wound; his proposal of trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76) names the way forward, a "co-realisation" of modernity and its negated alterity, keeping the rational goods while including the excluded. The Janus image suggests the same: turn the head so both faces are seen at once, and the project becomes one of extending the emancipation to those modernity originally wounded.

A counter-position says the wound is overstated or the emancipation undervalued. The reply is simply to look at the texts Dussel cites and the trade French traces. Modernity is Janus-faced because it always was, and any sociology that pretends otherwise is teaching one face while concealing the other. The honest task is to recognise both.

(~750 words)
