## 3. Information from the readings

### Halewood Part 1 — Durkheim, Marx, Weber (and Simmel) Revisited

Halewood's overarching claim is that **"the problem of the social" is unfinished business**. The classical canon did not deliver a settled definition of "society" or "the social"; rather, each of Durkheim, Marx and Weber struggled with the concept in different registers, and these struggles have been forgotten by twentieth-century sociology, which now uses "social" as if it were transparent.

**Durkheim.** The English title *The Division of Labour in Society* misleads — the French (*De la division du travail social*) puts the adjective on labour, not on a noun "society." Durkheim's question is not "what is society?" but "what makes us *solidaire*?" He refuses any bifurcation between the social and the natural: *the social is natural*. This is what makes a science of sociology possible — sociology abstracts an aspect of nature in the same way physics or biology does. Solidarity is not a biological instinct (against Parsons), but the manner of association within nature. Yet Durkheim's account rests, ultimately, on collective morality — sociality is grounded in the punishment of contraventions of a collective. The argument circles: sociability is a product of social life, but social life is grounded in collective morality, which is itself a manner of associating. Durkheim "should be approached with caution but also with admiration" — admired for refusing to take the social as given.

**Marx.** Marx provides no clean definition of "society" (Halewood: he was not a "professional sociologist"). He preserves two German adjectives — *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich* — and uses them in tension. Halewood translates *gesellschaftlich* as "societal" and *sozial* as "social." Capitalism is the predominant *societal* form, but "social" relations of prestige and esteem both reinforce and exceed it. The point: *nothing is settled*. Marx's chemistry analogy — butyric acid vs. propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement — makes the same point Durkheim makes about the living cell: *the manner of association* gives a thing its specificity. Marx's task is to historicise abstract categories ("civil society," "society in general") rather than to provide his own essentialist alternative.

**Weber.** Weber, alone of the three, gave a *precise* concept: only three things are properly "social" — social action, social relationships (*Beziehungen* not *Verhältnisse*), and social selection. Weber refuses to talk loosely of "social conditions" or "social factors" — that would dilute the concept. A common language does not make a community; only the actively shared consciousness that members of the group have this in common, oriented to others, makes a *Vergemeinschaftung* or *Vergesellschaftung*. This precision has a cost: no general "society" hides behind action. Notably, Weber comes to this only at the end of his life (1919-20) — proof that the battle for the concept was still alive in classical sociology.

**Simmel.** Society "is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*" (Simmel 1950, 4). It is the outcome of dyadic and triadic relations — Simmel's example of the secret: a secret is only a secret in relation to at least one other. Form, not content, is the proper object of sociology — hence "Formal Sociology" / "Sociology of Forms." *Vergesellschaftung* (sociation) is a noun made out of a process. Simmel sides with Weber against Durkheim in privileging *groups* over *totalities*.

**Conclusion to Part 1.** The classical canon converges on one point: **association is what matters; the manner, the adverb, is the social.** None of the four ever decided the concept once and for all. To reject "traditional" concepts of the social (as Latour does) is therefore premature.

### Halewood Part 2 — Whitehead on sociology, societies and the social

**Whitehead's "sociology."** Whitehead, the philosopher, splits two dyads carefully: *society/social* (metaphysical) vs. *sociology/sociological* (the study of human societies). He uses "sociology" in Adventures of Ideas only three times, but consistently to mean an attention to *how* humans were made human, not *what* they essentially are. The shift from "what" to "how" repeats the adverbial theme from Part 1.

**Ideas are things.** Whitehead's striking move (highlighted by Stengers): "I propose to consider critically the *sort* of history which ideas can have in the life of humanity." Ideas are not the creations of humans, nor are they outside humans; they have *adventures* and humans are the vehicles. Sociology, on this reading, is the study of how ideas combine with humanity in different epochs. Whitehead is "pre-Kantian" — he does not start from the question of how concepts relate to reality.

**Apes, humans, humanity.** "Clear, conscious discrimination is an accident of human existence. It makes us human. But it does not make us exist. It is of the essence of our humanity. But it is an accident of our existence" (1938, 158). Conscious discrimination converts "ape-ity" into "humanity." Imagine, Whitehead suggests, what Kant would have written had he read Darwin — human reason as a *kind of animal reason*, situated within nature.

**The order of nature, social order.** Whitehead's account of "societies" begins with *order* and *disorder* as correlative (not binary). Order is always a specific order, never order in the abstract. A society for Whitehead is *any* nexus of actual entities that share a common element of form and reproduce that form through their prehensions of each other. A hot stone is a society: its component parts "feel themselves hotly." This metaphysical move dissolves the bifurcation between natural and social — the social *is* the order of nature.

**Sociality as likeness.** Members of a society are alike because of their common character; differences between societies are differences between *manners of association*. Solidarity, on Whitehead's account, does not require a Durkheimian totality — it arises in each occasion of mutual prehension. "Each task of creation is a social effort" (Whitehead 1978, 223).

**Conclusion.** Sociological theory must begin from process. "No static maintenance of perfection is possible. Advance or Decadence are the only choices offered" (Whitehead 1933, 353-354). Social theory must be willing to wreck itself in order to advance: "the art of free society consists […] in fearlessness of revision" (Whitehead 1927, 88).

## 4. Critical analysis with key quotes

The main argumentative thread of the L1 reading and lecture is that **modernity is constituted by a series of bifurcations that simultaneously enable and disable thought**, and that critical sociology must work *adverbially* — on the *manner* of association — rather than essentially.

**Main tension 1 — Modernity's two faces.** Halewood and the lecture insist that the same period that produced Shakespeare, Luther, and "the modern organization of industry" produced the trans-Atlantic slave trade. Du Bois forces the recognition that these are not separable; they are constitutive of one another.

> "For four hundred years, from 1450 to 1850, European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings of such tremendous proportions that the physical, economic and moral effects are still plainly to be remarked throughout the world." — Du Bois, *The Negro* (1915), pp. 89-90

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." — Marx, *Selected Writings* (ed. McLellan), p. 203

**Main tension 2 — Critique vs criticism.** The lecture distinguishes finding fault from evaluating structure and pointing to alternatives. Marx's slogan that the point is to *change* the world makes critique inseparable from a future-oriented practice.

> "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it." — Marx, *Theses on Feuerbach*, in *Selected Writings*, p. 158

**Main tension 3 — Bifurcation as the cornerstone of modernity.** Halewood's signature thesis. Modern thought operates by splitting fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities. Once split, a *gulf* opens up.

> "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it." — Whitehead, *Adventures of Ideas* (1933), p. 217

**Main tension 4 — The problem of the social.** Halewood's reading of the canon shows that "social" was never a settled term; classical theory was *a contest over the concept*.

> "It is now no accident that the term 'social' [des Sozialen] which seems to have a quite general meaning, turns out to have, as soon as one carefully examines its application, a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity." — Weber, in Halewood (2014), p. 131 [VERIFY p.? — Weber 1949 [1904], 68]

> "Society' 'is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*. It does not exist outside and in addition to the individuals and the processes among them." — Simmel, *The Sociology of Georg Simmel* (1950), p. 4

> "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life." — Durkheim, *Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128

**Main tension 5 — Adverbial sociology.** Whitehead's "ideas are things" move runs alongside Marx's chemistry analogy, Durkheim's living-cell analogy, and Simmel's sociation. All four insist that the *how* — the manner, the form, the mode of prehension — is what is social.

> "The foundation of all understanding of sociological theory — that is to say, of all understanding of human life — is that no static maintenance of perfection is possible. […] The very essence of real actuality — that is, of the completely real, is *process*." — Whitehead, *Adventures of Ideas* (1933), pp. 353-354

> "It is the first step in sociological wisdom, to recognize that the major advances in civilization are processes which all but wreck the societies in which they occur […] The art of free society consists […] in fearlessness of revision." — Whitehead, *Symbolism* (1927), p. 88

**Critique of the canon.** Halewood's deepest claim is that twentieth-century sociology has *forgotten* the open-endedness of the founders. Parsons assumes social order is self-explanatory; Latour wants to abolish the social altogether. Both are wrong — for Halewood, the live question is to *reconstruct* a philosophy of the social that retains the canon's critical insights while breaking out of its bifurcations.

## 7. Essay answers

### 2025 (Q3): 'The fact-value distinction is a cornerstone of modernity. It is also one of its major problems.' Critically assess this statement.

I argue that the fact–value distinction is indeed a cornerstone of modernity, but it is a cornerstone in the architectural sense Halewood draws from Whitehead: the cornerstone holds the building up while at the same time *fixing* the bifurcations that the building requires. The distinction makes possible the modern sciences, the modern administration, the modern university — and at the same time it produces the gulfs into which modern thought repeatedly falls. My thesis is that the distinction is a *real* abstraction (an idea that does work in the world) but a *bad* one, and that a serious sociology of modernity must abandon the metaphysics of bifurcation while retaining the *practical* distinction between describing and judging.

The first move is to recognise where the distinction comes from. In the German neo-Kantian tradition that shaped Weber and Simmel — through Dilthey, Rickert and others — the separation of the "natural sciences" (concerned with causal explanation of fact) from the "human sciences" (concerned with meaning and value) was framed as a methodological necessity. Weber radicalised this into the doctrine of *Wertfreiheit* — value-freedom in social science. Halewood's account in *Rethinking the Social* shows that this elegant methodological apparatus is also an *ontological* bifurcation: it presupposes that the world really does come in two kinds, facts and values, and that sociology must straddle the gap. Whitehead's worry applies directly: "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (*Adventures of Ideas*, p. 217). Once you split fact from value you cannot get the two halves back together without smuggling.

Second, the fact–value distinction is a cornerstone in the more obvious sense: without it the modern division of intellectual labour collapses. Economists report facts, philosophers debate values. Public policy is supposed to operate on the "facts" while leaving "values" to democratic choice. The university is organised on the same principle: STEM versus humanities, science versus humanities — this is one of the six bifurcations Halewood lists in his lecture. The colonial archive that Howard French reconstructs in *Born in Blackness* is full of "facts" — tonnes of gold shipped, percentages of enslaved people sent to Brazil — that the modernist convention asks us to evaluate later, as a question of "values." Du Bois's 1915 passage refuses this: the slave trade is both "the most inexcusable and despicable blot on modern human history" *and* the factual condition of "the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). The factual and the evaluative are one description.

Third, the distinction is "one of its major problems" because it produces the kind of sociology that *cannot see* its own conditions of possibility. If you accept the value-free description of modernity as European rationalisation, slavery becomes an external "value" question — to be tacked on, lamented, regretted — rather than constitutive. Halewood's revision-notes-friendly reading of the canon is precisely that critical sociology must refuse this move. Marx already saw this when he wrote that "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). The fact of cotton and the value of human beings are not separable in the structure of industrial modernity.

The counter-argument is a Weberian one: without some version of the distinction, sociology cannot be a *science*. If we let our values infect our descriptions, we get propaganda. Weber's response in his methodological essays — that the choice of *which* facts matter is value-laden but the description of those facts must be controlled — is the most honest version of the position. I take this seriously. My own position is not that all descriptions are evaluations but that the *clean* metaphysical split between the two is unsustainable. In practice we move adverbially, in Halewood's sense: we describe *how* something happens (the manner of association) and the description is already loaded with evaluative implication. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is a fact; its standard art-historical interpretation that only "briefly mentions slavery" is a value-laden silence. Both descriptions are factual; one is better than the other.

In conclusion, the fact–value distinction is a cornerstone of modernity in the strong sense — it *holds up* a whole way of organising thought and institutions. It is also a major problem because, once cemented, it produces gulfs in which constitutive features of modernity (especially racial slavery and coloniality) are exiled to the "value" side and quietly excised from the "factual" account of modern industry. The way forward, on Halewood's reading, is not to abolish the practical distinction between describing and judging — that would be naive — but to refuse the metaphysical bifurcation. We should *critique* (in the lecture's strong sense — evaluate the structure, suggest alternatives) rather than merely *criticise*. Sociology after the fact–value distinction is the sociology that takes both Du Bois and Weber seriously at once, refusing to let either be filed away on the further side of a gulf.

### 2023 (Q3): Why has the distinction between "facts" and "values" been problematic for sociology?

I argue that the distinction between facts and values has been problematic for sociology in three interlocking ways: it imports a neo-Kantian metaphysics that the founders themselves had not fully digested; it produces a methodological neurosis that disables critique; and, most importantly, it makes constitutive features of modernity (particularly coloniality) appear as external "value" questions when in fact they are factual conditions of the modern world. My thesis, following Halewood's account of bifurcation, is that the problem is not the practical contrast between describing and judging but the *metaphysical* split that hardens that contrast into two separate realms.

The first dimension of the problem is genealogical. As Halewood traces in *Rethinking the Social*, Weber inherited the fact–value distinction from neo-Kantianism — Rickert and Dilthey, in particular — for whom the *Geisteswissenschaften* (human sciences) had to be distinguished from the *Naturwissenschaften* by reference to *meaning* and *value*, not by reference to facts. Sociology as a science (*Wissenschaft*) was supposed to navigate this divide. The problem is that Weber's own concept of "social action," which Halewood reconstructs carefully in Chapter 7, is in fact *both* descriptive *and* evaluative — it requires that the actor orient her behaviour to others in a way that means something. Weber knew the term "social" was problematic: "It is now no accident that the term 'social' […] turns out to have […] a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity" [VERIFY p.? — Weber 1949 [1904], 68]. The fact–value distinction was supposed to solve this; instead, it preserved it.

The second dimension is methodological. The doctrine of *Wertfreiheit* — value-freedom — was supposed to discipline the sociologist so that her descriptions would be unaffected by her commitments. In practice, the disciplining works in only one direction: it polices the appearance of evaluation in the text while leaving the *selection* of facts (which questions, which populations, which periods) entirely up to the prior values of the discipline. This is why, as the lecture emphasises, the standard sociological account of modernity for most of the twentieth century was a story of European industrialisation that mentioned slavery, if at all, in passing. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is the perfect example: its smarthistory.org interpretation "only briefly mentions slavery and does not seem to notice that West Africa had enormous resources." The "facts" of the map are reported; the values that selected those facts are invisible.

The third and deepest dimension is the substantive one. The fact–value distinction makes modernity's coloniality look like a "value" question that can be added in or left out. Du Bois's 1915 passage refuses this: the trans-Atlantic slave trade is *the* fact of modern industry, not an ethical regret. Marx put it bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) gives the empirical scaffolding: 600 kg of gold per year from West Africa by the 1490s; the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar published in 1629; 40% of all plantation-enslaved people ending up in Brazil. These are not values; they are facts. But they are facts that change what "modernity" *is* as a description.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously is the Weberian one — that without some version of the fact–value distinction sociology collapses into political pamphleteering. I accept the force of this. The point of the critique is not to dissolve all difference between describing and judging but to refuse the *bifurcation* — what Halewood calls, drawing on Whitehead, the splitting of one thing into two branches such that "a gulf is created" (Whitehead 1933, p. 217). The practical distinction between an empirical claim and a normative claim is workable; the metaphysical claim that there are two separate realms is not.

In conclusion, the fact–value distinction has been problematic for sociology because it carries an unowned neo-Kantian metaphysics, it produces a one-sided methodological discipline, and — most importantly — it systematically obscures the coloniality of modernity by treating slavery and racial extraction as "value" questions rather than as factual constituents of the modern world system. The remedy is the move Halewood recommends throughout his book: refuse the bifurcation, work adverbially on the *manner* of modern association, and recover the founders' (Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel) shared insight that the social is what happens between things, not a layer hiding behind them. Critique, in the strong lecture-sense, is what replaces the falsely modest value-free description.

### 2022 (Q1): Is Sociology a science? If so, what kind of science is it?

I argue that sociology is a science, but only if we are willing to revise what we mean by "science" — specifically, to abandon the bifurcation between the natural sciences and the human sciences that has shaped the question since the neo-Kantians. My thesis, drawing on Halewood's Whiteheadian reading of Durkheim, is that sociology is a *natural science of the manner of association* — its objects are real, its claims are correctable, but its method is adverbial rather than nomothetic in the physics-envy sense.

The first move is to take Durkheim seriously. Durkheim was clear that sociology had to put itself "in the same frame of mind as scientists" — but his deeper claim, as Halewood emphasises in Chapter 7 of *Rethinking the Social*, is that **the social is natural**. "If the social 'world' were not a part of nature then it would be impossible to treat it scientifically" (Halewood 2014, p. 124). The familiar twentieth-century picture in which the social is a *separate realm* from the natural is, on this reading, a Parsonian importation, not a Durkheimian inheritance. For Durkheim, sociology is a science because it abstracts an aspect of nature — *the manner of association* — just as biology and chemistry abstract other aspects. Durkheim's example of the living cell is instructive: "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (*Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128).

The second move is to draw the same lesson from Marx. Marx's chemistry analogy — butyric acid and propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement — makes the same point. Sociology, like chemistry, studies how the *arrangement* gives rise to the specific properties. Marx is therefore committed to a science of society in the strong sense, but a science whose objects are *historically* specific manners of association rather than ahistorical laws.

The third move is Weberian — and here we hit the difficulty. Weber's sociology is also a *Wissenschaft*, but Weber introduces the requirement of *Verstehen* — understanding the meaning that actors give to their actions. This appears to bifurcate sociology from physics: the meanings of actors are not on the same plane as the velocities of particles. The standard neo-Kantian reading concludes that sociology is a *different kind* of science — a "human science." Halewood resists this. He shows that Weber's "social action" is in fact a *very precise* concept — narrower than is usually thought — and that it is compatible with the recognition that animals can engage in social action too. The bifurcation between human meaning and natural cause is not as clean as the neo-Kantians wanted.

A fourth move comes from Whitehead. In Halewood's reading of *Process and Reality*, "society" is the name for any nexus of actual entities that share a common manner of prehension. A hot stone is a society. So is the British Sociological Association. Sociology, in this expanded sense, is the study of how societies — at any scale — manage to endure. This puts sociology back into continuity with the natural sciences without reducing it to them.

A counter-argument worth pressing is the postmodern one: sociology should *not* be a science, because the scientific ideal is itself a colonial-Eurocentric one that excludes other ways of knowing. I take this seriously, particularly in the light of Du Bois and the rethinking-the-canon move that the lecture flags. But I think the right response is not to abandon science — it is to *expand* what counts as science to include the adverbial, historical, and critical work that the canonical reformers (Du Bois, Simmel, Dussel) were already doing. Du Bois's 1915 description of the trans-Atlantic slave trade is a scientific description; it gathers facts, situates them in a system of causes, and produces a falsifiable account of how modern industry came to be.

In conclusion, sociology is a science — not in the imitative sense of physics-envy but in the Durkheimian sense of a discipline that abstracts a real aspect of nature: the manner of association by which entities (human and non-human) endure. Its method is adverbial; its claims are about *how* the social comes to be, not about what it ultimately *is*. It is a critical science in the lecture-sense — its job is not just to find faults but to evaluate the structure of association and to suggest alternatives. To answer the second half of the question: sociology is the science of association, the kind of science that takes Marx's "the point is to change it" as a methodological imperative as well as a political one.

### 2022 (Q2): How have the ideas of neo-Kantians influenced sociology?

I argue that the neo-Kantians (Rickert, Windelband, Dilthey) shaped twentieth-century sociology far more deeply than is usually acknowledged, and that their influence is double-edged: they bequeathed the discipline its conceptual rigour (the distinction between *Naturwissenschaften* and *Geisteswissenschaften*; the methodology of *Verstehen*; the doctrine of value-relevance) but they also installed the bifurcations that, on Halewood's account, generate the recurrent crises of social theory. My thesis is that the neo-Kantian inheritance is unavoidable but must be critically reconstructed rather than uncritically accepted.

The first dimension is the methodological one. Through Weber and Simmel — both of whom worked in the neo-Kantian intellectual climate of late-nineteenth-century Germany — sociology inherited the conviction that the social sciences are concerned with *meaning* and *value* in a way the natural sciences are not. Halewood notes in Chapter 8 of *Rethinking the Social* that "the specific version of German Idealist neo-Kantianism […] influenced Weber and Simmel." Weber's whole apparatus — ideal types, *Verstehen*, value-relevance, value-freedom — is unintelligible without this background. Likewise, Simmel's "Formal Sociology" or "Sociology of Forms" picks up the Kantian language of form against content.

The second dimension is the substantive one. The neo-Kantians installed the **fact–value distinction** at the heart of sociology. As I argued in the previous essay, this distinction is one of the cornerstones of modern thought — and one of its major problems. It produces the methodological neurosis of value-freedom; it generates the gulf between the description of the modern world and the moral evaluation of it; and it makes constitutive features of modernity such as racial slavery appear as "value" addenda rather than as facts of the system.

The third dimension is the *bifurcation* that Halewood specifically diagnoses. The neo-Kantian split between nomothetic (law-seeking) and idiographic (case-specific) sciences hardens into the modern split between sociology-as-science and sociology-as-cultural-criticism. The lecture lists six modern bifurcations — fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities — and the neo-Kantian inheritance is most clearly visible in the first and the last. Once you accept that science and humanities are two different kinds of knowing, you are committed to the second-order bifurcation that splits hard sociology (quantitative, causal) from soft sociology (interpretive, cultural). Halewood, drawing on Whitehead's pre-Kantian metaphysics, argues that this split is unsustainable: "the social is natural" (in Durkheim's sense, recovered against Parsons).

A fourth, more diffuse, dimension is the influence on the canon itself. Halewood is explicit that "the ideas of Kant and Hegel stretched into classical social theory through the various filters of writers such as Dilthey and Rickert, among others" (Chapter 8). The very category of "the social" — *des Sozialen* — that Weber problematised carries Kantian residues. The Durkheimian concept of "collective representations" is closer to Kant's transcendental categories than is usually admitted.

The most important counter-argument is that the neo-Kantian inheritance is precisely what *gave sociology its identity*. Without Rickert and Dilthey, Weber's account of meaningful action does not exist; without that account, the Frankfurt School, ethnomethodology, symbolic interactionism, and the cultural sociology of the spring term of this very module are all unintelligible. I take this seriously. The point of critique, in the lecture's strong sense, is not to abolish the neo-Kantian inheritance but to evaluate its structure and to suggest improvements. Halewood's project is exactly this: he wants to keep what is alive in Weber's precision and Simmel's "Sociology of Forms" while jettisoning the metaphysical bifurcation that the neo-Kantians smuggled in.

In conclusion, the neo-Kantians have influenced sociology in three main ways: they bequeathed the methodological apparatus of meaning-oriented social science (*Verstehen*, ideal types, value-relevance); they installed the fact–value distinction as a cornerstone of the discipline; and they shaped the canon through the channels of Weber and Simmel. The first contribution remains valuable, the second is the source of recurring crisis, and the third has had to be supplemented by recovering thinkers (Du Bois, Dussel, Whitehead) who were never inside the neo-Kantian conversation. To answer the question properly is therefore not just to *describe* the influence but to *critique* it — in the lecture's strong sense — and to point to the alternatives that a non-bifurcated philosophy of the social would open up.

### M1: "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it." Critically discuss with reference to Halewood's account of bifurcation.

I take Whitehead's sentence from *Adventures of Ideas* (1933, p. 217) as the conceptual hinge of Halewood's whole project. My thesis is that the quote is exactly right but only becomes critically useful once one notices what it *does not* say: it does not say that the two sides of a gulf are incommunicable, only that they are *opaque* to one another. Halewood's account of bifurcation builds on this asymmetry. The gulfs of modernity — fact/value, sex/gender, science/humanities, race/ethnicity, language/world, public/private — are not impassable, they are *forgetful*. Each side stops asking what the other is doing. The job of critique is to remember the question.

The first move is to anchor the quote in Halewood's six bifurcations. The slide-list of the lecture is precise. Once you split fact from value, the people who do facts stop asking after the values that selected them; once you split sex from gender, the people who do gender stop asking after the body. The gulf is *epistemic* before it is metaphysical: it organises ignorance. Halewood reads Whitehead as diagnosing the *productivity* of this ignorance — modern knowledge advances on each side of the gulf because nobody is asking the awkward questions across it. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is the lecture's emblem: a map that already shows a coastal, oceanic, slave-trading Atlantic, and a twentieth-century art-historical commentary that "only briefly mentions slavery." The gulf is doing work — keeping the slave trade on the further side of an aesthetic appreciation of cartography.

The second move is to test the quote against the fact-value distinction, which Halewood treats as the cornerstone bifurcation. The neo-Kantian inheritance (Rickert, Dilthey) gave Weber a way of separating *Wertfreiheit* (value-freedom) in description from value-relevance in topic-selection. The doctrine is elegant on one side. On the other side — the side from which the topics were selected — there is silence about who chose, and why. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) is full of facts that the standard sociology of modernity could not see because the gulf was doing exactly the work Whitehead describes: 600 kg of West African gold per year by the 1490s, the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar published in 1629 for use in the Andes, 40% of plantation-enslaved people sent to Brazil. These facts existed on the further side of the fact-value gulf, and a discipline that could not look across simply did not see them.

The third move is to specify what Halewood adds to Whitehead. Whitehead's quote is metaphysical; Halewood's bifurcation thesis is *sociological*. Bifurcation, for Halewood, is not just an unfortunate by-product of conceptual elegance; it is an active organising principle of modern *institutions*. The modern university institutionalises the science/humanities gulf in buildings, hiring lines, journal hierarchies. The modern state institutionalises the public/private gulf in law, in welfare, in policing. The modern colonial archive institutionalises the language/world gulf in maps, surveys, censuses. The gulf is not in the head — it is in the filing cabinet.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously is the pragmatist one: gulfs may be useful simplifications. We *need* to distinguish fact from value to make policy; we *need* to distinguish public from private to think about rights. I accept the practical force of this. But practical contrasts are not metaphysical bifurcations. Halewood's critique is aimed at the latter — at the moment when a working contrast hardens into two separate realms and the question of what happens between them disappears. Whitehead's verb is "is very difficult to know," not "is impossible to know." The gulf can be looked across; the difficulty is institutional, not principled.

A deeper objection: Whitehead's metaphor assumes that there *was* something single before the splitting — that fact and value, sex and gender, were originally one thing now divided. Is this not a romantic origin-story? Halewood's response, drawing on Whitehead's process metaphysics, is no: the "one thing" was never an original unity but a manner of association, a *how* that was working before modern thought tried to freeze it into a *what*. The recovery is not nostalgic; it is adverbial.

In conclusion, Whitehead's gulf-quote is correct, and Halewood's account of bifurcation is the right gloss. The opacity of the further side is not a failure of perception; it is the productive ignorance that modern institutions require. Critique in the strong lecture-sense — evaluate the structure, suggest alternatives — is precisely the work of refusing the gulf, of asking what is happening on the further side and how the splitting itself came to organise our knowledge. The cheat-sheet payoff: every bifurcation thesis is also a Whiteheadian one.

### M2: Critically assess Halewood's argument in *Rethinking the Social through Durkheim, Marx, Weber and Whitehead* that twentieth-century sociology has forgotten the open-endedness of the classical canon.

I argue that Halewood's argument is largely correct: the twentieth-century reception of Durkheim, Marx and Weber turned each of them into the author of a *settled* doctrine about "society," when in fact each was struggling, often inconclusively, with the very concept. My thesis is that the forgetting was not an accident but a *disciplinary* necessity — sociology needed a clean object to legitimate itself as a university discipline, and the price was the suppression of the canon's most interesting tensions. To rethink the social today is, on Halewood's reading, to remember what classical sociology *almost* said but did not finish saying.

The first move is textual. Halewood's chapters 4-7 of *Rethinking the Social* read each founder against the textbook version. Durkheim is not the theorist of an autonomous "social realm" floating above biology — he is the theorist who insists that *the social is natural*, "an aspect of nature" abstracted by sociology in the same way biology abstracts life (Halewood 2014, pp. 124-125). The English title *The Division of Labour in Society* misleads: Durkheim wrote *De la division du travail social* — the adjective is on labour, not on society. The textbook Durkheim of "society sui generis" is a Parsonian artefact, not a Durkheimian one.

The second move is Marxian. Marx, Halewood notes, "was not a professional sociologist" and refused to define "society" once and for all. He uses *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich* in tension — what Halewood translates as "social" versus "societal" — to preserve a distinction between prestige-and-esteem relations on one hand and the historically specific form of capitalist association on the other. The twentieth-century reception, especially the structuralist Marxism of Althusser, flattened this into a doctrine of "the social formation" with its three levels. Halewood reads Marx instead through the chemistry analogy of butyric acid and propyl formate: same atoms, different arrangement, different thing. Society is not a layer; it is a manner of association whose specificity has to be historicised.

The third move is Weberian. Weber, alone of the three, gave a precise concept — and gave it only at the end of his life (1919-20). Only three things are properly "social" for Weber: social action, social relationships (*Beziehungen*), and social selection. He refuses to talk loosely of "social conditions" or "social factors." The textbook Weber of *Verstehen* and ideal types is correct but incomplete; what is forgotten is Weber's insistence that the *concept itself* was contested. "It is now no accident that the term 'social' [des Sozialen] which seems to have a quite general meaning, turns out to have […] a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity" [VERIFY p.? — Weber 1949 [1904], 68].

The fourth move is Simmelian, and it is where Halewood's argument cuts deepest. Simmel was *inside* the founding generation yet excluded from the canonical trio. His claim that "Society' 'is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*" (Simmel 1950, p. 4) was precisely the kind of unsettlement the twentieth-century discipline could not afford. To include Simmel is to admit that the founders disagreed about whether sociology had an object at all. The forgetting of Simmel is the forgetting of the question.

Where Halewood's argument is convincing. The textual evidence is strong. Each founder is reconstructed from the German originals with attention to adjectives, verb forms, and unresolved tensions. Halewood does not claim the founders were secretly Whiteheadians; he claims they shared an adverbial sensibility that twentieth-century sociology converted into a substance.

Where the argument is contestable. Two reservations. First, Halewood writes as if the forgetting were *theoretical*, but it might equally be structural-institutional: sociology had to compete with economics and political science for university territory, and a clean object was a competitive necessity. Second, the recovery move risks aestheticising the founders' uncertainty — Marx's failure to define society might be unfinished business subsequent Marxists were right to finish, not dialectical wisdom.

A counter-argument: maybe twentieth-century sociology was right to settle the concept. Parsons gave us a working framework; Bourdieu gave us a working empirical programme; network analysis gives us a working measure. Recovering the founders' uncertainty might be a luxury the discipline cannot afford. I take this seriously. My reply, with Halewood, is that the live problems of contemporary sociology — coloniality, ecology, the more-than-human — are precisely the problems where the settled concept fails. The recovery of the canon's open-endedness is not nostalgia; it is a tool for the present.

In conclusion, Halewood's argument that twentieth-century sociology has forgotten the open-endedness of the canon is well-evidenced, conceptually productive, and politically timely. It allows us to read Durkheim, Marx, Weber and Simmel as resources for *current* disputes rather than as monuments. The cost is that the recovery has to be done carefully — the founders were not pre-emptive Whiteheadians and should not be turned into them — but Halewood's textual scrupulousness is exactly the right way to do that work. To rethink the social is to read the canon as it actually wrote, not as the twentieth century needed it to be.

### M3: What does Halewood mean by bifurcation, and why does he treat it as the cornerstone of modernity?

I take the question in two parts. The first half is conceptual: what is bifurcation, as Halewood uses the term? The second is structural: why does the concept earn the status of *cornerstone*? My thesis is that Halewood means by bifurcation a specific *operation* — the splitting of one thing into two such that a gulf opens — and that he treats it as the cornerstone because this operation produces both the cognitive architecture of modern thought *and* the institutional architecture of modern life, in a single move.

**Part one: what does Halewood mean by bifurcation?** The term is borrowed from Whitehead's *Adventures of Ideas* (1933). In Whitehead it is a metaphysical category — the splitting of nature into two branches, one of which has primary qualities (mass, motion) and the other secondary ones (colour, sound). Halewood inherits the splitting-into-two operation and generalises it. In his lecture-slides the operation is applied to six modern pairings: fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities. Each of these is a single phenomenon that modern thought has insisted on dividing into two branches that are then treated as belonging to different realms.

The crucial conceptual move is the gulf. "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (Whitehead 1933, p. 217). Bifurcation is not merely distinction-making. Distinctions are the bread and butter of thought; without them we cannot speak. Bifurcation is the *hardening* of a distinction into two separate realms, with traffic between them controlled, slowed, or stopped altogether. Once fact and value live on different sides of a gulf, the question of which facts deserve attention disappears, because the selection-question is a value-question and is therefore not factual.

A second feature: bifurcations *produce their own appearance of obviousness*. After several generations inside a bifurcation, the splitting seems natural. Students arrive convinced that fact is one kind of thing and value another, that body is one and identity is the other. The naturalisation is itself an effect of the operation.

**Part two: why does Halewood treat bifurcation as the cornerstone of modernity?** First, *architecturally*. A cornerstone aligns the rest of the building. Without the operation of bifurcation, the rest of modern thought does not stand. Modern science requires the fact-value split to legitimate value-free description; the modern university requires the science-humanities split to organise faculties; the modern state requires the public-private split to demarcate rights from regulation; the modern colonial project requires the race-ethnicity split to manage populations. Remove the cornerstone and these institutions lose their alignment.

Second, *genealogically*. The bifurcations trace to a specific lineage — Cartesian dualism, Kantian critique, neo-Kantian methodology, the German Idealist split between *Naturwissenschaften* and *Geisteswissenschaften*. Halewood (chapter 8) shows how this runs through Weber and Simmel into the bones of the discipline. The bifurcation thesis is therefore a claim about how a specific philosophical inheritance became a structural commitment.

Third, *politically*. Bifurcation does work. The split between civilisation and slavery in the standard narrative is what allows Shakespeare and the slave ship to coexist in textbooks without provoking the obvious question. Du Bois's 1915 essay refuses this: the *same* day cannot have two sides (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). The bifurcation made it possible for the discipline to teach industrial modernity without teaching the plantation.

A counter-argument worth pressing: why should we accept Halewood's privileging of bifurcation over other candidate cornerstones — capital, the nation-state, instrumental reason? My reply is that Halewood's choice is not exclusive. Capital, the nation-state and instrumental reason are themselves bifurcation-structured: capital splits use-value from exchange-value, the nation-state splits citizen from foreigner, instrumental reason splits means from ends. Bifurcation is the operation that *each* of these institutions runs at its core. Halewood's claim is that the operation, not the institution, is the cornerstone.

A second objection: is the cornerstone metaphor itself dangerously architectural — does it assume a static structure? Halewood's Whiteheadian commitment is to process, not stasis. The reply is that a cornerstone in process metaphysics is itself a manner of association — the way modern thought *keeps* aligning itself by repeating the splitting operation. The operation is ongoing, not finished.

In conclusion, by bifurcation Halewood means the operation of splitting one phenomenon into two branches separated by a gulf, naturalised over time. He treats it as the cornerstone of modernity because it aligns the cognitive, institutional and political architecture of the modern world in a single move, and because removing or refusing it dissolves the alignment that modern thought has come to rely on. The way forward is the adverbial sociology Part 2 of *Rethinking the Social* outlines — sociology done in the mode of the *how* rather than the mode of the *what*, with the manner of association recovered as the proper object of the discipline.

### M4: Compare and contrast Du Bois's account of slavery in *The Negro* (1915) with Marx's account of the slavery–cotton–industry chain in *Capital*.

I argue that the two accounts are *parallel but incommensurable*: they agree that slavery is constitutive of modern industry, but they differ in *how* they constitute it. My thesis is that Marx's chain-of-conditions argument is a *causal-structural* claim and Du Bois's "blot on modern human history" argument is a *moral-civilisational* claim, and that the most powerful reading of modernity requires holding both at once without collapsing one into the other.

The texts. Marx, in the cotton-manufacture passages of *Capital* and the letter to Annenkov, gives us the chain: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). Du Bois, in *The Negro* (1915), gives us the indictment: "For four hundred years, from 1450 to 1850, European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings […] It was the day of the greatest expansion of two of the world's most pretentious religions and of the beginnings of the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90).

**Compare — the shared core.** Both refuse to bifurcate slavery from modern industry. Marx: the chain is one chain, not two; remove slavery and the chain collapses. Du Bois: the slave trade is contemporaneous with Shakespeare, Luther, Raphael — not separable from "the beginnings of the modern organization of industry." Both refuse the standard move by which slavery is treated as a regrettable adjunct to modern progress. Both ground the analytic claim in concrete historical facts (Marx names cotton; Du Bois names 1450-1850, four hundred years).

**Contrast — the explanatory structure.** Marx's argument is *causal-structural*: slavery → cotton → industry → world trade → large-scale industry. Each link is a necessary condition for the next; remove slavery and the chain falls. Du Bois's argument is *civilisational-moral*. He gives no chain of necessary conditions; he gives a moral indictment of "European civilization" *as such* during the four-century period. The slave trade is not just one input to a system; it is the moral fact about modernity that subsequent history cannot palliate.

**Contrast — scope.** Marx's frame is capitalist modernity. Slavery matters because it underwrites the development of capital. Du Bois's frame is modern human history as a whole. He names religions, artists, statesmen, theologians — Shakespeare, Luther, Raphael, Haroun-al-Raschid, Lincoln — to insist that the slave trade is constitutive of modern *civilisation*, not just of modern *economy*. The Du Boisian frame is broader; the Marxian frame is sharper.

**Contrast — what each makes politically actionable.** Marx's chain-of-conditions argument makes the abolition of capital the route to addressing slavery's modern legacies — change the economic system and the conditions for the institutional reproduction of racial-economic hierarchy are removed. Du Bois's civilisational-moral argument refuses this: the harms of the slave trade are not exhausted by the conditions that produced it, and post-capitalist societies would still owe the debt. The reparations literature inherits Du Bois on this point; the Marxian tradition has tended to deprioritise it.

**Convergence on bifurcation.** Both writers refuse the fact-value bifurcation that the standard story of modern industry relies on. Marx will not allow the fact of cotton to be told without the fact of slavery; Du Bois will not allow the values of Renaissance art to be told without the violence of the slave ship. In Halewood's adverbial terms, both work on the *manner* in which modernity associates its goods with its harms — neither will let the goods and harms be filed separately.

A counter-argument: it might be said that Marx's structural-causal account is *more rigorous* and that Du Bois's moral-civilisational account, while powerful, is merely rhetorical. I reject the dichotomy. Du Bois's 1915 essay (and *Black Reconstruction* a generation later) is empirically dense — names, dates, percentages, institutions. The moral register does not displace the analytic register; it gives the analytic register its point. Conversely, Marx's *Capital* is not morally neutral; it is suffused with the language of "vampire," "blood," "skinning alive," directed at capital. The two writers differ in the *balance* of structural and moral register, not in whether they have both.

In conclusion, Du Bois and Marx converge in refusing to bifurcate slavery from modern industry and diverge in the structure of the argument they offer for the refusal. Marx gives us a causal-structural chain; Du Bois gives us a civilisational-moral indictment. Both are needed: the chain to identify the structural targets of change, the indictment to keep the question of modern civilisation's debt unforgettable. The lecture's "what would sociology be like if we had taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously?" is best answered by holding the two accounts together, not by choosing.

### M5: How does the concept of bifurcation help us analyse the contemporary divide between climate science and climate politics?

I argue that the contemporary divide between climate science and climate politics is a textbook case of modern bifurcation — fact/value, science/humanities, nature/society — all running together in the most consequential gulf of our time. My thesis is that Halewood's concept of bifurcation lets us see *why* climate communication has stalled despite the science being settled: not because the public is irrational but because the institutional architecture of modern knowledge has spent two centuries learning to separate description from decision, and this same architecture is now incapable of acting on a description that demands a decision.

The first move is to specify the gulf. Climate science, as institutionalised in the IPCC, the major research universities, and the peer-reviewed journals, sits on the "fact" side of Halewood's fact-value bifurcation. The science describes; it does not prescribe. The wording of the 2023 Synthesis Report is calibrated to a "high confidence" rhetoric that explicitly excludes value-claims. Climate politics, by contrast — Conferences of the Parties, national legislation, civic protest — sits on the "value" side: it argues about priorities, distributions, sacrifices. The gulf was installed at the founding of the IPCC, which was deliberately constituted to deliver value-free assessments to value-laden negotiators. The expectation was that the facts would do the political work. They have not.

The second move is to apply Whitehead's diagnostic. "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (Whitehead 1933, p. 217). Climate scientists report difficulty *understanding* the political response — the apparent irrationality of governments that accept the science and miss the targets. Climate activists report difficulty *understanding* the scientific reticence — the apparent timidity of researchers who know what is coming and decline to say it in plain language. Each side is opaque to the other. The bifurcation that was supposed to coordinate the two has produced mutual incomprehension.

The third move is to specify the second bifurcation in play: nature/society. The modern academic division between the natural sciences and the social sciences — Halewood's science/humanities bifurcation in its operational form — makes climate a problem that no single discipline is equipped to address. Atmospheric chemistry models the planet without modelling the social relations that produce greenhouse gases; sociology models social relations without modelling the planet that constrains them. Halewood's Whiteheadian alternative — "the social is natural," societies are nexuses of actual entities sharing a common manner of form — would let us treat the atmosphere and the petroleum-industrial complex as a single nexus. The bifurcation forbids this.

The fourth move is the political-economic one. The fact/value gulf does work for certain interests. As long as climate science is a fact and climate response is a value, the fossil-fuel industry can accept the science (it is a fact) while contesting the response (it is a value, and values are negotiable). The bifurcation is not just an epistemic mistake; it is a structural feature that protects the status quo. Halewood's lecture-point about the institutional underpinning of bifurcations — the modern university, the modern state, the modern colonial archive — applies here directly. The IPCC's institutional design *expresses* the bifurcation; it does not invent it.

**Example — the language of "net zero."** "Net zero by 2050" is a perfect bifurcation artefact. The scientific component (a quantifiable concentration of atmospheric CO2) sits on the fact side; the policy component (the trajectory of emissions reductions, the accounting of offsets, the distribution of burdens) sits on the value side. The phrase coordinates the two sides without integrating them — it lets governments claim to be following the science while choosing politically convenient interpretations of the policy implications. The result is the well-documented gap between net-zero pledges and actual emissions trajectories.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: maybe the divide between climate science and climate politics is *necessary* — democracies should not let scientists decide policy. I take this seriously. The point of the critique is not to abolish the distinction between empirical assessment and democratic decision; it is to refuse the *bifurcation* that converts a working distinction into two separate realms with controlled traffic between them. A non-bifurcated treatment would keep the working distinction (scientists describe, citizens decide) while admitting that the description is value-saturated (which problems we model, which timescales we adopt, which uncertainties we report) and the decision is fact-saturated (what is actually possible at given costs). The gulf would close even if the distinction remained.

In conclusion, Halewood's bifurcation diagnoses the climate impasse with unusual precision. The fact/value, nature/society and science/humanities bifurcations together produce the contemporary divide between climate science and climate politics, and this divide is not a communication failure but a structural feature of modern knowledge. The way forward, on Halewood's Whiteheadian model, is an adverbial sociology that treats the climate-and-the-society as a single manner of association — not as two realms whose translation can be deferred. The cheat-sheet payoff for any contemporary-application essay: bifurcation is the diagnostic to reach for whenever a "communication problem" is presented as the issue.

### M6: Is the fact-value distinction a coherent epistemological position or an ideological alibi? Discuss.

I argue that the fact-value distinction is *both* a coherent epistemological position — at the level of working methodological contrast — *and* an ideological alibi — at the level of metaphysical bifurcation — and that the trick is to distinguish the two levels rather than to choose between them. My thesis is that Halewood's bifurcation thesis lets us hold this double judgement: keep the working contrast, refuse the bifurcated metaphysics, name the alibi-function precisely where the metaphysics is doing the political work.

The first move is to take the "coherent epistemological position" reading seriously. Weber's *Wertfreiheit* is a methodological discipline, not a metaphysical doctrine. At the level of writing — when a sociologist is drafting a sentence about a phenomenon — the distinction between an empirical claim ("the unemployment rate rose by 0.3% last quarter") and a normative claim ("this is a moral catastrophe") is real and useful. The discipline of separating the two is what makes scholarly description differ from pamphleteering. In this minimal sense the distinction is coherent: it picks out two genuinely different illocutionary acts.

The neo-Kantian inheritance, traced in Halewood's chapter 8, gave this minimal distinction its philosophical scaffolding. Rickert and Dilthey distinguished the *Naturwissenschaften* from the *Geisteswissenschaften* by the kind of question they ask — causal-explanatory versus meaning-and-value oriented. Weber radicalised this into the doctrine of value-freedom *within* the social sciences themselves: the social scientist studies the values of others without endorsing or condemning them. The doctrine has internal coherence; it can be taught, applied, and falsified.

The second move is to ask what the doctrine *does* when it operates at the level of metaphysical bifurcation rather than working contrast. Halewood's lecture-thesis is that the fact-value distinction is the *cornerstone* bifurcation of modernity — the one that aligns the architecture of modern thought. Once the distinction hardens from a working contrast into two separate realms ("facts" over here, "values" over there), it stops being a methodological discipline and starts being an ontological commitment. The world really does come in two kinds; sociology straddles the gap; certain questions cannot be asked because they would violate the gap.

At this level the distinction begins to function as an alibi. The most consequential case is the standard sociology of modernity. The "facts" of the rise of industry are reported — population statistics, productivity figures, the dates of factory openings. The "values" of the slave trade are filed elsewhere — moral lament, ethics class, regrettable adjunct. The bifurcation does the alibi-work: it allows a sociology that *literally* describes modernity without describing its constitutive features. Du Bois's 1915 indictment is precisely the refusal of this alibi: "in the midst of this advance and uplift this slave trade and slavery spread more human misery […] than can well be calculated" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). The slave trade is not a value-question to be added later; it is a fact about modernity. The bifurcation hides it.

The third move is to specify the *political economy* of the alibi. Bifurcations persist when they serve material interests. The fact-value bifurcation persists because it does work for institutions whose authority depends on appearing value-free — universities, central banks, statistical bureaux, the IPCC, the Bank of International Settlements. These institutions cannot say "we are politically committed"; they have to say "we report facts." The bifurcation gives them the language. The corollary is that the value-laden choices these institutions actually make (which facts to count, which timescales to use, which populations to include) are insulated from democratic scrutiny because they appear as the technical machinery of fact-production.

A counter-argument. A Weberian might reply that without *Wertfreiheit* sociology collapses into political pamphleteering — that the alibi-objection slides too easily from "this distinction can be misused" to "this distinction is in itself ideological." I accept the force of the worry. The reply is that the critique is not aimed at the working contrast but at the metaphysical bifurcation. The Weberian discipline of separating description from prescription can survive — it is what makes scholarly writing scholarly. What cannot survive is the additional, smuggled claim that the two kinds of statement belong to two separate realms of reality.

A second counter-argument. A pragmatist might say that the alibi-charge is overblown because in practice scientists and policymakers do communicate across the supposed gulf. I disagree. The empirical literature on science-policy interface is full of evidence that the communication is asymmetric, slow, distorted by the institutional commitment to value-freedom on the science side. The IPCC's communication problems are the obvious case. The bifurcation is doing the work the alibi-reading attributes to it.

In conclusion, the fact-value distinction is a coherent epistemological position at the level of working methodological contrast and an ideological alibi at the level of metaphysical bifurcation. Halewood's diagnostic — keep the contrast, refuse the bifurcation — is the right one. The cheat-sheet payoff: any question framed as "Is X coherent or ideological?" should be answered by *distinguishing the levels at which X operates* rather than by picking one of the two horns of the question. That is what critique-not-criticism looks like on an exam.

### M7: Critique or criticism?

I argue that the question forces a choice it should not force — critique and criticism are not equally weighted alternatives; they are an asymmetric pair in which critique is the disciplined sociological operation and criticism is its degraded cousin. My thesis, following the lecture's explicit injunction, is that sociology at its best is *critique* and that the disciplinary slogan "be critical" has slowly translated into the more impoverished "find fault," with measurable costs.

The lecture's distinction. "Criticise" means to find fault. "Critique" means (i) to evaluate the structure, (ii) to see what works well, (iii) to suggest clarifications, (iv) to suggest improvements. The four points are presented in the lecture as a checklist. The lecture is explicit: "Remember this for your essays and exam." The two words look like synonyms in everyday English; in the discipline they name two different operations.

What criticism does. Criticism identifies a problem and stops. It is the move of saying "this is wrong" without saying what would be right. It has its uses — it can mobilise opposition, it can name harms, it can puncture complacency. But it has a characteristic exhaustion: after the third or fourth iteration of criticism on the same target, the audience knows the target is bad and has no new way to think about it. The contemporary "discourse" — on social media, in opinion writing, in much of what passes for cultural commentary — runs on criticism.

What critique does. Critique identifies a *structure* and asks what would change if the structure were different. Marx's *Capital* is critique: it specifies the wage form, the commodity, the circulation of capital, and asks what an alternative manner of association would look like. Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction* is critique: it specifies why Reconstruction failed (the alliance of Northern capital with Southern landowners) and what an alternative trajectory would have looked like. Halewood's *Rethinking the Social* is critique: it specifies which moves of the canon were lost and what an adverbial recovery would offer.

The first reason critique is the right answer. Critique produces *new conceptual moves*; criticism produces only verdicts. The lecture's worry is that a generation of sociology students fluent in "being critical" becomes good at identifying problems and incapable of imagining alternatives. The Beauvoirian sociological imagination that the lecture revives — "she imagined what social theory would look like if it took women seriously" — is critique in active form. It does not just diagnose the absence of women; it pictures what their inclusion would change.

The second reason. Critique respects what works. The lecture's checklist includes "see what works well" as a separate point. This is not a concession to the status quo; it is a recognition that any structure worth critiquing also has features worth preserving. Halewood admires Durkheim while critical of his integral-collective grounding; Marx admires the productivity of capital while critical of the wage form; Du Bois admires Lincoln while critical of the white labour movement. Criticism has only one register (fault-finding); critique varies the register.

The third reason. Critique points to alternatives. Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* XI: "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 158). Change requires a destination, not just a complaint. Critique closes by pointing somewhere; criticism closes by listing what is wrong. The first is generative; the second is exhausting.

**Example — modernity itself.** The standard student-essay critique of "modernity" is often actually criticism: modernity is colonial, modernity is bifurcated, modernity is alienating. All true; all unproductive on their own. Halewood's lecture asks for critique: identify the structure (bifurcations, the manner of association, the suppressed canon), evaluate what works, and point to an alternative (an adverbial, Whiteheadian, decolonised sociology). The first essay finds fault and stops; the second moves the discipline.

A counter-argument: the distinction is a semantic gloss, and in practice every critique includes moments of criticism. I partly accept this. The point is not that critique excludes fault-finding — Marx finds fault with capital, Halewood with the bifurcated canon — but that the *organising frame* must be structural-evaluative, not merely fault-finding.

A second objection: what the discipline really needs is *more* criticism, not less — sharper, faster, more public-facing fault-finding. I disagree. The world is awash in criticism (comment threads, op-eds, cable news). What is in short supply is critique — the patient, structural, alternative-imagining work academic sociology is uniquely placed to do.

In conclusion, critique or criticism: the answer is critique, not because criticism is wrong but because critique includes everything criticism does and more. The lecture's four-point checklist — structure, what works, clarifications, improvements — is the disciplinary form of synoptic vision. The exam payoff: every answer should close by *pointing somewhere*, not by listing what is wrong. That is what makes the writing critique.

### M8: The myth of progress in 2026 — does the concept still do critical work?

I argue that the myth of progress is still doing critical work in 2026, but in a more attenuated way than in earlier critiques, and that the concept's continuing usefulness depends on resisting two contemporary temptations: triumphalist progress-talk (now repackaged as "innovation," "longtermism," "the AI revolution") and the symmetrical despair-talk that treats modernity itself as a write-off. My thesis is that the concept, used carefully, names a *bifurcation* — between the parts of modern history we are willing to count as progress and the parts we file as collateral — and in 2026 that bifurcation is being aggressively re-installed, which is exactly when critique of the myth is most needed.

The concept in its classical formulation. The myth of progress is a way of telling modernity as a single arc of improvement: from superstition to science, from feudalism to capitalism, from empire to liberal democracy. The myth presupposes the standard rationalisation narrative the lecture asks us to invert. Du Bois (1915) named the myth's blind spot a century ago: "These were not days of decadence, but a period that gave the world Shakespeare, Martin Luther, and Raphael […] In the midst of this advance and uplift this slave trade and slavery spread more human misery […] than can well be calculated" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). The progress story is true on one side; the slave trade is true on the other; the myth is the act of telling only the first.

The first 2026 iteration: the AI vindication story. Generative AI is routinely narrated as the latest installment of progress. The framing is familiar: scientific breakthrough, productivity boom, civilisational uplift. The critical residue of the myth-of-progress concept is precisely Du Bois's question: what is on the further side? The training data scraped without consent, the click-workers in Kenya and Manila labelling harmful content for $2/hour, the energy and water consumption of data centres, the bias embedded in models. These are not values to be added later; they are constitutive conditions of the "progress."

The second iteration: longtermism. Effective Altruism's longtermist wing reformulates progress as a moral imperative — civilisation must endure for the trillions of future people whose existence depends on present technological development. The framing converts the colonial structure of progress-talk into an ethical mandate: anyone who slows AI development is sacrificing astronomical future welfare. The myth-of-progress concept asks whose progress, paid for by whom. Longtermist accounting is Du Bois's "advance and uplift" with the slave-ship narrowed out of the frame.

The third iteration: green-tech vindication. The same logic appears in climate discourse: solar panels, EVs, carbon capture as the new triumphalism. The critical residue is to ask after the cobalt-mining child labour in the DRC, the lithium extraction in indigenous territories in Chile and Australia, the green colonialism that exports environmental costs to the global South. Progress is real on one side; extraction is real on the other. The myth is the bifurcation.

Why the concept still does critical work. The work is to keep the bifurcation visible. The temptation in 2026 is to accept the new progress-stories at face value because their content (AI, green tech, longevity) is genuinely different from the steam-engine progress-stories of the nineteenth century. The myth-of-progress concept reminds us that the *form* — single-arc, monocausal, with constitutive harms filed elsewhere — is the same. The progress-narrative is the fact-value bifurcation extended over time.

Where the concept needs updating. The classical critique (Adorno, Horkheimer, Benjamin) read progress as a single ideological structure of the bourgeois West. In 2026 the structure is polyvocal: Chinese state-developmentalism, Indian Hindutva, Silicon Valley each narrate a different progress. The myth is now a family of myths sharing a form. The critical concept must travel.

A counter-argument: the myth-of-progress critique risks lapsing into anti-progress nihilism — the very thing the "critique not criticism" distinction warns against. If every progress-story is a myth, what is sociology supposed to recommend? The reply is that the concept is critique, not refusal. The alternative to triumphalist progress-talk is *honest* progress-talk: a description of modernity that counts goods *and* harms, refuses the bifurcation, and does not require collateral to be filed under "values" while gains are filed under "facts."

A second counter-argument: the concept is a Eurocentric invention and may be redundant in non-European contexts. I disagree. Du Bois showed that the concept is most powerful precisely when used from a position outside the standard progress story — the slave's view, the colony's view. The concept is portable; its critical use is sharpest from the further side of the gulf.

In conclusion, the myth of progress in 2026 still does critical work, and the work is what it has always been: to name the operation by which modernity narrates its goods without its harms. In the age of AI, longtermism and green tech the operation is being re-installed with new vocabulary; the concept lets us see the operation through the vocabulary. Halewood's bifurcation thesis is the deeper diagnostic; the myth-of-progress concept is the surface tool. The cheat-sheet payoff: any contemporary-application essay can use myth-of-progress to identify *which* progress-story the phenomenon presupposes.

### M9: Does the rise of generative AI vindicate or refute Whitehead's claim that "ideas are things"?

I argue that the rise of generative AI strongly *vindicates* Whitehead's claim — perhaps more clearly than any phenomenon since the printing press — while also revealing what the claim does *not* explain. My thesis is that large language models are ideas-as-things in a literal-empirical sense (linguistic regularities materialised in matrices of weights, propagated across hardware and bodies), and that the public confusion about whether they "think" is the confusion of a culture trained on the bifurcation between mental contents and material objects that Whitehead's claim was designed to dissolve.

The Whiteheadian claim, recalled. "I propose to consider critically the *sort* of history which ideas can have in the life of humanity" (Whitehead 1933, p. 3). Halewood, drawing on Stengers, glosses this as "ideas are things." Ideas, for Whitehead, are not the contents of human minds. They have careers; they combine with humans, with practices, with materials; they enter and exit different societies. Conscious discrimination is the essence of humanity but only the accident of human existence: "It makes us human. But it does not make us exist" (Whitehead 1938, p. 158).

The vindication. Generative AI makes Whitehead's claim almost obvious. GPT-class models are statistical compressions of the textual record of humanity. The model is a thing — a matrix of parameters that occupies disk space and consumes electricity — and what it does is enact ideas. When the model produces a paragraph on the fact-value distinction, it is not "having a thought" in the Cartesian sense; it is reproducing a manner of association among linguistic items that humans have been propagating for two centuries. The idea exists in the matrix as a manner of association; the matrix is a thing. Whitehead's claim is descriptively correct.

The deeper vindication: ideas have careers that exceed their human vehicles. Whitehead's example was ape-to-human becoming, civilisation-to-civilisation transmission. The LLM is a sharper case. The idea-careers Whitehead saw operating in centuries are now operating in months: a corpus assembles, a model trains, outputs propagate, new training data is composed from those outputs, the next model trains on them. Ideas are literally things with careers; the human vehicles are increasingly intermediate stations rather than originating sources.

The bifurcation generative AI exposes. Public discourse runs on the bifurcation Whitehead's claim is designed to refuse. Either the model "really thinks" (and is therefore a mental-content-bearing entity) or it is "just statistics" (and is therefore not-mental). Both horns presuppose that thinking and stuff are different realms. Halewood's reading is that they are not: ideas are things, and statistics is what a particular kind of idea-thing looks like at scale. The model is neither thinking-in-the-Cartesian-sense nor merely-stuff; it is an idea-thing with the manner of a statistical regularity.

The colonial residue, vindicated again. Whitehead's claim was always more political than it looked. Ideas-as-things means ideas-with-trajectories — they travel with sugar, gold, ships. Trained on a corpus that overrepresents English-language, Northern, white, male voices, the LLM propagates a manner of association that reproduces these overweightings. The corpus is the thing, the manner of association in the corpus is the idea, and the idea has the bias of the thing.

Where the claim is partially refuted. Whitehead's metaphysics assumed ideas have *adventures* — Stengers's "agentic" register — and that the adventures are productive even when they exceed conscious intention. The LLM complicates this. The model has no goals; its outputs are produced by sampling. The "adventure" of an idea propagated by an LLM is not teleological agency; it is the propagation of a statistical regularity. Whitehead may have over-romanticised idea-agency; the contemporary case asks us to use his frame while stripping the teleology.

A counter-argument. A Marxist sociologist of knowledge will object that the LLM case is best analysed in terms of capital and ideology, not Whiteheadian idea-things. The model is owned, trained for a market, deployed for profit. My reply: the Marxist analysis is necessary but insufficient. It tells us why the LLM exists; it does not tell us *what kind of entity* it is. Whitehead's "ideas are things" supplies the missing ontology. Halewood's synthesis — Whitehead at the conceptual level, Marx at the material — is exactly right.

A second objection: ascribing "ideas" to a matrix of weights risks the reification Marxists have warned against. I disagree. Whitehead's claim is anti-reification in the deepest sense: it refuses to make ideas a separate realm requiring a special mode of existence. To say the model is an idea-thing is to insist that ideas are in the world, made of stuff, available for analysis. The reification charge is itself an artefact of the bifurcation Whitehead refused.

In conclusion, the rise of generative AI strongly vindicates Whitehead's claim that "ideas are things." Large language models make the claim almost literal: a thing is the materialisation of an idea (a manner of association in a corpus), and the thing propagates the idea across the world. The vindication comes with one refinement — Whitehead's teleological residue must be stripped — but the core ontology is right. The cheat-sheet payoff for a hypothetical-style question: ask what the contemporary phenomenon makes *visible* about the theorist's claim, not just whether it is consistent with it.

### M10: How convincing do you find Simmel's account of sociation? Use specific examples.

I find Simmel's account of sociation broadly convincing as a *negative* move — it correctly refuses the reification of "society" into a quasi-agent — and only partially convincing as a *positive* one, because the dyadic-and-triadic toolkit it offers undersells the macro-structural features that classical and contemporary sociology need to name. My thesis is that the sociation concept is necessary but insufficient, and that Halewood is right to use it as a stepping stone toward a Whiteheadian account that retains the anti-reification while regaining the macrostructural traction.

Simmel's account, recalled. "Society" "is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*. It does not exist outside and in addition to the individuals and the processes among them" (Simmel 1950, p. 4). The positive concept is *Vergesellschaftung* — sociation — the verbal noun for the process by which interactions densify into recognisable patterns. "One should properly speak, not of society, but of *sociation*" (Simmel 1950, p. 10). Form, not content, is the sociologist's proper object; hence "Formal Sociology."

**Example 1 — the secret.** Simmel's own example. A secret is only a secret in relation to at least one other; the form (concealment from some, disclosure to others) makes the content sociologically interesting. The same information functions completely differently depending on its manner of association with the parties. Modern equivalents — the encrypted message, the leaked document — confirm Simmel's intuition that the form of the secret is the social fact.

**Example 2 — money.** Simmel's *Philosophy of Money* (1900) shows the sociation tool reaching for structural ambition. Money's form — impersonality, quantitative comparability, the coordination of strangers — produces a recognisable modern manner of association (metropolitan, impersonal, calculating). The form-level analysis reaches the macro through accumulation; the blasé attitude of the *Großstadt* is the manner of association of money users at scale.

**Example 3 — the metropolis.** Simmel's *Metropolis and Mental Life* (1903) demonstrates the convincing pay-off. The form of metropolitan association — strangers, density, schedule, protective reserve — is captured precisely by the sociation toolkit. The essay still teaches because the form-level description outlives any particular metropolis (Berlin 1903, London 2026).

Where I am less convinced. The sociation account works best when the relations under analysis are *reciprocal* — both parties are oriented to each other in roughly symmetric ways. It works less well when the relations are *asymmetric in a structural rather than a personal sense*. The slave on the plantation and the planter are in a relation; they orient to one another; sociation occurs. But the relation is structured by a transnational system of legal status, capital flow, and racial categorisation that exceeds the bilateral relation. Simmel's tool describes the encounter; it does not describe the system that pre-shapes the encounter. The lecture's emphasis on the colonial project — Madeira to São Tomé to Brazil — is precisely a structure that the sociation toolkit struggles to reach.

The deeper limitation. Simmel's account is methodologically individualist in a soft sense — it builds society up from the dyads and triads. Macro-structures exist but they are reconstructions from the micro. Marx's and Whitehead's accounts go the other direction: the macro-pattern (capitalist relations, the common element of form in a Whiteheadian nexus) is itself the unit of analysis, and the micro-encounter is one moment in its reproduction. For modernity — which is paradigmatically a macro-structural phenomenon — the directionality matters.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: defenders of Simmel (Pyyhtinen, the contemporary "rebirth of interest" Halewood flags in his lecture) will say the sociation toolkit is more flexible than I credit. Simmel on the stranger, on superordination and subordination, on conflict, all reach toward asymmetric and structural relations. I partly accept this. But even in these essays the conceptual centre remains the dyadic relation, and the asymmetry is read off the dyad rather than analysed at its own scale. The plantation system is not a large stranger-relation.

A second objection: the "broadly convincing" verdict is too generous because Simmel's anti-reification move is itself questionable — maybe "society" *is* a real thing, and Durkheim was right. I disagree. The reification of society into a quasi-agent ("society demands," "society values") is the move that hides agency behind a noun. Simmel's negative work survives.

In conclusion, I find Simmel's account of sociation convincing in the work it does and unconvincing in the work it refuses to do. The dyadic-triadic toolkit captures reciprocal interaction beautifully; the form-level analysis of money and the metropolis reaches the structural through accumulation; but the colonial-plantation-modernity that Halewood's lecture foregrounds requires a macrostructural tool that Simmel does not supply. The Whiteheadian alternative — society as any nexus with a common element of form — keeps Simmel's anti-reification while regaining the macro-scale. The exam payoff: the answer to "how convincing" is not yes-or-no but *at what scale and for what kind of relation*. That is what makes the answer critical.

### M11: Why has the sociological canon been so resistant to the inclusion of Du Bois? Answer with reference to Howard French, *Born in Blackness* (2021).

I argue that the canon's resistance to Du Bois has not been an oversight; it has been a *structural feature* of how the discipline of sociology was constituted, and that French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) names exactly the structural reason: the Eurocentric narrative of modernity which the discipline took as its empirical object excluded the African pre-history of capitalism that Du Bois's 1915 essay had already begun to reconstruct. My thesis is that including Du Bois requires more than a reading list change; it requires inverting the empirical baseline against which the canon was assembled.

The resistance, recapped. Du Bois was a contemporary of Weber. He had a German doctorate; he attended the same conferences; Weber recommended his work. He published, by 1915, a structural account of modernity-as-coloniality whose argumentative form — slavery as constitutive of modern industry — was already available for canonical absorption. The discipline absorbed it for almost a century in marginalised forms only: as a "race" sub-specialty, as a "Black sociology" tributary, as a pedagogical adjunct. The Marx-Weber-Durkheim trio became canonical; Du Bois did not. The resistance was not personal indifference. It was structural.

French's diagnosis. French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) reconstructs the empirical history that the Eurocentric sociology canon assumed away. Portugal was shipping nearly 600 kg of gold per year from West Africa by the 1490s. Indian cotton was being shipped to the Gold Coast to buy that gold. Plantation slavery began in Madeira and São Tomé/Príncipe before crossing the Atlantic. By 1629 a Kimbundu-Spanish grammar was published for use in Bolivia and Peru. Approximately 40% of all plantation-enslaved people went from Africa to Brazil. The point is not that these facts were unknown — they were available in archives — but that the canon was assembled around an empirical baseline that did not include them. Sociology took European industrialisation as its empirical horizon; this horizon required Africa to be peripheral.

The first structural reason. The canon was assembled in late-nineteenth and early-twentieth century European universities whose institutional self-understanding was Eurocentric. Sociology was supposed to explain why Europe had modernised; the question presupposes that modernity *is* Europe's accomplishment. Du Bois's 1915 essay refuses the presupposition. To include him was to refuse the question the canon was assembled to answer.

The second structural reason. The fact-value bifurcation, traced in Halewood's lecture, organised the discipline's research priorities. The "facts" of modernisation were European (urbanisation, factory employment, secularisation, bureaucracy); the slave trade was assigned to the "values" side. Du Bois's move was to refuse the bifurcation — to insist the slave trade was a fact of modernity, not a value-question about it. A canon committed to the bifurcation could not absorb Du Bois without dismantling itself.

The third structural reason. The canon's geographical centre of gravity was Europe. Du Bois wrote from the United States, but his analytic centre was the Black Atlantic. French's title is precise: *Born in Blackness*. Modernity is born in the African coastal trade, not in the European factory. To centre this is to displace the canon's geography — and the discipline preferred to keep the geography.

The fourth structural reason. Du Bois's method — historical-archival, statistical, ethnographic, political-economic — refuses the methodological bifurcation between "theory" and "history." A canon that organises itself around the theory-history distinction reads Du Bois as a historian. French's book is in the same tradition: empirical history with a structural-theoretical argument, a form the discipline still has not quite digested.

The lecture's framing. Halewood: "What would sociology be like if we had actually taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously all along?" The counterfactual operationalises French's empirical inversion. If the canon's baseline had included the 600 kg of gold per year, the Madeira plantations, the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar, Du Bois would not have been excluded — because the canon's central questions would already have been his.

A counter-argument: the canon has now substantially included Du Bois — *ASR* and *BJS* publish Du Boisian work, curricula include him, the centenary of *The Souls of Black Folk* was widely commemorated. I partly accept this. The inclusion is real but the *inversion* (in French's sense) is still pending. Du Bois has been added; the centre of gravity has not been shifted.

A second counter-argument: the resistance might be intellectual rather than structural — perhaps Du Bois's work was simply not at the conceptual level of Weber's or Marx's. I reject this. Double consciousness, the wages of whiteness, the colour line, the public-and-parade theory of Reconstruction are theoretical concepts at the same level of generality as the spirit of capitalism or the wage form. The "not theoretical enough" charge is itself a symptom of the canon's structural commitments.

In conclusion, the canon has been resistant to Du Bois because its empirical baseline, geographical centre, and methodological commitments were all assembled around a Eurocentric narrative of modernity. French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) supplies the empirical reconstruction that would force the inversion. To include Du Bois is not to add him; it is to re-found the canon on the empirical ground he was already standing on in 1915. The cheat-sheet payoff: any why-question about disciplinary resistance should diagnose *structure*, not just attitude.

### M12: Using the work of at least two authors, critically assess the claim that "the manner of association" is what is sociologically interesting about modern societies.

I argue, drawing primarily on Durkheim and Whitehead with supporting work from Marx and Simmel, that the manner of association is *almost* what is sociologically interesting about modern societies — the qualifier matters because the four canonical accounts of manner-of-association converge less than Halewood's synthesis suggests, and the live disagreements among them are themselves part of what is sociologically interesting. My thesis is that the adverbial recovery is the right *first* move but it conceals a *second* set of disagreements about which the discipline still needs to choose.

The adverbial recovery, recapped. Halewood's Part 1 argues that the canon converges on the adverbial. **Durkheim:** "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (*Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128). **Whitehead:** a society is a nexus of actual entities sharing "a common element of form" through their mutual prehensions (*Adventures of Ideas*, pp. 260-261). Both centre the *how* — the manner — and not the *what* — the substance.

Why the move is correct as a first move. It dissolves two temptations at once. The methodological-individualist temptation — society as an aggregate of individuals — is turned off: individuals are themselves manners of association (of cells, of habits, of memberships). The holist reification temptation — "society demands," "society values" — is also turned off: if society is the manner of association rather than a thing, no quasi-agent formulation is available.

Where the two authors disagree about the manner. **Durkheim's** manner is the manner of collective morality. The cell-analogy underwrites a science of *integration* — solidarity, the collective conscience, the punishment of contraventions. The manner is centripetal: things hold together because their manner of association reproduces moral cohesion. **Whitehead's** manner is the manner of mutual prehension. Things hold together because each takes account of the others' having-the-same-character. There is no moral residue; the manner is metaphysical, not ethical. A hot stone is a society on Whitehead's account; it is not on Durkheim's.

The disagreement matters for modernity. Durkheim's adverbial sociology analyses the modern nation-state as a manner of association reproducing a collective morality (national rituals, civil religion, the cult of the individual). Whitehead's analyses it as a nexus reproducing a common element of form (legal documents, currency, infrastructure, language). The first is humanistic; the second post-humanistic. Halewood, on my reading, sides with Whitehead but does not fully spell out the cost: a Whiteheadian adverbial sociology is *not* the deeper Durkheim, it is something different.

**Example — the colonial plantation.** Durkheim's tool struggles: the moral collective of the plantation is fractured along the slave/free divide, with no single conscience-collective. Whitehead's tool fits better: the plantation is a nexus of actual entities (the enslaved, the overseers, the planters, the sugar mill, the ledger) sharing a common element of form (the manner of coerced extraction) reproduced through mutual prehension across the parts. The Whiteheadian register lets us name the plantation as a society in the same frame as the household; the Durkheimian register cannot.

Where Marx and Simmel sit. Marx's chemistry analogy sides with the move structurally (same atoms, different arrangement, different thing) but reads the manner *historically* (capitalist relations as one historically specific manner). Simmel's *Vergesellschaftung* sides with the move at the form-level (the dyad, the triad, the secret) but underweights the macro-structural dimension. Both contribute partial tools.

A counter-argument: the manner-of-association framing risks losing the *substantive* content that sociology needs. A focus on the *how* might leave us with no tools for the *what* — the specific institutions, classes, races, genders modernity organises. The reply is that manner-of-association is not opposed to substance; it is the level at which substance is reproduced. The plantation is a substantive institution; its substance *is* its manner. The wage form is a substantive relation; its substance *is* its manner. The adverbial move does not eliminate substance; it specifies what kind of thing substance is.

A second counter-argument: modernity itself may be the wrong scale for the tool, which is at home in small cases (the cell, the secret, the household). I disagree. Halewood's point, on Whitehead's authority, is that the tool scales: any nexus with a common element of form is a society. The plantation system, the world economy, the colonial archive — all are nexuses with common elements of form reproduced through mutual prehension across their parts.

In conclusion, manner-of-association is what is *first* sociologically interesting about modern societies, but the move conceals a live disagreement between Durkheim (humanistic, centripetal, moral) and Whitehead (metaphysical, mutual-prehension, post-humanistic) about which adverbial sociology we are doing. Halewood sides with Whitehead; I think he is right, but the choice should be explicit. The cheat-sheet payoff: any two-author manner-of-association answer should specify *which* account it uses and what is gained and lost by the choice.

### M13: Can sociology think outside the categories of European modernity?

I argue that sociology can think outside the categories of European modernity, but only with considerable effort and only by treating the limits of its inherited vocabulary as data rather than as scenery. My thesis is that the categories of European modernity — society, the individual, the state, the public, the private, the rational, the religious — are not just descriptors; they are *operations* of the bifurcation that Halewood diagnoses, and thinking outside them requires interrogating the operation, not just substituting different nouns.

The structural problem. Sociology is itself a modernity-product. Marx, Weber, Durkheim, Simmel — all wrote within the institutional, linguistic and conceptual horizons of nineteenth-century Europe. The discipline's basic vocabulary (society, social action, the social) was elaborated in German, French and English with the assumed referents being European populations, European states, European economies. The Eurocentrism is not an accidental bias; it is the embedded geography of the conceptual apparatus. To ask whether sociology can think outside this is to ask whether a discipline can interrogate its own foundations.

The first reason to say yes. Du Bois already did it. *The Negro* (1915) and *Black Reconstruction* (1935) are works of sociology that take the Black Atlantic, not the European nation-state, as the empirical and conceptual centre. The vocabulary is partly European (he writes in English, uses the categories of "civilization," "religion," "industry"), but the analytic ambition is to bend the vocabulary toward a referent it was not designed to capture. The slave trade, in Du Bois's account, is a sociological fact of the same level as the rise of the joint-stock company; the wages of whiteness is a concept of the same level as the spirit of capitalism. Du Bois's work shows that sociology *can* be done outside the categories — at the cost of having to re-make the categories as one goes.

The second reason to say yes. Dussel's transmodernity, French's *Born in Blackness*, and the Latin American "coloniality" tradition (Quijano, Mignolo, Lugones) are extended demonstrations. Each takes a category of European modernity (modernity itself, capitalism, the public sphere, gender) and shows that the category was constituted *through* the colonial relation rather than being subsequently extended to it. The work is not the rejection of the categories; it is the historicisation of them, which lets us see what they were built to do and where they fail.

The third reason to say yes. Halewood's adverbial move offers a methodological route. If we shift attention from the substantive categories (society, the state) to the manner of association, we can ask: *how* is association organised in this context, *which* manner of mutual prehension obtains here? The shift from noun to adverb is partly portable across cultural contexts. The colonial plantation is not a "society" in Durkheim's centripetal sense, but it is a society in Whitehead's nexus-with-common-element-of-form sense. The adverbial move loosens the European specificity of the noun-categories.

The first reason to be sceptical. Using the word "society" — even an adverbially rethought "society" — to describe the African coastal trade, the pre-Columbian Andes, or the Sahelian Islamic empires is itself the imposition of a European category. Indigenous epistemologies (Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Glen Coulthard) argue that the imposition is the deeper problem and that no internal sociological reform can address it. On this view, sociology cannot think outside its categories; it can only mark them as not-the-only-categories.

The second reason. The discipline's institutional infrastructure (journals, peer review, doctoral training) is European-modern in its organisation. A sociologist trying to think outside still publishes in English-language journals and situates her work in relation to the canonical trio. The institutional architecture pulls the work back inside the categories even when the writer pushes outside.

The third reason. The very *question* "can sociology think outside?" is asked from inside; it presupposes a coherent inside-outside boundary, which is itself a European-modern way of organising the question. Halewood's bifurcation-thesis applies: the inside-outside framing is itself the bifurcation that needs refusing.

A counter-argument: every discipline has constitutive vocabularies; sociology is not specially handicapped. I partly accept this. The reply is that sociology's categories were assembled around a particular geography (Europe) and a particular history (modernisation) whose universality was assumed rather than tested. The discipline carries a heavier reform burden than, say, mathematics.

Where I land. Sociology can think outside the categories of European modernity, but the thinking is labour-intensive and structurally limited. It requires three moves: (i) recover the unsettled moments in the canon when the categories were not yet stabilised (Halewood's project); (ii) include the voices (Du Bois, Dussel, French, Quijano, Smith) who were outside the European canonical conversation; (iii) historicise the categories so that their European specificity becomes visible rather than presupposed.

In conclusion, sociology can think outside the categories of European modernity to the extent that it is willing to do the threefold work of canonical recovery, voice-inclusion, and category-historicisation. The work is necessarily *inside* the discipline (no other discipline to think with) and necessarily *against* it (the categories must be re-made). Each European-modern category is a bifurcation, and refusing the bifurcation is what thinking outside looks like. The cheat-sheet payoff: "can sociology...?" questions are best answered by specifying what kind of work would make the answer yes, not by a yes-or-no verdict.

### M14: The sociological imagination at third year.

I argue that the sociological imagination at third year has to do something different from what it does at first year, and that the difference is not merely a matter of difficulty but of *kind*. My thesis, following the lecture's Beauvoirian framing, is that first-year sociological imagination is *descriptive-linking* (Mills: connecting personal trouble to public issue) and third-year sociological imagination is *generative-counterfactual* (Beauvoir: picturing what social theory would look like if it took a previously excluded category seriously). The shift is not optional; it is what makes third-year sociology *third-year*.

The Millsian first-year version. Mills's *The Sociological Imagination* (1959) is the staple: the capacity to see how individual biography is shaped by historical structure. The unemployed individual in 1933 is not a personal failure but a casualty of a system-level crisis. The pedagogical pay-off is enormous; students arrive convinced that life-outcomes are personal and leave able to read the structural conditioning.

The Millsian limit. The first-year version is *descriptive*. It teaches you to see what *is* — the structural conditioning of the present, the contingency of what appears natural. By third year, the descriptive move is not enough. The student who sees that unemployment is structurally produced has not yet imagined what an unemployment-free society would look like, what it would cost, or what its trade-offs would be.

The lecture's Beauvoirian alternative. Halewood revives the concept via Beauvoir: she "tried to understand the role and status of 'woman' and 'women' in the 20th century. To do so, she imagined what the world and social theory would look like if it took women seriously." The verb is *imagined*. Beauvoir did not just describe the structural conditioning of women's lives; she pictured what social theory would have looked like with women as a foundational category from the start. The result — *The Second Sex* (1949) — is a work of generative-counterfactual sociology that re-imagined the conceptual apparatus. The lecture is explicit: "we need to use our imagination […] to think about what the world would (or could) be like if we thought and acted differently."

**Example 1 — race in sociology.** Millsian: see how individual racialised experience is conditioned by structural racism. Necessary. Beauvoirian: imagine what sociology would look like if race had been a foundational category from the start, alongside class. The lecture's Du Bois counterfactual is exactly this. Once imagined, the gaps in the standard story (no race chapter in Weber's *Economy and Society*, none in Durkheim's *Division of Labour*) become legible as constitutive omissions, not gaps in coverage.

**Example 2 — animals.** The autumn-term L3 (Carter & Charles, animals) is a Beauvoirian exercise in disguise. The Millsian move describes how individual human relations with animals are structured by industrial agriculture. The Beauvoirian move imagines what sociology would look like if non-human animals were taken seriously as social actors. Different syllabi.

**Example 3 — non-bifurcated sociology.** Halewood's bifurcation thesis depends on the strong imagination. Six bifurcations become visible only when we *picture* what a non-bifurcated sociology would look like. The bifurcation, like the gulf, is invisible from inside.

**Example 4 — generative AI.** A first-year Millsian move describes how individual workers are structurally affected by AI deployment. A third-year Beauvoirian move imagines what social theory would look like if it took computational systems as social actors from the start, not as tools deployed by human actors. Different research programme.

Why the shift matters. First, it changes what counts as a finished essay. A Millsian essay closes with "see how this is structurally conditioned." A Beauvoirian essay closes with "see how the discipline would look if it had taken X seriously from the start." The Beauvoirian close is generative; it points to a research programme. Second, the shift is what the discipline's reformers (Du Bois, Beauvoir, Halewood, Carter & Charles) all did. To imitate them at third year, students must do the same kind of work, not just summarise its results.

A counter-argument: the strong sociological imagination risks *utopianism* — speculation about what *could be* without analysis of what *is*. I accept the warning but reject the dichotomy. Beauvoir's *Second Sex* is not utopian; it is densely descriptive while simultaneously generative. The strong imagination is the imaginative supplement to analysis, not its replacement. Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* XI applies: "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 158).

A second counter-argument: third-year students might lack the *empirical mastery* to do generative-counterfactual work responsibly; the strong imagination might be a senior researcher's prerogative. I disagree. The course-design (a third-year *Current Disputes* module) presupposes students can do this work. The point of third year is to *learn* the strong move, with whatever empirical resources one has.

In conclusion, the sociological imagination at third year is the strong, Beauvoirian, generative-counterfactual move: picture what social theory would look like if a previously excluded category were taken seriously from the start. The Millsian descriptive-linking move remains in the toolkit but is no longer the centre. The lecture's instruction is precise: imagine differently. The cheat-sheet payoff: every third-year essay should close with an imagined alternative configuration of the discipline, not just a diagnosis of the present.

### M15: Compare and contrast Durkheim's living-cell analogy in *The Rules of Sociological Method* with Marx's butyric-acid/propyl-formate analogy in *Capital*. What does each do for its respective sociology?

I argue that Durkheim's living-cell analogy and Marx's butyric-acid/propyl-formate analogy do the *same* fundamental work — they assert that the manner of association is the proper sociological object — but they are deployed against *different* opponents and they license *different* downstream sociologies. My thesis is that Halewood is right to read the two analogies together as evidence of the canon's shared adverbial commitment, but that the differences in their polemical deployment matter for what each tradition can do.

The two analogies. **Durkheim:** "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (*Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128). **Marx:** "butyric acid is a different substance from propyl formate. Yet both are made up of the same chemical substances, carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O). Moreover, these substances are combined together in the same proportions in each case, namely C4H8O2 […] their different physical forms would be neglected" (*Capital* vol. 1, in *CWME* 35, p. 33).

**Compare — the logical structure.** Both make the same point: same components, different arrangement, different thing. The cell has the same molecules as non-cellular matter; what differs is the arrangement. Butyric acid and propyl formate have the same atomic composition; what differs is the manner of association of the atoms. Both writers use chemistry to break with substance-thinking in favour of arrangement-thinking — the adverbial commitment Halewood traces through Part 1 of *Rethinking the Social*. Both ground sociology in a *continuity* with natural science rather than a separation from it: Durkheim explicitly (sociology abstracts an aspect of nature as biology abstracts life), Marx implicitly (historical-material analysis as a science of arrangements). Both reject the neo-Kantian bifurcation Weber will later try to manage.

**Contrast — the polemical target.** Durkheim's target is the methodological individualist (and, behind him, the Cartesian-Lockean individualism grounding liberal political theory). The cell analogy underwrites the doctrine that *social facts must be explained by social facts*: you cannot reduce the cell to molecules, you cannot reduce the social fact to individual psychology. Marx's target is the abstract value-theory of classical political economy — Ricardo's treatment of commodity and wage as homogeneous quantities. The chemistry analogy underwrites Marx's argument that the same atoms (labour, commodity, exchange) produce different societies depending on arrangement. Capitalist labour is not feudal labour is not slave labour; the manner of association is what differs.

**Contrast — what each licenses downstream.** Durkheim's cell analogy licenses a *stability-and-integration* sociology: the cell stays alive only if the molecules continue to associate in the constitutive manner; disruption means death. Sociology, on the cell-analogy, becomes the study of what keeps the manner of association stable — norms, rituals, the collective conscience. Marx's chemistry doublet licenses a *transformation-and-historical-specificity* sociology: the two molecules are interchangeable atomic configurations, so the same atoms can rearrange themselves into either. Sociology becomes the study of how arrangements change — feudal to capitalist labour, slave to wage labour. Durkheim's analogy implies that the manner is *necessary*; Marx's implies it is *contingent*. The contrast maps onto the standard Durkheim-Marx difference (order vs. conflict) at the level of the analogy itself.

**Convergence on what they refuse.** Both refuse the nineteenth-century opposition between materialism and idealism, the methodological individualism of the liberal canon, and the bifurcation between natural and social science that Weber later tried to manage. The convergence is what makes Halewood read them together as evidence of the canon's shared adverbial commitment.

A counter-argument: the analogies are merely decorative — Durkheim's real sociology lives in the *Rules* and the empirical studies, Marx's in the analysis of value and capital, and the chemistry vignettes are pedagogical sweeteners. I disagree. The analogies are *conceptual machines* that authorise the move from substance to arrangement that the rest of the sociology assumes. Without the cell analogy, Durkheim cannot ground the claim that the social is a separate level of analysis. Without the chemistry analogy, Marx cannot ground the claim that capitalist relations are historically specific rather than eternal.

A second counter-argument: chemistry analogies risk smuggling positivism into social theory. The reply, with Halewood, is that modern chemistry (and modern biology) is itself adverbial — it pays attention to arrangement, not just substance. The analogies do not import a positivist commitment to laws-of-society; they import an adverbial commitment shared by all sciences sensitive to arrangement.

In conclusion, Durkheim's living-cell analogy and Marx's butyric-acid/propyl-formate analogy do the same fundamental conceptual work — they ground sociology in the manner of association rather than in substance — and differ in polemical deployment (against individualism for Durkheim, against abstract value-theory for Marx) and in downstream modality (stability for Durkheim, transformation for Marx). For the sociology of modernity, the analogies are a reminder that we already have the conceptual tools to analyse modern societies as historically specific arrangements, and that the canon's adverbial commitment was there before twentieth-century sociology forgot it.
