## 3. Information from the readings

### Halewood Part 1 — Durkheim, Marx, Weber (and Simmel) Revisited

Halewood's overarching claim is that **"the problem of the social" is unfinished business**. The classical canon did not deliver a settled definition of "society" or "the social"; rather, each of Durkheim, Marx and Weber struggled with the concept in different registers, and these struggles have been forgotten by twentieth-century sociology, which now uses "social" as if it were transparent.

**Durkheim.** The English title *The Division of Labour in Society* misleads — the French (*De la division du travail social*) puts the adjective on labour, not on a noun "society." Durkheim's question is not "what is society?" but "what makes us *solidaire*?" He refuses any bifurcation between the social and the natural: *the social is natural*. This is what makes a science of sociology possible — sociology abstracts an aspect of nature in the same way physics or biology does. Solidarity is not a biological instinct (against Parsons), but the manner of association within nature. Yet Durkheim's account rests, ultimately, on collective morality — sociality is grounded in the punishment of contraventions of a collective. The argument circles: sociability is a product of social life, but social life is grounded in collective morality, which is itself a manner of associating. Durkheim "should be approached with caution but also with admiration" — admired for refusing to take the social as given.

**Marx.** Marx provides no clean definition of "society" (Halewood: he was not a "professional sociologist"). He preserves two German adjectives — *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich* — and uses them in tension. Halewood translates *gesellschaftlich* as "societal" and *sozial* as "social." Capitalism is the predominant *societal* form, but "social" relations of prestige and esteem both reinforce and exceed it. The point: *nothing is settled*. Marx's chemistry analogy — butyric acid vs. propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement — makes the same point Durkheim makes about the living cell: *the manner of association* gives a thing its specificity. Marx's task is to historicise abstract categories ("civil society," "society in general") rather than to provide his own essentialist alternative.

**Weber.** Weber, alone of the three, gave a *precise* concept: only three things are properly "social" — social action, social relationships (*Beziehungen* not *Verhältnisse*), and social selection. Weber refuses to talk loosely of "social conditions" or "social factors" — that would dilute the concept. A common language does not make a community; only the actively shared consciousness that members of the group have this in common, oriented to others, makes a *Vergemeinschaftung* or *Vergesellschaftung*. This precision has a cost: no general "society" hides behind action. Notably, Weber comes to this only at the end of his life (1919-20) — proof that the battle for the concept was still alive in classical sociology.

**Simmel.** Society "is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*" (Simmel 1950, 4). It is the outcome of dyadic and triadic relations — Simmel's example of the secret: a secret is only a secret in relation to at least one other. Form, not content, is the proper object of sociology — hence "Formal Sociology" / "Sociology of Forms." *Vergesellschaftung* (sociation) is a noun made out of a process. Simmel sides with Weber against Durkheim in privileging *groups* over *totalities*.

**Conclusion to Part 1.** The classical canon converges on one point: **association is what matters; the manner, the adverb, is the social.** None of the four ever decided the concept once and for all. To reject "traditional" concepts of the social (as Latour does) is therefore premature.

### Halewood Part 2 — Whitehead on sociology, societies and the social

**Whitehead's "sociology."** Whitehead, the philosopher, splits two dyads carefully: *society/social* (metaphysical) vs. *sociology/sociological* (the study of human societies). He uses "sociology" in Adventures of Ideas only three times, but consistently to mean an attention to *how* humans were made human, not *what* they essentially are. The shift from "what" to "how" repeats the adverbial theme from Part 1.

**Ideas are things.** Whitehead's striking move (highlighted by Stengers): "I propose to consider critically the *sort* of history which ideas can have in the life of humanity." Ideas are not the creations of humans, nor are they outside humans; they have *adventures* and humans are the vehicles. Sociology, on this reading, is the study of how ideas combine with humanity in different epochs. Whitehead is "pre-Kantian" — he does not start from the question of how concepts relate to reality.

**Apes, humans, humanity.** "Clear, conscious discrimination is an accident of human existence. It makes us human. But it does not make us exist. It is of the essence of our humanity. But it is an accident of our existence" (1938, 158). Conscious discrimination converts "ape-ity" into "humanity." Imagine, Whitehead suggests, what Kant would have written had he read Darwin — human reason as a *kind of animal reason*, situated within nature.

**The order of nature, social order.** Whitehead's account of "societies" begins with *order* and *disorder* as correlative (not binary). Order is always a specific order, never order in the abstract. A society for Whitehead is *any* nexus of actual entities that share a common element of form and reproduce that form through their prehensions of each other. A hot stone is a society: its component parts "feel themselves hotly." This metaphysical move dissolves the bifurcation between natural and social — the social *is* the order of nature.

**Sociality as likeness.** Members of a society are alike because of their common character; differences between societies are differences between *manners of association*. Solidarity, on Whitehead's account, does not require a Durkheimian totality — it arises in each occasion of mutual prehension. "Each task of creation is a social effort" (Whitehead 1978, 223).

**Conclusion.** Sociological theory must begin from process. "No static maintenance of perfection is possible. Advance or Decadence are the only choices offered" (Whitehead 1933, 353-354). Social theory must be willing to wreck itself in order to advance: "the art of free society consists […] in fearlessness of revision" (Whitehead 1927, 88).

## 4. Critical analysis with key quotes

The main argumentative thread of the L1 reading and lecture is that **modernity is constituted by a series of bifurcations that simultaneously enable and disable thought**, and that critical sociology must work *adverbially* — on the *manner* of association — rather than essentially.

**Main tension 1 — Modernity's two faces.** Halewood and the lecture insist that the same period that produced Shakespeare, Luther, and "the modern organization of industry" produced the trans-Atlantic slave trade. Du Bois forces the recognition that these are not separable; they are constitutive of one another.

> "For four hundred years, from 1450 to 1850, European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings of such tremendous proportions that the physical, economic and moral effects are still plainly to be remarked throughout the world." — Du Bois, *The Negro* (1915), pp. 89-90

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." — Marx, *Selected Writings* (ed. McLellan), p. 203

**Main tension 2 — Critique vs criticism.** The lecture distinguishes finding fault from evaluating structure and pointing to alternatives. Marx's slogan that the point is to *change* the world makes critique inseparable from a future-oriented practice.

> "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it." — Marx, *Theses on Feuerbach*, in *Selected Writings*, p. 158

**Main tension 3 — Bifurcation as the cornerstone of modernity.** Halewood's signature thesis. Modern thought operates by splitting fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities. Once split, a *gulf* opens up.

> "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it." — Whitehead, *Adventures of Ideas* (1933), p. 217

**Main tension 4 — The problem of the social.** Halewood's reading of the canon shows that "social" was never a settled term; classical theory was *a contest over the concept*.

> "It is now no accident that the term 'social' [des Sozialen] which seems to have a quite general meaning, turns out to have, as soon as one carefully examines its application, a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity." — Weber, in Halewood (2014), p. 131 [VERIFY p.? — Weber 1949 [1904], 68]

> "Society' 'is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*. It does not exist outside and in addition to the individuals and the processes among them." — Simmel, *The Sociology of Georg Simmel* (1950), p. 4

> "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life." — Durkheim, *Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128

**Main tension 5 — Adverbial sociology.** Whitehead's "ideas are things" move runs alongside Marx's chemistry analogy, Durkheim's living-cell analogy, and Simmel's sociation. All four insist that the *how* — the manner, the form, the mode of prehension — is what is social.

> "The foundation of all understanding of sociological theory — that is to say, of all understanding of human life — is that no static maintenance of perfection is possible. […] The very essence of real actuality — that is, of the completely real, is *process*." — Whitehead, *Adventures of Ideas* (1933), pp. 353-354

> "It is the first step in sociological wisdom, to recognize that the major advances in civilization are processes which all but wreck the societies in which they occur […] The art of free society consists […] in fearlessness of revision." — Whitehead, *Symbolism* (1927), p. 88

**Critique of the canon.** Halewood's deepest claim is that twentieth-century sociology has *forgotten* the open-endedness of the founders. Parsons assumes social order is self-explanatory; Latour wants to abolish the social altogether. Both are wrong — for Halewood, the live question is to *reconstruct* a philosophy of the social that retains the canon's critical insights while breaking out of its bifurcations.

## 7. Essay answers

### 2025 (Q3): 'The fact-value distinction is a cornerstone of modernity. It is also one of its major problems.' Critically assess this statement.

I argue that the fact–value distinction is indeed a cornerstone of modernity, but it is a cornerstone in the architectural sense Halewood draws from Whitehead: the cornerstone holds the building up while at the same time *fixing* the bifurcations that the building requires. The distinction makes possible the modern sciences, the modern administration, the modern university — and at the same time it produces the gulfs into which modern thought repeatedly falls. My thesis is that the distinction is a *real* abstraction (an idea that does work in the world) but a *bad* one, and that a serious sociology of modernity must abandon the metaphysics of bifurcation while retaining the *practical* distinction between describing and judging.

The first move is to recognise where the distinction comes from. In the German neo-Kantian tradition that shaped Weber and Simmel — through Dilthey, Rickert and others — the separation of the "natural sciences" (concerned with causal explanation of fact) from the "human sciences" (concerned with meaning and value) was framed as a methodological necessity. Weber radicalised this into the doctrine of *Wertfreiheit* — value-freedom in social science. Halewood's account in *Rethinking the Social* shows that this elegant methodological apparatus is also an *ontological* bifurcation: it presupposes that the world really does come in two kinds, facts and values, and that sociology must straddle the gap. Whitehead's worry applies directly: "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (*Adventures of Ideas*, p. 217). Once you split fact from value you cannot get the two halves back together without smuggling.

Second, the fact–value distinction is a cornerstone in the more obvious sense: without it the modern division of intellectual labour collapses. Economists report facts, philosophers debate values. Public policy is supposed to operate on the "facts" while leaving "values" to democratic choice. The university is organised on the same principle: STEM versus humanities, science versus humanities — this is one of the six bifurcations Halewood lists in his lecture. The colonial archive that Howard French reconstructs in *Born in Blackness* is full of "facts" — tonnes of gold shipped, percentages of enslaved people sent to Brazil — that the modernist convention asks us to evaluate later, as a question of "values." Du Bois's 1915 passage refuses this: the slave trade is both "the most inexcusable and despicable blot on modern human history" *and* the factual condition of "the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). The factual and the evaluative are one description.

Third, the distinction is "one of its major problems" because it produces the kind of sociology that *cannot see* its own conditions of possibility. If you accept the value-free description of modernity as European rationalisation, slavery becomes an external "value" question — to be tacked on, lamented, regretted — rather than constitutive. Halewood's revision-notes-friendly reading of the canon is precisely that critical sociology must refuse this move. Marx already saw this when he wrote that "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). The fact of cotton and the value of human beings are not separable in the structure of industrial modernity.

The counter-argument is a Weberian one: without some version of the distinction, sociology cannot be a *science*. If we let our values infect our descriptions, we get propaganda. Weber's response in his methodological essays — that the choice of *which* facts matter is value-laden but the description of those facts must be controlled — is the most honest version of the position. I take this seriously. My own position is not that all descriptions are evaluations but that the *clean* metaphysical split between the two is unsustainable. In practice we move adverbially, in Halewood's sense: we describe *how* something happens (the manner of association) and the description is already loaded with evaluative implication. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is a fact; its standard art-historical interpretation that only "briefly mentions slavery" is a value-laden silence. Both descriptions are factual; one is better than the other.

In conclusion, the fact–value distinction is a cornerstone of modernity in the strong sense — it *holds up* a whole way of organising thought and institutions. It is also a major problem because, once cemented, it produces gulfs in which constitutive features of modernity (especially racial slavery and coloniality) are exiled to the "value" side and quietly excised from the "factual" account of modern industry. The way forward, on Halewood's reading, is not to abolish the practical distinction between describing and judging — that would be naive — but to refuse the metaphysical bifurcation. We should *critique* (in the lecture's strong sense — evaluate the structure, suggest alternatives) rather than merely *criticise*. Sociology after the fact–value distinction is the sociology that takes both Du Bois and Weber seriously at once, refusing to let either be filed away on the further side of a gulf.

### 2023 (Q3): Why has the distinction between "facts" and "values" been problematic for sociology?

I argue that the distinction between facts and values has been problematic for sociology in three interlocking ways: it imports a neo-Kantian metaphysics that the founders themselves had not fully digested; it produces a methodological neurosis that disables critique; and, most importantly, it makes constitutive features of modernity (particularly coloniality) appear as external "value" questions when in fact they are factual conditions of the modern world. My thesis, following Halewood's account of bifurcation, is that the problem is not the practical contrast between describing and judging but the *metaphysical* split that hardens that contrast into two separate realms.

The first dimension of the problem is genealogical. As Halewood traces in *Rethinking the Social*, Weber inherited the fact–value distinction from neo-Kantianism — Rickert and Dilthey, in particular — for whom the *Geisteswissenschaften* (human sciences) had to be distinguished from the *Naturwissenschaften* by reference to *meaning* and *value*, not by reference to facts. Sociology as a science (*Wissenschaft*) was supposed to navigate this divide. The problem is that Weber's own concept of "social action," which Halewood reconstructs carefully in Chapter 7, is in fact *both* descriptive *and* evaluative — it requires that the actor orient her behaviour to others in a way that means something. Weber knew the term "social" was problematic: "It is now no accident that the term 'social' […] turns out to have […] a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity" [VERIFY p.? — Weber 1949 [1904], 68]. The fact–value distinction was supposed to solve this; instead, it preserved it.

The second dimension is methodological. The doctrine of *Wertfreiheit* — value-freedom — was supposed to discipline the sociologist so that her descriptions would be unaffected by her commitments. In practice, the disciplining works in only one direction: it polices the appearance of evaluation in the text while leaving the *selection* of facts (which questions, which populations, which periods) entirely up to the prior values of the discipline. This is why, as the lecture emphasises, the standard sociological account of modernity for most of the twentieth century was a story of European industrialisation that mentioned slavery, if at all, in passing. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is the perfect example: its smarthistory.org interpretation "only briefly mentions slavery and does not seem to notice that West Africa had enormous resources." The "facts" of the map are reported; the values that selected those facts are invisible.

The third and deepest dimension is the substantive one. The fact–value distinction makes modernity's coloniality look like a "value" question that can be added in or left out. Du Bois's 1915 passage refuses this: the trans-Atlantic slave trade is *the* fact of modern industry, not an ethical regret. Marx put it bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) gives the empirical scaffolding: 600 kg of gold per year from West Africa by the 1490s; the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar published in 1629; 40% of all plantation-enslaved people ending up in Brazil. These are not values; they are facts. But they are facts that change what "modernity" *is* as a description.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously is the Weberian one — that without some version of the fact–value distinction sociology collapses into political pamphleteering. I accept the force of this. The point of the critique is not to dissolve all difference between describing and judging but to refuse the *bifurcation* — what Halewood calls, drawing on Whitehead, the splitting of one thing into two branches such that "a gulf is created" (Whitehead 1933, p. 217). The practical distinction between an empirical claim and a normative claim is workable; the metaphysical claim that there are two separate realms is not.

In conclusion, the fact–value distinction has been problematic for sociology because it carries an unowned neo-Kantian metaphysics, it produces a one-sided methodological discipline, and — most importantly — it systematically obscures the coloniality of modernity by treating slavery and racial extraction as "value" questions rather than as factual constituents of the modern world system. The remedy is the move Halewood recommends throughout his book: refuse the bifurcation, work adverbially on the *manner* of modern association, and recover the founders' (Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel) shared insight that the social is what happens between things, not a layer hiding behind them. Critique, in the strong lecture-sense, is what replaces the falsely modest value-free description.

### 2022 (Q1): Is Sociology a science? If so, what kind of science is it?

I argue that sociology is a science, but only if we are willing to revise what we mean by "science" — specifically, to abandon the bifurcation between the natural sciences and the human sciences that has shaped the question since the neo-Kantians. My thesis, drawing on Halewood's Whiteheadian reading of Durkheim, is that sociology is a *natural science of the manner of association* — its objects are real, its claims are correctable, but its method is adverbial rather than nomothetic in the physics-envy sense.

The first move is to take Durkheim seriously. Durkheim was clear that sociology had to put itself "in the same frame of mind as scientists" — but his deeper claim, as Halewood emphasises in Chapter 7 of *Rethinking the Social*, is that **the social is natural**. "If the social 'world' were not a part of nature then it would be impossible to treat it scientifically" (Halewood 2014, p. 124). The familiar twentieth-century picture in which the social is a *separate realm* from the natural is, on this reading, a Parsonian importation, not a Durkheimian inheritance. For Durkheim, sociology is a science because it abstracts an aspect of nature — *the manner of association* — just as biology and chemistry abstract other aspects. Durkheim's example of the living cell is instructive: "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (*Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128).

The second move is to draw the same lesson from Marx. Marx's chemistry analogy — butyric acid and propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement — makes the same point. Sociology, like chemistry, studies how the *arrangement* gives rise to the specific properties. Marx is therefore committed to a science of society in the strong sense, but a science whose objects are *historically* specific manners of association rather than ahistorical laws.

The third move is Weberian — and here we hit the difficulty. Weber's sociology is also a *Wissenschaft*, but Weber introduces the requirement of *Verstehen* — understanding the meaning that actors give to their actions. This appears to bifurcate sociology from physics: the meanings of actors are not on the same plane as the velocities of particles. The standard neo-Kantian reading concludes that sociology is a *different kind* of science — a "human science." Halewood resists this. He shows that Weber's "social action" is in fact a *very precise* concept — narrower than is usually thought — and that it is compatible with the recognition that animals can engage in social action too. The bifurcation between human meaning and natural cause is not as clean as the neo-Kantians wanted.

A fourth move comes from Whitehead. In Halewood's reading of *Process and Reality*, "society" is the name for any nexus of actual entities that share a common manner of prehension. A hot stone is a society. So is the British Sociological Association. Sociology, in this expanded sense, is the study of how societies — at any scale — manage to endure. This puts sociology back into continuity with the natural sciences without reducing it to them.

A counter-argument worth pressing is the postmodern one: sociology should *not* be a science, because the scientific ideal is itself a colonial-Eurocentric one that excludes other ways of knowing. I take this seriously, particularly in the light of Du Bois and the rethinking-the-canon move that the lecture flags. But I think the right response is not to abandon science — it is to *expand* what counts as science to include the adverbial, historical, and critical work that the canonical reformers (Du Bois, Simmel, Dussel) were already doing. Du Bois's 1915 description of the trans-Atlantic slave trade is a scientific description; it gathers facts, situates them in a system of causes, and produces a falsifiable account of how modern industry came to be.

In conclusion, sociology is a science — not in the imitative sense of physics-envy but in the Durkheimian sense of a discipline that abstracts a real aspect of nature: the manner of association by which entities (human and non-human) endure. Its method is adverbial; its claims are about *how* the social comes to be, not about what it ultimately *is*. It is a critical science in the lecture-sense — its job is not just to find faults but to evaluate the structure of association and to suggest alternatives. To answer the second half of the question: sociology is the science of association, the kind of science that takes Marx's "the point is to change it" as a methodological imperative as well as a political one.

### 2022 (Q2): How have the ideas of neo-Kantians influenced sociology?

I argue that the neo-Kantians (Rickert, Windelband, Dilthey) shaped twentieth-century sociology far more deeply than is usually acknowledged, and that their influence is double-edged: they bequeathed the discipline its conceptual rigour (the distinction between *Naturwissenschaften* and *Geisteswissenschaften*; the methodology of *Verstehen*; the doctrine of value-relevance) but they also installed the bifurcations that, on Halewood's account, generate the recurrent crises of social theory. My thesis is that the neo-Kantian inheritance is unavoidable but must be critically reconstructed rather than uncritically accepted.

The first dimension is the methodological one. Through Weber and Simmel — both of whom worked in the neo-Kantian intellectual climate of late-nineteenth-century Germany — sociology inherited the conviction that the social sciences are concerned with *meaning* and *value* in a way the natural sciences are not. Halewood notes in Chapter 8 of *Rethinking the Social* that "the specific version of German Idealist neo-Kantianism […] influenced Weber and Simmel." Weber's whole apparatus — ideal types, *Verstehen*, value-relevance, value-freedom — is unintelligible without this background. Likewise, Simmel's "Formal Sociology" or "Sociology of Forms" picks up the Kantian language of form against content.

The second dimension is the substantive one. The neo-Kantians installed the **fact–value distinction** at the heart of sociology. As I argued in the previous essay, this distinction is one of the cornerstones of modern thought — and one of its major problems. It produces the methodological neurosis of value-freedom; it generates the gulf between the description of the modern world and the moral evaluation of it; and it makes constitutive features of modernity such as racial slavery appear as "value" addenda rather than as facts of the system.

The third dimension is the *bifurcation* that Halewood specifically diagnoses. The neo-Kantian split between nomothetic (law-seeking) and idiographic (case-specific) sciences hardens into the modern split between sociology-as-science and sociology-as-cultural-criticism. The lecture lists six modern bifurcations — fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities — and the neo-Kantian inheritance is most clearly visible in the first and the last. Once you accept that science and humanities are two different kinds of knowing, you are committed to the second-order bifurcation that splits hard sociology (quantitative, causal) from soft sociology (interpretive, cultural). Halewood, drawing on Whitehead's pre-Kantian metaphysics, argues that this split is unsustainable: "the social is natural" (in Durkheim's sense, recovered against Parsons).

A fourth, more diffuse, dimension is the influence on the canon itself. Halewood is explicit that "the ideas of Kant and Hegel stretched into classical social theory through the various filters of writers such as Dilthey and Rickert, among others" (Chapter 8). The very category of "the social" — *des Sozialen* — that Weber problematised carries Kantian residues. The Durkheimian concept of "collective representations" is closer to Kant's transcendental categories than is usually admitted.

The most important counter-argument is that the neo-Kantian inheritance is precisely what *gave sociology its identity*. Without Rickert and Dilthey, Weber's account of meaningful action does not exist; without that account, the Frankfurt School, ethnomethodology, symbolic interactionism, and the cultural sociology of the spring term of this very module are all unintelligible. I take this seriously. The point of critique, in the lecture's strong sense, is not to abolish the neo-Kantian inheritance but to evaluate its structure and to suggest improvements. Halewood's project is exactly this: he wants to keep what is alive in Weber's precision and Simmel's "Sociology of Forms" while jettisoning the metaphysical bifurcation that the neo-Kantians smuggled in.

In conclusion, the neo-Kantians have influenced sociology in three main ways: they bequeathed the methodological apparatus of meaning-oriented social science (*Verstehen*, ideal types, value-relevance); they installed the fact–value distinction as a cornerstone of the discipline; and they shaped the canon through the channels of Weber and Simmel. The first contribution remains valuable, the second is the source of recurring crisis, and the third has had to be supplemented by recovering thinkers (Du Bois, Dussel, Whitehead) who were never inside the neo-Kantian conversation. To answer the question properly is therefore not just to *describe* the influence but to *critique* it — in the lecture's strong sense — and to point to the alternatives that a non-bifurcated philosophy of the social would open up.

### M1: To what extent is modernity best understood as a colonial project rather than as European rationalisation?

I argue that modernity is best understood as a *colonial project that disguises itself as European rationalisation* — that is, the two formulations are not alternatives but layers, with the rationalisation-narrative functioning as the ideology of the colonial-project reality. My thesis follows the lecture's reading of Du Bois, French and Dussel: the very dates and institutions that the standard story uses to define modernity (1500s onwards; the rise of industry; the universities; the public sphere) are saturated with the colonial relation. To narrate modernity as rationalisation alone is to participate in the myth of progress that the colonial project produces.

The first move is empirical. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* (2021), summarised in the lecture, shows that by the 1490s — *before* anything we would usually call the European Enlightenment — Portugal was shipping nearly 600 kg of gold per year from West Africa, using Indian cotton to buy that gold, and the resulting coinage to lubricate European commerce. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 already maps a world organised around coasts, oceans, and connections — not around the introspective European subject. Plantation slavery began in Madeira and São Tomé/Príncipe in the early 1500s. By 1629 a Kimbundu-Spanish grammar was being printed for use in Bolivia and Peru. By the eighteenth century, 40% of all plantation-enslaved people had gone from Africa to Brazil. The "rationalisation" of modern industry is conceptually downstream from this colonial infrastructure.

The second move is conceptual. Marx, who is usually mobilised as the theorist of European industrialisation, says the opposite: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203). The chain of conditions runs slavery → cotton → industry, not industry → contact-with-other-peoples-as-an-afterthought.

The third move is critical-archival. Du Bois's 1915 passage forces us to hold two things in mind at once: "These were not days of decadence, but a period that gave the world Shakespeare, Martin Luther, and Raphael […] In the midst of this advance and uplift this slave trade and slavery spread more human misery, inculcated more disrespect for and neglect of humanity […] than can well be calculated" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). The standard rationalisation story is *not wrong about the facts* — Shakespeare and Luther did exist — but it is wrong about *which facts go together*. Du Bois's intervention is to refuse the bifurcation that puts "civilisation" and "slavery" on different sides of a gulf.

The fourth move is the one the lecture flags as central: Halewood's claim that modernity is structured by *bifurcations*. The European rationalisation narrative is itself a bifurcation — it splits the modern world into a European inside (rationality, industry, science) and a non-European outside (raw materials, labour, the not-yet-modern). Howard French's project, on the lecture's reading, is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to change, to add to, to invert the current view." This is critique in the strong sense the lecture identifies: not just finding fault, but evaluating the structure and pointing to alternatives.

A serious counter-argument: it might be said that European rationalisation has *internal* features (the printing press, double-entry bookkeeping, the scientific method, the legal-rational state) that cannot be reduced to colonial extraction. I take this seriously. My own position is not that the rationalisation features are *unreal* — they exist and they matter — but that they are constitutively entangled with the colonial project rather than independent of it. The Bank of England, the East India Company, the modern insurance industry, the modern actuarial science — all of these are *both* rationalisation *and* coloniality. To narrate them as rationalisation alone is to perform the very ideological move that the lecture identifies as the myth of progress.

In conclusion, modernity is best understood as a colonial project rather than as European rationalisation *if we are forced to choose* — because the colonial-project framing keeps both the slave ship and Shakespeare in the picture, while the rationalisation framing keeps only Shakespeare. But the deeper move, which I prefer, is to refuse the bifurcation and to read modernity as the system in which the two are inextricable. Du Bois, French, Dussel and the lecture's Halewood agree: rethinking modernity is rethinking *how* we narrate this entanglement, in the adverbial sense that Part 1 of *Rethinking the Social* identifies as the through-line of the canon.

### M2: Critically discuss Halewood's claim that modernity is structured by bifurcation. Use at least two examples of bifurcation.

I argue that Halewood's claim is correct in its diagnostic part — modernity *is* structured by a series of bifurcations that produce conceptual and political gulfs — but that the claim becomes more powerful if we follow Halewood through to his Whiteheadian alternative: an adverbial, processual sociology that refuses to start from binaries. My thesis is that bifurcation is not just an *idea* about modernity but an active organising principle of modern institutions, and that the way out is the recovery of what Halewood calls "the manner of association."

The diagnostic claim. In the slides of Lecture 1, Halewood lists six paradigmatic bifurcations: fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, and science/humanities. Each splits a single phenomenon into two branches and creates "a gulf." The slide quotes Whitehead: "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (Whitehead 1933, p. 217). This is not a metaphorical observation — Halewood's whole programme in *Rethinking the Social* is that the gulf has *epistemic consequences*: once you can no longer see across, you stop asking how the two sides interact, and the bifurcation becomes self-sealing.

**Example 1 — sex/gender.** The first bifurcation Halewood flags. Modern feminist theory inherited the distinction between "sex" (biological, fixed, factual) and "gender" (social, constructed, value-laden) as a critical tool. The distinction did important work — it allowed second-wave feminism to argue that the subordination of women was *not* a natural fact but a social construction. But, as Halewood notes in his lecture and as the spring-term feminist theory weeks elaborate, the distinction also produced a gulf. It separated the "factual" body from the "social" identity, leaving the body itself unanalysed and producing the now-familiar set of problems about trans, intersex, and racialised bodies that the sex/gender distinction cannot accommodate. Halewood's diagnostic point is that this is not a *failure* of feminist theory but a *symptom* of the bifurcation it inherited from modernity.

**Example 2 — fact/value.** This is the cornerstone bifurcation, inherited via the neo-Kantians and crystallised in Weber's doctrine of *Wertfreiheit*. The 2025 and 2023 exam questions are both about this. As I argued in those answers, the fact–value distinction makes possible the modern division of intellectual labour (economists report facts, philosophers debate values) and at the same time produces the silence about slavery that Du Bois names. The gulf is real and it has consequences: most modern accounts of "the rise of capitalism" are accounts of facts that are silent about the value-laden selection of which facts to report.

**Example 3 — language/world.** Halewood's own first book, *Language and Process*, is dedicated to this bifurcation. The modern philosophy of language (from Frege onwards) splits the linguistic representation from the represented world. The political consequences — that language is treated as a "veil" over reality rather than as a way of organising reality — show up in everything from the gender-neutral pronoun debate to the politics of indigenous languages erased by colonial education.

The deeper claim — and the one that does most of the critical work in Halewood's project — is that bifurcation is itself a bifurcation. The modernist habit of splitting one thing into two is not natural; it is the product of a particular intellectual genealogy (broadly Cartesian-Kantian) and a particular set of institutional needs (the modern university, the modern state, the modern colonial archive). The recovery of the canon's adverbial language — Durkheim's "manner of association," Marx's "gesellschaftlich-," Simmel's "form," Whitehead's "mode of prehension" — is Halewood's alternative.

A counter-argument: bifurcations might be *useful simplifications* that we cannot do without. To run a hospital you need to distinguish facts (the patient's blood pressure) from values (whether to continue treatment). To run a university you need to distinguish the science library from the humanities library. I take this seriously. The point of the critique is not to abolish *practical* contrasts but to refuse *metaphysical* bifurcations — to use the contrast adverbially (fact-ish-ness and value-ish-ness as modes of one description) rather than as a wall.

In conclusion, Halewood's claim that modernity is structured by bifurcation is correct, and it generates the most productive critical move in the lecture: the move from criticism to critique, from finding fault with each bifurcation to evaluating the structural commitment that produces all of them. The two examples I have used — sex/gender and fact/value — both show the same pattern: bifurcations enable critical work for one generation and disable it for the next. The way forward is the adverbial sociology that Part 2 of *Rethinking the Social* outlines: a sociology in which the social is the manner of association, not a noun behind the action.

### M3: Compare Du Bois's and Marx's accounts of the relationship between slavery and modern industry.

I argue that Du Bois and Marx converge on the substantive claim — slavery is constitutive of modern industry — but diverge in two important ways: Du Bois insists on the moral/evaluative dimension that Marx's economistic framing tends to push to the margin, and Du Bois universalises the indictment to "modern human history" in a way Marx, focused on capitalist relations, does not. My thesis is that we need both: Marx for the structural-causal story, Du Bois for the moral-historical reckoning.

The shared claim. The two famous quotations the lecture pairs are: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203); and "European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings of such tremendous proportions […] It was the day of the greatest expansion of two of the world's most pretentious religions and of the beginnings of the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois 1915, pp. 89-90). On the *fact* of constitutive entanglement, the two writers agree.

Where they diverge — moral register. Marx's mode is structural-causal. His sentence has the form of a chain of necessary conditions: A is the precondition of B is the precondition of C. The slave trade is wrong, on the Marx of *Capital*, but the indictment is *systemic* rather than personal — capitalism as a whole is what is at fault, and slavery is one of its constitutive moments. Du Bois's register is openly moral. The 1915 passage uses words like "misery," "callousness," "disrespect," "hatred," "inexcusable," "despicable." He insists that the moral verdict cannot be deferred to later analysis: "We may excuse and palliate it, and write history so as to let men forget it; it remains the most inexcusable and despicable blot on modern human history." Marx is not interested in palliation, but he is also not particularly interested in the moral verdict — for him, the verdict follows from the analysis.

Where they diverge — scope. Marx's frame is *capitalist modernity*. Slavery matters because it is the precondition of capitalist industry. Du Bois's frame is *modern human history* as a whole. The slave trade lasts four centuries and overlaps with Shakespeare, Luther, Raphael, Haroun-al-Raschid, and Lincoln — Du Bois deliberately drops names from across the political and religious spectrum to make the point that this is *not* a story about one mode of production but about the moral fabric of the modern era. Du Bois's broader scope allows him to indict modernity as such, not just capitalism within modernity.

Where they converge — the refusal of bifurcation. Both writers refuse the fact/value bifurcation that the standard story of modern industry depends on. For Marx, the "fact" of industry is unintelligible without the "fact" of slavery; for Du Bois, the "values" of Shakespeare are inseparable from the "facts" of the slave ship. Neither will let modernity off the hook by partitioning its goods from its harms. In Halewood's terms, both work *adverbially* — they look at the manner in which modernity associates its goods with its harms, not at the goods and harms as if they were separable entities.

A counter-argument. It might be said that Marx's framing is *politically more useful* because it gives us a structural target (capitalism) rather than a moral lament (modernity as a whole). I take this seriously, but I note two things. First, Du Bois is not merely lamenting — *The Negro* and *Black Reconstruction* are concrete historical-analytical works that name actors and institutions. Second, the Marxian "structural target" has its own political problem: by locating slavery as a *moment* of capitalist development, it can be read as suggesting that the abolition of capitalism would *suffice* to repair the harms of slavery, which is an argument the reparations literature has long contested.

In conclusion, Du Bois and Marx converge in refusing to bifurcate modern industry from slavery — both make the constitutive entanglement the analytic centre — and diverge in moral register (Marx structural-causal, Du Bois morally direct) and scope (Marx capitalist modernity, Du Bois modern human history). The lecture's "rethinking the canon" move — "what would sociology be like if we had taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously all along?" — is best answered by saying: sociology would have the Marxian structural-causal account *and* the Du Boisian moral-historical reckoning, instead of having to choose. The two are complementary critiques of the same bifurcated modernity.

### M4: "Critique is not criticism." Discuss with examples drawn from the sociology of modernity.

I argue, following the lecture's direct injunction, that critique and criticism are distinct intellectual operations and that the sociology of modernity has been ill-served by their conflation. Criticism finds fault; critique evaluates the structure and points to alternatives. My thesis is that the most powerful classical and contemporary work in the sociology of modernity is critique in this strong sense, and that mistaking critique for criticism produces the disabling "negative sociology" that the lecture worries about.

The distinction laid out. The lecture is explicit: "Criticise" means to say what is wrong — to find fault. "Critique" means to (i) evaluate the structure, (ii) see what works well, (iii) suggest clarifications, (iv) suggest improvements. The four points are presented as a checklist for essay and exam writing. The lecture insists: "Remember this for your essays and exam."

**Example 1 — Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach*.** "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 158). This is the canonical statement of critique-not-criticism. It does not say "the philosophers are wrong" — it says the *structure* of philosophical interpretation needs to be evaluated, and the alternative (changing the world) suggested. Marx's *Capital* is critique in exactly this sense: it evaluates the structure of capitalist exchange, identifies what works (capital accumulation, productivity gains) and what doesn't (alienation, immiseration, the cycle of crisis), and suggests an alternative (the abolition of the wage form).

**Example 2 — Du Bois on modernity.** Du Bois's 1915 passage is often read as a *moral condemnation* of modernity. On a closer reading it is critique in the strong sense. He evaluates the structure ("the modern organization of industry"); he acknowledges what worked (Shakespeare, Luther, Raphael, Lincoln); he points to the structural feature that the standard story occludes (the slave trade); and he suggests an alternative — a history that *does not let men forget*. This is not merely fault-finding; it is a programme for how modern history ought to be written.

**Example 3 — Howard French on the canon.** French's project, on the lecture's gloss, is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to change, to add to, to invert the current view." The verbs matter. "Add" alone would be criticism (the canon is wrong because it omits Africa). "Invert" is critique (the canon's structure is wrong because it makes Africa peripheral when it is constitutive). The Cantino Planisphere example — a 1502 map that *already* foregrounds the coastal-oceanic Atlantic system that European art history later forgot — is critique in action: it evaluates the structure of the standard interpretive frame and points to an alternative reading that the map itself already affords.

**Example 4 — Halewood on bifurcation.** Halewood's whole project in *Rethinking the Social* is critique in the strong sense. He does not say Durkheim, Marx, Weber are *wrong*; he says they have insights that have been forgotten and structural commitments that need re-evaluation. The bifurcation thesis is critique: it identifies the structure (modernity organises thought via splits), evaluates what works (the splits enabled certain sciences), and suggests an alternative (a Whiteheadian adverbial sociology).

A counter-argument: it might be said that the criticism/critique distinction is mere semantic gloss, and that in practice good sociology mixes both. I partly agree. The point is not that critique excludes the moments of criticism — Marx finds fault with capitalism, Du Bois finds fault with European slavers — but that the *organising frame* must be structural-evaluative rather than merely fault-finding. The lecture's worry is that a generation of students trained to "be critical" have become good at finding faults and bad at proposing alternatives.

In conclusion, critique is not criticism, and the sociology of modernity at its best — in Marx, Du Bois, French, Halewood — operates in the strong critique mode. The exam-and-essay payoff of the distinction is concrete: a critique essay always closes by *pointing somewhere* (an alternative interpretation, a different concept, a future configuration of the discipline). A criticism essay closes by *putting the writer to the side of those who saw the problem*. The lecture asks for the first.

### M5: To what extent does Whitehead's notion of "bifurcation" provide a better critical tool than Marx's notion of "ideology"?

I argue that Whitehead's "bifurcation" and Marx's "ideology" are different kinds of critical tools doing different kinds of work, and that for the diagnosis of *modernity-as-a-structure-of-thought* Whitehead's notion is the more fundamental, while for the diagnosis of *modernity-as-a-relation-of-class-power* Marx's notion remains indispensable. My thesis is that the right answer is to use them together — Whitehead at the conceptual level, Marx at the material level — and that Halewood's *Rethinking the Social* is exactly this synthesis.

The Whiteheadian tool. Bifurcation, as Whitehead introduces it in *Adventures of Ideas* and as Halewood develops it across his work, is the *conceptual* move of splitting one thing into two branches such that "a gulf is created" (Whitehead 1933, p. 217). The lecture lists six modern bifurcations. The diagnostic power of the concept is that it identifies the *form* of modern thought rather than its content. Whitehead is not telling us that fact/value or sex/gender are wrong distinctions; he is telling us that the modernist habit of producing such distinctions is itself a structural commitment that disables certain kinds of seeing.

The Marxian tool. Ideology, in Marx's sense (especially in *The German Ideology* and *Capital*), is the *systematic misrecognition* of social relations produced by the structure of class society. Workers misrecognise wage-exchange as a fair contract because the commodity form obscures the extraction of surplus value. Ideology has a material base — it arises from and serves the interests of the dominant class. Marx's tool tells us *why* certain misrecognitions persist (because they serve material interests) in a way Whitehead's does not.

Where Whitehead is sharper. Marx's tool needs a class location to identify the ideology; Whitehead's tool can identify a structural commitment without first locating the interest it serves. This is particularly useful for diagnosing bifurcations like sex/gender or science/humanities, where the "class" mapping is awkward. Whitehead's tool also has a *generative* dimension — once you see the bifurcation, you can ask what an unbifurcated description would look like. Halewood's project is exactly this generative move.

Where Marx is sharper. Whitehead's tool is *conceptual*, and it can leave you with a beautiful diagnosis of a structural commitment without any account of *why* the commitment persists. Marx forces the question: who benefits? The fact/value distinction persists not just because it is conceptually elegant but because it enables a particular division of intellectual labour in the modern university, which in turn enables a particular relation between universities, states and capital. This material-political reading is what Marx's notion of ideology contributes that Whitehead's notion does not.

Where they converge. Both Whitehead and Marx refuse the *naturalisation* of the categories they critique. For Marx, capitalism appears natural to those inside it; for Whitehead, bifurcation appears natural to those inside modernity. Both write to *denaturalise*. And both are committed to critique in the strong lecture-sense: evaluate the structure, see what works, suggest alternatives.

A counter-argument worth pressing: it might be said that bifurcation is just a fancy name for ideology, and that the comparison is therefore otiose. I disagree. Bifurcation names a *formal* feature (the splitting) that holds across different kinds of contents; ideology names a *content-specific* feature (the misrecognition produced by a specific class structure). The two tools are complementary, not equivalent.

In conclusion, Whitehead's bifurcation is the better tool for diagnosing the conceptual architecture of modernity; Marx's ideology is the better tool for diagnosing why that architecture persists materially. The lecture's "to what extent" framing wants me to pick one, but the honest answer — and the answer Halewood's whole project supports — is that the critical sociology of modernity needs both. The bifurcation analysis gets us into the structure of modern thought; the ideology analysis explains why the structure is so hard to dislodge.

### M6: Critically discuss Whitehead's claim that "ideas are things" and its implications for the sociology of modernity.

I argue that Whitehead's claim is the most counter-intuitive and the most productive move in Halewood's reading of the canon — counter-intuitive because it offends both common sense (ideas are surely *not* things) and the standard sociology of knowledge (ideas are *produced by* social positions); productive because it short-circuits the bifurcation between idealism and materialism that has dogged social theory since Hegel and Marx. My thesis is that "ideas are things" is the conceptual key to an adverbial sociology of modernity in which colonial ideas, scientific ideas, and gendered ideas can be analysed as things with their own careers, without reducing them either to mental contents or to class positions.

The claim. Halewood, citing Stengers, reports that Whitehead is asking us to realise that "ideas are things." The claim is unpacked in the long passage from *Adventures of Ideas* about ape-to-human becoming: "Fragmentary intellectual agencies co-operated blindly to turn apes into men, to turn the classic civilization into mediaeval Europe, to overwhelm the Renaissance by the Industrial Revolution. Men knew not what they did" (Whitehead 1933, p. 9). Ideas, on Whitehead's account, are not *what humans have*; they are *intellectual agencies* with their own careers, which humans serve as vehicles. Conscious discrimination is the *essence* of humanity but only the *accident* of human existence: "It makes us human. But it does not make us exist" (Whitehead 1938, p. 158).

The first implication. The sociology of modernity is *not* the history of human ideas but the history of how humanity and ideas have combined. This is a substantive reframing. The standard story of modernity is the story of the rise of certain ideas (the scientific method, the rights of man, the market) in the heads of certain Europeans. Whitehead's reframing makes those ideas *things* that travelled with sugar, gold, ships and slaves — and that combined with humans in different ways in different places. The Cantino Planisphere is one such combination; the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar published in 1629 in the Andes is another; Du Bois's 1915 essay is a third. None of these is the "creation" of a heroic European mind.

The second implication. The bifurcation between idealism (history is moved by ideas) and materialism (history is moved by material forces) is dissolved. Ideas are real, but they are not Cartesian mental contents; they are agencies in the world. This is why Halewood thinks Whitehead is more useful than Marx-against-Hegel: Whitehead's pre-Kantian metaphysics never accepted the bifurcation in the first place.

The third implication — and this is the one most directly relevant to modernity. The colonial project is not just *enabled* by ideas of European superiority; the ideas are *part* of the project. The Cantino Planisphere is an idea-and-a-thing — a map that organises a worldview and a trade route. Plantation slavery is an idea-and-a-thing — a method of organising labour that travels from Madeira to São Tomé to Brazil to the Caribbean. The categorisation of human populations by "race" is an idea-and-a-thing — a conceptual schema that does material work in slave registers, in the law, in immigration policy.

A counter-argument. The Marxist sociologist of knowledge will object that "ideas are things" risks reifying the ideological. If ideas are agencies with their own careers, who is responsible for their effects? Doesn't Whitehead's move dissolve the political accountability of ideologists? I take this seriously. The response is two-step. First, Whitehead's claim that ideas are things does not mean that ideas are *independent* of humans — they have their adventures only "in the life of humanity" (1933, p. 3). Second, the claim does not exempt humans from responsibility for the ideas they serve; it sharpens the question of how humans get *caught up* in the careers of ideas that exceed their conscious intentions. This is exactly what the lecture means by the "myth of progress" — a real idea-thing that organises modern self-understanding and obscures coloniality.

A further objection: "ideas are things" is incompatible with mainstream sociology of knowledge (Mannheim, Bourdieu) that locates ideas in social positions. Halewood's response — and mine — is that the sociology-of-knowledge tradition is itself bifurcated; it splits ideas (mental contents) from things (material conditions) and then asks how the latter explains the former. Whitehead's move dissolves the bifurcation by refusing the split in the first place.

In conclusion, Whitehead's claim that "ideas are things" is a serious metaphysical proposal with serious sociological consequences. It enables an adverbial sociology of modernity in which the colonial project, scientific rationalisation, and bifurcated thought-forms are analysed as *agencies* with careers, not as either mental contents *or* material conditions. The claim is risky — it disrupts both the standard idealist and the standard materialist story — but the lecture's "fearlessness of revision" (Whitehead 1927, p. 88) is exactly what is required. For a sociology of modernity that wants to take Du Bois seriously and Halewood seriously, "ideas are things" is the conceptual lever.

### M7: Durkheim, Marx and Weber gave three different answers to the problem of the social. Which is the most useful for thinking about modernity today?

I argue that the three answers are partial in different ways, and that the most useful *single* answer for thinking about modernity today is Marx's — because Marx alone keeps the question of *capital* and *coloniality* at the centre — but that the *strongest* approach is Halewood's synthesis, which uses Durkheim's "the social is natural" to anchor sociology in process, Weber's precision to discipline the concept, and Marx's structural-causal analysis to keep the political stakes visible. My thesis is that the question forces a choice that I will partly refuse, but if forced I choose Marx.

The three answers, recapped from Halewood Part 1. Durkheim grounds the social in *collective morality* and the *manner of association* within nature; the social is natural, an aspect of nature like life is an aspect of nature. Marx splits the social into *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich* and refuses to define either; what matters is the historically specific manner of association (capitalist relations as one such manner). Weber gives the most precise account — social action, social relationships, social selection — and refuses to use "social" as a loose adjective for "conditions" or "factors."

Why Durkheim is partial. Durkheim's account is anchored in collective morality and a notion of the *integral* collective. For thinking about modernity, this is a problem. The collective whose morality grounds the social is, in Durkheim's primary examples, the modern nation-state (or "European society"). The colonial relation, which makes modern European society possible, is *outside* the collective whose morality is being analysed. Du Bois's 1915 indictment is precisely an indictment of "European civilization" as a moral collective that organised itself around the trade in human beings — but a Durkheimian sociology of European morality has nothing to say about the moralities of the enslaved.

Why Weber is partial. Weber's precision is admirable but produces a *narrow* concept of the social — only action, relationship and selection count. For thinking about modernity-as-structure, this is too narrow. The slave plantation is not primarily a site of meaningful social action in the Weberian sense; it is a site of compulsion, violence, and the systemic production of value. Weber's tools work better for the bureaucratic apparatus of European modernity than for its colonial outside.

Why Marx is more useful for modernity today. Marx's account of the social keeps capital at the centre. The slave-cotton-industry chain (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203) is unintelligible without it. Marx's adjectival flexibility — using *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich* in tension — lets us name capitalist modernity *as a specific manner of association* without essentialising it. And Marx's commitment to critique in the strong sense ("the point is to change it," p. 158) is essential to a sociology that wants to do more than describe.

Where Marx is also partial. Marx's account is *capital-centric*. The lecture's rethinking-the-canon move — "what would sociology be like if we had taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously?" — is the lever. Marx tells us about the structural entanglement of slavery and industry; Du Bois tells us about the moral and racial logics that the structural account leaves under-theorised.

A counter-argument: it might be said that "the most useful for thinking about modernity today" is whichever framework best handles the live problems of climate, AI, and global migration — and that none of the three classical answers is sufficient. I take this seriously, but I think the right response is the Halewoodian one: the live problems require concepts that the classical canon already half-articulated. Climate is a question of "the social is natural" (Durkheim, re-read through Whitehead). AI is a question of "ideas are things" (Whitehead, re-read through Marx on the means of production). Migration is a question of capitalist-colonial extraction (Marx, re-read through Du Bois). The canon is not exhausted; it is *underused*.

In conclusion, if forced to choose, Marx is the most useful single answer because he keeps modernity-as-coloniality and modernity-as-capital at the centre, and because his concept of social/societal flexibility lets us name historically specific manners of association without essentialism. But the deeper answer, which the lecture and Halewood's whole project both support, is that the three answers are complementary and that the rethinking of modernity requires the whole canon plus the recovered voices (Du Bois, Dussel, French) plus the Whiteheadian metaphysics that makes the synthesis possible.

### M8: Discuss with examples the claim that "the manner of association" is what is sociologically interesting about modern societies.

I argue that "the manner of association" is the single most important conceptual recovery in Halewood's reading of the canon, and that it is the right *level* at which to do the sociology of modernity. My thesis is that focusing on the manner — the *how*, the adverb, the form, the mode of prehension — short-circuits both the methodological-individualist temptation (to reduce society to individuals) and the holist temptation (to reify society as a thing), and lets us describe modernity as a specific, historically situated way of associating that is neither inevitable nor irreversible.

The conceptual claim. Halewood's whole Part 1 argues that the canon converges on the adverbial. Durkheim: "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (*Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128). Marx's chemistry analogy: butyric acid and propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement (Marx, *Capital* vol. 1, in *CWME* 35, 33). Simmel: society is "an abstraction […] it is no real *object*. […] One should properly speak, not of society, but of *sociation*" (Simmel 1950, pp. 4, 10). Whitehead: a society is a nexus of actual entities that share a "common element of form" through their mutual prehensions (*Adventures of Ideas*, pp. 260-261). The four agree: the *manner* is the social.

**Example 1 — the sugar plantation.** The sugar plantation, as the lecture flags, is the paradigmatic modern institution before the factory. What makes it sociologically distinctive is not its inputs (people, land, water, mills) but its *manner of association*: a hierarchical, racialised, coerced organisation of labour designed to produce a single commodity for a distant market. The same inputs in a smallholder agricultural setting produce a different society. The same labour-coercion in a non-monocrop setting produces a different society. The plantation is its manner.

**Example 2 — the factory.** Marx's analysis of the factory in *Capital* is an analysis of a manner of association — the wage relation. The factory is not "industry" in the abstract; it is a specific way of associating workers and capital. The slave plantation and the wage factory share many components (discipline, hierarchy, productivity) but differ in their manner (coerced versus contractual labour, racial versus class subordination). Marx's point is that the *transition* from one to the other is not a transition from the social-inferior to the social-superior but a re-arrangement of the manner of association.

**Example 3 — the colonial bureaucracy.** Weber's account of bureaucracy, applied to colonial administration, is an account of a manner of association: rule-bound, hierarchical, file-keeping, the production of "subjects" through paperwork. The colonial bureaucracy is not just the European bureaucracy "exported"; it is a specific colonial-bureaucratic manner of association in which Weberian rule-following is combined with non-Weberian violence and racial categorisation.

**Example 4 — the modern household.** Simmel's "Formal Sociology" can be applied to the modern household: the manner in which spouses, children, parents and domestic workers associate is what makes the household what it is. The same persons in a different manner (the eighteenth-century domestic economy, the contemporary chosen family) constitute a different society in Whitehead's sense.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: a focus on "manner" risks becoming purely descriptive, losing the structural-causal punch that classical theory promised. I take this seriously. The response is that manner is *not* mere style — it is the *form* by which social properties arise. Durkheim's molecules become a cell because of their association; Marx's atoms become butyric acid or propyl formate because of their arrangement; in both cases the manner is causal. The same applies sociologically: the plantation manner causes a specific kind of person (the enslaved, the overseer, the planter); the factory manner causes a different kind (the worker, the foreman, the capitalist).

In conclusion, "the manner of association" is what is sociologically interesting about modern societies because it is the level at which historical specificity, causal explanation and political critique all converge. Modernity is not a *thing*; it is a *way* of associating — colonial, capitalist, bifurcated, oceanic, plantation-based, bureaucratic. To rethink modernity is to interrogate the manner. The four examples above all point to the same conceptual lesson: the social is adverbial, and the sociology of modernity must follow.

### M9: To what extent does the sociological canon need to be rewritten to include Du Bois, Dussel and Howard French?

I argue that the canon needs to be rewritten in two senses — *inclusion* (Du Bois, Dussel, French and others must take their place in the foundational reading list) and *inversion* (their inclusion changes what the canon *means*) — and that the second is the more important. My thesis is that adding Du Bois without inverting the canon is mere tokenism; inverting the canon without adding Du Bois is impossible. The lecture's framing — "the point is not simply to 'add' Africa in but to change, to add to, to invert the current view" — is the right one.

The inclusion case. Du Bois's 1915 essay anticipates by decades the structural account of modernity-as-coloniality that contemporary post-colonial theory has slowly reconstructed. Enrique Dussel's "transmodernity" reframes the canonical story by starting in 1492 rather than 1789 or 1776. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* gives the empirical scaffolding for both — 600 kg of gold from West Africa per year by the 1490s, the Madeira/São Tomé plantation origins, the 40% Brazilian destination of plantation-enslaved people. To leave these out of the foundational canon is to teach modernity as if its constitutive features were epiphenomenal.

The inversion case. The deeper move is that including Du Bois changes what Marx *means*. Marx's "without slavery you have no cotton" sentence is one passage among many in the standard reading of Marx; placed beside Du Bois's 1915 indictment, it becomes a central argument of Marx's account of capitalist origins. Including Dussel changes what Kant means — Kant's anthropology and his hierarchies of "race" become part of his philosophical project, not embarrassments to be excised. Including French changes what Durkheim means — the question of European "social solidarity" is now visible as a question about a specific collective whose conditions of existence include the colonial relation.

The lecture's question — "what would sociology be like if we had actually taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously all along?" — is a counterfactual that the discipline is only now beginning to take seriously. The answer is that sociology would have started in 1471 (the Portuguese on the West African coast) rather than 1789 (the French Revolution) or 1776 (the Wealth of Nations); it would have treated the plantation as the paradigmatic modern institution; it would have made "race" a foundational sociological category alongside "class"; it would have read modernity as *global from the start* rather than as European-and-then-globalised.

A counter-argument. It might be said that the canonical Marx-Weber-Durkheim trio has held up because of the depth of its conceptual contributions, and that Du Bois, Dussel and French are *historians* rather than *theorists*. I disagree on each count. Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk* and *Black Reconstruction* are theoretical works with concepts (double consciousness, the wages of whiteness, the colour line) at least as deep as Weber's. Dussel's "Eurocentric paradigm" is a theoretical framing with the same critical bite as Foucault's "episteme." French is empirically rigorous but his book is an argument about how to read the global archive of modernity. The "historians not theorists" objection is itself an artefact of the bifurcated canon.

A second counter-argument. Inclusion of new voices risks *tokenism* if the structure of the canon is preserved. I take this seriously — it is exactly why the inversion move matters more than the inclusion move. Adding Du Bois as a fourth founder next to Marx, Weber and Durkheim, without inverting the meaning of the trio, would be tokenism. The inversion is what makes the inclusion substantive.

In conclusion, the canon needs to be rewritten to a substantial extent — not just by adding new names but by re-reading the old names through the new. Du Bois, Dussel and French belong in the foundational reading list; their inclusion forces a re-reading of Marx (as a theorist of capitalist-colonial entanglement), of Weber (as a theorist of bureaucratic-colonial rationality), and of Durkheim (as a theorist of a specific European solidarity whose conditions of existence include the colonial outside). The lecture's verb — "invert" — is correct. The canon is to be rewritten not as a list but as a relational structure, and the new structure has the colonial relation at its centre.

### M10: Critically discuss Simmel's claim that "society" is an abstraction with no real existence.

I argue that Simmel's claim is correct as a *negative* move — society is not an object — but incomplete as a *positive* one, because Simmel's substitute (sociation, dyadic relations, form) underplays the durable structural features that make modern societies what they are. My thesis is that Simmel's claim is the right antidote to Durkheimian reification but needs to be supplemented by Marx's and Whitehead's accounts of how the social *endures* once it has been "sociated."

The claim. Simmel writes that "Society" "is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*. It does not exist outside and in addition to the individuals and the processes among them" (Simmel 1950, p. 4). His positive proposal: society is what we name once a sufficient density of dyadic and triadic reciprocal relations has been formed. "One should properly speak, not of society, but of *sociation*" (p. 10). The neologism *Vergesellschaftung* — sociation, becoming-social — is the conceptual centre.

The strength of the claim. The negative move is decisive. It refuses the reification by which "society" becomes a quasi-agent ("society demands," "society values") and forces the analyst back to the processes by which the apparent thing comes to be. This is precisely what Halewood's Whiteheadian move does at a higher level of generality: societies are nexuses of actual entities held together by common modes of prehension; they have no independent existence.

The strength against Durkheim. Simmel's negative move is the most direct critique of the Durkheimian collective. Where Durkheim wants a collective morality that explains sociality, Simmel says the collective is what we name *after* the sociation has happened, not what causes it. For analysing modern societies, this is liberating: it lets us ask how a "society" (the United Kingdom, the EU, "Western civilisation") has been *produced* rather than taking it as given.

The weakness. Simmel's account of *what* the dyadic relations produce — once enough secret-keeping, gift-giving, conversation, etc. has occurred — is underdeveloped. He stops at the formal level (the dyad, the triad, the form). Marx and Whitehead go further: Marx by insisting that the resulting "society" has a specific historical-material structure (capitalist relations, the wage form, the commodity), Whitehead by insisting that the resulting "society" has a *common element of form* that reproduces itself through the prehensions of its members. Simmel's society dissolves into too many small interactions; Marx and Whitehead let it congeal into something durable.

The weakness for thinking about modernity. The colonial project, the plantation, the modern state — these are not best captured at the dyadic level. They are durable, transnational, hierarchical, racialised systems whose existence over centuries exceeds any localised reciprocal relation. Simmel's tool gives us, at best, an account of the conversation between a slaver and an enslaved person; it gives us very little for the plantation system.

A counter-argument: Simmel's defenders (Pyyhtinen, the contemporary "rebirth of interest" in his work that Halewood flags) will say that Simmel's "Sociology of Forms" is precisely a tool for analysing durable structures — the form of money, the form of the city, the form of fashion. I partly accept this. Simmel's essays on money and on the metropolis do reach the structural level. But even there, the conceptual centre remains the small reciprocal relation, and the macro-structure is reconstructed from the micro rather than analysed at its own level. For modernity, where the macro-structures (capital, the state, race) have their own logics that exceed the micro, this is insufficient.

In conclusion, Simmel's claim that society is an abstraction is correct as a critical move against reification and is the conceptual ancestor of every contemporary attempt to dissolve "society" into networks, assemblages, or sociations. But the claim is incomplete without an account of how the dissolved-into-relations social manages to congeal into durable, asymmetric, modern systems. Marx and Whitehead supply what Simmel does not: an account of how the manner of association produces *durable*, *structured* societies that endure precisely because their members continue to associate in the same way. For modernity, the synthesis is essential. Society is not a thing — but the *manner* in which modern societies are not-things is itself a structural property that needs to be named and critiqued.

### M11: Compare the use of the chemistry analogy in Marx (butyric acid / propyl formate) and Durkheim (the living cell). What do these analogies do for their respective sociologies?

I argue that Marx's and Durkheim's chemistry analogies do the same conceptual work — they assert the *adverbial* primacy of the manner of association over the substance of the components — but they are deployed against different opponents and so produce different sociological commitments. My thesis is that both analogies are arguments for a *natural-scientific* sociology grounded not in atom-counting but in arrangement-analysis, and that Halewood is right to read them together as evidence that the canon already had an adverbial sociology in mind before the twentieth century forgot it.

The two analogies. Durkheim: "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (*Rules of Sociological Method*, p. 128). Marx: butyric acid and propyl formate "are made up of the same chemical substances, carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O). Moreover, these substances are combined together in the same proportions in each case, namely C4H8O2. […] Therefore, by thus equating the two substances, expression would be given to their chemical composition, while their different physical forms would be neglected" (*Capital*, in *CWME* 35, 33). The two analogies make the same logical point: same components, different arrangement, different thing.

What Durkheim does with the analogy. Durkheim deploys the cell analogy to argue that sociology has a *natural object* of its own — the manner of association — and is therefore a science in continuity with biology, chemistry and physics. The polemical target is the methodological individualist who wants to reduce social facts to individual psychology. Durkheim's response: the social fact is the *cell-level* phenomenon that emerges from the *molecule-level* individuals. The analogy underwrites the famous Durkheimian doctrine that social facts must be explained by social facts.

What Marx does with the analogy. Marx deploys the propyl formate / butyric acid analogy to argue against a *false abstraction* in classical economics. The standard economist treats labour-power, the commodity, the wage, as if they were homogeneous quantities. Marx's response: they are made of the same atoms but arranged differently, and the difference matters. The polemical target is Ricardo and his abstract value-theory. Marx's analogy underwrites the historical-materialist doctrine that the same elements (labour, capital, exchange) produce different societies depending on their arrangement.

What the two analogies share. The deepest commonality is the rejection of *substance-thinking*. Neither Marx nor Durkheim wants to ground sociology in some pre-given essence (human nature, social atoms, the contract). Both ground sociology in the *manner* of association — the form, the arrangement, the mode. This is the through-line that Halewood traces from the canon to Whitehead: the social is adverbial.

What the two analogies differ on. Durkheim's cell stays alive only if the molecules continue to associate in a specific way; the analogy underwrites *stability* and *consensus*. Marx's chemistry doublet shows that the same atoms can rearrange themselves into different molecules; the analogy underwrites *transformation* and *historical specificity*. The two analogies anticipate the standard difference between Durkheimian "order" sociology and Marxian "conflict" sociology — but at the level of the analogy itself, the disagreement is about *what the chemistry teaches*, not about whether the chemistry analogy is appropriate.

A counter-argument: it might be said that chemistry analogies in social theory are dangerous because they smuggle in a positivist commitment to natural-scientific norms. I take this seriously. The response, following Halewood's reading, is that the analogies do *not* import positivism — they import the *adverbial* commitment that natural science also makes when it pays attention to arrangement. Modern chemistry (and biology) is itself adverbial; the chemistry analogies are not a humanities-to-physics reduction but a recognition that arrangement-sensitivity is what makes any science a science.

In conclusion, Marx's and Durkheim's chemistry analogies do the same fundamental work — they assert that the manner of association is the proper object of sociological knowledge — and they differ in their polemical deployment (against methodological individualism for Durkheim, against abstract classical economics for Marx). Both anticipate Halewood's Whiteheadian sociology in which "the social is natural" and the *how* matters more than the *what*. For the sociology of modernity, the analogies are a reminder that we already have the conceptual tools to analyse modern societies as historically specific arrangements rather than as universal stages — we just need to use them.

### M12: Discuss with examples the claim that the sugar plantation, not the factory, is the paradigmatic institution of modernity.

I argue that the sugar plantation has at least as strong a claim as the factory to be the paradigmatic institution of modernity, and that the standard textbook bias toward the factory is itself a symptom of the Eurocentric narrative the lecture asks us to invert. My thesis is that the plantation precedes the factory, models the factory's labour-discipline, articulates the global trading system that the factory presupposes, and constitutes the racial categorisations that organise modern life — and that, for these four reasons, *Born in Blackness* is correct to treat the plantation as the paradigmatic modern institution.

The temporal argument. The lecture is explicit: "Sugar was where the idea of a plantation and plantation slavery started. In Madeira. And Sao Tome and Principe (islands near Africa)." The sugar plantation is operational in the early 1500s; the British cotton factory is operational from the 1760s and 1770s. By the time the factory appears, the plantation system has been running for two and a half centuries. To treat the factory as paradigmatic and the plantation as background is to invert the actual historical sequence.

The labour-discipline argument. The plantation pioneered the techniques of labour discipline that the factory later adopted — time-discipline, regimentation, hierarchical supervision, the separation of skilled from unskilled labour, the production of a single commodity for a distant market. The 1760s English factory did not invent these; it inherited them from the plantation. Sidney Mintz's *Sweetness and Power* (which the lecture's sugar-and-modernity gesture invokes) makes this argument explicit.

The trading-system argument. Marx's chain — "without slavery no cotton, without cotton no modern industry, without colonies no world trade, without world trade no large-scale industry" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 203) — names the factory as the *terminus* of a chain that begins with the plantation. The factory is unintelligible without the global trading system; the global trading system is unintelligible without the plantation. The plantation is therefore prior in *both* historical and conceptual senses.

The racial-categorisation argument. The plantation produced the racial categorisations (Black, white, mixed-race, "free people of colour") that organise modern life. The factory inherited these categorisations and reproduced them; it did not invent them. Du Bois's 1915 essay on the trans-Atlantic slave trade reads modernity through the plantation precisely for this reason.

**Example 1 — Madeira and São Tomé.** The lecture's first concrete example. Sugar plantation slavery starts here, on islands off the African coast, in the early 1500s. The institutions, techniques, and racial categories that will define plantation life in the Caribbean and Brazil are pioneered here.

**Example 2 — Brazil.** The lecture flags that 40% of all plantation-enslaved people went from Africa to Brazil (French 2021, p. 177). Brazil is the largest single recipient of trans-Atlantic enslaved people; Brazilian sugar and gold are central to the early modern world economy.

**Example 3 — the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar of 1629.** Published for use in Bolivia and Peru. The plantation system is *already global* by 1629 — its labour discipline, its religious apparatus, and its linguistic infrastructure span the Atlantic. The factory will not match this global reach until the nineteenth century.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: the factory might still be paradigmatic because it institutionalised the wage relation, which is the specifically *capitalist* form of labour. The plantation runs on slavery, not wages, and so (the argument goes) is pre-capitalist or only protocapitalist. I take this seriously, but I note Marx's own answer: the wage form *presupposes* the prior accumulation of capital that the plantation provided. The plantation is not pre-capitalist; it is the *originary* moment of capitalist accumulation. Eric Williams's *Capitalism and Slavery* makes this argument decisively.

A second counter-argument: paradigm cases should be the ones that *generalised most* — and the factory, not the plantation, generalised to organise twentieth-century industrial life. I respond: the factory's generalisation depended on the plantation's prior labour-discipline and capital accumulation. The fact that the factory came to dominate twentieth-century imagery does not retroactively make the plantation a side-story.

In conclusion, the sugar plantation has at least as strong a claim as the factory to be the paradigmatic institution of modernity — it precedes the factory in time, models the factory's discipline, articulates the global trade the factory presupposes, and produces the racial categories the factory reproduces. To treat the factory as paradigmatic is to participate in the Eurocentric narrative the lecture asks us to invert. To treat the plantation as paradigmatic is to read modernity through Du Bois, French, Dussel, Williams and Mintz, which is what the rethinking-the-canon move requires.

### M13: To what extent is the "sociological imagination" still a useful concept for third-year sociology?

I argue that the sociological imagination is still indispensable for third-year sociology, but only on a *strong* reading that emphasises *active reinterpretation* against common sense rather than the weaker Millsian reading that treats it as the connection between biography and history. My thesis follows the lecture's Beauvoirian framing: imagination is what lets us picture what the world *could be* if we thought and acted differently, and this is precisely what a critique-not-criticism sociology needs.

The lecture's framing. Halewood revives the concept by way of Simone de Beauvoir: Beauvoir "tried to understand the role and status of 'woman' and 'women' in the 20th century. To do so, she imagined what the world and social theory would look like if it took women seriously." The sociological imagination, on this reading, is not just the linking of personal trouble and public issue (Mills) — it is the *active picturing* of an alternative configuration of the social. The lecture is explicit: "we need to use our imagination […] to think about what the world would (or could) be like if we thought and acted differently."

The weak reading and its limit. The Millsian sociological imagination — the capacity to see how personal experience is shaped by historical structure — has been the staple of first-year sociology for sixty years. It is true, useful and pedagogically powerful. But it is also *descriptive*: it teaches you to see what is, not what could be. By third year, the descriptive move is not enough.

The strong reading. The strong sociological imagination is critique-not-criticism in active form. It evaluates a structure and *imagines* an alternative. Beauvoir's *Second Sex* is the model: she did not just describe the position of women, she imagined social theory that took women seriously. Du Bois's 1915 essay is the model: he did not just describe the slave trade, he imagined the history of modernity rewritten so that "men" do not "forget." Halewood's *Rethinking the Social* is the model: he does not just describe the failures of the canon, he imagines an adverbial sociology grounded in Whiteheadian metaphysics.

**Example 1 — race.** What would sociology look like if "race" had been a foundational category from the start, alongside class? The Du Bois counterfactual the lecture flags. The strong sociological imagination lets us picture this — and once pictured, the gaps in the standard story become visible.

**Example 2 — animals.** The autumn-term Topic 3 question (animals and sociology) is a direct exercise in the strong sociological imagination. What would sociology look like if it took non-human animals seriously as social actors? The strong imagination, not the weak, lets us pose the question.

**Example 3 — bifurcation.** Halewood's six bifurcations (fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities) become visible only when we *imagine* what an unbifurcated sociology would look like. The bifurcation, like the gulf, is invisible from inside.

A counter-argument: the sociological imagination, in its strong reading, risks utopianism — speculation about what *could be* without analysis of what *is*. I take this seriously. The response is that the strong imagination is not utopianism; it is the imaginative supplement to analysis, the move from "this is the structure" to "this structure could be otherwise." Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* XI is the canonical statement: "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it" (Marx, *Selected Writings*, p. 158). Change requires imagination, not just analysis.

In conclusion, the sociological imagination is still indispensable for third-year sociology, but on a strong rather than a weak reading. The weak reading (biography-and-history) is for first year; the strong reading (active reinterpretation against common sense, picturing the otherwise) is for third year and beyond. The lecture's Beauvoirian framing is the right one. The exam-and-essay payoff is concrete: every critique-style answer should close with an imagined alternative, not just a diagnosis. That is what makes it critique rather than criticism.

### M14: Critically discuss Weber's claim that the generality of the term "social" rests on its ambiguity.

I argue that Weber's claim is correct as a diagnosis — the loose use of "social" *does* trade on ambiguity rather than precision — but that Weber's own positive proposal (only "social action," "social relationships," and "social selection" are properly social) is too narrow for the sociology of modernity. My thesis is that Halewood's reading is right: Weber's precision is admirable, but it has a cost, and the cost is paid in our ability to analyse the macro-structures of modern life.

The claim. Weber writes that "It is now no accident that the term 'social' [des Sozialen] which seems to have a quite general meaning, turns out to have, as soon as one carefully examines its application, a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity" [VERIFY p.? — Weber 1949 [1904], 68]. Halewood discusses this passage in Chapter 7 of *Rethinking the Social*.

The diagnostic correctness. Weber is right. The term "social" is used in modern sociology to mean wildly different things — collective representations (Durkheim), class relations (Marx), meaningful action (Weber), reciprocal interaction (Simmel), the manner of association (Whitehead), the cultural-symbolic order (Parsons), the network of ties (recent sociology), and so on. The generality of the term is sustained by the fact that it picks out *none* of these specifically. If you press any user of "social" they will offer one of these specific senses, and they will disagree with the others.

Weber's positive proposal. Weber narrows the concept to three precise notions: *social action* (action oriented to the behaviour of others), *social relationships* (*Beziehungen* — one-to-one orientations that sustain over time), and *social selection* (the differential survival of forms of action and relationship). Outside these three, Weber refuses to call anything "social" — no "social conditions," no "social factors," no "social existence." Halewood notes that this restraint has a cost.

The cost. Weber's narrow concept works well for analysing the micro-textures of meaningful interaction — the bureaucratic encounter, the religious vocation, the political following of a charismatic leader. It works less well for the macro-structures that modern sociology also needs to name. The plantation, the world economy, the colonial state, the global migration regime — these involve "social" features (in the loose sense) that exceed any aggregation of social action, social relationships and social selection in Weber's narrow sense. The slave on the plantation does not "orient her action to the behaviour of others" in the Weberian sense; she is *compelled* by violence. The colonial economy is not the aggregate of meaningful interactions; it is a structure that pre-shapes who can act and how.

A defence of Weber. Weber's narrowness might be the *honest* response to the ambiguity he diagnoses. If "social" cannot mean everything, it might be better to make it mean something very specific. The cost of macro-structural blindness is the cost of precision. I take this seriously. The Weberian response to the plantation example would be: the plantation is *not* primarily a social phenomenon in the strict sense — it is an *economic* or *coercive* phenomenon, and we should describe it as such. The "social" parts (the meaningful interactions among slaves, between overseers and planters, among free people of colour) are real but distinct from the structural-coercive parts.

A critical reply. The Weberian defence works only if we accept the *bifurcation* between the social, the economic, and the coercive. Halewood's whole project is to refuse such bifurcations. The plantation is both social and economic and coercive at once; the manner of association *just is* the manner of coerced extraction. A sociology that cannot describe this is too narrow.

A second critical move. The lecture's Whiteheadian framing offers a way out. Whitehead retains the precision that Weber wanted (a society is a nexus with a common element of form) without the narrowness that Weber imposed (the form does not have to be meaningful action). The plantation, on Whitehead's account, is a society — a nexus whose members share a common manner of prehension (the racial-economic ordering of bodies and time) and reproduce that manner through their interactions. Weber's three categories are *examples* of social action; they are not the limits of the social.

In conclusion, Weber's claim that the generality of "social" rests on its ambiguity is correct and diagnostically sharp. His positive narrowing of the concept is admirable but too restrictive for the sociology of modernity. The Whiteheadian alternative that Halewood develops in Part 2 of *Rethinking the Social* gives us a way to keep Weber's precision without his narrowness: the social as the manner of association, where the manner can include meaning, coercion, racial ordering, capital, and the form-of-life of non-human entities. For modernity, this is the right scale.

### M15: Compare Whitehead's and Durkheim's claims that "the social is natural." Are they saying the same thing?

I argue that Whitehead and Durkheim are not saying the same thing, although they are saying *compatible* things and Whitehead's claim subsumes Durkheim's. My thesis is that Durkheim naturalises the social by making it an aspect of nature in continuity with biology; Whitehead naturalises the social by making the natural itself social, all the way down — every nexus of actual entities is a "society." The difference matters for the sociology of modernity: Durkheim's move grounds sociology as a science; Whitehead's move dissolves the bifurcation between sociology and the rest of science.

The two claims. Durkheim, on Halewood's reading: "the social is natural" — the social is an aspect of nature that sociology abstracts in the same way biology abstracts life. The point is that there is *no separate social realm* hovering above the natural; the social is what happens in nature when certain manners of association obtain. Whitehead, on Halewood's reading in Chapter 8: societies are the elements of nature that exhibit and express orderliness; "as far as nature is ordered it is social; it exhibits a 'social order'." A hot stone is a society; the components mutually prehend their hotness, and this common manner of prehension makes the stone what it is.

What they share. Both reject the modern bifurcation between nature and society. Both make the social a *natural* phenomenon rather than a separate realm. Both make the manner of association the *focus* of sociological attention.

Where they differ. Durkheim's "the social is natural" is a claim about the *specificity* of the social as one aspect of nature among others. The social is natural in the way life is natural — it is a *region* of the natural that sociology studies. Whitehead's "social is natural" is the much stronger claim that *all* enduring natural entities are societies. The social is not a region; it is the form of orderly endurance as such. As Stengers puts it in Halewood's reading: "Everything is sociology."

The implications. Durkheim's move grounds sociology as one science among others, with a specific object (the manner of human association). Whitehead's move dissolves the bifurcation between sociology and physics; both are studying the manner in which nexuses of actual entities hold together. For modernity, this matters in two ways. First, Whitehead's move lets us include non-human entities (animals, machines, ecosystems) in the sociology of modernity without violating any disciplinary boundary — the autumn-term Topic 3 (animals) follows directly. Second, Whitehead's move lets us see that the "social" features of modern life are *continuous* with the natural-physical features (climate, biology, materials) rather than separated from them.

A counter-argument for Durkheim. Durkheim's more modest naturalism is *more defensible* because it does not require us to call a hot stone a "society." Whitehead's expanded sense of society is metaphorical at best, the objection goes. I take this seriously. The response is that Whitehead's "society" is not metaphorical — it is a technical metaphysical term, defined precisely (a nexus with a common element of form sustained by mutual prehension). The hot stone really is a society in this sense. The objection is that we shouldn't use the same word for the British Sociological Association and a stone. The Whiteheadian reply is that the same *manner* of holding-together-by-mutual-prehension obtains in both cases, and the word is meant to flag this commonality.

A counter-argument for Whitehead. Durkheim's collective-morality grounding is *too narrow*. It makes the human collective the centre of sociology, with the non-human relegated to the merely "natural." Whitehead's expanded sense decentres the human. For thinking about modernity in the Anthropocene, where climate, animals, and infrastructure are visibly social actors, Whitehead's move is the more useful.

Where I land. I do not think the two claims are *competing*; I think Whitehead's claim *generalises* Durkheim's. Durkheim's "the social is natural" is the human-scale claim; Whitehead's is the metaphysical-scale claim. Both are true, and the human-scale claim is a special case of the metaphysical claim. For the sociology of modernity, the Whiteheadian generalisation is the more powerful tool, because it lets us include non-human entities, refuse the bifurcation between nature and society, and analyse modern life adverbially across the full range of its components.

In conclusion, Whitehead and Durkheim are *not* saying the same thing, but they are saying compatible things, with Whitehead's claim including Durkheim's as a special case. The difference matters: Durkheim's move grounds sociology as a science; Whitehead's move dissolves the bifurcation between sociology and the rest of science and opens the discipline to non-human social actors. For the sociology of modernity, particularly in its post-colonial, ecological, and technological registers, the Whiteheadian generalisation is the right tool. The lecture's bifurcation-thesis and the Whiteheadian sociology that underwrites it are, on this reading, the most useful conceptual gift L1 leaves us with.
