## 3. Information from the readings

### Halewood Part 1. Durkheim, Marx, Weber (and Simmel) Revisited

Halewood's overarching claim is that **"the problem of the social" is unfinished business**. The classical canon did not deliver a settled definition of "society" or "the social"; rather, each of Durkheim, Marx and Weber struggled with the concept in different registers, and these struggles have been forgotten by twentieth-century sociology, which now uses "social" as if it were transparent.

**Durkheim.** The English title *The Division of Labour in Society* misleads: the French (*De la division du travail social*) puts the adjective on labour, not on a noun "society." Durkheim's question is not "what is society?" but "what makes us *solidaire*?" He refuses any bifurcation between the social and the natural: *the social is natural*. This is what makes a science of sociology possible; sociology abstracts an aspect of nature in the same way physics or biology does. Solidarity is not a biological instinct (against Parsons), but the manner of association within nature. Yet Durkheim's account rests, ultimately, on collective morality: sociality is grounded in the punishment of contraventions of a collective. The argument circles: sociability is a product of social life, but social life is grounded in collective morality, which is itself a manner of associating. Durkheim "should be approached with caution but also with admiration," admired for refusing to take the social as given.

**Marx.** Marx provides no clean definition of "society" (Halewood: he was not a "professional sociologist"). He preserves two German adjectives, *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich*, and uses them in tension. Halewood translates *gesellschaftlich* as "societal" and *sozial* as "social." Capitalism is the predominant *societal* form, but "social" relations of prestige and esteem both reinforce and exceed it. The point: *nothing is settled*. Marx's chemistry analogy (butyric acid vs. propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement) makes the same point Durkheim makes about the living cell: *the manner of association* gives a thing its specificity. Marx's task is to historicise abstract categories ("civil society," "society in general") rather than to provide his own essentialist alternative.

**Weber.** Weber, alone of the three, gave a *precise* concept: only three things are properly "social"; social action, social relationships (*Beziehungen* not *Verhältnisse*), and social selection. Weber refuses to talk loosely of "social conditions" or "social factors"; that would dilute the concept. A common language does not make a community; only the actively shared consciousness that members of the group have this in common, oriented to others, makes a *Vergemeinschaftung* or *Vergesellschaftung*. This precision has a cost: no general "society" hides behind action. Notably, Weber comes to this only at the end of his life (1919-20); proof that the battle for the concept was still alive in classical sociology.

**Simmel.** Society "is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*" (Simmel, 1950: 4). It is the outcome of dyadic and triadic relations. Simmel's example of the secret: a secret is only a secret in relation to at least one other. Form, not content, is the proper object of sociology, hence "Formal Sociology" / "Sociology of Forms." *Vergesellschaftung* (sociation) is a noun made out of a process. Simmel sides with Weber against Durkheim in privileging *groups* over *totalities*.

**Conclusion to Part 1.** The classical canon converges on one point: **association is what matters; the manner, the adverb, is the social.** None of the four ever decided the concept once and for all. To reject "traditional" concepts of the social (as Latour does) is therefore premature.

### Halewood Part 2. Whitehead on sociology, societies and the social

**Whitehead's "sociology."** Whitehead, the philosopher, splits two dyads carefully: *society/social* (metaphysical) vs. *sociology/sociological* (the study of human societies). He uses "sociology" in *Adventures of Ideas* only three times, but consistently to mean an attention to *how* humans were made human, not *what* they essentially are. The shift from "what" to "how" repeats the adverbial theme from Part 1.

**Ideas are things.** Whitehead's striking move (highlighted by Stengers): "I propose to consider critically the *sort* of history which ideas can have in the life of humanity." Ideas are not the creations of humans, nor are they outside humans; they have *adventures* and humans are the vehicles. Sociology, on this reading, is the study of how ideas combine with humanity in different epochs. Whitehead is "pre-Kantian"; he does not start from the question of how concepts relate to reality.

**Apes, humans, humanity.** "Clear, conscious discrimination is an accident of human existence. It makes us human. But it does not make us exist. It is of the essence of our humanity. But it is an accident of our existence" (Whitehead, 1938: 158). Conscious discrimination converts "ape-ity" into "humanity." Imagine, Whitehead suggests, what Kant would have written had he read Darwin: human reason as a *kind of animal reason*, situated within nature.

**The order of nature, social order.** Whitehead's account of "societies" begins with *order* and *disorder* as correlative (not binary). Order is always a specific order, never order in the abstract. A society for Whitehead is *any* nexus of actual entities that share a common element of form and reproduce that form through their prehensions of each other. A hot stone is a society: its component parts "feel themselves hotly." This metaphysical move dissolves the bifurcation between natural and social; the social *is* the order of nature.

**Sociality as likeness.** Members of a society are alike because of their common character; differences between societies are differences between *manners of association*. Solidarity, on Whitehead's account, does not require a Durkheimian totality; it arises in each occasion of mutual prehension. "Each task of creation is a social effort" (Whitehead, 1978: 223).

**Conclusion.** Sociological theory must begin from process. "No static maintenance of perfection is possible. Advance or Decadence are the only choices offered" (Whitehead, 1933: 353-354). Social theory must be willing to wreck itself in order to advance: "the art of free society consists […] in fearlessness of revision" (Whitehead, 1927: 88).

## 4. Critical analysis with key quotes

The main argumentative thread of the L1 reading and lecture is that **modernity is constituted by a series of bifurcations that simultaneously enable and disable thought**, and that critical sociology must work *adverbially* (on the *manner* of association) rather than essentially.

**Main tension 1. Modernity's two faces.** Halewood and the lecture insist that the same period that produced Shakespeare, Luther, and "the modern organization of industry" produced the trans-Atlantic slave trade. Du Bois forces the recognition that these are not separable; they are constitutive of one another.

> "For four hundred years, from 1450 to 1850, European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings of such tremendous proportions that the physical, economic and moral effects are still plainly to be remarked throughout the world." (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90)

> "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry." (Marx, 1977: 203)

**Main tension 2. Critique vs criticism.** The lecture distinguishes finding fault from evaluating structure and pointing to alternatives. Marx's slogan that the point is to *change* the world makes critique inseparable from a future-oriented practice.

> "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it." (Marx, 1977: 158)

**Main tension 3. Bifurcation as the cornerstone of modernity.** Halewood's signature thesis. Modern thought operates by splitting fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities. Once split, a *gulf* opens up.

> "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it." (Whitehead, 1933: 217)

**Main tension 4. The problem of the social.** Halewood's reading of the canon shows that "social" was never a settled term; classical theory was *a contest over the concept*.

> "It is now no accident that the term 'social' [des Sozialen] which seems to have a quite general meaning, turns out to have, as soon as one carefully examines its application, a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity." (Weber, 1949 [1904]: 68; cited in Halewood, 2014: 131)

> "Society' 'is an abstraction […] it is no real *object*. It does not exist outside and in addition to the individuals and the processes among them." (Simmel, 1950: 4)

> "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life." (Durkheim, 1895: 128)

**Main tension 5. Adverbial sociology.** Whitehead's "ideas are things" move runs alongside Marx's chemistry analogy, Durkheim's living-cell analogy, and Simmel's sociation. All four insist that the *how* (the manner, the form, the mode of prehension) is what is social.

> "The foundation of all understanding of sociological theory, that is to say, of all understanding of human life, is that no static maintenance of perfection is possible. […] The very essence of real actuality, that is, of the completely real, is *process*." (Whitehead, 1933: 353-354)

> "It is the first step in sociological wisdom, to recognize that the major advances in civilization are processes which all but wreck the societies in which they occur […] The art of free society consists […] in fearlessness of revision." (Whitehead, 1927: 88)

**Critique of the canon.** Halewood's deepest claim is that twentieth-century sociology has *forgotten* the open-endedness of the founders. Parsons assumes social order is self-explanatory; Latour wants to abolish the social altogether. Both are wrong; for Halewood, the live question is to *reconstruct* a philosophy of the social that retains the canon's critical insights while breaking out of its bifurcations.

## 7. Essay answers

### 2025 (Q3): 'The fact-value distinction is a cornerstone of modernity. It is also one of its major problems.' Critically assess this statement.

I argue that the fact-value distinction is indeed a cornerstone of modernity, but it is a cornerstone in the architectural sense Halewood draws from Whitehead: it holds the building up while *fixing* the bifurcations the building requires. The distinction makes possible the modern sciences, the modern administration and the modern university, and at the same time produces the gulfs into which modern thought repeatedly falls. My thesis is that the distinction is a *real* abstraction (an idea that does work in the world) but a *bad* one, and that a serious sociology of modernity must abandon the metaphysics of bifurcation while retaining the *practical* distinction between describing and judging.

The first move is to recognise where the distinction comes from. In the German neo-Kantian tradition that shaped Weber and Simmel, through Dilthey and Rickert, the separation of the "natural sciences" (causal explanation of fact) from the "human sciences" (meaning and value) was framed as a methodological necessity. Weber radicalised this into the doctrine of *Wertfreiheit*. Halewood's account (Halewood, 2014) shows the elegant methodological apparatus is also an *ontological* bifurcation: it presupposes that the world really does come in two kinds, and that sociology must straddle the gap. Once you split fact from value you cannot get the two halves back together without smuggling.

Second, the fact-value distinction is a cornerstone in the more obvious sense: without it the modern division of intellectual labour collapses. The natural sciences report facts; the humanities deal in values; the social sciences are caught between. Halewood (2014) lists science/humanities as one of six bifurcations of modernity. The colonial archive Howard French reconstructs in *Born in Blackness* (French, 2021) is full of "facts" the modernist convention asks us to evaluate later, as "values." Du Bois's 1915 passage refuses this: the slave trade is both "the most inexcusable and despicable blot on modern human history" *and* the factual condition of "the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). The factual and the evaluative are one description.

Third, the distinction is "one of its major problems" because it produces the kind of sociology that *cannot see* its own conditions of possibility. If you accept the value-free description of modernity as European rationalisation, slavery becomes an external "value" question, tacked on, lamented, rather than constitutive. Marx already saw this when he wrote that "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). The fact of cotton and the value of human beings are not separable in the structure of industrial modernity.

The counter-argument is a Weberian one: without some version of the distinction, sociology cannot be a *science*. If we let our values infect our descriptions, we get propaganda. Weber's response, that the choice of *which* facts matter is value-laden but the description of those facts must be controlled, is the most honest version of the position. I take this seriously, but the most developed feminist response to it, Harding's "strong objectivity," shows the Weberian position pays too high a price. Harding argues that conventional value-freedom "is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128) because it polices only those values researchers disagree about while leaving culture-wide assumptions invisible; the remedy is to put the knower on the same critical plane as the known. My own position is not that all descriptions are evaluations but that the *clean* metaphysical split between the two is unsustainable. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is a fact; its art-historical interpretation that only "briefly mentions slavery" is a value-laden silence. Both are factual; one is better than the other.

In conclusion, the fact-value distinction is a cornerstone of modernity in the strong sense; it *holds up* a whole way of organising thought and institutions. It is also a major problem because, once cemented, it produces gulfs in which constitutive features of modernity (especially racial slavery and coloniality) are exiled to the "value" side and quietly excised from the "factual" account of modern industry. The way forward is not to abolish the practical distinction between describing and judging but to refuse the metaphysical bifurcation. We should *critique* in the strong sense (evaluate structure, suggest alternatives) rather than merely *criticise*. Sociology after the fact-value distinction is the sociology that takes both Du Bois and Weber seriously at once, refusing to let either be filed away on the further side of a gulf.

### 2023 (Q3): Why has the distinction between "facts" and "values" been problematic for sociology?

I argue that the distinction between facts and values has been problematic for sociology in three interlocking ways: it imports a neo-Kantian metaphysics that the founders themselves had not fully digested; it produces a methodological neurosis that disables critique; and, most importantly, it makes constitutive features of modernity (particularly coloniality) appear as external "value" questions when in fact they are factual conditions of the modern world. My thesis, following Halewood's account of bifurcation, is that the problem is not the practical contrast between describing and judging but the *metaphysical* split that hardens that contrast into two separate realms.

The first dimension is genealogical. Halewood (2014) traces how Weber inherited the fact-value distinction from neo-Kantianism (Rickert and Dilthey, in particular), for whom the *Geisteswissenschaften* had to be distinguished from the *Naturwissenschaften* by reference to *meaning* and *value*. Sociology as *Wissenschaft* was supposed to navigate this divide. The problem is that Weber's own concept of "social action" is *both* descriptive *and* evaluative. Weber knew the term "social" was problematic: "It is now no accident that the term 'social' […] turns out to have […] a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity" (Weber, 1949 [1904]: 68; cited in Halewood, 2014: 131).

The second dimension is methodological. *Wertfreiheit* was supposed to discipline the sociologist's descriptions. In practice, the disciplining works in only one direction: it polices the appearance of evaluation in the text while leaving the *selection* of facts (which questions, which populations, which periods) entirely up to the prior values of the discipline. This is why the standard twentieth-century account of modernity was a story of European industrialisation that mentioned slavery, if at all, in passing. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is the example: its smarthistory.org interpretation "only briefly mentions slavery." The "facts" are reported; the values that selected them are invisible.

The third and deepest dimension is the substantive one. The fact-value distinction makes modernity's coloniality look like a "value" question that can be added in or left out. Du Bois's 1915 passage refuses this: the trans-Atlantic slave trade is *the* fact of modern industry, not an ethical regret. Marx put it bluntly: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* (French, 2021) gives the empirical scaffolding: 600 kg of gold per year from West Africa by the 1490s; the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar published in 1629; 40% of all plantation-enslaved people ending up in Brazil. These are not values; they are facts. But they are facts that change what "modernity" *is* as a description.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously is the Weberian one: that without some version of the fact-value distinction sociology collapses into political pamphleteering. I accept the force of this. The point of the critique is not to dissolve all difference between describing and judging but to refuse the *bifurcation*, what Halewood calls, drawing on Whitehead, the splitting of one thing into two branches such that "a gulf is created" (Whitehead, 1933: 217). The practical distinction between an empirical claim and a normative claim is workable; the metaphysical claim that there are two separate realms is not. Harding's standpoint epistemology is the most developed feminist response to exactly this point: her "strong objectivity" insists that conventional *Wertfreiheit* is "*not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128) because it disciplines researchers' disagreements while shielding the culture-wide assumptions that selected the topic in the first place. The remedy is reflexive disclosure, not silence.

In conclusion, the fact-value distinction has been problematic for sociology because it carries an unowned neo-Kantian metaphysics, it produces a one-sided methodological discipline, and (most importantly) it systematically obscures the coloniality of modernity by treating slavery and racial extraction as "value" questions rather than as factual constituents of the modern world. The remedy is the move Halewood recommends throughout his book: refuse the bifurcation, work adverbially on the *manner* of modern association, and recover the founders' (Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Simmel) shared insight that the social is what happens between things, not a layer hiding behind them. Critique in the strong, structural sense is what replaces the falsely modest value-free description.

### 2022 (Q1): Is Sociology a science? If so, what kind of science is it?

I argue that sociology is a science, but only if we are willing to revise what we mean by "science"; specifically, to abandon the bifurcation between the natural sciences and the human sciences that has shaped the question since the neo-Kantians. My thesis, drawing on Halewood's Whiteheadian reading of Durkheim, is that sociology is a *natural science of the manner of association*: its objects are real, its claims are correctable, but its method is adverbial rather than nomothetic in the physics-envy sense.

The first move is to take Durkheim seriously. Durkheim was clear that sociology had to put itself "in the same frame of mind as scientists," but his deeper claim, as Halewood emphasises in Chapter 7 of *Rethinking the Social*, is that **the social is natural**. "If the social 'world' were not a part of nature then it would be impossible to treat it scientifically" (Halewood, 2014: 124). The familiar twentieth-century picture in which the social is a *separate realm* from the natural is, on this reading, a Parsonian importation, not a Durkheimian inheritance. For Durkheim, sociology is a science because it abstracts an aspect of nature (*the manner of association*) just as biology and chemistry abstract other aspects. Durkheim's example of the living cell is instructive: "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (Durkheim, 1895: 128).

The second move is to draw the same lesson from Marx. Marx's chemistry analogy (butyric acid and propyl formate, same atoms different arrangement) makes the same point. Sociology, like chemistry, studies how the *arrangement* gives rise to the specific properties. Marx is therefore committed to a science of society in the strong sense, but a science whose objects are *historically* specific manners of association rather than ahistorical laws.

The third move is Weberian, and here we hit the difficulty. Weber's sociology is also a *Wissenschaft*, but it requires *Verstehen*, understanding the meaning actors give to their actions. This appears to bifurcate sociology from physics. The standard neo-Kantian reading concludes that sociology is a "human science." Halewood resists this: Weber's "social action" is a *very precise* concept, narrower than usually thought, and compatible with the recognition that animals can engage in social action too. The bifurcation between human meaning and natural cause is not as clean as the neo-Kantians wanted.

A fourth move comes from Whitehead. In Halewood's reading of *Process and Reality*, "society" is the name for any nexus of actual entities that share a common manner of prehension. A hot stone is a society. So is the British Sociological Association. Sociology, in this expanded sense, is the study of how societies (at any scale) manage to endure. This puts sociology back into continuity with the natural sciences without reducing it to them.

A counter-argument worth pressing is the postmodern one: sociology should *not* be a science, because the scientific ideal is itself a colonial-Eurocentric one that excludes other ways of knowing. I take this seriously. But the right response is not to abandon science; it is to *expand* what counts as science to include the adverbial, historical and critical work the canonical reformers (Du Bois, Simmel, Dussel) were already doing. Du Bois's 1915 description is one example; another is *The Souls of Black Folk*, where the "peculiar sensation" of double consciousness, "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), is offered not as poetic confession but as a properly sociological description of the racialised modern self that Weber's unitary actor could not register.

In conclusion, sociology is a science, not in the imitative sense of physics-envy but in the Durkheimian sense of a discipline that abstracts a real aspect of nature: the manner of association by which entities (human and non-human) endure. Its method is adverbial; its claims are about *how* the social comes to be, not about what it ultimately *is*. It is a critical science in the strong sense; its job is not just to find faults but to evaluate the structure of association and to suggest alternatives. To answer the second half of the question: sociology is the science of association, the kind of science that takes Marx's "the point is to change it" as a methodological imperative as well as a political one.

### 2022 (Q2): How have the ideas of neo-Kantians influenced sociology?

I argue that the neo-Kantians (Rickert, Windelband, Dilthey) shaped twentieth-century sociology far more deeply than is usually acknowledged, and that their influence is double-edged: they bequeathed the discipline its conceptual rigour (the distinction between *Naturwissenschaften* and *Geisteswissenschaften*; the methodology of *Verstehen*; the doctrine of value-relevance) but they also installed the bifurcations that, on Halewood's account, generate the recurrent crises of social theory. My thesis is that the neo-Kantian inheritance is unavoidable but must be critically reconstructed rather than uncritically accepted.

The first dimension is the methodological one. Through Weber and Simmel (both of whom worked in the neo-Kantian intellectual climate of late-nineteenth-century Germany) sociology inherited the conviction that the social sciences are concerned with *meaning* and *value* in a way the natural sciences are not. Halewood notes in Chapter 8 of *Rethinking the Social* that "the specific version of German Idealist neo-Kantianism […] influenced Weber and Simmel" (Halewood, 2014). Weber's whole apparatus (ideal types, *Verstehen*, value-relevance, value-freedom) is unintelligible without this background. Likewise, Simmel's "Formal Sociology" or "Sociology of Forms" picks up the Kantian language of form against content.

The second dimension is the substantive one. The neo-Kantians installed the **fact-value distinction** at the heart of sociology. As I argued in the previous essay, this distinction is one of the cornerstones of modern thought, and one of its major problems. It produces the methodological neurosis of value-freedom; it generates the gulf between the description of the modern world and the moral evaluation of it; and it makes constitutive features of modernity such as racial slavery appear as "value" addenda rather than as facts of the system. The most developed contemporary response is feminist standpoint theory: Harding (2004: 128) argues that Weberian *Wertfreiheit* is "*not rigorous or objectifying enough*" because it disciplines researcher disagreements while leaving the culture-wide selections that produced the research agenda unexamined. Her "strong objectivity" is in effect a critical descendant of the neo-Kantian inheritance, retaining its rigour while refusing its bifurcation.

The third dimension is the *bifurcation* that Halewood specifically diagnoses. The neo-Kantian split between nomothetic (law-seeking) and idiographic (case-specific) sciences hardens into the modern split between sociology-as-science and sociology-as-cultural-criticism. Halewood identifies six modern bifurcations (fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities) and the neo-Kantian inheritance is most clearly visible in the first and the last. Once you accept that science and humanities are two different kinds of knowing, you are committed to the second-order bifurcation that splits hard sociology (quantitative, causal) from soft sociology (interpretive, cultural). Halewood, drawing on Whitehead's pre-Kantian metaphysics, argues that this split is unsustainable: "the social is natural" (in Durkheim's sense, recovered against Parsons).

A fourth, more diffuse, dimension is the influence on the canon itself. Halewood is explicit that "the ideas of Kant and Hegel stretched into classical social theory through the various filters of writers such as Dilthey and Rickert, among others" (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 8). The very category of "the social" (*des Sozialen*) that Weber problematised carries Kantian residues. The Durkheimian concept of "collective representations" is closer to Kant's transcendental categories than is usually admitted.

The most important counter-argument is that the neo-Kantian inheritance is precisely what *gave sociology its identity*. Without Rickert and Dilthey, Weber's account of meaningful action does not exist; without that account, the Frankfurt School, ethnomethodology, symbolic interactionism, and most of cultural sociology are all unintelligible. I take this seriously. The point of critique, in the strong, structural sense, is not to abolish the neo-Kantian inheritance but to evaluate its structure and to suggest improvements. Halewood's project is exactly this: he wants to keep what is alive in Weber's precision and Simmel's "Sociology of Forms" while jettisoning the metaphysical bifurcation that the neo-Kantians smuggled in.

In conclusion, the neo-Kantians have influenced sociology in three main ways: they bequeathed the methodological apparatus of meaning-oriented social science (*Verstehen*, ideal types, value-relevance); they installed the fact-value distinction as a cornerstone of the discipline; and they shaped the canon through the channels of Weber and Simmel. The first contribution remains valuable, the second is the source of recurring crisis, and the third has had to be supplemented by recovering thinkers (Du Bois, Dussel, Whitehead) who were never inside the neo-Kantian conversation. To answer the question properly is therefore not just to *describe* the influence but to *critique* it in the structural-evaluative sense and to point to the alternatives that a non-bifurcated philosophy of the social would open up.

The mill in Manchester runs on the plantation in Barbados; the same operation produces both.

The third move is to name the myth of progress as the operation that hides this. The myth tells modernity as a single forward arc and files the harms under a separate heading marked "regrettable past". Halewood's bifurcation thesis lets us specify what the myth actually does: it splits the description of modernity into a fact side (industry, science, rationalisation) and a value side (slavery, racial extraction, coloniality), and treats the two as belonging to separate realms. "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (Whitehead, 1933: 217). The myth keeps the slave ship on the far side of the gulf from the steam engine.

A concrete contemporary illustration. When commentators tell the story of modern global supply chains, they typically front-load the rational planning, the algorithmic logistics, the AI-assisted scheduling, and back-load the labour conditions and resource extraction as questions of "ethics" to be addressed in a separate audit. The shape of the narrative is the same shape Du Bois diagnosed: rationality up front, coercion as appendix. Brexit-era debates about Britain's "great industrial heritage" reproduce the operation by celebrating cotton-mill engineering while keeping the cotton's provenance offstage.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: treating modernity as essentially coercive risks anti-progress nihilism. I reject the dichotomy. Du Bois did not deny that modernity produced Shakespeare; he insisted it produced the slave trade in the same breath. Dussel makes the same move: the critique is not refusal but historicisation, breaking the "myth of modernity" so that emancipation and violence can be told together (Dussel, 1993). Harding refines this methodologically: conventional value-freedom is "*not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128) precisely because it lets the conditions of rationality sit on the further side of the bifurcation.

In conclusion, modernity does both, and the myth of progress is the bifurcation that makes this look like two separate questions when it is one. Rationality and coercion in modernity are co-produced; sociology's job is to refuse the bifurcation rather than to choose a side. Du Bois's 1915 paragraph is still the cleanest statement of that refusal.

The fact-value split, the public-private split, the human-animal split, the European-non-European split each pass into common sense in the same way. Imagination is what restores their contingency.

The fourth move is to specify what active imagination looks like in practice. Three features. First, it pictures alternatives rather than only diagnosing problems. Beauvoir does not only describe women's subordination; she imagines a social theory grounded in women's experience. Du Bois (a Beauvoirian avant la lettre) does not only describe the slave trade; in *The Negro* he reorganises the timeline of modernity itself, dating it to 1450 and placing the African coast at the centre. Second, it makes the operation of common sense visible. Beauvoir's account makes the assumption "men are the human, women are the other" available for criticism. Third, it commits to revision: Whitehead's "the art of free society consists [...] in fearlessness of revision" (Whitehead, 1927: 88) is the methodological corollary.

The hinge is the word *change*; without it, sociology halts at description.

In conclusion, the sociological imagination is active when it pictures alternatives, when it makes common sense available for question, and when it commits to revision rather than to summary. Beauvoir is the model; Mills is the floor not the ceiling.

### M8: "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it." Critically discuss with reference to Halewood's account of bifurcation.

I take Whitehead's sentence from *Adventures of Ideas* (Whitehead, 1933: 217) as the conceptual hinge of Halewood's whole project. My thesis is that the quote is exactly right but only becomes critically useful once one notices what it *does not* say: it does not say that the two sides of a gulf are incommunicable, only that they are *opaque* to one another. Halewood's account of bifurcation builds on this asymmetry. The gulfs of modernity (fact/value, sex/gender, science/humanities, race/ethnicity, language/world, public/private) are not impassable, they are *forgetful*. Each side stops asking what the other is doing. The job of critique is to remember the question.

The first move is to anchor the quote in Halewood's six bifurcations. The catalogue is precise. Once you split fact from value, the people who do facts stop asking after the values that selected them; once you split sex from gender, the people who do gender stop asking after the body. The gulf is *epistemic* before it is metaphysical: it organises ignorance. Halewood reads Whitehead as diagnosing the *productivity* of this ignorance: modern knowledge advances on each side of the gulf because nobody is asking the awkward questions across it. The Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is the emblem: a map that already shows a coastal, oceanic, slave-trading Atlantic, and a twentieth-century art-historical commentary that "only briefly mentions slavery." The gulf is doing work, keeping the slave trade on the further side of an aesthetic appreciation of cartography.

The second move is to test the quote against the fact-value distinction, which Halewood treats as the cornerstone bifurcation. The neo-Kantian inheritance gave Weber a way of separating *Wertfreiheit* in description from value-relevance in topic-selection. The doctrine is elegant on one side; on the other there is silence about who chose the topics, and why. French's *Born in Blackness* (French, 2021) is full of facts the standard sociology of modernity could not see: 600 kg of West African gold per year by the 1490s, the Kimbundu-Spanish grammar published in 1629, 40% of plantation-enslaved people sent to Brazil. A discipline that could not look across the gulf simply did not see them.

The third move is to specify what Halewood adds to Whitehead. Whitehead's quote is metaphysical; Halewood's bifurcation thesis is *sociological*. Bifurcation, for Halewood, is not an unfortunate by-product of conceptual elegance but an active organising principle of modern *institutions*. The modern university institutionalises the science/humanities gulf in buildings and journal hierarchies; the modern state institutionalises the public/private gulf in law and policing; the colonial archive institutionalises the language/world gulf in maps and censuses. Carter and Charles extend exactly this point to the animal-human binary, calling for "a reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The animal/human gulf is the same operation in another register: it is institutionalised in the carve-up between biology departments and social-science departments.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously is the pragmatist one: gulfs may be useful simplifications. We *need* to distinguish fact from value to make policy; we *need* to distinguish public from private to think about rights. I accept the practical force of this. But practical contrasts are not metaphysical bifurcations. Halewood's critique is aimed at the latter, at the moment when a working contrast hardens into two separate realms and the question of what happens between them disappears. Whitehead's verb is "is very difficult to know," not "is impossible to know." The gulf can be looked across; the difficulty is institutional, not principled.

A deeper objection: the metaphor assumes there *was* something single before the splitting. Is this not a romantic origin-story? The Whiteheadian reply is no: the "one thing" was never an original unity but a manner of association, a *how* that was working before modern thought tried to freeze it into a *what*. The recovery is adverbial, not nostalgic.

In conclusion, Whitehead's gulf-quote is correct, and Halewood's account of bifurcation is the right gloss. The opacity of the further side is not a failure of perception; it is the productive ignorance that modern institutions require. Critique in the strong structural sense (evaluate the structure, suggest alternatives) is precisely the work of refusing the gulf, of asking what is happening on the further side and how the splitting itself came to organise our knowledge. Every bifurcation thesis is also a Whiteheadian one.

### M9: Critically assess Halewood's argument in *Rethinking the Social through Durkheim, Marx, Weber and Whitehead* that twentieth-century sociology has forgotten the open-endedness of the classical canon.

I argue that Halewood's argument is largely correct: the twentieth-century reception of Durkheim, Marx and Weber turned each into the author of a *settled* doctrine about "society," when in fact each was struggling with the very concept. My thesis is that the forgetting was a *disciplinary* necessity: sociology needed a clean object to legitimate itself as a university discipline, and the price was the suppression of the canon's most interesting tensions. To rethink the social today is to remember what classical sociology *almost* said but did not finish saying.

The first move is textual. Halewood reads each founder against the textbook version. Durkheim is not the theorist of an autonomous "social realm" floating above biology; he is the theorist who insists that *the social is natural*, "an aspect of nature" abstracted by sociology in the same way biology abstracts life (Halewood, 2014: 124-125). The English title *The Division of Labour in Society* misleads: Durkheim wrote *De la division du travail social*. The adjective is on labour, not on society. The textbook Durkheim of "society sui generis" is a Parsonian artefact, not a Durkheimian one.

The second move is Marxian. Marx "was not a professional sociologist" and refused to define "society" once and for all. He uses *sozial* and *gesellschaftlich* in tension to preserve a distinction between prestige-and-esteem relations and the historically specific form of capitalist association. Twentieth-century reception flattens this into a single doctrine of "the social formation" in which capitalism is the answer and the question is closed. Halewood reads Marx through the chemistry analogy of butyric acid and propyl formate: same atoms, different arrangement, different thing. Society is not a layer; it is a manner of association whose specificity has to be historicised. "In his later texts, Marx refers less and less to society ('Gesellschaft') and relies more and more upon adjectival forms" (Halewood, 2014: 130). The textbook Marx misses this drift.

The third move is Weberian. Weber alone gave a precise concept, and only at the end of his life. Only three things are properly "social" for Weber: social action, social relationships, and social selection. He refuses to talk loosely of "social conditions" or "social factors." What the textbook forgets is Weber's insistence that the *concept itself* was contested: "the term 'social' [...] turns out to have [...] a particular specifically colored though often indefinite meaning. Its 'generality' rests on nothing but its ambiguity" (Weber, 1949 [1904]: 68; cited in Halewood, 2014: 131).

The fourth move is Simmelian, and it is where Halewood's argument cuts deepest. Simmel was *inside* the founding generation yet excluded from the canonical trio. His claim that "Society' 'is an abstraction [...] it is no real *object*" (Simmel, 1950: 4) was precisely the kind of unsettlement the twentieth-century discipline could not afford. To include Simmel is to admit that the founders disagreed about whether sociology had an object at all. Du Bois belongs in the same diagnosis: *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) had already proposed "double consciousness," "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), as the structural feature of the modern racialised self that Weber's, Goffman's and Garfinkel's unitary actor could not register. The canon's forgetting of Du Bois is the same disciplinary operation as its forgetting of Simmel.

Where Halewood's argument is contestable. Two reservations. First, the forgetting might be structural-institutional rather than theoretical: sociology had to compete with economics and political science for university territory, and a clean object was a competitive necessity. Second, the recovery move risks aestheticising the founders' uncertainty: Marx's failure to define society might be unfinished business subsequent Marxists were right to finish, not wisdom.

A counter-argument: maybe twentieth-century sociology was right to settle the concept. Parsons gave us a working framework of "social order" within which the discipline could build cumulative knowledge. My reply is that the very problems with which the founders wrestled are precisely the problems where the settled concept fails. The Parsonian settlement relies on an "irreconcilable split between the social and the natural" (Halewood, 2014: 124) not actually found in Durkheim. The recovery is a tool for the present.

In conclusion, Halewood's argument is well-evidenced and politically timely. It allows us to read Durkheim, Marx, Weber and Simmel as resources for *current* disputes rather than as monuments. The recovery has to be done carefully (the founders were not pre-emptive Whiteheadians) but Halewood's textual scrupulousness is the right way to do the work.

### M10: What does Halewood mean by bifurcation, and why does he treat it as the cornerstone of modernity?

I take the question in two parts. The first is conceptual: what is bifurcation, as Halewood uses the term? The second is structural: why does the concept earn the status of *cornerstone*? My thesis is that Halewood means by bifurcation a specific *operation*, the splitting of one thing into two such that a gulf opens, and that he treats it as the cornerstone because this operation produces both the cognitive architecture of modern thought *and* the institutional architecture of modern life, in a single move.

**Part one: what does Halewood mean by bifurcation?** The term is borrowed from Whitehead's *Adventures of Ideas* (Whitehead, 1933). In Whitehead it is a metaphysical category: the splitting of nature into two branches, one with primary qualities (mass, motion) and the other secondary ones (colour, sound). Halewood inherits the operation and generalises it, applying it to six modern pairings: fact/value, public/private, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, language/world, science/humanities. Each is a single phenomenon that modern thought has insisted on dividing into two branches treated as belonging to different realms. Carter and Charles (2018) extend the catalogue, identifying the animal/human binary as a seventh and arguably foundational bifurcation that has organised sociology around "anthroponormativity" since Durkheim, treating animals as mute "nature" rather than as constitutive of social life. The list is open; the operation is one.

The crucial conceptual move is the gulf. "The worst of a gulf is, that it is very difficult to know what is happening of the further side of it" (Whitehead, 1933: 217). Bifurcation is not mere distinction-making; it is the *hardening* of a distinction into two separate realms, with traffic between them controlled or stopped. Once fact and value live on different sides of a gulf, the question of which facts deserve attention disappears. Bifurcations also *produce their own appearance of obviousness*: after several generations the splitting seems natural, and the naturalisation is itself an effect of the operation.

**Part two: why does Halewood treat bifurcation as the cornerstone of modernity?** First, *architecturally*. A cornerstone aligns the rest of the building. Without bifurcation, the rest of modern thought does not stand. Modern science requires the fact-value split to legitimate value-free description; the modern university requires the science-humanities split to organise faculties; the modern state requires the public-private split to demarcate rights from regulation; the modern colonial project requires the race-ethnicity split to manage populations. Remove the cornerstone and these institutions lose their alignment.

Second, *genealogically*. Halewood traces the bifurcations to a specific philosophical lineage: Kant and Hegel through the neo-Kantian filters of Dilthey and Rickert, into Weber and Simmel, and onward into the bones of the discipline (Halewood, 2014: 143). Whitehead's own philosophy is "a recurrence to pre-Kantian modes of thought" (Whitehead, 1978: xi; cited in Halewood, 2014: 143), which is what makes him useful: he is outside the lineage that installed the bifurcations.

Third, *politically*. Bifurcation does work. The split between civilisation and slavery in the standard narrative is what allows Shakespeare and the slave ship to coexist in textbooks without provoking the obvious question. Du Bois's 1915 essay refuses this: the *same* day cannot have two sides (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). The bifurcation made it possible for the discipline to teach industrial modernity without teaching the plantation.

A counter-argument: why privilege bifurcation over other candidate cornerstones (capital, the nation-state, instrumental reason)? My reply is that the choice is not exclusive. Each of these is itself bifurcation-structured: capital splits use-value from exchange-value, the nation-state splits citizen from foreigner, instrumental reason splits means from ends. Bifurcation is the operation that *each* runs at its core. The operation, not the institution, is the cornerstone.

A second objection: is the cornerstone metaphor itself dangerously architectural? Does it assume a static structure? Halewood's Whiteheadian commitment is to process. The reply is that a cornerstone in process metaphysics is itself a manner of association: the way modern thought *keeps* aligning itself by repeating the splitting operation. The operation is ongoing, not finished.

In conclusion, by bifurcation Halewood means the operation of splitting one phenomenon into two branches separated by a gulf, naturalised over time. He treats it as the cornerstone of modernity because it aligns the cognitive, institutional and political architecture of the modern world in a single move. The way forward is the adverbial sociology Part 2 of *Rethinking the Social* outlines: sociology done in the mode of the *how* rather than the *what*, with the manner of association as the proper object of the discipline.

### M11: Compare and contrast Du Bois's account of slavery in *The Negro* (1915) with Marx's account of the slavery-cotton-industry chain in *Capital*.

I argue that the two accounts are *parallel but incommensurable*: they agree that slavery is constitutive of modern industry, but differ in *how* they constitute it. My thesis is that Marx's chain-of-conditions argument is a *causal-structural* claim and Du Bois's "blot on modern human history" argument is a *moral-civilisational* claim, and the most powerful reading of modernity requires holding both at once without collapsing one into the other.

The texts. Marx, in *Capital* and the letter to Annenkov, gives the chain: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Du Bois, in *The Negro* (1915), gives the indictment: "For four hundred years, from 1450 to 1850, European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings [...] It was the day of the greatest expansion of two of the world's most pretentious religions and of the beginnings of the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90).

**Compare. The shared core.** Both refuse to bifurcate slavery from modern industry. Marx: the chain is one chain; remove slavery and it collapses. Du Bois: the slave trade is contemporaneous with Shakespeare, Luther, Raphael; not separable from "the beginnings of the modern organization of industry." Both refuse the standard move by which slavery is treated as a regrettable adjunct to modern progress.

**Contrast. The explanatory structure.** Marx's argument is *causal-structural*: slavery to cotton to industry to world trade to large-scale industry. Each link is a necessary condition for the next. Du Bois's argument is *civilisational-moral*. He gives no chain of necessary conditions; he gives a moral indictment of "European civilization" *as such* during the four-century period. The slave trade is not just one input to a system; it is the moral fact about modernity that subsequent history cannot palliate.

**Contrast. Scope.** Marx's frame is capitalist modernity; slavery matters because it underwrites capital. Du Bois's frame is modern human history as a whole: he names Shakespeare, Luther, Raphael, Haroun-al-Raschid, Lincoln to insist the slave trade is constitutive of modern *civilisation*, not just modern *economy*. The Du Boisian frame is broader; the Marxian sharper.

**Contrast. What each makes politically actionable.** Marx's chain makes the abolition of capital the route to addressing slavery's modern legacies. Du Bois's civilisational-moral argument refuses this. The wrongs of the slave trade are not exhausted by the conditions that produced them; "we may excuse and palliate it, and write history so as to let men forget it," Du Bois warns, but "it remains the most inexcusable and despicable blot on modern human history" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). The Du Boisian indictment commits modern civilisation to a moral reckoning that survives the abolition of capital itself. *The Souls of Black Folk* makes the same point at the level of selfhood: the colour line is "the problem of the Twentieth Century" (Du Bois, 1903: 1), and double consciousness is its lived sediment. The structural account in *The Negro* and the phenomenological account in *Souls* are one project.

**Convergence on bifurcation.** Both writers refuse the fact-value bifurcation that the standard story of modern industry relies on. Marx will not allow the fact of cotton to be told without the fact of slavery; Du Bois will not allow the values of Renaissance art to be told without the violence of the slave ship. In Halewood's adverbial terms, both work on the *manner* in which modernity associates its goods with its harms.

A counter-argument: Marx's structural-causal account is *more rigorous* and Du Bois's account, while powerful, is merely rhetorical. I reject the dichotomy. Du Bois's 1915 essay is empirically dense: dates, institutions, comparative cultural geographies. The moral register does not displace the analytic; it gives the analytic its point. Marx's chain-statement is itself a moral indictment of "modern industry" as much as a causal claim. The two writers differ in *balance*, not in whether they have both.

In conclusion, Du Bois and Marx converge in refusing to bifurcate slavery from modern industry and diverge in the structure of the argument they offer for the refusal. Both are needed: the chain to identify the structural targets of change, the indictment to keep the question of modern civilisation's debt unforgettable. The question "what would sociology be like if we had taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously?" is best answered by holding the two accounts together, not by choosing.

### M12: How does the concept of bifurcation help us analyse the contemporary divide between climate science and climate politics?

I argue that the divide between climate science and climate politics is a textbook case of modern bifurcation: fact/value, science/humanities, and (most fundamentally) the bifurcation between nature and the social that Halewood diagnoses as constitutive of modern thought. My thesis is that Halewood's concept lets us see *why* the description of a planetary threat does not translate into political response: not because the public is irrational, but because the institutional architecture of modern knowledge has spent two centuries learning to separate description from decision, fact from value, the natural from the social, and is now incapable of treating the planet and the polity as a single problem.

Climate politics sits on the "value" side: it argues about priorities, distributions, sacrifices. The two sides can be brought back together, but the architecture makes it hard.

The third move is to specify the second bifurcation in play: the split between nature and the social. Carter and Charles make exactly this point for sociology's treatment of non-human animals: they call for "a reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). A non-bifurcated approach would let us treat the planet and the industrial-economic-political nexus as a single society in Whitehead's sense. The bifurcation forbids this; one discipline models the atmosphere, another the economy, coordinated only by the assumption that they belong to separate orders of reality.

The fourth move is institutional. Bifurcations, on Halewood's account, are not just conceptual errors. They are institutionalised in "the modern university [in] buildings, hiring lines, journal hierarchies," in the modern state, and in the modern colonial archive. The fact-value gulf does *work* for institutions whose authority depends on appearing value-free; the science-humanities split organises faculties and funding councils. To diagnose the climate divide as a bifurcation is therefore to name a structural feature of how modern knowledge is produced and distributed.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: maybe the divide between description and decision is *necessary*; we should not let scientists decide policy. I take this seriously. The point of the critique is not to abolish the working distinction between an empirical assessment and a democratic choice; it is to refuse the *bifurcation* that converts that working distinction into two separate realms. A working contrast can survive without hardening into a gulf. The scientist's description is value-laden in its selections (which problems, which timescales, which uncertainties to report); the citizen's decision is fact-saturated in its constraints. The "two-realms" picture is what hides this mutual implication. Whitehead's adverbial alternative (asking *how* description and decision are mutually constituted, not *what* they each separately are) is what would let the two sides re-engage.

In conclusion, Halewood's concept of bifurcation diagnoses the climate impasse with unusual precision. The fact/value and nature/society bifurcations together produce the contemporary divide, and this divide is not primarily a communication failure but a structural feature of modern knowledge. The way forward is an adverbial sociology that treats the planetary and the political as a single manner of association rather than as two realms whose translation can be deferred.

### M13: Is the fact-value distinction a coherent epistemological position or an ideological alibi? Discuss.

I argue that the fact-value distinction is *both* a coherent epistemological position (at the level of working methodological contrast) *and* an ideological alibi (at the level of metaphysical bifurcation), and that the trick is to distinguish the two levels rather than to choose between them. My thesis is that Halewood's bifurcation thesis lets us hold this double judgement: keep the working contrast, refuse the bifurcated metaphysics, name the alibi-function precisely where the metaphysics is doing the political work.

The first move is to take the "coherent epistemological position" reading seriously. Weber's *Wertfreiheit* is a methodological discipline, not a metaphysical doctrine. At the level of writing, the distinction between an empirical claim ("the unemployment rate rose by 0.3% last quarter") and a normative claim ("this is a moral catastrophe") is real and useful. The discipline of separating the two is what makes scholarly description differ from pamphleteering. In this minimal sense the distinction is coherent: it picks out two genuinely different illocutionary acts.

The neo-Kantian inheritance, traced in Halewood's chapter 8, gave this minimal distinction its philosophical scaffolding. Rickert and Dilthey distinguished *Naturwissenschaften* from *Geisteswissenschaften* by the kind of question they ask: causal-explanatory versus meaning-and-value oriented. Weber radicalised this into the doctrine of value-freedom *within* the social sciences themselves. The doctrine has internal coherence; it can be taught, applied, and falsified.

The second move is to ask what the doctrine *does* at the level of metaphysical bifurcation rather than working contrast. Once the distinction hardens from a working contrast into two separate realms ("facts" here, "values" there), it stops being a methodological discipline and starts being an ontological commitment. The world really does come in two kinds; sociology straddles the gap; certain questions cannot be asked because they would violate the gap.

At this level the distinction begins to function as an alibi. The most consequential case is the standard sociology of modernity. The "facts" of the rise of industry are reported; the "values" of the slave trade are filed elsewhere: moral lament, ethics class, regrettable adjunct. The bifurcation does the alibi-work: it allows a sociology that *literally* describes modernity without describing its constitutive features. Du Bois's 1915 indictment refuses this alibi: "in the midst of this advance and uplift this slave trade and slavery spread more human misery [...] than can well be calculated" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). The slave trade is not a value-question to be added later; it is a fact about modernity. The bifurcation hides it.

The third move is to specify the *political economy* of the alibi. Bifurcations persist when they serve material interests. The fact-value bifurcation persists because it does work for institutions whose authority depends on appearing value-free: the modern university, the state's statistical apparatus, the technocratic edifices whose pronouncements gain authority from their alleged disinterestedness. These institutions cannot say "we are politically committed"; they say "we report facts." The value-laden choices they actually make (which facts to count, which timescales, which populations) are insulated as the technical machinery of fact-production.

A counter-argument. A Weberian might reply that without *Wertfreiheit* sociology collapses into political pamphleteering. I accept the worry. The reply is that the critique is not aimed at the working contrast but at the metaphysical bifurcation. The Weberian discipline of separating description from prescription can survive; what cannot survive is the additional, smuggled claim that the two kinds of statement belong to two separate realms of reality. Harding's "strong objectivity" articulates the alternative precisely: the conventional ideal is "*not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128) because it polices only those values researchers disagree about, leaving culture-wide assumptions undisturbed. Strong objectivity widens scrutiny to include the knower; reflexive disclosure replaces the alibi.

A second counter-argument. A pragmatist might say the alibi-charge is overblown because in practice scientists and policymakers do communicate. The asymmetry of that communication is precisely Whitehead's point about gulfs. Communication slow, distorted by value-freedom on the description side, and one-directional is what the alibi-reading predicts.

In conclusion, the fact-value distinction is a coherent epistemological position at the level of working methodological contrast and an ideological alibi at the level of metaphysical bifurcation. Halewood's diagnostic (keep the contrast, refuse the bifurcation) is the right one. Any question framed as "Is X coherent or ideological?" should be answered by *distinguishing the levels at which X operates* rather than by picking one of the two horns. That is what critique-not-criticism looks like.

### M14: Critique or criticism?

I argue that the question forces a choice it should not force: critique and criticism are not equally weighted alternatives but an asymmetric pair in which critique is the disciplined sociological operation and criticism its degraded cousin. My thesis is that sociology at its best is *critique* and that the disciplinary slogan "be critical" has slowly translated into the more impoverished "find fault," with measurable costs.

The distinction. "Criticise" means to find fault. "Critique" means (i) to evaluate the structure, (ii) to see what works well, (iii) to suggest clarifications, (iv) to suggest improvements. The two words look like synonyms in everyday English; in the discipline they name two different operations.

What criticism does. Criticism identifies a problem and stops. It is the move of saying "this is wrong" without saying what would be right. It can mobilise opposition, name harms, puncture complacency. But it has a characteristic exhaustion: after the third or fourth iteration on the same target, the audience knows the target is bad and has no new way to think about it. A discipline confined to criticism would interpret the world without producing the conceptual moves to change it.

What critique does. Critique identifies a *structure* and asks what would change if the structure were different. Marx's *Capital* is critique: it specifies the manner of association of capitalist labour and asks what an alternative would look like. *Theses on Feuerbach* XI makes the imperative explicit: "the philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it" (Marx, 1977: 158). Du Bois's 1915 essay is critique: it specifies the structural character of European modernity and refuses the alibi by which subsequent history "let men forget it" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). Halewood's *Rethinking the Social* is critique: it specifies which moves of the canon were lost.

The first reason critique is the right answer. Critique produces *new conceptual moves*; criticism produces only verdicts. The worry is that a generation of sociology students fluent in "being critical" becomes good at identifying problems and incapable of imagining alternatives. The Beauvoirian sociological imagination ("she imagined what social theory would look like if it took women seriously") is critique in active form. It does not just diagnose the absence of women; it pictures what their inclusion would change.

The second reason. Critique respects what works. The checklist includes "see what works well" as a separate point. Any structure worth critiquing has features worth preserving. Halewood says Durkheim "should be approached with caution but also with admiration" (Halewood, 2014: 127). Marx's critique of capital takes seriously the productivity of modern industry at the same time as it indicts its conditions. Du Bois places Shakespeare and Lincoln alongside the slave trade; he critiques modern civilisation, not denies its achievements. Criticism has only one register; critique varies it.

The third reason. Critique points to alternatives. Change requires a destination, not just a complaint. Critique closes by pointing somewhere; criticism closes by listing what is wrong. The first is generative; the second is exhausting.

**Example. Modernity itself.** The standard student-essay critique of "modernity" is often actually criticism: modernity is colonial, modernity is bifurcated, modernity is alienating. All true; all unproductive on their own. Halewood asks for critique: identify the structure (bifurcations, the manner of association, the suppressed canon), evaluate what works, and point to an alternative (an adverbial, Whiteheadian, decolonised sociology). Dussel's "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993) is critique in this strong sense: he names the structure by which modernity packages emancipation with a concealed irrational violence on a "civilising" mission, and he points beyond it to "trans-modernity," alternative routes that refuse Europe as sole template. The first move would have been only to indict; Dussel does both.

A counter-argument: the distinction is a semantic gloss, and in practice every critique includes moments of criticism. I partly accept this. The point is not that critique excludes fault-finding but that the *organising frame* must be structural-evaluative, not merely fault-finding.

A second objection: the discipline needs *more* criticism. Criticism is cheap; it requires only that a problem be named. What is in short supply is critique: the patient, structural, alternative-imagining work academic sociology is uniquely placed to do.

In conclusion, the answer is critique, not because criticism is wrong but because critique includes everything criticism does and more. The four-point checklist (structure, what works, clarifications, improvements) is the disciplinary form of synoptic vision. Every answer should close by *pointing somewhere*, not by listing what is wrong. That is what makes the writing critique.

### M15: The myth of progress in 2026: does the concept still do critical work?

I argue that the myth of progress still does critical work, and that the work is essentially the work Du Bois named in 1915: to identify the operation by which modernity tells the story of its goods without telling the story of its constitutive harms. My thesis is that the concept, read through Halewood's bifurcation thesis, names a *split* between the parts of modern history we are willing to count as progress and the parts we file as collateral, and this operation is alive whenever modernity narrates itself as a single arc of improvement.

The concept in its classical formulation. The myth of progress is a way of telling modernity as a single forward arc: from superstition to science, from feudalism to capitalism, from local trade to industry. The conventional account associates modernity with "rationality and industrialisation as part of the economic / social / cultural development of Europe." The myth is the move that lets the conventional account stand without the coercion and unequal exchange.

Du Bois (1915) named the myth's blind spot a century ago:

> "For four hundred years, from 1450 to 1850, European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings [...] These were not days of decadence, but a period that gave the world Shakespeare, Martin Luther, and Raphael, Haroun-al-Raschid and Abraham Lincoln. It was the day of the greatest expansion of two of the world's most pretentious religions and of the beginnings of the modern organization of industry." (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90)

The progress story is true on one side; the slave trade is true on the other. The myth is the act of telling only the first.

The first dimension of the concept's critical work is to refuse the bifurcation of "advance and uplift" from "human misery." Du Bois says the *same* civilisation that produced Shakespeare produced the slave ship. Marx makes the conceptual machinery visible: "Without slavery you have no cotton; without cotton you have no modern industry. It is slavery that gave the colonies their value; it is the colonies that created world trade and it is world trade that is the pre-condition of large-scale industry" (Marx, 1977: 203). Industry, on this reading, *is* slavery refigured upstream. The myth of progress is the bifurcation that lets industry be filed separately.

The second dimension is to refuse the temporal alibi. The myth puts the harms in the past: yes, slavery happened; we have moved on. French's *Born in Blackness* (French, 2021) refuses this. The features French documents are constitutive of the global, oceanic, mercantile modernity that the standard story misrepresents as a later European industrial achievement. Dussel sharpens the diagnosis by naming the conceptual machinery: the "myth of modernity" (Dussel, 1993) packages emancipation with a concealed irrational violence justified as a "civilising" mission, and dates modernity to 1492 rather than the industrial revolution. Du Bois's "advance and uplift" and Dussel's "myth" are two names for the same operation; the Cantino Planisphere of 1502 is the visual proof.

The third dimension is conceptual. The myth relies on the fact-value bifurcation. The "facts" of progress are reported; the "values" of who paid are filed elsewhere. This filing is the operation by which modernity hides its conditions of possibility. The cost-of-living crisis works the same way: GDP growth is filed as fact, who pays as value.

Why the concept still does critical work. Because the operation it names is still active. Wherever the conditions of "progress" are filed as "values" while the achievements are filed as "facts," the myth is doing the work Du Bois diagnosed. The concept is portable: it works against contemporary tech-progress narratives as readily as against nineteenth-century industrial ones.

A counter-argument: the critique risks anti-progress nihilism. The reply is that the concept is *critique*, not refusal. The alternative is honest progress-talk: a description of modernity that counts goods *and* harms together. Du Bois did not deny modernity produced Shakespeare; he insisted it produced the slave trade in the same period.

A second counter-argument: the concept is itself Eurocentric. I disagree. Du Bois showed it is most powerful precisely when used from outside the standard progress story. French's project of "inverting the current view" is the same operation.

In conclusion, the myth of progress still does critical work: it keeps visible the bifurcation by which modernity narrates its goods without its harms. The tool was sharpened by Du Bois in 1915; the empirical evidence extended by French; Halewood's bifurcation thesis names the deeper operation.

### M16: Does the rise of generative AI vindicate or refute Whitehead's claim that "ideas are things"?

I argue that Whitehead's claim, as Halewood reconstructs it, is intended to do conceptual work that the rise of any technology (including generative AI) might *illustrate* but cannot *settle*: the claim is metaphysical, not predictive. My thesis is that asking whether generative AI vindicates or refutes "ideas are things" is the wrong question; the right question is what Whitehead's claim asks us to *see* in a phenomenon like generative AI that we would otherwise miss. Read this way, the claim is neither vindicated nor refuted by new technology; it is *put to work* by it, and the work is to dissolve a bifurcation, not to make a forecast.

The Whiteheadian claim, recalled from Halewood's Part 2. Halewood, drawing on Stengers, glosses Whitehead as "ideas are things" (Halewood, 2014: 144). Ideas, for Whitehead, are not the contents of human minds. They have *adventures*; they combine with humans, with practices, with materials; they enter and exit different epochs. Conscious discrimination is the essence of humanity but only the accident of human existence: "It makes us human. But it does not make us exist" (Whitehead, 1938: 158). Humans are "vehicles which enabled ideas to come into efficacy" (Halewood, 2014: 145), not the originating source of ideas.

What "ideas are things" is *for*. Halewood's reading of Whitehead is that the claim refuses the bifurcation between thought and stuff. The neo-Kantian inheritance places ideas on one side (the realm of meaning) and material things on the other (the realm of cause). Whitehead's whole metaphysics is anti-bifurcatory; his philosophy is "a recurrence to pre-Kantian modes of thought" (Halewood, 2014: 143). Ideas are not the contents of a separate mental realm; they are aspects of process in the world.

Where generative AI sharpens the point. A technology that materialises linguistic regularities and propagates them across the population is a case in which "ideas are things" is hard to evade. The neo-Kantian default treats AI either as "just a machine" or as "really thinking." Both readings keep the bifurcation intact. Whitehead's claim refuses both: the technology is one moment in the career of an idea with no existence apart from its successive material-historical embodiments. Carter and Charles make the cognate move for non-human animals: their critique of "anthroponormativity" insists animals are "constitutive" of social life (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80), not its background. The bifurcation refused in both cases is the same: thought versus stuff, human versus everything else.

The deeper vindication: ideas have careers that exceed their human vehicles. Whitehead's example was the slow transformation by which "Fragmentary intellectual agencies co-operated blindly to turn apes into men [sic]" (Whitehead, 1933: 9; cited in Halewood, 2014: 145). Ideas combine with humans without being reducible to human intentions.

Where the claim resists the framing. To ask whether generative AI "vindicates or refutes" Whitehead would be to treat his metaphysics as a predictive theory and the technology as evidence. Halewood is explicit that a philosophy of the social "is not intended to provide a comprehensive theory of the social which solves or explains, once and for all, all the facets of the problem of the social" (Halewood, 2014: 139-140). It is a conceptual *tool*, not a hypothesis.

A counter-argument. One might object that Whitehead's "adventures" imply a kind of agency for ideas that any modern technology disconfirms: a model has no goals. My reply is that Whitehead's "adventures" never required teleological agency: "ideas did not themselves have foresight [...] but they were the primary factor in this process" (Halewood, 2014: 145). The Whiteheadian point is not that ideas have wills but that they have *trajectories* through different materialisations; this is exactly what a technology that propagates linguistic patterns at scale exhibits.

A second objection: ascribing "ideas" to a non-human thing risks reification. I disagree. Whitehead's claim is *anti*-reification: it refuses to make ideas a separate realm requiring a special mode of existence. To say a technology is one site in the career of an idea is to insist that ideas are in the world, made of stuff, available for analysis.

In conclusion, generative AI does not vindicate or refute Whitehead's claim that "ideas are things"; it puts the claim to work by making the bifurcation between thought and stuff hard to maintain. Whitehead's metaphysics is a conceptual tool for rethinking the social, not a forecast about technology. The exam payoff: refuse the framing that turns a metaphysical claim into a predictive hypothesis; ask what the claim asks us to *see* in a phenomenon that we would otherwise miss.

### M17: How convincing do you find Simmel's account of sociation? Use specific examples.

I find Simmel's account of sociation broadly convincing as a *negative* move (it correctly refuses the reification of "society" into a quasi-agent) and only partially convincing as a *positive* one, because the dyadic-and-triadic toolkit undersells the macro-structural features sociology needs to name. My thesis is that the sociation concept is necessary but insufficient, and Halewood is right to use it as a stepping stone toward a Whiteheadian account that retains the anti-reification while regaining macrostructural traction.

Simmel's account, recalled. "Society" "is an abstraction [...] it is no real *object*. It does not exist outside and in addition to the individuals and the processes among them" (Simmel, 1950: 4). The positive concept is *Vergesellschaftung*, sociation, the verbal noun for the process by which interactions densify into recognisable patterns. "One should properly speak, not of society, but of *sociation*" (Simmel, 1950: 10). Form, not content, is the sociologist's proper object.

**Example 1. The secret.** A secret is only a secret in relation to at least one other: "this minimum of two, with which the secret ceases to be the property of one individual, is at the same time the maximum at which its preservation is relatively secure" (Simmel 1950: 123; cited in Halewood, 2014: 134). The form (concealment from some, disclosure to others) makes the content sociologically interesting. The unit of analysis is the reciprocal relation, not the individual or the society. The same logic applies to gossip on Instagram or a leaked group chat: the form is what matters.

**Example 2. The dyad and the triad.** Simmel's larger claim: "we should not start with individuals but with the relations between what we consider to be individuals" (Halewood, 2014: 134). The triad introduces a third person; the conceptual centre (reciprocal relation as primary) remains. Individuals are an outcome of association, not its starting point.

**Example 3. Sociation as a verbal noun.** *Vergesellschaftung*, in Halewood's reading, "is a noun which incorporates the notion of movement and process" (Halewood, 2014: 135). The grammatical form is the conceptual claim: society is a *doing*, not a thing.

Where I am less convinced. The sociation account works best when the relations are *reciprocal*. It works less well when they are *asymmetric in a structural rather than personal sense*. The slave on the plantation and the planter are in a relation; sociation occurs. But the relation is structured by a transnational system of legal status, capital flow, and racial categorisation that exceeds the bilateral relation. Standpoint theory makes the missing move: a feminist standpoint is an achievement won from political struggle that exposes how the dominant group's view attempts to structure the world for everybody (Harding, 2004: 124). Sociation describes the encounter; standpoint describes the asymmetric system that pre-shapes the encounter, which the dyadic toolkit alone cannot reach.

The deeper limitation. Simmel's account is methodologically individualist in a soft sense; it builds society up from dyads and triads. Macro-structures exist but they are reconstructions from the micro. Marx's and Whitehead's accounts go the other direction: the macro-pattern is itself the unit of analysis, and the micro-encounter is one moment in its reproduction. For modernity, paradigmatically a macro-structural phenomenon, the directionality matters.

A counter-argument: defenders of Simmel say the sociation toolkit is more flexible than I credit. Simmel on the stranger, on superordination, on conflict, all reach toward asymmetric relations. I partly accept this. But even there the conceptual centre remains the dyadic relation, and the asymmetry is read off the dyad rather than analysed at its own scale. The plantation is not a large stranger-relation.

A second objection: maybe "society" *is* a real thing, and Durkheim was right. I disagree. Reifying society into a quasi-agent ("society demands") hides agency behind a noun. Simmel's negative work survives.

In conclusion, I find Simmel's account of sociation convincing in the work it does and unconvincing in the work it refuses to do. The dyadic-triadic toolkit captures reciprocal interaction beautifully; the secret-example is the conceptual demonstration that the form of association is the social fact; but the colonial-plantation-modernity Halewood foregrounds requires a macrostructural tool Simmel does not supply. The Whiteheadian alternative keeps Simmel's anti-reification while regaining the macro-scale. Halewood himself signals this when he says Simmel "does not set out a coherent philosophical approach to the problems of the social" but his ideas "are suggestive as to what a 'philosophy of the social' might entail" (Halewood, 2014: 137). The answer to "how convincing" is not yes-or-no but *at what scale and for what kind of relation*.

### M18: Why has the sociological canon been so resistant to the inclusion of Du Bois? Answer with reference to Howard French, *Born in Blackness* (2021).

I argue that the canon's resistance to Du Bois has not been an oversight; it has been a *structural feature* of how sociology was constituted, and French's *Born in Blackness* (French, 2021) names exactly the structural reason: the Eurocentric narrative of modernity which the discipline took as its empirical object excluded the African pre-history of capitalism that Du Bois's 1915 essay had already begun to reconstruct. My thesis is that including Du Bois requires more than a reading list change; it requires inverting the empirical baseline against which the canon was assembled.

The resistance, recapped. Du Bois was a contemporary of the canonical founders. By 1915 he had published a structural account of modernity-as-coloniality whose argumentative form (slavery as constitutive of modern industry) was already available for canonical absorption. The discipline did not absorb it: the Marx-Weber-Durkheim trio became canonical, and Du Bois did not. The counterfactual question ("What would sociology be like if we had taken Du Bois (not just Marx) seriously all along?") presupposes precisely this: the resistance was not personal indifference; it was a structural feature.

French's diagnosis. French's *Born in Blackness* reconstructs the empirical history that the Eurocentric sociology canon assumed away. The point is not that these facts were unknown (they were available in archives) but that the canon was assembled around an empirical baseline that did not include them. Sociology took European industrialisation as its empirical horizon; this horizon required Africa to be peripheral.

The first structural reason. The canon was assembled in European universities whose self-understanding was Eurocentric. Sociology was supposed to explain why Europe had modernised; the question presupposes modernity *is* Europe's accomplishment. Du Bois's 1915 essay refuses the presupposition. To include him was to refuse the question the canon was assembled to answer.

The second structural reason. The fact-value bifurcation organised research priorities. The "facts" of modernisation were European; the slave trade was assigned to "values." Du Bois refused the bifurcation, insisting the slave trade was a fact of modernity. A canon committed to the bifurcation could not absorb him without dismantling itself.

The third structural reason. The canon's geographical centre of gravity was Europe. Du Bois's analytic centre was the Black Atlantic. French's title is precise: *Born in Blackness*. Modernity is born in the African coastal trade, not the European factory. To centre this is to displace the canon's geography.

The fourth structural reason. Du Bois's method refuses the bifurcation between "theory" and "history." A canon organised around the theory-history distinction reads Du Bois as a historian. French's book is in the same tradition: empirical history with a structural-theoretical argument, a form the discipline still has not quite digested. *The Souls of Black Folk* sharpens the point: "double consciousness," "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), is a theoretical concept of the modern self at the same level of generality as Weber's social action, and the canon's refusal to read it as such is the same operation by which French's empirical reconstruction was deferred.

The Du Bois counterfactual operationalises French's empirical inversion. If the canon's baseline had included the African gold trade and the Madeira plantations, Du Bois would not have been excluded, because the canon's central questions would already have been his.

A counter-argument: the canon has now substantially included Du Bois. He appears on syllabi, in journal special issues, in centenary commemorations. I partly accept this. The inclusion is real but the *inversion* (in French's sense) is still pending. Du Bois has been added; the centre of gravity has not shifted. The counterfactual is precisely about the centre of gravity, not about the reading list.

A second counter-argument: the resistance might be intellectual rather than structural. I reject this. The 1915 passage is a structural account of modernity at the same level of generality as Marx's chain-statement: a four-hundred-year period, a global trade, "the beginnings of the modern organization of industry," "the most inexcusable and despicable blot on modern human history" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). The conceptual ambition is identical. The "not theoretical enough" charge is itself a symptom of the canon's structural commitments.

In conclusion, the canon has been resistant to Du Bois because its empirical baseline, geographical centre, and methodological commitments were all assembled around a Eurocentric narrative of modernity. French's *Born in Blackness* supplies the empirical reconstruction that would force the inversion. To include Du Bois is not to add him; it is to re-found the canon on the empirical ground he was already standing on in 1915.

### M19: Using the work of at least two authors, critically assess the claim that "the manner of association" is what is sociologically interesting about modern societies.

I argue, drawing primarily on Durkheim and Whitehead with supporting work from Marx and Simmel, that the manner of association is *almost* what is sociologically interesting about modern societies. The qualifier matters because the four canonical accounts of manner-of-association converge less than Halewood's synthesis suggests, and the live disagreements among them are themselves part of what is sociologically interesting. My thesis is that the adverbial recovery is the right *first* move but it conceals a *second* set of disagreements about which the discipline still needs to choose.

The adverbial recovery, recapped. Halewood's Part 1 argues the canon converges on the adverbial. **Durkheim:** "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (Durkheim, 1895: 128). **Whitehead:** a society is a nexus of actual entities sharing "a common element of form" through mutual prehensions (Whitehead, 1933: 260-261). Both centre the *how*, not the *what*.

Why the move is correct as a first move. It dissolves two temptations at once. The methodological-individualist temptation (society as an aggregate of individuals) is turned off: individuals are themselves manners of association (of cells, of habits, of memberships). The holist reification temptation ("society demands," "society values") is also turned off: if society is the manner of association, no quasi-agent formulation is available.

Where the two authors disagree about the manner. **Durkheim's** manner is the manner of collective morality. The cell-analogy underwrites a science of *integration*: solidarity, the collective conscience, the punishment of contraventions. The manner is centripetal: things hold together because their manner of association reproduces moral cohesion. **Whitehead's** manner is the manner of mutual prehension. Things hold together because each takes account of the others' having-the-same-character. There is no moral residue; the manner is metaphysical, not ethical. A hot stone is a society on Whitehead's account; it is not on Durkheim's.

The disagreement matters for modernity. Durkheim's adverbial sociology analyses the modern nation-state as a manner of association reproducing collective morality (national rituals, civil religion). Whitehead's analyses it as a nexus reproducing a common element of form (legal documents, currency, language). The first is humanistic; the second post-humanistic. Halewood sides with Whitehead but does not fully spell out the cost: a Whiteheadian adverbial sociology is *not* the deeper Durkheim, it is something different.

**Example. The colonial plantation.** Durkheim's tool struggles: the moral collective is fractured along the slave/free divide. Whitehead's fits better: the plantation is a nexus of actual entities (the enslaved, the overseers, the planters, the sugar mill, the ledger) sharing a common element of form (the manner of coerced extraction) reproduced through mutual prehension across the parts. The Whiteheadian register lets us name the plantation as a society in the same frame as the household; the Durkheimian cannot. Carter and Charles push the same logic across the species line: they call for "a reconceptualization of society that recognizes that animals are constitutive of it rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). The cattle, the horses, the bacteria of the plantation are sociological parts of the manner of association, not background.

Where Marx and Simmel sit. Marx's chemistry analogy sides with the move (same atoms, different arrangement, different thing) but reads the manner *historically*. Simmel's *Vergesellschaftung* sides with it at the form-level but underweights the macro-structural dimension. Both contribute partial tools.

A counter-argument: the manner-of-association framing risks losing the *substantive* content sociology needs. The reply is that manner-of-association is not opposed to substance; it is the level at which substance is reproduced. The plantation is a substantive institution; its substance *is* its manner. The adverbial move specifies what kind of thing substance is.

A second counter-argument: modernity may be the wrong scale for the tool, which is at home in small cases (the cell, the secret, the household). I disagree. The tool scales: the plantation system, the world economy, and the colonial archive are all nexuses with common elements of form reproduced through mutual prehension.

In conclusion, manner-of-association is what is *first* sociologically interesting about modern societies, but the move conceals a live disagreement between Durkheim (humanistic, centripetal, moral) and Whitehead (metaphysical, mutual-prehension, post-humanistic) about which adverbial sociology we are doing. Halewood sides with Whitehead; I think he is right, but the choice should be explicit. Any two-author manner-of-association answer should specify *which* account it uses and what is gained and lost by the choice.

### M20: Can sociology think outside the categories of European modernity?

I argue that sociology can think outside the categories of European modernity, but only with considerable effort and only by treating the limits of its inherited vocabulary as data rather than as scenery. My thesis is that the categories of European modernity (society, the individual, the state, the public, the private, the rational, the religious) are not just descriptors; they are *operations* of the bifurcation Halewood diagnoses, and thinking outside them requires interrogating the operation, not just substituting different nouns.

The structural problem. Sociology is itself a modernity-product. Marx, Weber, Durkheim, Simmel: all wrote within the institutional, linguistic and conceptual horizons of nineteenth-century Europe. The discipline's basic vocabulary was elaborated in German, French and English with the assumed referents being European populations, states, economies. The Eurocentrism is not an accidental bias; it is the embedded geography of the conceptual apparatus.

The first reason to say yes. Du Bois already did it. His 1915 essay is a work of sociology that takes the trans-Atlantic slave system, not the European nation-state, as the empirical and conceptual centre. The vocabulary is partly European (he writes in English, uses "civilization," "religion," "industry") but the analytic ambition is to bend the vocabulary toward a referent it was not designed to capture: a four-hundred-year period in which "European civilization carried on a systematic trade in human beings" while simultaneously producing "the beginnings of the modern organization of industry" (Du Bois, 1915: 89-90). The slave trade, in Du Bois's account, is a sociological fact of the same theoretical generality as Marx's chain. Du Bois shows that sociology *can* be done outside the categories, at the cost of having to re-make them as one goes.

The second reason to say yes. Dussel's critique of Eurocentric modernity and French's *Born in Blackness* (French, 2021) are two extended demonstrations of the same operation. Dussel dates modernity to 1492 rather than to industrial Europe (Dussel, 1993): modernity is "born" when Europe is in a position to pose itself against an other, and "the other is not 'dis-covered' but 'covered-up'." Each takes a category of European modernity and shows it was constituted *through* the colonial relation rather than being subsequently extended to it. The work is not the rejection of the categories; it is the historicisation of them.

The third reason to say yes. Halewood's adverbial move offers a methodological route. If we shift attention from substantive categories (society, the state) to the manner of association, we can ask: *how* is association organised, *which* manner of mutual prehension obtains? The shift from noun to adverb is partly portable across cultural contexts. The colonial plantation is not a "society" in Durkheim's centripetal sense but it is a society in Whitehead's nexus-sense. The adverbial move loosens the European specificity of the noun-categories.

The first reason to be sceptical. Using the word "society" (even adverbially rethought) for non-European arrangements may still impose a European category. Halewood warns against assuming we "know what society is" (Halewood, 2014: 124). Sociology cannot think *fully* outside its categories; it can only mark them as not-the-only-categories.

The second reason. The discipline's institutional infrastructure is European-modern. A sociologist trying to think outside still publishes in journals and operates within an apparatus whose centre of gravity is European. The architecture pulls the work back inside.

The third reason. The very *question* "can sociology think outside?" is asked from inside; it presupposes a coherent inside-outside boundary, itself a European-modern way of organising the question. The bifurcation-thesis applies recursively.

A counter-argument: every discipline has constitutive vocabularies; sociology is not specially handicapped. The reply is that sociology's categories were assembled around a particular geography and history whose universality was assumed rather than tested. The discipline carries a heavier reform burden than mathematics.

Where I land. Sociology can think outside the categories of European modernity, but the thinking is labour-intensive and structurally limited. It requires three moves: (i) recover the unsettled moments in the canon when the categories were not yet stabilised; (ii) include the voices (Du Bois, Dussel, French) who rethink modernity from outside the European centre; (iii) historicise the categories so that their European specificity becomes visible rather than presupposed.

In conclusion, sociology can think outside the categories of European modernity to the extent that it is willing to do the threefold work of canonical recovery, voice-inclusion, and category-historicisation. The work is necessarily *inside* the discipline and necessarily *against* it. Each European-modern category is a bifurcation, and refusing the bifurcation is what thinking outside looks like.

### M21: The sociological imagination at third year.

I argue that the sociological imagination at third year has to do something different from what it does at first year, and the difference is not merely difficulty but *kind*. My thesis, following Beauvoir, is that first-year sociological imagination is *descriptive-linking* (Mills: connecting personal trouble to public issue) and third-year is *generative-counterfactual* (Beauvoir: picturing what social theory would look like if it took a previously excluded category seriously). The shift is not optional; it is what makes third-year sociology *third-year*.

The Millsian first-year version. Mills's *The Sociological Imagination* (Mills, 1959): the capacity to see how individual biography is shaped by historical structure. The unemployed individual in 1933 is not a personal failure but a casualty of a system-level crisis. Students arrive convinced that life-outcomes are personal and leave able to read the structural conditioning.

The Millsian limit. The first-year version is *descriptive*. It teaches you to see what *is*. By third year, this is not enough. The student who sees that unemployment is structurally produced has not yet imagined what an unemployment-free society would look like, what it would cost, or what its trade-offs would be.

The Beauvoirian alternative. Beauvoir imagined what the world and social theory would look like if it took women seriously. The verb is *imagined*. She did not just describe the structural conditioning of women's lives; she pictured what social theory would have looked like with women as a foundational category from the start. *The Second Sex* (1949) is a work of generative-counterfactual sociology that re-imagined the conceptual apparatus. Harding's standpoint epistemology is its later disciplinary descendant: she insists that "starting from women's lives" produces "better, more robust" knowledge (Harding, 2004: 128), and that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134). The strong imagination is Beauvoir's gesture rendered as method.

**Example 1. Race in sociology.** Millsian: see how individual racialised experience is conditioned by structural racism. Beauvoirian: imagine what sociology would look like if race had been a foundational category from the start, alongside class. Once imagined, the gaps in the standard story (no race chapter in Weber's *Economy and Society*, none in Durkheim's *Division of Labour*) become legible as constitutive omissions, not gaps in coverage.

**Example 2. Animals.** Carter and Charles's project is a Beauvoirian exercise in disguise. The Millsian move describes how individual human relations with animals are structured by industrial agriculture. The Beauvoirian move imagines what sociology would look like if non-human animals were taken seriously as social actors.

**Example 3. Non-bifurcated sociology.** Halewood's bifurcation thesis depends on the strong imagination. Six bifurcations become visible only when we *picture* what a non-bifurcated sociology would look like. The bifurcation, like the gulf, is invisible from inside.

**Example 4. The canon itself.** The Millsian move describes how the canon was assembled out of particular European positions; the Beauvoirian move imagines what sociology would have been like if Du Bois had been read alongside Marx, Durkheim and Weber from the start, with the trans-Atlantic slave trade as a foundational sociological fact.

Why the shift matters. First, it changes what counts as a finished essay. A Millsian essay closes with "see how this is structurally conditioned." A Beauvoirian essay closes with "see how the discipline would look if it had taken X seriously." The Beauvoirian close is generative; it points to a research programme. Second, the shift is what the discipline's reformers (Du Bois, Beauvoir, Halewood, Carter & Charles) all did.

A counter-argument: the strong sociological imagination risks *utopianism*; speculation about what *could be* without analysis of what *is*. I accept the warning but reject the dichotomy. Beauvoir's *Second Sex* is densely descriptive while simultaneously generative. The strong imagination is the imaginative supplement to analysis, not its replacement. Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* XI applies: "The philosophers have only interpreted the world; the point is to change it" (Marx, 1977: 158).

A second counter-argument: third-year students might lack the *empirical mastery* to do generative-counterfactual work responsibly. The course-design presupposes students can do this work. The point is to *learn* the strong move with whatever empirical resources one has.

In conclusion, the sociological imagination at third year is the strong, Beauvoirian, generative-counterfactual move: picture what social theory would look like if a previously excluded category were taken seriously from the start. The Millsian descriptive-linking move remains in the toolkit but is no longer the centre. Every third-year essay should close with an imagined alternative configuration of the discipline, not just a diagnosis of the present.

### M22: Compare and contrast Durkheim's living-cell analogy in *The Rules of Sociological Method* with Marx's butyric-acid/propyl-formate analogy in *Capital*. What does each do for its respective sociology?

I argue that Durkheim's living-cell analogy and Marx's butyric-acid/propyl-formate analogy do the *same* fundamental work (they assert that the manner of association is the proper sociological object) but are deployed against *different* opponents and license *different* downstream sociologies. My thesis is that Halewood is right to read the two analogies together as evidence of the canon's shared adverbial commitment, but the differences in polemical deployment matter for what each tradition can do.

The two analogies. **Durkheim:** "It is indeed certain that in the living cell there are only molecules of crude matter. But they are in association, and it is this association which is the cause of the new phenomena which characterise life" (Durkheim, 1895: 128). **Marx:** "butyric acid is a different substance from propyl formate. Yet both are made up of the same chemical substances [...] combined together in the same proportions in each case, namely C4H8O2 [...] their different physical forms would be neglected" (Marx, 1867: 33).

**Compare. The logical structure.** Both make the same point: same components, different arrangement, different thing. The cell has the same molecules as non-cellular matter; what differs is the arrangement. Butyric acid and propyl formate have the same atomic composition; what differs is the manner of association. Both use chemistry to break with substance-thinking in favour of arrangement-thinking, the adverbial commitment Halewood traces through Part 1. Both ground sociology in a *continuity* with natural science rather than a separation from it. Both reject the neo-Kantian bifurcation Weber will later try to manage.

**Contrast. The polemical target.** Durkheim's target is the assumption that the social can be reduced to the biological or to the individual. The cell analogy underwrites the doctrine that "association is natural; what is of interest is the manner of association" (Halewood, 2014: 125). Marx's target is the abstract value-theory that treats commodity, labour, and exchange as homogeneous quantities. The chemistry analogy underwrites the argument that the same components produce different societies depending on arrangement. Capitalist labour is not feudal labour is not slave labour.

**Contrast. What each licenses downstream.** Durkheim's cell analogy licenses a *stability-and-integration* sociology: the cell stays alive only if the molecules continue to associate in the constitutive manner. Sociology becomes the study of what keeps the manner stable: norms, rituals, the collective conscience. Marx's chemistry doublet licenses a *transformation-and-historical-specificity* sociology: the same atoms can rearrange. Sociology becomes the study of how arrangements change: feudal to capitalist, slave to wage labour. Durkheim's analogy implies the manner is *necessary*; Marx's implies it is *contingent*. The contrast maps onto the standard Durkheim-Marx difference at the level of the analogy itself.

**Convergence on what they refuse.** Both refuse the nineteenth-century opposition between materialism and idealism, the methodological individualism of the liberal canon, and the bifurcation between natural and social science. The convergence is what makes Halewood read them together as evidence of the canon's shared adverbial commitment. Carter and Charles draw the same lesson forward: if the manner of association is what matters, then animals "are constitutive" of social life "rather than part of a 'nature' with which sociologists are unconcerned" (Carter and Charles, 2018: 80). Once Durkheim's cell and Marx's chemistry are taken seriously, the species line cannot mark the limit of the social.

A counter-argument: the analogies are merely decorative. Durkheim's real sociology lives in the *Rules* and the empirical studies, Marx's in the analysis of value and capital. I disagree. The analogies are *conceptual machines* that authorise the move from substance to arrangement. Without the cell analogy, Durkheim cannot ground the claim that the social is a separate level of analysis. Without the chemistry analogy, Marx cannot ground the claim that capitalist relations are historically specific rather than eternal.

A second counter-argument: chemistry analogies risk smuggling positivism into social theory. The reply is that modern chemistry is itself adverbial; it pays attention to arrangement, not just substance. The analogies do not import a positivist commitment to laws-of-society; they import an adverbial commitment shared by all sciences sensitive to arrangement.

In conclusion, the two analogies do the same fundamental conceptual work (they ground sociology in the manner of association rather than substance) and differ in polemical deployment (against individualism for Durkheim, against abstract value-theory for Marx) and in downstream modality (stability for Durkheim, transformation for Marx). For the sociology of modernity, the analogies are a reminder that we already have the conceptual tools to analyse modern societies as historically specific arrangements, and that the canon's adverbial commitment was there before twentieth-century sociology forgot it.
