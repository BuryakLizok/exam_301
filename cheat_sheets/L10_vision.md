# L10 — Ways of Seeing: Vision, Knowledge and Power

## 1. Lecture overview

This lecture argues that vision is never innocent. To see is to occupy a position from which power flows, knowledge gets organised and social hierarchies are produced or contested. Drawing on Schroeder, Berger, Mulvey, hooks, Foucault and Haraway, the lecture shows that visual representations do not simply mirror a pre-existing reality; they construct it. From the colonial gaze of nineteenth-century Human Zoos to the panoptic gaze inside prisons, schools and hospitals; from the male gaze that converts women into objects of vision to the oppositional gaze that re-politicises black spectatorship; from the god trick of bird's-eye objectivity to Haraway's situated knowledge — vision is bound up with modernity, inequality, colonisation, slavery, gender and the discipline of bodies. The lecture's overarching question is "how is vision political?" and the answer is that every act of looking distributes light and shadow, includes some bodies as subjects and reduces others to objects, naturalises some perspectives as universal and renders others invisible.

## 2. Key thinkers and concepts

- **John Berger (*Ways of Seeing*, 1972):** Seeing comes before words. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 7). Images embody a way of seeing; reproduction and photography transform art's social meaning. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The surveyor/surveyed split inside women. Cultural mystification of the past.
- **Jonathan Schroeder (1998):** "To gaze implies more than to look at — it signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998 [VERIFY p.?]).
- **Laura Mulvey:** the male gaze in cinema — women as passive objects of a heterosexual male spectator's pleasure and control.
- **bell hooks (*Black Looks*, 1992):** the oppositional gaze. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life … There is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). Enslaved black people denied the right to gaze; the oppositional gaze as agency: "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 116).
- **Michel Foucault (*Discipline and Punish*):** the panoptic gaze. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 200 [VERIFY p.?]); "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable." Disciplinary power produces schoolchildren, patients, criminals as objects of knowledge.
- **Donna Haraway (*Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, 1991; *Staying with the Trouble*, 2016):** situated knowledge against the god trick — "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 189). "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, p. 190). "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, p. 196). In *Staying with the Trouble* she adds "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*, p. 12).
- **Sarah Kember (*Virtual Anxiety*):** medical scans render the pregnant woman invisible — "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information."

## 3. Glossary of named concepts

- **the male gaze** — Mulvey's term for cinema's heterosexual male spectator position.
- **the oppositional gaze** — hooks's defiant black female look that refuses objectification.
- **situated knowledge** — Haraway's principle that all knowing is embodied, partial, locatable.
- **the god trick** — Haraway's name for vision claiming to come from nowhere.
- **ways of seeing** — Berger's framing of vision as culturally and historically shaped.
- **surveyor and surveyed** — Berger's split inside women trained to watch themselves being looked at.
- **the colonial gaze / the imperial gaze** — the European look that turned colonised peoples into spectacle (Human Zoos, ethnographic photography, cartography).
- **the panoptic gaze** — Foucault's disciplinary visibility modelled on Bentham's Panopticon.
- **scopic regime** — the historically specific organisation of seeing in a given society.
- **the disciplinary gaze** — Foucault's normalising look that produces docile subjects.
- **second sight / double consciousness** — Du Bois's terms (cross-applied here): the ability of the oppressed to see both themselves and the dominant culture's image of them.

## 4. Concrete contemporary examples

Instagram filters and selfie culture; Snapchat dysmorphia; surveillance capitalism (Zuboff); facial recognition in policing; airport biometric border control; deepfakes and consent; revenge porn; #FreeTheNipple; Western depictions of war (Iraq, Afghanistan) versus Ukraine; Met Gala fashion politics; medical imaging and the female body (mammography, transvaginal ultrasound); the museum gaze and Indigenous remains; Beyoncé's *Lemonade* visual album; Disney princesses; the Mercator Projection versus the Gall-Peters Projection; CCTV in British cities; bodycams and police accountability; satellite imagery and drone warfare.

## 5. Cross-links to other lectures

- **Foucault / discipline** (panopticon, normalisation) — direct continuation.
- **Said and Orientalism** — the colonial gaze and the imperial production of "the East" as object.
- **Du Bois and double consciousness** — the doubled vision of the oppressed maps onto hooks.
- **Marx and ideology** — Berger's "cultural mystification" links to the ruling-class production of a naturalised past.
- **Bauman / modernity and the Holocaust** — the bureaucratic gaze that converts persons into administrable objects.
- **Goffman / presentation of self** — surveyor/surveyed and the dramaturgical self.
- **Beck / risk society** — the scientific gaze that produces calculable risks.
- **Haraway / cyborg and companion species** — situated knowledge as feminist epistemology.

## 6. Common exam pitfalls

- Treating "the male gaze" as Mulvey's entire theory; pair it with Berger's surveyor/surveyed.
- Reducing all vision to gender; Foucault insists disciplinary vision is "more than the male gaze."
- Quoting Haraway as a relativist; she defends strong objectivity through acknowledged partiality.
- Forgetting the colonial/racial dimension (hooks, Human Zoos, Mercator).
- Treating images as neutral reflections; remember Berger — every image embodies a way of seeing.
- Missing the political stakes: vision produces, reproduces and can challenge inequality.

### 2025 Q12 / 2023 Q12 — Donna Haraway stated 'How we see the world is always a matter of power'. Explain and discuss its relevance for sociology today.

I read Haraway's claim as the keystone of a feminist epistemology that breaks with the modern dream of disembodied objectivity. To say that "how we see the world is always a matter of power" is to insist that vision is not the passive reception of given facts but an active, embodied, historically located practice. In this essay I unpack what Haraway means, place her in the wider lineage of theorists of vision and power, and argue that her position is more sociologically urgent today than when she first articulated it in 1988.

Haraway's situated knowledge begins as a critique of what she calls "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 189). The bird's-eye view that scientists, mapmakers and policy experts claim — the view from above, with no body, no history, no interests — is in fact, she argues, the subjective view of the dominant unmarked group passed off as universal. Leonardo's 1502 map of Imola, used in the lecture, is the perfect emblem: a perspective no actual eye can occupy, yet presented as the truth of the city. For Haraway, "knowledge that is un-locatable is also knowledge that is irresponsible." Her positive alternative is that "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, p. 190). Strong objectivity is built not from pretended neutrality but from openly acknowledged partiality and from "the standpoints of the subjugated" which are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." Her famous formula — "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, p. 196) — captures the paradox: bigger truth comes from owning where you are.

I situate this claim inside a longer conversation. Schroeder's reminder that "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998 [VERIFY p.?]) tells us looking is never flat. Berger had already shown in *Ways of Seeing* that "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 7). Mulvey's notion of the male gaze named one specific scopic regime in which women appear as objects. Foucault's panoptic gaze, where "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* [VERIFY p.?]), described another, in which the disciplinary gaze produces schoolchildren, patients and criminals as analysable objects. hooks's oppositional gaze added the racial dimension, recalling that "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). Haraway gathers these strands into a meta-claim: every act of seeing is positioned, and every position is shot through with power.

Why does this matter for sociology today? First, surveillance capitalism (Zuboff) and facial recognition in policing have made the politics of vision palpable. When the Metropolitan Police trial live facial recognition at Notting Hill Carnival and misidentify black faces at higher rates, we see a god-trick algorithm presenting itself as neutral while encoding a particular standpoint. Second, the platform economy industrialises the surveyor/surveyed split Berger first described. Instagram filters and Snapchat dysmorphia are the apotheosis of his claim that women "watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, p. 47), now extended across genders and ages, with the front-facing camera supplying the mirror. Third, the visual coverage of war reveals whose suffering counts: Western media gave Ukrainian refugees in 2022 a humanised, intimate gaze withheld from Iraqi or Afghan civilians for two decades. Whose body becomes a sight and whose remains unseen is a matter of geopolitical power. Fourth, the museum gaze on Indigenous remains and the medical gaze on female bodies (the transvaginal ultrasound, where, as Kember argues, the pregnant woman is reduced to "a vehicle for scientific information") show that the god trick still organises knowledge production.

I want to underline the relevance for sociological method itself. Haraway is not just doing critique; she is reforming how sociology should work. If we accept her claim, then survey research, ethnography, big-data analysis and computational modelling must all declare their position. The interviewer's class, race, gender and history are not contaminants to be controlled away but constitutive of the knowledge produced. Reflexive sociology in the Bourdieusian sense becomes an epistemological requirement, not a stylistic flourish. When I read a study of welfare claimants written from a Treasury standpoint, I am reading a situated knowledge masquerading as objective fact; when I read a study by claimants themselves I am reading a different — and, on Haraway's argument, potentially stronger — objectivity because its situatedness is owned.

The relevance also extends to the politics of platforms and AI. Generative models trained on scraped Western internet data encode the god trick at industrial scale: a particular cultural standpoint reproduces itself as "the world." Deepfakes weaponise vision against consent, particularly women's consent. The #FreeTheNipple campaign exposes how platform moderation regimes encode the male gaze in their rules. Each of these is a power-laden way of seeing that sociology must read situatedly.

There are limits. Haraway's situated knowledge can be misread as relativism — every standpoint is just one among many — and used to defend reactionary "common-sense" views. Her actual position is sharper: situatedness plus accountability plus a preference for subjugated standpoints. There is also a risk of identitarian closure, where only members of a group can speak about that group; Haraway herself prefers webs of partial connection and coalition.

Overall, Haraway's statement is not a slogan but a research programme. It tells me, as a sociology student, that every dataset is also a gaze, every theory a position, every photograph a way of seeing. To take it seriously is to make sociology answerable for what it learns how to see. (732 words)

### 2022 Q14 — Critically examine the links between vision, power and knowledge.

In this essay I argue that vision, power and knowledge form an inseparable triad in modern sociology, and that the lecture's central concept — ways of seeing — is the most productive way to grasp it. I move from Berger's account of seeing as culturally formed, through Foucault's disciplinary gaze, Mulvey's male gaze and hooks's oppositional gaze, to Haraway's situated knowledge, before pulling them together around contemporary digital cases.

Berger's account of seeing opens the problem. "Seeing comes before words … The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 7). I find this useful because it refuses the common-sense idea that vision is a window onto reality. For Berger, "every image embodies a way of seeing" (Berger, p. 10 [VERIFY p.?]); reproduction by camera and screen detaches images from their original context and circulates them inside new regimes of meaning. He shows that the European nude tradition encoded a particular knowledge: that women are "to-be-looked-at," that "men act and women appear" (Berger, p. 47), that beauty is a competitive prize awarded to the woman judged most desirable by an external male spectator. The painting is not a record of what was; it is a production of how women were to be known. So vision already produces knowledge, and that knowledge already encodes power.

Foucault's panoptic gaze sharpens the link. In *Discipline and Punish* the Panopticon is the architectural diagram of modern power because it organises visibility itself: "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* [VERIFY p.?]); "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable." The inmate, never knowing when watched, internalises the inspector's gaze. Crucially, Foucault links this to knowledge: the disciplinary gaze "enables us to see some kinds of subject" — schoolchildren, patients, criminals — "and to analyse them as objects." The clinic, the prison, the school and the army produce the knowledges that constitute their subjects. Power/knowledge is not a slogan but a procedure: a way of distributing light. Crucially Foucault adds that this disciplinary gaze "has infiltrated the others" so as to "bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, p. 316), giving us an infinitesimal distribution of power that vision makes possible.

The feminist critique of cinema extends Foucault's argument into gender. Mulvey's notion of the male gaze identifies a scopic regime in which the camera is the eye of a heterosexual male, and women's bodies appear as objects of his pleasure and control. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split provides the interior mechanism: women learn to inhabit the gaze, to "watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, p. 47). Together these accounts show that gender as social knowledge is produced through specific habits of looking.

hooks complicates this by adding race. She recalls that in slavery "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115) — power was exercised by denying the right to gaze. Out of that trauma, she argues, an oppositional gaze emerged: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 116). Vision becomes the site of both subjection and resistance; knowledge produced from below contests knowledge produced from above.

Haraway's situated knowledge gives the synthesis. There is no view from nowhere; "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 189) is the trick that dominant knowledges use to universalise their position. Real knowledge is partial, embodied, locatable, and "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, p. 190). I read this as the meta-thesis that subsumes Foucault, Mulvey and hooks: vision constructs knowledge; knowledge constitutes power; therefore acknowledging where one sees from is an ethical and political act.

Now I turn to the contemporary. Facial recognition technologies promise objective identification but encode the standpoints of their training data — disproportionately misidentifying Black faces, as the ACLU's tests of Amazon Rekognition showed. Surveillance capitalism, theorised by Zuboff, generalises the panoptic gaze across consumer life: every click is a visibility that produces both a knowledge product (predictive analytics) and a power effect (behavioural modification). The museum gaze on Indigenous remains continues the imperial gaze of the nineteenth century. Coverage of war in Ukraine versus Iraq reveals whose bodies are visible as fully human and whose remain numbers. Instagram and Snapchat industrialise the surveyor/surveyed split into a multi-billion user economy. In each case knowledge is produced through a way of seeing that distributes power.

Critically, the triad has limits. Foucault's panopticon can over-determine subjects as docile; hooks's oppositional gaze reminds us that the surveyed look back. Haraway's emphasis on situatedness can collapse into identitarian closure unless paired with her insistence on partial connection. And vision is not the only sensory politics: hearing, smell and touch are also organised by power, and a fully sociological account would integrate them.

Still, the lecture's central insight holds. Vision, power and knowledge are mutually constitutive. Every photograph is a position; every dataset is a gaze; every map is an argument. Sociology's task is to make these positions visible — to refuse the god trick and to take responsibility for how it learns to see. (745 words)

### 2021 Q14 — 'There are various ways of seeing the world, but they are all linked to questions of power.' Critically analyse.

The statement names what I take to be the most important single insight of the sociology of vision: that pluralism of perspectives does not dissolve power but instead expresses it. In this essay I unpack the claim, defend it against simple relativism, and explore where it leads in contemporary practice.

The lecture opens with the Mercator and Gall-Peters projections side by side. Both are maps of the world; both are accurate in their own terms; yet they encode radically different politics. Mercator's 1569 cylindrical projection inflates the high latitudes — northern Europe and Canada bulge, Africa shrinks — and was designed for European navigators sailing trade and colonial routes. Gall-Peters preserves relative area, restoring Africa, South America and South Asia to their true proportions. Two ways of seeing the same earth, both linked to questions of power: the imperial gaze that miniaturises the Global South, and the post-colonial counter-gaze that returns it to size. The "Upside Down" world map goes further by inverting north and south, exposing the political nature of the convention that puts Europe at the top. Maps, like all images, are ways of seeing tied to standpoints.

Berger gives the theoretical hinge. "The way we see things is affected by what we know or what we believe" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 8 [VERIFY p.?]); "every image embodies a way of seeing." But Berger does not stop at plurality. He asks whose interest plurality serves and whose it occludes. The cultural mystification of the past, he argues, is performed because "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 11). When art is mystified, the ruling class's past is naturalised as everyone's past. Multiple ways of seeing exist, but the dominant one masquerades as the only one. The phrase "all linked to questions of power" thus does not mean every way of seeing is equally powerful; it means every way of seeing has a location in a field of power.

Foucault deepens this. The panoptic gaze is not just one perspective among many; it is the perspective that knows it is a perspective and uses that knowledge to discipline others into self-monitoring. The disciplinary gaze produces particular subjects — schoolchildren, patients, prisoners — and analyses them as objects. Knowledge built from these gazes is then used to govern. Bentham's Auto-Icon, the preserved body of the utilitarian philosopher displayed at UCL, is a visual reminder that the gaze is itself displayed and disciplined. Multiple gazes exist (medical, juridical, pedagogical), but all are linked to power because all distribute light, visibility and knowability.

Mulvey's notion of the male gaze and Berger's surveyor/surveyed give a gendered case. Two people can stand in the same gallery and see the same Bronzino nude; one is positioned as the addressed male spectator, the other as the surveyed woman who learns from earliest childhood "to survey herself continually" (Berger, p. 46). Different ways of seeing — but both produced inside, and constitutive of, the patriarchal power relation. hooks's oppositional gaze multiplies this further: black female spectators of Hollywood develop a defiant counter-look that refuses the white male position cinema invites. Plurality of gazes is real, but each gaze has political coordinates.

Haraway's situated knowledge is the meta-statement of the claim under analysis. She insists that "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 189) must be refused, but she does not slide into "anything goes." Situated knowledge is rigorous because it owns its position and is therefore accountable. Her preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" is not because they are intrinsically virtuous but because they are less likely "to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." The plurality of ways of seeing, in other words, is asymmetrically distributed: dominant gazes pretend to universality, subjugated gazes know they are positioned.

I now critically test the claim against three contemporary cases. First, the museum gaze and Indigenous remains: when the Pitt Rivers Museum displayed shrunken heads as ethnographic curiosities, that was a way of seeing tied to imperial power; when descendant communities reclaim them and demand repatriation, that is another way of seeing tied to a counter-power. Two ways, both political. Second, medical imaging: the transvaginal ultrasound, the mammogram, the foetal scan are different ways of seeing the female body, each entangled with reproductive politics, insurance economics and contested moralities. Kember's argument that the pregnant woman becomes "a vehicle for scientific information" exposes the power inside the medical way of seeing. Third, Beyoncé's *Lemonade* visual album: a black woman directing the camera at black women in Southern landscapes is a deliberate counter-gaze to Hollywood's white male gaze, and its power is precisely its refusal of the standard scopic regime.

There are critical objections. First, the claim can sound deterministic — as if no neutral seeing is possible. I respond that "linked to power" does not mean "reducible to power"; it means we should ask the question. Second, it can ignore the materiality of vision (optics, neurology). I respond that the materiality of seeing is itself organised socially (glasses, screens, cameras, algorithms). Third, it can flatten resistance into mere "another perspective." I respond, with hooks, that resistance is real but always inside power.

The statement therefore holds. Plurality of ways of seeing is the condition of the modern visual field; power is what organises and weights that plurality. Sociology's task is to map the field, expose the god trick and amplify the subjugated gazes. (755 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 1 — "Men act and women appear" (Berger). Discuss.

I read Berger's aphorism — "Men act and women appear" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47) — as the most compressed statement in modern visual sociology of how gender hierarchies are reproduced through looking. In this essay I unpack the line within Berger's wider argument, place it against Mulvey's male gaze and hooks's oppositional gaze, test it against contemporary platform culture, and weigh critical objections.

Berger develops the line across the middle of *Ways of Seeing* by contrasting the social presence of men and women. "A man's presence is dependent upon the promise of power which he embodies" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 45). The man's presence is outward, indicating what he can do to or for others. A woman's presence, by contrast, "expresses her own attitude to herself, and defines what can and cannot be done to her." She is born into "an allotted and confined space, into the keeping of men," and so her presence develops at the cost of "a woman's self being split into two." Berger names this the surveyor/surveyed split: from earliest childhood "she has been taught and persuaded to survey herself continually." The result, in his crystallised line, is that "men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47).

The European nude tradition is Berger's chief evidence. From Susanna and the Elders, to Bronzino's Venus, to Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne, the painted woman is arranged for an unpainted male spectator standing outside the frame. Berger writes that to be nude in this tradition is "to be seen naked by others and yet not recognized for oneself" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 54 [VERIFY p.?]). The convention is not innocent. It encodes the bourgeois patriarchal order — the man as proprietor of the look, the woman as ornamental property — and circulates that order as taste, beauty and aesthetic judgement.

Mulvey extends Berger from the gallery to the cinema. Her notion of the male gaze identifies a scopic regime in which Hollywood's camera operates as a heterosexual male spectator. Active narrative agency runs through the male protagonist; the woman halts narrative motion to "appear" — the close-up on the leg, the lingering on the face, the slow tracking shot up the body. The mid-twentieth-century Calvin Klein advertisement with Kate Moss reclining for "Obsession for Men" literalises the formula: she appears, he acts by buying.

hooks complicates the diagnosis. The black female spectator has never been the addressed audience of Hollywood, and her relation to "men look at women" is doubled by race. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115); her oppositional gaze refuses the white-male position cinema invites and declares "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 116). Berger's aphorism therefore needs racial specification: the woman who appears in the European nude tradition is white; the black female body has been seen through a different, often pathologising, scopic regime, from Saartjie Baartman's exhibition to contemporary stereotypes.

The platform economy is where the aphorism finds its most thorough contemporary realisation. Instagram externalises the surveyor as infrastructure: the front-facing camera, the filter library, the like count, the follower tally, the algorithmic feed. Young women report hours of preparation per post; the Royal Society for Public Health's 2017 *#StatusOfMind* report ranked Instagram as the platform with the most negative impact on young women's mental health, and the leaked Facebook Files showed 32% of teen girls felt worse about their bodies because of it. Snapchat dysmorphia, named by surgeon Tijion Esho, names the moment when patients request cosmetic procedures to look like their filtered selfies — Berger's surveyor become a surgical brief. The #FreeTheNipple campaign names exactly the visual contract Berger described: the female nipple is regulated as a sight; the male nipple is not. Disney princesses, even after post-2010 "active" redesigns, still deliver narrative resolution through a beauty close-up — girls learn the visual grammar before they can read. Deepfakes and revenge porn weaponise the cultural premise that a woman's body is to-be-seen.

There are real objections. First, Berger universalises from European oil painting; he himself notes that "in other non-European traditions … nakedness is never supine in this way" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 53 [VERIFY p.?]). Second, the binary erases queer, lesbian and trans spectatorship and the gay male gaze. Third, the rise of male fitness influencers, K-pop idol culture and OnlyFans male performers suggests an expanding zone where men also appear — though the surveyor inside that man is still typically gendered male, and women still face higher rates of body-image harm and image-based abuse. Fourth, hooks's reminder that the surveyor inside a black woman is both male and white makes the aphorism intersectional, not pristine. Fifth, agency complicates appearance: Beyoncé's *Lemonade* visual album appears black women in Southern landscapes precisely to subvert the spectator-owner relation.

Despite the complications, the core diagnosis holds. The surveyor/surveyed split, anchored in centuries of European visual culture and intensified by platform infrastructures, is reproduced from the gallery to the algorithm. Berger's aphorism remains the indispensable starting point for analysing gendered vision — provided we situate it racially with hooks, cinematically with Mulvey, and infrastructurally with the platform economy. To take the line seriously is to refuse the convention by which one half of humanity acts and the other half merely appears. (757 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 2 — Critically assess Berger's argument in *Ways of Seeing* in light of contemporary Instagram aesthetics.

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* was published in 1972, the same year the BBC television series ran. In this essay I assess its argument by holding it up to the contemporary aesthetic regime of Instagram. I find the book extraordinarily prescient on the surveyor/surveyed split, on the photographic reproduction of images, and on the politics of taste — and in need of supplementation on race, algorithm and platform economics.

Berger's central thesis is that "seeing comes before words" but is never innocent: "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 7). Every image embodies a way of seeing — and so does every act of looking. The book is structured around two arguments. The first concerns the reproduction of images: mechanical reproduction (the camera, the printed plate, the television screen) detaches images from their original context, multiplies them, and circulates them inside new regimes of meaning. The second concerns the production of women as objects of vision in European oil painting: "men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The surveyor inside the woman is male; she is "almost continually accompanied by her own image of herself" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 46).

The first argument anticipates Instagram with uncanny accuracy. Berger writes that "the painter's way of seeing is reconstituted by the marks he makes on the canvas" but "photographs are not, as is often assumed, a mechanical record" — "every time we look at a photograph, we are aware, however slightly, of the photographer selecting that sight from an infinity of other possible sights" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 10). Substitute "phone camera" for "camera" and "feed" for "gallery wall" and the analysis transposes intact. Instagram is the largest reproduction-and-circulation regime in human history. It detaches images from their context (the dinner photographed becomes content not food), multiplies them (Stories, Reels, reposts) and recodes them as carriers of social value (likes, saves, follows). Berger argued the European art tradition's apparent neutrality was a production: "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 11). The Instagram feed naturalises a particular Silicon Valley aesthetic — clean lines, "candid" framings, beige minimalism, lifestyle aspiration — as if it were spontaneous taste rather than the engineered output of attention-economy design.

The second argument finds its industrial realisation on the platform. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split is engineered into the interface. The front-facing camera, the filter library, the like count and the algorithm act as components of a continuous external surveyor. What Berger called a psychological accompaniment is now an infrastructural condition. Snapchat dysmorphia — the term coined by plastic surgeon Tijion Esho for patients seeking surgical alteration to match their filtered selfies — is the surveyor/surveyed split made surgical. Berger argued that the woman "comes to consider the surveyor and the surveyed within her as the two constituent yet always distinct elements of her identity as a woman" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 46). On Instagram, the surveyor is partially outsourced to the algorithm, but the internal split intensifies.

Concrete cases. First, the lifestyle influencer. The genre is *Ways of Seeing* chapter four made flesh: "publicity" Berger wrote, "is always about the future buyer … speaks to him of himself"; the influencer post is publicity that fuses spectator and seller, displaying the influencer as the surveyed life the viewer is invited to want. Second, the wedding-industrial complex on Instagram. The painted bride of European tradition (Berger's Bronzino brides) is now the styled bride of the Pinterest mood-board feeding the Instagram reel. Third, beauty filters and "the Instagram face" (Jia Tolentino's coinage) — a globally homogenising aesthetic of high cheekbones, full lips, contoured nose — instantiates the male surveyor at platform scale.

Where Berger needs supplementation. First, race. hooks's reminder that "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115) shows that the surveyor inside many Instagram users is also white. The god trick names the conjuring trick by which a particular standpoint — embodied, located, partial — presents itself as a view from nowhere. The conjurer's hands move; the audience attends to the apparent omniscience. The scientist looks down the microscope, the bureaucrat reads the population pyramid, the cartographer draws the Mercator projection, the satellite returns the cloud-image of Earth — and each presents what they see as if no body, no history, no funding stream and no interest had selected it. Haraway writes that such "vision from nowhere" depends on "the unmarked positions of Man and White" being treated as universal. Knowledge that is locationless, she insists, is "irresponsible" — incapable of being held to account for where it came from, who paid for it, whose interests it served.

First, the god trick is the move by which Western science took itself to be uniquely truth-producing. From Cartesian metaphysics through Enlightenment empiricism to twentieth-century positivism, the dominant epistemic tradition has staked its claim on the disembodied observer — the rational mind that sees clearly because it sees from nowhere in particular. Haraway argues this self-image is false: every observer is somewhere, every instrument is built somewhere, every dataset is collected somewhere. The trick is constitutive of the tradition, not incidental.

Second, the god trick licenses the universalisation of dominant standpoints. The "human" of the rights tradition turns out to have been the European male propertied human; the "average body" of anatomy textbooks is the 70 kg white male; the "common reader" of literary studies is the white English-speaking middle-class reader. Each universal is a particular wearing a god trick. The error compounds because each new universal builds on the prior universal.

Third, the god trick disables ethics. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 190) is her positive injunction. To be answerable requires owning the position from which one sees. A vision claiming to come from nowhere cannot be held responsible for what it does. Imperial geography, eugenic biology, structural-adjustment economics and big-data policing have all hidden behind the apparent neutrality of vision. Haraway's claim is that the god trick is therefore not a stylistic embarrassment but the central enabling fiction of Western epistemic violence.

The positive programme is situated knowledge. "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 196). Strong objectivity is built not from pretended neutrality but from openly acknowledged partiality, with a structural preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" because they are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." A worker's eye on the factory sees what the manager's eye misses; the patient's eye on the clinic sees what the consultant overlooks. This is not relativism. It is the demand that vision become accountable.

Haraway's framework can be misread as licensing identitarian closure ("only insiders may speak"), which she explicitly refuses through her later vocabulary of "partial connection" and coalition. The disjunctive is real but not exclusive. The same act of looking can be both possession and recognition. The portrait painter possesses the sitter (decides on pose, light, ornament) and recognises the sitter (acknowledges their particularity, dignity, history). And hooks reminds us that recognition without redistribution is empty: a humanising photograph that does not change material conditions is part of the very economy of looking it claims to interrupt.

The gaze is therefore both possession and recognition, never one without the trace of the other. Sociology's task is to map the gradient: which institutions, technologies and practices tip looking toward possession, and which open the possibility, in hooks's words, of a look that wants to change reality. ### Mock 7 — Mercator, Gall-Peters and the cartography of empire.

Scandinavia inflates; the Indian subcontinent shrinks. Antarctica becomes a vast white frieze along the bottom. The conventions are doubly political. The orientation is north-up — a choice with no astronomical necessity, simply the convention of European mapmakers who placed their own continent at the top. The centre is the prime meridian at Greenwich, fixed only in 1884 by political negotiation at the International Meridian Conference. Mercator's projection was therefore designed for the European navigators of the early modern world-system. It served the maritime empires that needed to sail rhumb lines from Lisbon, Amsterdam, London and Seville to Africa, Asia, the Caribbean and the Americas. The map is the visual handbook of empire.

The projection's afterlife was even more political than its origin. Mercator became, through nineteenth- and twentieth-century classroom dissemination, the dominant wall-map representation of the world. Generations of children were taught geography on a map that visually inflated the colonising centre and miniaturised the colonised periphery. Greenland next to Africa is not just a distortion of area; it is a distortion of imagination. The Global South appears small, peripheral and oriented down. The Global North appears large, central and oriented up. Berger's principle — "every image embodies a way of seeing" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 10 [VERIFY p.?]) — applies decisively. Leonardo's 1502 map of Imola, used in the lecture, exemplifies a perspective no human eye occupies. The cartographer is invisible; the map appears as the world itself. The Mercator–Gall-Peters opposition has been criticised by professional cartographers who note that all flat projections distort; that no map is "true"; that Peters's projection has its own shape distortions. The point of the lecture's juxtaposition is not that one map is true and the other false but that every map is a situated argument. Equally, maps are not only instruments of domination — Snow's cholera map, public health maps and electoral district maps can serve emancipatory ends. The argument is not that all maps are imperial but that all maps are arguments.

Cartography is a technology of vision; the imperial gaze used it to inflate the centre and miniaturise the periphery; the decolonising counter-gaze reorganises proportions and orientations. ### Mock 14 — How do facial recognition in policing and border biometrics extend the colonial gaze?

Facial recognition and border biometrics are sometimes presented as neutral technical infrastructures. In this essay I argue they are direct continuations of the colonial gaze — the European way of seeing colonised peoples as objects of classification, measurement and discipline — and that reading them through Foucault, hooks, Haraway and the colonial archive makes the continuity visible. I focus on three contemporary deployments: facial recognition in UK and US policing, biometric border control in the European Union and the United States, and the colonial cartographies of the EU's "Frontex" data architecture.

The colonial archive. The nineteenth-century European empire produced a vast visual archive of colonised peoples: ethnographic photographs (subjects posed in standard anthropometric postures, measured, classified), Human Zoos (Saartjie Baartman in European cabinets, the Congolese village at the 1897 Brussels Exposition, Ota Benga at the Bronx Zoo in 1906), skull-measurement plates, skin-colour charts, nose-width gauges. The archive's purpose was not aesthetic but governmental: it produced racial types as visual objects amenable to classification, hierarchy and rule. The colonial gaze did not just look; it inventoried. Foucault's analysis of the disciplinary gaze that "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, p. 316) applies precisely here. Said extended Foucault in *Orientalism* (1978) to argue that European representations constituted "the Orient" as a knowable, manageable object distinct from "the West." hooks added the slavery counterpart: "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). The colonial scopic regime was a one-way visibility that produced both knowledge and discipline.

Facial recognition in policing. The continuity with facial recognition is direct. The technology measures faces (interpupillary distance, jaw width, nasal bridge angle), classifies them against databases, and returns identification probabilities. The procedural ancestry runs through Bertillon's late-nineteenth-century anthropometric criminal photography, the colonial mugshot, and the eugenic skull measurement. What changes is the apparatus, not the gaze. The 2024 EU Migration Pact extends biometric capture to facial images and ages eligibility down to six years old. The Visa Information System, the Schengen Information System and the Entry/Exit System aggregate biometrics at every European border. African and Middle Eastern travellers face longer queues, more refusals, more iris and fingerprint scans. Second, asymmetry. Both produce one-way visibility: the colonised subject was photographed without consent; the asylum seeker is biometrically enrolled as a precondition of access. Third, governmentality. Both produce knowledge for rule. Bertillon's mugshot enabled the modern criminal archive; the Eurodac fingerprint enables the deportation infrastructure.

The "face" the algorithm recognises is not a neutral feature but a constructed object, just as the "racial type" of the nineteenth-century ethnographic plate was.

### Mock 15 — If Hollywood's male gaze were absolute, *Lemonade*, *Barbie* and Disney's recent "active princess" reboots would be impossible. Discuss.

The hypothetical posits a counterfactual: were Mulvey's male gaze absolute, the visible counter-currents in twenty-first-century mainstream visual culture could not exist. I take the conditional seriously, examine three exhibits — Beyoncé's *Lemonade* (2016), Greta Gerwig's *Barbie* (2023) and Disney's post-2010 princess reboots (*Brave*, *Frozen*, *Moana*, *Encanto*, *Wish*) — and ask whether they refute, qualify or confirm Mulvey's account. The hypothetical is rhetorically sharp but analytically misleading: Mulvey did not claim absoluteness; the male gaze was always a dominant convention against which resistance has been possible. The right reading is that *Lemonade*, *Barbie* and the Disney reboots both confirm Mulvey's structure and demonstrate hooks's oppositional gaze and Haraway's situated counter-vision operating inside the mainstream.

Mulvey's actual claim. Mulvey's 1975 essay argued that classical Hollywood operated "a determining male gaze" through scopophilia and ego-identification. The camera was structurally heterosexual male; the woman appeared; identification routed through the male protagonist. The argument was about dominant cinema, not all cinema, and Mulvey herself revised it in her 1981 "Afterthoughts" to address female spectatorship. The male gaze was always a hegemonic convention, not a metaphysical absolute. The hypothetical "if absolute" is a strawman — but the serious question remains: do the visible counter-currents in mainstream visual culture refute the diagnosis?

Exhibit one: *Lemonade*. Beyoncé's 2016 visual album is the clearest contemporary instance of hooks's oppositional gaze made cinematic. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115); "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 116). *Lemonade* directs the camera at black women — in Southern landscapes, mourning rituals, mother-daughter scenes — and refuses to position them for a white male spectator. The album quotes Warsan Shire and Malcolm X; it visualises the mothers of Trayvon Martin, Michael Brown and Eric Garner holding photographs of their dead sons. The work is mainstream — distributed by HBO, debuted at number one on Billboard 200 — and operates a gaze that is neither white nor male nor heterosexual in the Hollywood sense. Does *Lemonade* refute Mulvey? No: it confirms the structure of the male gaze precisely by refusing it. The film is legible as resistance because the convention it resists is real and dominant.

Exhibit two: *Barbie*. Greta Gerwig's *Barbie* (2023) grossed over $1.4 billion globally. It is a 114-minute meditation on the male gaze. The opening homage to *2001: A Space Odyssey*, Barbie's discovery of "Patriarchy" in the real world, Ken's seduction by horse iconography and corporate-cowboy aesthetics, the final America Ferrera monologue cataloguing the surveyor/surveyed double-bind — "You have to be thin but not too thin … you have to be a boss but not be mean" — all map directly onto Berger's analysis (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 46). The film cannot operate without Mulvey's framework as its presupposed background. Its commercial success is the success of the framework's recognisability: a billion-dollar audience could read the film's politics because the male gaze is the visual common sense it addresses. Far from refuting Mulvey, *Barbie* converts the diagnosis into spectacle.

Exhibit three: Disney's reboots. *Brave* (2012), *Frozen* (2013), *Moana* (2016), *Encanto* (2021), *Wish* (2023) attempt to make heroines "active." Merida refuses marriage; Elsa is liberated from the romance plot; Moana navigates the Pacific without a love interest; Mirabel rescues her family by recognising her grandmother's pain; Asha sings a charter for collective political action. But three caveats. First, the visual register remains substantially within Berger's surveyor/surveyed convention: even the "active" princess is rendered with large eyes, narrow waists and pose conventions inherited from the studio era. Second, the studio architecture remains disproportionately white and male behind the camera. Third, the films function as brand expansions — Disney+ subscribers, theme-park integrations, merchandise. The "active" princess is still produced for an audience whose visual literacy has been shaped by decades of male-gaze conventions.

Synthesis. The three exhibits show three responses to Mulvey: refusal (*Lemonade*), reflexive mainstreaming (*Barbie*) and incremental reform (Disney). All three presuppose, rather than refute, Mulvey's diagnosis. Were the male gaze absolute, none could exist — but Mulvey never said it was absolute. She said it was the dominant convention of mainstream cinema, and the dominant convention is precisely what gives oppositional, reflexive and reformist work its purchase.

Haraway adds the meta-frame. Each work embodies a particular situated knowledge. *Lemonade* situates itself in black diasporic memory; *Barbie* situates itself inside the postmodern Hollywood apparatus that can ironise its own conventions; the Disney reboots situate themselves inside a corporate brand strategy. None claims the god trick (Haraway, *Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, p. 189) — none pretends to a view from nowhere. The works are political precisely because they are positioned.

hooks's intervention deepens the reading. The oppositional gaze opens "the possibility of agency" inside structures of domination. *Lemonade* is the paradigm. *Barbie*'s ending leaves Barbie in the human world choosing her own life. The Disney heroines refuse marriage. Each is a moment of agency the classical male gaze foreclosed — legible only against the convention it interrupts.

The contrast between Western media's humanising coverage of Ukrainian refugees in 2022 and dehumanising coverage of Iraqi refugees a decade earlier shows that mainstream visual culture still distributes recognition unequally. Mulvey's diagnosis remains operative because the apparatus persists.

Both observations are reconciled by Mulvey's actual claim, hooks's oppositional gaze and Haraway's situated knowledge. The male gaze remains the dominant convention; *Lemonade*, *Barbie* and the Disney reboots are visible because they negotiate, refuse or reform it.
