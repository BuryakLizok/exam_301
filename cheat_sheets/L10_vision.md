# L10 - Ways of Seeing: Vision, Knowledge and Power

## 1. Lecture overview

This lecture argues that vision is never innocent. To see is to occupy a position from which power flows, knowledge gets organised and social hierarchies are produced or contested. Drawing on Schroeder, Berger, Mulvey, hooks, Foucault and Haraway, the lecture shows that visual representations do not simply mirror a pre-existing reality; they construct it. From the colonial gaze of nineteenth-century Human Zoos to the panoptic gaze inside prisons, schools and hospitals; from the male gaze that converts women into objects of vision to the oppositional gaze that re-politicises black spectatorship; from the god trick of bird's-eye objectivity to Haraway's situated knowledge, vision is bound up with modernity, inequality, colonisation, slavery, gender and the discipline of bodies. The lecture's overarching question is "how is vision political?" and the answer is that every act of looking distributes light and shadow, includes some bodies as subjects and reduces others to objects, naturalises some perspectives as universal and renders others invisible.

## 2. Key thinkers and concepts

- **John Berger (*Ways of Seeing*, 1972):** Seeing comes before words. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Images embody a way of seeing; reproduction and photography transform art's social meaning. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). The surveyor/surveyed split inside women. Cultural mystification of the past.
- **Jonathan Schroeder (1998):** "To gaze implies more than to look at, it signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998).
- **Laura Mulvey (1975):** the male gaze in cinema, women as passive objects of a heterosexual male spectator's pleasure and control.
- **bell hooks (*Black Looks*, 1992):** the oppositional gaze. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life … There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Enslaved black people denied the right to gaze; the oppositional gaze as agency: "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116).
- **Michel Foucault (*Discipline and Punish*, 1977):** the panoptic gaze. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]); "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable." Disciplinary power produces schoolchildren, patients, criminals as objects of knowledge.
- **Donna Haraway (*Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, 1991; *Staying with the Trouble*, 2016):** situated knowledge against the god trick. "The god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189). "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196). In *Staying with the Trouble* she adds "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12).
- **Sarah Kember (*Virtual Anxiety*, 1998):** medical scans render the pregnant woman invisible. "The body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]).

## 6. Common exam pitfalls

- Treating "the male gaze" as Mulvey's entire theory; pair it with Berger's surveyor/surveyed.
- Reducing all vision to gender; Foucault insists disciplinary vision is "more than the male gaze."
- Quoting Haraway as a relativist; she defends strong objectivity through acknowledged partiality.
- Forgetting the colonial/racial dimension (hooks, Human Zoos, Mercator).
- Treating images as neutral reflections; remember Berger, every image embodies a way of seeing.
- Missing the political stakes: vision produces, reproduces and can challenge inequality.

## 7. Past-year exam essays

### 2025 Q12 / 2023 Q12 - Donna Haraway stated 'How we see the world is always a matter of power'. Explain and discuss its relevance for sociology today.

Haraway's claim is the keystone of a feminist epistemology that breaks with the modern dream of disembodied objectivity. To say "how we see the world is always a matter of power" is to insist that vision is an active, embodied, historically located practice. I unpack what Haraway means, place her in the lineage of theorists of vision and power, and argue her position is more sociologically urgent today than in 1988.

Haraway's situated knowledge begins as a critique of "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189). The bird's-eye view scientists, mapmakers and policy experts claim, with no body and no interests, is in fact the subjective view of the dominant unmarked group passed off as universal. Leonardo's 1502 *Map of Imola* is the emblem: a perspective no actual eye can occupy, presented as the truth of the city. "Knowledge that is un-locatable is also knowledge that is irresponsible." Her positive alternative is that "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Strong objectivity is built from openly acknowledged partiality and from "the standpoints of the subjugated" which are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196).

I situate this in a longer conversation. Schroeder's reminder that "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998) tells us looking is never flat. Berger showed that "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Mulvey's male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) named the regime in which women appear as objects. Foucault's panoptic gaze, where "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200), produces schoolchildren, patients and criminals as analysable objects. hooks added the racial dimension, recalling "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). Haraway gathers these into a meta-claim: every act of seeing is positioned, and every position is shot through with power.

Why does this matter today? First, the nineteenth-century Human Zoos show the imperial god trick at industrial scale: European spectators reduced colonised persons to ethnographic objects. The disciplinary gaze still organises pupils, patients and prisoners; airport biometrics and CCTV extend it. Second, Kember's obstetric ultrasound shows the god trick in reproductive bodies: "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998); a technological view masquerades as transparent access to the foetus. Third, Mercator alongside Gall-Peters shows that what counts as "the world" is a situated argument; the unmarked European centre presents itself as the world while concealing its projection. Fourth, Berger's surveyor/surveyed split (Berger, 1972: 46) is the god trick at the scale of gendered selves, now intensified by Instagram filters and selfie culture.

The relevance extends to method. Survey research, ethnography and observational study must declare their position. The interviewer's class, race, gender and history are not contaminants but constitutive of the knowledge produced. A study of welfare claimants written from a Treasury standpoint is situated knowledge masquerading as objective fact; a study by claimants themselves carries potentially stronger objectivity. Berger makes the same point about art history: the "authoritative" Frans Hals study (Berger, 1972: 11) reproduces the standpoint of a "privileged minority … striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes."

Haraway in *Staying with the Trouble* adds that "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12); the partial connections we use to compose knowledge are themselves consequential.

There are limits. Situated knowledge can be misread as relativism and used to defend reactionary common sense. Haraway's actual position is sharper: situatedness plus accountability plus preference for subjugated standpoints. There is also a risk of identitarian closure; Haraway prefers webs of partial connection and coalition.

Haraway's statement is not a slogan but a research programme. Every dataset is also a gaze, every theory a position, every photograph a way of seeing. To take it seriously is to make sociology answerable for what it learns how to see.

### 2022 Q14 - Critically examine the links between vision, power and knowledge.

Vision, power and knowledge form an inseparable triad in modern sociology, and ways of seeing is the most productive way to grasp it. I move from Berger through Foucault, Mulvey and hooks to Haraway, before pulling them together around the course's examples.

Berger opens the problem. "Seeing comes before words … The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). "Every image embodies a way of seeing" (Berger, 1972: 10). The European nude tradition encoded knowledge that women are "to-be-looked-at," that "men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47), that beauty is a competitive prize. Vision already produces knowledge, and that knowledge encodes power.

Foucault sharpens the link. The Panopticon is the architectural diagram of modern power: "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200); "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable." The inmate, never knowing when watched, internalises the inspector's gaze. The disciplinary gaze "enables us to see some kinds of subject", schoolchildren, patients, criminals, "and to analyse them as objects." Power/knowledge is a procedure of distributing light. Disciplinary power "has infiltrated the others" to "bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, 1977: 316). Bentham's Auto-Icon at UCL makes this material.

Mulvey extends Foucault into gender. The male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) names a regime in which the camera is the eye of a heterosexual male. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split provides the interior mechanism: women learn to "watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements literalise this; Instagram filters and TikTok aesthetics extend the regime into digital self-surveillance.

hooks complicates this by adding race. In slavery "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); power was exercised by denying the right to gaze. From that trauma the oppositional gaze emerged: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, 1992: 116). Vision becomes the site of both subjection and resistance. The Human Zoos at nineteenth-century European and US expositions are the colonial counterpart of the slave-owner's prohibition: colonised persons were forced to be visible as ethnographic spectacle while the European spectator's own position remained unmarked.

Haraway gives the synthesis. There is no view from nowhere; "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the trick dominant knowledges use to universalise their position. Real knowledge is partial, embodied, locatable, and "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). This is the meta-thesis: vision constructs knowledge; knowledge constitutes power; acknowledging where one sees from is an ethical and political act.

The course's exhibits stage the triad concretely. Leonardo's *Map of Imola* presents a perspective no human eye can occupy: the god trick in cartographic form. Mercator inflates Greenland and miniaturises Africa; Gall-Peters and the "Upside Down" map make the political conventions of orientation and proportion visible. Kember's obstetric ultrasound shows the medical gaze producing the foetus as visible while the pregnant woman exists "only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998); mammography operates the same logic. The convex mirror in van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* makes the same point inside the picture: vision is always reflected from somewhere. Facial recognition systems and streaming algorithms are the contemporary apparatuses that the triad reads. Deepfakes radicalise the question of what counts as visible evidence.

The triad has limits. Foucault's panopticon can over-determine subjects as docile; hooks reminds us the surveyed look back. Haraway's situatedness can collapse into identitarian closure unless paired with partial connection. Vision is not the only sensory politics; hearing, smell and touch are also organised by power, and a complete sociological account would integrate them.

Still, the central insight holds. Vision, power and knowledge are mutually constitutive. Every photograph is a position; every dataset is a gaze; every map is an argument. Sociology's task is to make these positions visible, refuse the god trick and take responsibility for how it learns to see.

### 2021 Q14 - 'There are various ways of seeing the world, but they are all linked to questions of power.' Critically analyse.

The statement names the most important insight of the sociology of vision: pluralism of perspectives does not dissolve power but expresses it. I unpack the claim, defend it against simple relativism and explore where it leads.

The lecture's cartographic argument pairs Mercator and Gall-Peters. Both are accurate in their own terms but encode radically different politics. Mercator inflates the high latitudes and served European navigators sailing colonial routes. Gall-Peters preserves relative area, restoring Africa, South America and South Asia to true proportions. Two ways of seeing the same earth, both linked to power: the imperial gaze that miniaturises the Global South, and the post-colonial counter-gaze. The "Upside Down" map exposes the convention that puts Europe at the top.

Berger gives the theoretical hinge. "Every image embodies a way of seeing." But Berger does not stop at plurality; he asks whose interest it serves. The cultural mystification of the past is performed because "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, 1972: 11). When art is mystified (his Frans Hals example), the ruling class's past is naturalised as everyone's. "All linked to questions of power" does not mean every way of seeing is equally powerful; it means every way of seeing has a location in a field of power.

Foucault deepens this. The panoptic gaze is the perspective that uses its knowledge to discipline others into self-monitoring. The disciplinary gaze produces schoolchildren, patients and prisoners as analysable objects. Bentham's Auto-Icon at UCL is the visual reminder. Multiple gazes exist (medical, juridical, pedagogical), but all distribute light. Contemporary CCTV and content moderation algorithms extend this distribution.

Mulvey and Berger's surveyor/surveyed give a gendered case. Two people stand before Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love*; one as addressed male spectator, the other as surveyed woman who learns "to survey herself continually" (Berger, 1972: 46). Berger shows the same image can be read as Venus for moral cover or, in Lely's Nell Gwynne for Charles II, as the king's mistress signalling "her submission to the owner's feelings or demands." hooks's oppositional gaze multiplies the plurality: black female spectators develop a counter-look refusing the white male position. Plurality is real; each gaze has political coordinates.

Haraway is the meta-statement. "The god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) must be refused, but this is not "anything goes." Situated knowledge is rigorous because it owns its position and is accountable. Her preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" is not because they are intrinsically virtuous but because they are less likely "to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." Plurality is asymmetrically distributed: dominant gazes pretend to universality, subjugated gazes know they are positioned.

I test the claim against three exhibits. First, the Human Zoos. Colonised persons displayed at nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century European and US expositions were not shown alongside the spectator's own way of seeing; the spectator's position was unmarked. Second, Kember's medical gaze: the scan renders "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998). Third, Berger's contrast between the European nude tradition (Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders*, Memling's *Vanity*, Cranach's *Judgement of Paris*) and non-European traditions where "nakedness is never supine in this way" (Berger, 1972: 53). Two ways of seeing the body, neither neutral.

There are objections. The claim can sound deterministic; I respond that "linked to power" does not mean "reducible to power." It can ignore the materiality of vision; I respond that materiality is itself organised socially (the Snellen eye chart, glasses, framing). It can flatten resistance; I respond, with hooks, that resistance is real but always inside power.

The statement holds. Plurality of ways of seeing is the condition of the modern visual field; power is what organises and weights that plurality. Sociology's task is to map the field, expose the god trick and amplify the subjugated gazes that have always been positioned within it.

## 8. Mock exam essays

### Mock 1 - "Men act and women appear" (Berger). Discuss.

Berger's aphorism, "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47), is the most compressed statement in modern visual sociology of how gender hierarchies are reproduced through looking. I unpack the line, place it against Mulvey and hooks, test it against the lecture's exhibits and weigh objections.

Berger develops the line by contrasting social presence. "A man's presence is dependent upon the promise of power which he embodies" (Berger, 1972: 45). A woman's presence "expresses her own attitude to herself, and defines what can and cannot be done to her." She is born into "an allotted and confined space, into the keeping of men," and her presence develops at the cost of "a woman's self being split into two." Berger names this the surveyor/surveyed split: "she has been taught and persuaded to survey herself continually" (Berger, 1972: 46). "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47).

The European nude tradition is the chief evidence. Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* (in one version she "looks back at us looking at her"; in the other she joins her own spectators via a mirror); Memling's *Vanity*, where the painter "put a mirror in her hand and called the painting *Vanity*, thus morally condemning the woman whose nakedness you had depicted for your own pleasure"; Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris*, in which "Beauty becomes competitive"; Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne for Charles II showing "her submission to the owner's feelings or demands"; Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love*, "a painting of sexual provocation" (Berger, 1972: 54). The painted woman is arranged for an unpainted male spectator. To be nude is "to be seen naked by others and yet not recognized for oneself." The convention encodes the bourgeois patriarchal order and circulates it as taste.

Mulvey extends Berger from gallery to cinema. The male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) identifies a scopic regime in which Hollywood's camera operates as a heterosexual male spectator. Narrative agency runs through the male protagonist; the woman halts motion to "appear" in close-up on leg, face, tracking up the body. The Calvin Klein "Obsession" and "Obsession for Men" advertisements literalise the formula: the same body sells perfume to women and men under the possessive sign of the male gaze. Instagram filters, Snapchat dysmorphia and selfie culture have intensified the surveyor inside the woman across digital platforms.

hooks complicates the diagnosis. The black female spectator has never been Hollywood's assumed audience, and her relation to "men look at women" is doubled by race. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115); her oppositional gaze refuses the white-male position cinema invites and declares "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). Berger's aphorism needs racial specification: the woman who appears in the European nude tradition is white; the black female body has been seen through a different scopic regime, most starkly in the Human Zoos at European and US expositions, where colonised women were displayed as ethnographic spectacle.

Foucault sharpens the institutional analysis. Disciplinary power "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316), not reducible to gender. Kember's obstetric ultrasound renders the pregnant body "a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998); the surveyor is the medical apparatus detaching the foetus from her body. Facial recognition extends the disciplinary surveyor across public space.

There are objections. First, Berger universalises from European oil painting; he himself notes that in "Indian art, Persian art, African art, Pre-Columbian art, nakedness is never supine in this way" and figures appear as "two people, the woman as active as the man" (Berger, 1972: 53). Second, the binary erases queer and lesbian spectatorship and the gay male gaze. Third, hooks's reminder that the surveyor inside a black woman is both male and white makes the aphorism intersectional, not pristine.

Despite the complications, the core diagnosis holds. The surveyor/surveyed split, anchored from the medieval Adam-and-Eve cycles through Mabuse and Slevogt to Bronzino and Lely, is reproduced through Berger's conventions. The aphorism remains the indispensable starting point for analysing gendered vision, provided we situate it racially with hooks, cinematically with Mulvey and institutionally with Foucault and Kember. To take the line seriously is to refuse the convention by which one half of humanity acts and the other half merely appears.

### Mock 2 - Critically assess Berger's argument in *Ways of Seeing*.

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* (1972) is the foundational text. I assess its argument on its own terms and through Mulvey, hooks, Foucault and Haraway. The book is extraordinarily rich on the surveyor/surveyed split, on photographic reproduction and on the politics of taste, but needs supplementation on race, institutional vision and method.

Berger's central thesis is that "seeing comes before words" but is never innocent: "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). The book has two arguments. The first concerns reproduction: mechanical reproduction (camera, printed plate, television screen) detaches images from their context and circulates them inside new regimes of meaning. The second concerns the production of women as objects of vision in European oil painting: "men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). The surveyor inside the woman is male; she is "almost continually accompanied by her own image of herself" (Berger, 1972: 46).

The first argument is precise. "Photographs are not, as is often assumed, a mechanical record"; "every time we look at a photograph, we are aware, however slightly, of the photographer selecting that sight" (Berger, 1972: 10). Magritte's *The Treachery of Images* (1948) and *The Key of Dreams* stage the gap between image and word. The Snellen eye-chart and the *voir/savoir* etymology reinforce the point. The European art tradition's apparent neutrality was a production: "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, 1972: 11). The Frans Hals study presents itself as authoritative while reproducing the ruling-class standpoint.

The second argument tours the nude tradition. Berger reads Mabuse's *Adam and Eve* alongside Pol de Limbourg's *Fall and Expulsion from Paradise* and Slevogt's *The Couple* to show Genesis transformed: in the medieval cycle Adam and Eve "became aware of being naked because, as a result of eating the apple, each saw the other differently" (Berger, 1972: 48), whereas in the Renaissance "their shame is not so much in relation to one another as to the spectator." From there Berger moves to Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders*, Memling's *Vanity*, Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris*, Lely's Nell Gwynne and Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love*. The convention is "always conventionalized" and "the authority for its conventions derives from a certain tradition of art" (Berger, 1972: 53). Berger rejects Kenneth Clark's *The Nude*: the nude is a form of dress, not a form of art.

Mulvey extends the second argument to cinema (Mulvey, 1975), and the Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements show the convention persisting in twentieth-century perfume marketing; Instagram and TikTok aesthetics extend it into digital self-surveillance. hooks complicates it with race: the woman who appears in Berger's tradition is white, and "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); the oppositional gaze refuses the white male position. The Human Zoos remind us that European spectatorship of colonised persons was a scopic regime entangled with conquest.

Where Berger needs supplementation. First, institutional vision. Foucault's panoptic gaze, where "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200), names a scope Berger did not theorise. Kember's obstetric ultrasound shows the medical apparatus Berger's aesthetic critique cannot reach. Second, method. Haraway's "god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) and the call to "become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190) push Berger's critique into a positive epistemological programme.

Critical limits. The surveyor/surveyed split assumes a unified European tradition; Berger qualifies this when he notes that in "Indian art, Persian art, African art, Pre-Columbian art" (Berger, 1972: 53) sexual imagery is structured differently. The binary erases queer and trans spectatorship. And Berger's pedagogy of slow looking leaves open what active counter-practice looks like, a gap hooks's oppositional gaze partially fills.

Despite these limits, *Ways of Seeing* remains the indispensable handbook for the sociology of vision. It named, with precision and concrete visual evidence, the conventions by which the European visual order produces gendered, classed knowledge. Its critical lesson, refuse the apparent innocence of images, expose the conventions, ask whose look is encoded, remains the analytic the field requires.

### Mock 3 - What does Haraway mean by the god trick, and why does she treat it as the central failure of Western epistemology?

Two questions follow. What does Haraway mean by "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189)? Why does she treat it as the central failure of Western epistemology? I take the parts in turn, then sketch the positive programme.

The god trick names the move by which a particular standpoint (embodied, located, partial) presents itself as a view from nowhere. The cartographer, the anatomist, the ethnographer each presents what they see as if no body, no history, no funding stream had selected it. Leonardo's *Map of Imola* exemplifies the trick: a vertical view no human eye can occupy. "Vision from nowhere" depends on "the unmarked positions of Man and White" being treated as universal. Knowledge that is locationless is "irresponsible". The convex mirror in van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* makes the same argument inside a painting: vision is reflected from somewhere.

Why is this the central failure? Three reasons. First, the god trick is the move by which Western science took itself to be uniquely truth-producing. The tradition stakes its claim on the disembodied observer. Haraway argues this self-image is false: every observer is somewhere, every instrument built somewhere, every dataset collected somewhere.

Second, the god trick licenses the universalisation of dominant standpoints. The "human" of the rights tradition was the European male propertied human; the unmarked spectator of Berger's European nude is the same figure. Each universal is a particular wearing a god trick.

Third, the god trick disables ethics. A vision claiming to come from nowhere cannot be held responsible. Imperial geography and the Human Zoos used the apparent neutrality of "observation" to convert colonised persons into ethnographic specimens. The god trick is the central enabling fiction of Western epistemic violence.

The positive programme is situated knowledge. "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196). Strong objectivity is built from openly acknowledged partiality, with a structural preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" because they are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." A worker's eye on the factory sees what the manager's misses; the patient's eye on the clinic sees what the consultant overlooks. This is not relativism. It is the demand that vision become accountable. In *Staying with the Trouble* Haraway adds "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12); the partial connections through which knowledge is composed are themselves consequential.

Tests against the exhibits. First, cartography. Mercator presents itself as "the world" while inflating Europe; Gall-Peters and the "Upside Down" map declare their choices. Only Mercator performs the god trick. Second, the medical gaze. Kember's ultrasound shows the technological view appearing as transparent access; "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998). Mammography operates the same logic. Third, the disciplinary gaze. Foucault's panoptic principle, "power should be visible and unverifiable", encodes a god-trick variant: the tower is natural architecture. Disciplinary power "has infiltrated the others … making it possible to bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, 1977: 316). Airport biometrics extend it. Fourth, the male gaze. Mulvey shows the camera presenting itself as neutral observer while operating as heterosexual male; Berger's surveyor inside the woman (Berger, 1972: 46) is the internalised god trick, now intensified by Instagram filters.

Critical limits. Haraway's framework can be misread as licensing identitarian closure, which she refuses through her later vocabulary of "partial connection". It can be co-opted by reactionary appeals to lived experience untethered from analysis; her insistence on accountability resists this. Situated knowledge is a regulative ideal, not an algorithm.

The diagnosis holds. The god trick is the central failure because it is the foundation move on which other epistemic violences depend. To refuse the trick is to begin sociology properly: as a discipline that knows where it stands, that names its instruments, that owns its standpoint.

### Mock 4 - Compare Mulvey's male gaze with hooks's oppositional gaze.

Mulvey and hooks both write about cinema and looking, and both treat the screen as a site where power is exercised through visual conventions. I compare them on five axes and argue hooks does not refute Mulvey but racialises her diagnosis and supplies the resistance her account lacked.

Origin and object. Mulvey's "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (Mulvey, 1975) emerges from a psychoanalytic-Marxist reading of classical Hollywood. Her object is the mainstream feature film. hooks's *Black Looks* (hooks, 1992) emerges from black feminist criticism crossed with film studies. Her object is at once Hollywood and the historical regime of looking under slavery. Where Mulvey theorises the camera, hooks theorises the spectator she actually is. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115).

Scopic mechanism. Mulvey identifies the male gaze: the Hollywood camera operates as heterosexual male, positions the woman as object, routes identification through the male protagonist. The woman halts the story to "appear." The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements literalise the mechanism in print; Instagram aesthetics extend it to digital self-image. hooks insists this is racially specific. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, 1992: 116). Where Mulvey describes how the apparatus captures the spectator, hooks describes how the spectator looks back.

Account of agency. This is the sharpest contrast. Mulvey's intervention is largely diagnostic; her cinema remains a machine of capture. hooks's is at once diagnostic and emancipatory. The oppositional gaze "opens up the possibility of agency" inside structures of domination. It is an epistemic act (producing alternative knowledge), a political act (refusing the regime) and a creative practice (informing black feminist filmmaking, criticism and viewership). Schroeder's reminder that "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998) names the structural superiority both analyse. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split (Berger, 1972: 46) supplies the interior mechanism by which the male gaze becomes internal to the woman; hooks's oppositional gaze names the refusal of that internalisation. Foucault's disciplinary gaze, "a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316), is not reducible to gender; Mulvey's male gaze is one scopic regime among Foucault's many. Haraway's situated knowledge supplies the epistemological frame: Mulvey's camera and hooks's spectator are both somewhere in particular, and the god trick by which Hollywood pretends to be nowhere is the move both expose. The Human Zoos slide gives the colonial counterpart Mulvey did not theorise and hooks centres.

Critical points. Mulvey's account has been faulted for heterosexism, psychoanalytic universalism and white default. hooks's framework foregrounds black female spectatorship and does not translate automatically to other marginalised positions. Both frameworks were articulated before contemporary visual cultures; both name structures that persist on TikTok, streaming platforms and content moderation systems.

Conclusion. Mulvey gives the diagnostic vocabulary; hooks racialises the diagnosis and supplies the politics of refusal. Read together, they show mainstream cinema operates a heterosexual white male gaze, that this gaze is internalised but contestable, and that the oppositional gaze names vision that refuses to be captured. The sociology of cinematic vision needs both, read through Berger, Schroeder, Foucault and Haraway.

### Mock 5 - How does the panoptic gaze help us understand contemporary regimes of visibility?

Foucault's panoptic gaze is the most influential analysis of vision in modern sociology. I argue it remains indispensable for understanding contemporary regimes of visibility, drawing from *Discipline and Punish* through Berger, Kember, hooks and Haraway.

Foucault's account is the starting point. Bentham's Panopticon ensures the inmate "is seen, but he does not see." "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200). Because the inmate cannot tell whether the inspector is watching, he behaves as if always watched. Power becomes "visible and unverifiable." Disciplinary power "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" and "has infiltrated the others … making it possible to bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, 1977: 316). The Panopticon is not a building but a diagram, recurring across prisons, schools, hospitals and armies. Bentham's Auto-Icon at UCL makes the diagram material: the architect of panoptic visibility becomes its subject.

Four mechanisms organise the contemporary application.

First, asymmetric visibility. The inmate is seen but does not see; the same asymmetry holds in prison, clinic and school. Disciplinary power "enables us to see some kinds of subject (schoolchildren, patients, criminals) and to analyse them as objects." Berger's European nude tradition stages a parallel: the unpainted male spectator stands outside the frame "with his clothes still on" (Berger, 1972: 54) while the painted woman is seen, classified and consumed. CCTV networks and airport biometrics extend asymmetric visibility into public space.

Second, internalisation. Foucault's consequential claim is that the panoptic subject internalises the inspector's position. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split is the same mechanism at the scale of the gendered self: women under patriarchy are "almost continually accompanied by [their] own image of [themselves]" (Berger, 1972: 46) and have been "taught and persuaded to survey [themselves] continually." The panoptic principle "visible and unverifiable" operates inside the woman. Instagram filters and Snapchat dysmorphia intensify the internalisation, producing continuous self-surveillance on the screen.

Third, normalisation. The disciplinary gaze produces docile subjects through continuous comparison against the norm. The lecture's slide on dominant ways of seeing as "normal" makes the point: because the knowledges of dominant groups are "taken for granted as 'true', it is difficult to question them." Haraway's god trick names the same move: a particular standpoint normalised as universal. The Mercator projection naturalises the European centre; Leonardo's *Map of Imola* naturalises the vertical observer.

Fourth, distribution of light. Kember's obstetric ultrasound shows the medical apparatus distributing visibility: the foetus appears in the light; the pregnant woman "exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998). Some bodies are illuminated as objects of clinical knowledge; others are made structurally invisible. hooks's analysis of slavery shows the racial distribution: "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); the disciplinary gaze on black bodies has historically denied them the right of return-look.

Foucault is explicit that the disciplinary gaze "is not reducible to gender" and is "about more than the male gaze." Mulvey's male gaze names one scopic regime among many. The medical gaze (Kember), the colonial gaze of the Human Zoos at nineteenth-century European and US expositions, and the disciplinary gazes of school and prison all instantiate the panoptic diagram while operating different distributions of light. Streaming-platform algorithms now sort populations into recommended content profiles, a contemporary distribution of light.

Berger's diagnosis of mystification adds a complementary mechanism. The European art tradition uses apparent neutrality to disguise its political function: "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, 1972: 11). The Frans Hals study performs a god trick that is also a disciplinary distribution of light.

hooks's oppositional gaze names the counter-mechanism the panoptic diagram does not theorise. The look that "wants to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116) refuses the trap by becoming an active gaze. Haraway's positive injunction, "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190), is the methodological version of the same counter-move.

Critical limits. Foucault's panopticon imagined one central watcher; contemporary regimes distribute observation across multiple sites. Foucault under-theorised race; hooks must be added. And the panoptic subject is not pure inmate: counter-practices proliferate.

Foucault nonetheless remains indispensable. "Visibility is a trap" is the founding diagnosis. Read alongside Berger, hooks, Kember and Haraway, the panoptic gaze is the analytic the contemporary visual order requires.

### Mock 6 - Is the gaze possession or recognition? Discuss.

The disjunctive maps two tendencies. The "gaze as possession" tendency runs through Schroeder, Berger, Mulvey and Foucault: to gaze is to claim, classify, dominate. The "gaze as recognition" tendency runs through hooks and Haraway: to look back, to acknowledge one's position. I argue the disjunctive is real but not exclusive (the same act of looking can be both) and that sociology's task is to map when looking tips one way or the other.

Possession. Schroeder: "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998). Berger's European nude tradition shows the possessive structure: the painted woman is arranged for the unpainted male spectator-owner. Lely's Nell Gwynne for Charles II shows "her submission to the owner's feelings or demands"; Memling's *Vanity* uses the moralising title to disguise the painter's enjoyment; Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love* is "a painting of sexual provocation" (Berger, 1972: 54). Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* makes it explicit: Susannah is spied upon by the Elders inside and the spectator outside; nakedness is "to be seen naked by others and yet not recognized for oneself." The gaze possesses by converting a person into a property of vision.

Foucault institutionalises this. The Panopticon's gaze is possession-by-knowledge: the inmate becomes an "object." "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200). The disciplinary gaze produces schoolchildren, patients and criminals as objects. Mulvey extends to cinema: the male gaze possesses the woman's body as visual property. The Calvin Klein advertisements make possession the marketing grammar.

The colonial archive radicalises possession. The Human Zoos at nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century European and US World Fairs converted colonised persons into spectacle. The Mercator Projection performs cartographic possession. Kember's medical scan possesses by extracting the foetus: "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998).

The recognition tendency. hooks's oppositional gaze names a politics of refused possession. "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). Under slavery "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); the oppositional gaze claims that right back. The lecture's slide on hooks juxtaposed with the Human Zoos slide stages the contrast: the imperial gaze possesses; the oppositional gaze refuses possession and demands recognition. Beyoncé's *Lemonade* visual album is a cultural performance of recognition reclaimed from the regime that denied it.

Haraway's situated knowledge is the meta-frame. The god trick attempts possession (Haraway, 1991: 189). Situated vision attempts recognition by acknowledging the body, history and standpoint of the seer. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Van Eyck's convex mirror makes the point inside the painting. "It matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12).

The disjunctive is not exclusive. The portrait painter possesses (pose, light) and recognises (particularity). The doctor's gaze possesses (case, diagnosis) and recognises (suffering, biography). Berger notes the spectator is "for our own reasons … the one we have eyes for" (Berger, 1972: 10). The Gall-Peters Projection partially recognises while still operating as flat possessive abstraction.

Tests across the exhibits. Magritte's *The Treachery of Images* stages possession-and-recognition together. Bentham's Auto-Icon is possession and recognition (theory materialised so its operation is visible). The "Upside Down" map inverts north and south to expose the convention by which possession masquerades as recognition. Berger's non-European traditions (Berger, 1972: 53) show "the woman as active as the man, the actions of each absorbing the other", a recognition-structured imagery against the possessive European nude.

Critical limits. The opposition can become moralistic (possession bad, recognition good) when sociological work requires possessive operations (categorisation, measurement) that can be ethically conducted. Recognition can tip into idealisation that erases inequality. And hooks reminds us that recognition without redistribution is empty.

The gaze is both possession and recognition, never one without the trace of the other. Sociology's task is to map the gradient: which institutions and technologies tip looking toward possession, and which open the possibility of a look that wants to change reality.

### Mock 7 - Mercator, Gall-Peters and the cartography of empire.

The map is one of the oldest visual technologies of empire. I treat Mercator and Gall-Peters not as alternative projections of "the same" world but as cartographic arguments about what kind of world the viewer should imagine, and show how the contrast lights up the wider relation between vision, power and knowledge.

Mercator's projection was developed as a navigation instrument. Lines of constant compass bearing appear as straight lines, making transoceanic course-setting calculable. The cost: areas distort drastically with latitude. Greenland appears the size of Africa; Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. North-up has no astronomical necessity; it is the convention of European mapmakers. The map served the maritime empires whose colonising voyages built modernity.

Mercator became the dominant wall-map. Generations of children were taught geography on a map that inflated the colonising centre and miniaturised the colonised periphery. The Global South appears small, peripheral, oriented down. "Every image embodies a way of seeing" (Berger, 1972: 10) applies decisively.

The Gall-Peters Projection preserves relative area. Africa expands; Greenland shrinks. The juxtaposition is the paradigm of Berger's "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Two ways of seeing the same earth, both linked to power.

The "Upside Down" map goes further. Inverting north and south exposes the political convention of orientation. There is nothing in the cosmos that puts Europe at the top of the page. The map is an argument.

Schroeder anchors the analysis: "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998). The cartographer is the gazer; the territory is the gazed-upon. Foucault's disciplinary gaze illuminates the map as instrument: the cadastral map and the colonial map produce territory as a manageable, taxable, policeable object. Disciplinary power "distributes light" (Foucault, 1977: 316); the colonial map of Africa drawn in nineteenth-century European chancelleries distributed administrative visibility across populations whose own spatial knowledges were occluded. The dotted lines of imperial borders are the residue of a gaze that drew through populations as if they were transparent.

The lecture pairs Mercator with Human Zoos. The same European empires that drew the maps "imported" colonised people for "exhibit" at World Fairs in France, the UK, Belgium, Spain and the US. The map abstracts the territory; the zoo displays its inhabitants. Both perform the imperial gaze.

Haraway's god trick. The overhead view is "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) in cartographic form. Leonardo's *Map of Imola* displays a perspective no human eye occupies. Situated cartography would put the maker and the silenced names back into the image. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Gall-Peters declares its area-preservation; the "Upside Down" map declares its inversion; Mercator hides its conformality behind neutrality.

hooks's oppositional gaze gives the human counterpart. The same regime that drew the imperial map and staged the Human Zoo also "denied [slaves] their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). The map is the gaze that possessed the territory; the prohibition was the gaze that possessed the body. The oppositional gaze refuses both possessions and "wants to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116).

Critical limits. The Mercator/Gall-Peters opposition can be misread as a contest in which one map is true and the other false. The point is that every map is a situated argument. All flat projections distort; the question is which distortions are politically consequential. Maps are not only instruments of domination: counter-cartographies can serve emancipatory ends. The argument is not that all maps are imperial but that all maps are arguments.

Mercator and Gall-Peters together make the lecture's central claim concrete. Cartography is a technology of vision; the imperial gaze used it to inflate the centre and miniaturise the periphery; the counter-gaze reorganises proportions and orientations. The sociological reading of any map asks who drew it, for whom, with what projection, against what alternatives, and to what effect.

### Mock 8 - Does Berger's claim that "seeing comes before words" still hold?

Berger opens *Ways of Seeing* with "seeing comes before words. The child looks and recognizes before it can speak" (Berger, 1972: 7). I test the claim against the lecture's theorists and the visual regimes the course examines. The claim holds, read as Berger meant it (developmental, phenomenological and epistemic) rather than as metaphysical priority.

Berger's claim. "Seeing comes before words … It is seeing which establishes our place in the surrounding world; we explain that world with words, but words can never undo the fact that we are surrounded by it. The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Three claims: developmental (sight precedes speech), phenomenological (vision orients us before description) and epistemic (seeing and knowing never coincide). All three are grounded in Magritte's *The Key of Dreams*, where the painted door is labelled "the wind" and the painted horse "the door". *The Treachery of Images* (1948) repeats the lesson.

The developmental claim. The Snellen eye-chart and *voir/savoir* etymology reinforce common observation: children orient by sight before speech. Berger is not making a metaphysical claim; he is naming a developmental fact to support a phenomenological one.

The phenomenological claim. The visible world structures our orientation before we articulate it. Kember's ultrasound is a useful test: even when the pregnant woman is reduced to "a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998), the sonographic image orients clinician and public before any caption. The image precedes its interpretation.

The epistemic claim. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" carries two readings. First, vision exceeds knowledge: a residue of the visible we have not articulated. Second, knowledge exceeds vision: we know more than we can show. Berger holds both. The Frans Hals example (Berger, 1972: 11) shows the second; the European nude tradition shows the first: the painted woman is more than her caption (Venus, Vanity, Susannah, Nell Gwynne, the Allegory of Time and Love), which channels her visibility into male spectatorship.

Tests against the theorists. Mulvey's male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) extends Berger: the camera operates as heterosexual male whose look conditions the visible woman before narrative. The Calvin Klein advertisements show the same; Instagram filters and TikTok aesthetics operate similarly. Foucault adds an institutional twist: "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200) operates by making subjects visible before they articulate themselves. The disciplinary gaze "distributes light" (Foucault, 1977: 316); the subject is illuminated before being asked to speak. hooks's oppositional gaze is the reply: "afraid to look, but fascinated by the gaze. There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Haraway agrees with the priority of vision but adds the demand for accountability: "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190).

Tests against the visual exhibits. Mercator is registered as the world before the viewer is taught how it distorts; Gall-Peters and the "Upside Down" map re-present the visible before retraining the words. Leonardo's *Map of Imola* presents itself as the city before any explanation acknowledges the impossible perspective. The visible is registered before the viewer can recover whose position constructed it. The convex mirror in van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* shows the painter in the room before the caption can name him.

Where Berger needs supplementation. Berger's claim assumed indexical photography; he himself argued that "photographs are not, as is often assumed, a mechanical record" (Berger, 1972: 10). Reproduction and circulation change the conditions: the visible is partly composed of images that have travelled. Deepfakes radicalise this: the visible is no longer indexically anchored. Haraway's god trick adds that a vision presenting itself as natural may be situated knowledge masquerading as universal.

Critical complications. Vision is not innocent of mediation. The point is not that seeing is unmediated but that vision is constitutive of orientation before words name it. The priority is phenomenological and developmental, not metaphysical.

Berger's claim holds. Seeing comes before words; the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled. The political question is which words we then have, and that is what Mulvey, hooks, Foucault, Kember and Haraway answer.

Disney princesses as cultural touchstones operate exactly this white default. Mulvey's account needs hooks's racial supplement.

Haraway's reflexive supplement. Mulvey's account is itself situated. Her psychoanalytic-Marxist reading of classical Hollywood is a particular standpoint. Haraway's god trick (Haraway, 1991: 189) warns against universalising the male gaze.

Empirical strength. The claim retains force across print, cinematic and digital regimes. Instagram filters and TikTok aesthetics extend the male gaze into self-surveillance; the OnlyFans creator economy literalises the camera's possessive grammar.

### Mock 10 - Discuss the politics of the medical gaze, with reference to Kember's *Virtual Anxiety*.

Sarah Kember's *Virtual Anxiety* (Kember, 1998) is the lecture's anchor text on medical imaging and the female body. Her central claim is that contemporary obstetric ultrasound "eliminates the mother's body from view" so that "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information." I take Kember's claim as the starting point, locate it in Foucault's disciplinary vision, extend it through Haraway, Berger and hooks, and consider critical limits.

Kember's argument. The claim is precise: "the scanning process eliminates the mother's body from view and the doctor has unmediated access to the image of the foetus. The body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998). Two moves at once. First, phenomenological: the ultrasound image foregrounds the foetus and renders the pregnant woman as ambient infrastructure. Second, political: the foetus, made visually present, becomes a candidate for moral and clinical attention in a way the pregnant woman is not. Vision produces persons; the persons it produces have political weight. The lecture's slide-headers summarise: "Woman as a problem for vision/knowledge"; "Woman as invisible."

Foucault's disciplinary gaze. The medical gaze is one mode of disciplinary vision. Disciplinary power "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316). The clinical gaze on the female body has been historically intensive: the speculum, the pelvic exam, the ultrasound. Mammography operates the same logic of visualising one bodily region while rendering the rest infrastructural. Kember's intervention is to show that the technological visualisation of the foetus is the latest mutation. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200): the woman whose pregnancy is visualised is enrolled in a particular regime of knowability. The disciplinary gaze "enables us to see some kinds of subject (schoolchildren, patients, criminals) and to analyse them as objects"; the pregnant patient is the contemporary case.

Berger's surveyor/surveyed split intersects. Kember's pregnant woman is watched by a clinical-and-foetal spectator. The ultrasound image becomes shareable: printout, announcement, keepsake. The pregnant body is doubled: lived body experiencing pregnancy, and visual body broadcasting the foetus. Berger's claim that the painted woman is "almost continually accompanied by her own image of herself" finds a sonographic analogue.

Haraway's situated knowledge. The ultrasound presents itself as transparent but is a particular technological mediation: sonographer-operated, machine-calibrated, image-processed. Haraway's "god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is performed by sonography. Situated obstetrics would "become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Leonardo's *Map of Imola* and the ultrasound are kin: vertical views no human eye can occupy, both presented as transparent access.

hooks's racial supplement. The disciplinary gaze on black bodies has historically functioned as terror rather than reform. "Slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); the medical gaze on black women has a particular history that intersects with this denial. Kember's general claim is intensified for black women: the gaze that sees a body without recognising the person is the same gaze that historically denied black persons the right to look back.

Schroeder's structural statement. "To gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998). The clinical gaze on the pregnant body instantiates the relation: the apparatus is superior to its object; the pregnant woman is rendered as object so the foetus becomes visible as subject. War coverage on TV news performs a comparable distribution of light, illuminating some bodies as subjects of grief and others as infrastructure of conflict.

Critical limits. Kember's analysis can be read deterministically. In practice, midwives and pregnant women negotiate the technology, and many report the scan as a moment of connection rather than alienation. The political consequences depend on the surrounding institutional regime, not the technology alone. The medical gaze is not only oppressive: the diagnostic image is also an instrument of care. The sociological reading does not condemn the medical gaze but situates it.

The medical gaze remains one of the densest sites where vision, power and knowledge intersect. Kember named the contemporary form; Foucault gave the analytic; Berger and hooks supplied the gendered and racial dimensions; Haraway gave the epistemological frame. Sociology's task is to render the medical gaze accountable: situated, attentive to the body the imaging technology makes invisible, answerable for what it learns how to see.

### Mock 11 - Using at least two authors, evaluate whether the male gaze has fragmented in modern visual culture.

I argue the male gaze as Mulvey and Berger described it has been multiplied across new exhibits and supplemented by competing scopic regimes, but the core mechanism (surveyor/surveyed split, woman as appearing object, spectator as superior gazer) persists. I evaluate through Mulvey and Berger, with hooks, Foucault and Haraway as supplementary voices.

The classical male gaze. Mulvey (1975) theorised for the single-screen studio cinema: one screen, one audience, one apparatus positioning the spectator as heterosexual male. The woman appeared; the man acted; identification routed through the male protagonist. Berger's analysis of European oil painting described the same scopic regime in a different medium: the painted woman arranged for the unpainted male spectator-owner. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). The surveyor inside the woman was male; she "watched herself being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 46). The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements show the convention crossing media: from Cranach and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris* through Lely's Nell Gwynne and Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love* to the printed perfume ad.

The fragmentation thesis. Two multiplications. First, the male gaze sits alongside other scopic regimes Foucault names: the medical gaze (Kember), the colonial gaze of the Human Zoos, the disciplinary gazes of school and prison. Foucault is explicit that disciplinary vision is "about more than the male gaze." Second, hooks's oppositional gaze (hooks, 1992: 116) operates as a counter-current refusing the male gaze from within.

Does this fragment the male gaze? On one reading, yes. The unified heterosexual male spectator has been complicated by Foucault's disciplinary gazes and by hooks's oppositional gaze. Mulvey's "determining male gaze" no longer determines a single mainstream.

But on a stronger reading, no. The male gaze has been redistributed rather than dissolved. Three observations support this.

First, Berger's surveyor/surveyed split persists structurally. The woman is "almost continually accompanied by her own image of herself" (Berger, 1972: 46) and has been "taught and persuaded to survey herself continually." Memling's *Vanity* and Tintoretto's mirror version of *Susannah and the Elders* catalogue the internal split any medium can amplify. Instagram filters and Snapchat dysmorphia are the contemporary apparatuses.

Second, the wider gendered regime persists. Berger's tour (Mabuse, Pol de Limbourg, Slevogt, Cranach, Rubens, Lely, Bronzino) shows conventions reproduced for centuries. The Calvin Klein advertisements are not the end but the latest reproduction. TikTok aesthetics and selfie culture continue the lineage.

Third, Foucault's disciplinary gaze "infiltrates the others" (Foucault, 1977: 316) without replacing them. The medical gaze on the pregnant body does not abolish the male gaze; it operates alongside.

hooks's intervention bears here. The male gaze was also a white gaze (hooks, 1992: 115). The Human Zoos remind us that the imperial gaze on colonised women operated alongside the male gaze on white women. Fragmentation has redistributed but not dissolved the racial dimension. "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116) operates inside the multi-regime visual order.

Haraway adds the meta-frame. Each scopic regime presents its visual order as natural while encoding particular standpoints (Haraway, 1991: 189). Fragmentation does not abolish the god trick by which any one regime can present itself as natural.

Tests across the exhibits. The European nude tradition is the male gaze in painting; the Calvin Klein advertisements are the male gaze in print; obstetric ultrasound is the medical gaze; the Mercator Projection is the cartographic god trick; the Human Zoos are the colonial gaze; facial recognition extends the disciplinary gaze digitally. Each is a distinct visual regime; each operates the structural superiority of the gazer over the object (Schroeder, 1998). Berger's "men act and women appear" persists across the first two; Foucault's disciplinary gaze persists across the rest; hooks's oppositional gaze interrupts where it can.

Critical limits. The "fragmentation" thesis can over-egg the dispersal: the underlying mechanism (gazer superior to object) is structurally constant. The "redistribution" thesis can under-credit hooks's counter-currents and Haraway's situated alternatives.

The male gaze has been multiplied across visual regimes but not dissolved. Mulvey's mechanism survives in altered form. Berger's surveyor inside the woman persists. hooks's racial supplement remains necessary. Foucault's institutional inventory expands the field. Sociology's reading of any visual order must ask: whose look is encoded, what scopic regime is operating, what alternatives are situated alongside? The male gaze has not been dissolved; it has been situated among its siblings.

### Mock 12 - Can a vision be sociological without being political?

The question asks whether a sociologically valid account of vision can be politically neutral. My answer is that the political is constitutive (not as ideology but as the situatedness of every act of looking) and that "neutral" sociology of vision is a contradiction in terms. I argue through Haraway, Foucault, Berger and hooks, then defend against the objection that the position collapses into advocacy.

Haraway's foundational answer. Every act of seeing is "from somewhere." "The god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the pretence by which knowledge escapes its situation. Sociology that adopts the god trick is not less political; it is more political, because it imposes its standpoint as universal while denying that it has one. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). A vision that refuses answerability is power masquerading as neutrality.

The question is not whether the analyst's vision has a politics but whether the politics is acknowledged. Acknowledged politics is open to revision; denied politics is closed. "It matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12).

Foucault's institutional answer. The medical, juridical and pedagogical gazes are not neutral; they produce patients, criminals and pupils as objects of knowledge. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200). Disciplinary power "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316). Any sociology that takes the clinical case-record or the criminal mugshot as transparent data inherits the institution's politics.

Berger's cultural answer. The European nude was not a record but a production for a male spectator-owner. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). The Frans Hals example (Berger, 1972: 11): an "authoritative" study reproduces, by silence, the ruling-class standpoint. Sociology of art that treats Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love* as transparent depiction of "beauty" inherits the politics of the spectator-owner.

hooks's racial answer. "Slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). Sociology of cinema or art that takes the white spectator as default audience reproduces, by silence, the racial politics of the visual regime.

Tests across the exhibits. First, Human Zoos. A "neutral" sociology of European and US expositions that catalogued attendance without naming the colonial gaze would reproduce by silence the politics of the displays. The museum gaze on Indigenous remains operates the same logic. Second, Mercator. A sociology of cartography that took Mercator as "the world" reproduces the imperial standpoint; Gall-Peters and the "Upside Down" map are situated alternatives. Third, obstetric ultrasound. A sociology that did not name the erasure of the pregnant woman ("the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information", Kember, 1998) reproduces the gaze it claims to study. Fourth, the European nude. A sociology of art that treats Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* as transparent biblical illustration ignores Berger's reading of the Elders as spectator's surrogate.

The objection. The strongest objection is that my claim collapses sociology into advocacy. If every account is political, why prefer one to another?

Haraway anticipates this. Situated knowledge is not relativism. It is a demand for "strong objectivity" built from acknowledged partiality, with structural preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" because they are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." A sociology that names its position is more rigorous than one that hides it. Disclosure enables critique; concealment forecloses it. A propagandist hides her standpoint; a situated sociologist names hers.

A weaker version asks whether sociology can be politically modest, describing without prescribing. Yes: sociology can be modest about specific policy recommendations while remaining politically situated. Describing the imperial gaze of the Human Zoos is sociological; prescribing how a museum should reorganise an ethnographic collection is policy. But the description is not neutral; it discloses the position of the analyst.

Critical limits. The position can over-politicise: not every visual choice is consequential. Some images are banal. The point is not that every detail carries political weight but that banality is not neutrality; even the family snapshot, as Berger noted, encodes a way of seeing (Berger, 1972: 10).

A vision cannot be sociological without being political, because sociological vision is constitutively positioned. The question is not whether to have a politics but whether to have one openly. Haraway's answerability, Foucault's disclosure, Berger's demystification and hooks's oppositional naming are the modes by which sociology of vision becomes political and rigorous.

### Mock 13 - "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault). Discuss.

Foucault's epigram "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200) named the mechanism of the Panopticon. I argue the epigram organises the lecture's account of vision more broadly: it names the panoptic gaze itself but also reads forward into Berger, Kember, hooks and Haraway.

Foucault's trap precisely. Bentham's Panopticon makes the inmate continuously seeable from the central tower. Because the inmate cannot tell whether the inspector is watching, he behaves as if always watched. Power becomes "visible and unverifiable": the tower is visible, the inspector's actual occupation is not. The inmate internalises the gaze and becomes self-disciplining. The trap is not in being watched but in not knowing when. The disciplinary gaze "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316) and "has infiltrated the others … making it possible to bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements." The Panopticon is a diagram, recurring across prisons, schools, hospitals and armies. Bentham's Auto-Icon at UCL makes the diagram material: the architect becomes its subject. CCTV networks and airport biometrics extend the diagram across contemporary public space.

Berger's surveyor/surveyed as panoptic trap. Women learn to inhabit their own visibility (Berger, 1972: 46): "she has been taught and persuaded to survey herself continually." The male surveyor is "visible and unverifiable" inside the woman, present as continuous structure without any particular male watching. Berger's tour (Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* in its mirror version; Memling's *Vanity*; Lely's Nell Gwynne, "a sign of her submission to the owner's feelings or demands") catalogues the trap inside paintings. The Calvin Klein advertisements stage it in print; Instagram filters and selfie culture intensify it on the screen.

Kember's medical gaze as panoptic trap. Obstetric ultrasound "eliminates the mother's body from view" so that "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998). The pregnant woman is seen (body imaged, foetus made visible) and yet not seen (body rendered ambient infrastructure). Visibility traps because the visible is structured against her even as it depends on her.

hooks's racial supplement. Foucault under-theorised race. Under slavery "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); "slaves were denied their right to gaze." The trap was asymmetric: enslaved people were forced into visibility as objects of disciplinary surveillance while denied the visibility that constitutes a subject. The oppositional gaze, "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116), refuses this asymmetric trap.

Haraway's god trick as enabling fiction. Haraway's god trick (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the move by which the panoptic apparatus presents itself as natural observation. The Panopticon disguises itself as architecture; the medical scan disguises itself as transparent access to the foetus; the European nude disguises itself as aesthetic appreciation. Each performs the god trick that lets the trap operate without acknowledgement. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190) names the counter-practice: to make the trap visible as a trap is to begin to escape it.

Other instances. The Human Zoos at nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century European and US World Fairs are the colonial form: colonised persons were "imported" for "exhibit" as ethnographic objects; their visibility was the trap by which the imperial gaze constituted them. The Mercator Projection is the cartographic form: the world is made visible in a way that organises the visible against the Global South. Leonardo's *Map of Imola* and the convex mirror in van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* are early instances; the mirror in van Eyck shows the visible is reflected from somewhere, even when the painting pretends to a flat record. The "World Turned Upside Down" map exposes the convention of orientation. Deepfakes invert the trap: the visible is fabricated, while the viewer is trapped into trusting it.

Critical limits. Foucault may overstate compliance. Counter-practices abound: hooks's oppositional gaze, Berger's pedagogy of demystification, Haraway's situated knowledge. Visibility traps but is also negotiated. The Gall-Peters Projection negotiates Mercator's trap; the "Upside Down" map negotiates the orientation trap; the oppositional gaze negotiates the racial trap. The diagnosis is not destiny.

The epigram holds. "Visibility is a trap" reads through the panopticon, the surveyor inside the woman, the obstetric ultrasound, the colonial exposition and the cartographic projection. Foucault's diagnosis is the lecture's most compressed claim: vision distributes power, and the distribution is asymmetric.

### Mock 14 - How does the imperial gaze of the nineteenth-century Human Zoos help us read modernity's visual order?

The lecture pairs the Mercator Projection with Human Zoos to make a single argument: modernity's visual order is constitutively imperial. The Human Zoos. World Fairs and Expositions held by major European countries (France, the UK, Belgium, Spain) and the US in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries "imported" colonised people for "exhibit". The colonial gaze converted persons into spectacle. Visitors paid to look; the displayed persons were classified, photographed, measured. The expositions were where empire was visible to its metropolitan publics.

Schroeder. "To gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998). The European spectator is constituted as unmarked observer; the colonised person as marked object. The architecture (paths, platforms, fencing) makes the asymmetry literal.

Foucault. The disciplinary gaze "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316) and "enables us to see some kinds of subject … and to analyse them as objects." The Human Zoo distributed light across colonised populations. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200). The museum gaze on Indigenous remains operates as contemporary residue.

hooks's racial counter-history. Under slavery "white slave-owners (men, women, and children) punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); "slaves were denied their right to gaze." The Human Zoo is the European-public counterpart: the imperial subject given an unimpeded look at colonised persons whose right to return the look was denied. Berger. The imperial gaze is continuous with the gendered gaze. The painted woman is arranged for the unpainted male spectator (Berger, 1972: 47); the displayed colonised person for the unmarked European spectator. Both are "to-be-looked-at". They are visual evidence that the colonial gaze and Foucault's disciplinary gaze are instances of a single scopic regime. The European spectator who looked at the displayed colonised person at the exposition was the same subject who, under Berger's reading, looked at Cranach's *Judgement of Paris* in the gallery, and the same subject who, under Foucault's reading, looked through the inspection slit of the prison. Facial recognition systems and airport biometrics now extend the imperial gaze into algorithmic forms that classify populations by phenotype. The Mercator Projection abstracts territory rather than displaying bodies. The mechanisms differ in important ways. The point is not that all imperial vision is identical but that the imperial gaze persists as a structural pattern across distinct visual regimes.

The imperial gaze has not been dismantled by the end of the Human Zoos; it has been reorganised. The lecture's juxtaposition tells us the visual order of modernity is constitutively imperial, that the post-imperial counter-gazes (Gall-Peters, oppositional gaze, situated knowledge) are necessary, and that the sociological reading of any contemporary visual order must ask what kind of imperial gaze it inherits.

### Mock 15 - Counter-currents to the male gaze: hooks, Haraway and the limits of refusal.

I argue hooks's oppositional gaze and Haraway's situated knowledge name the principal counter-currents to the male gaze, that they presuppose rather than refute the gaze they contest, and that the limits of refusal sharpen rather than weaken the sociological reading.

Mulvey's actual claim. Mulvey (1975) argued classical Hollywood operated "a determining male gaze" through scopophilia and ego-identification. The camera was structurally heterosexual male; the woman appeared; identification routed through the male protagonist. The argument was about dominant cinema, not all cinema. The male gaze was a hegemonic convention, not a metaphysical absolute.

Berger's anchoring. Berger's analysis of the European nude tradition (Berger, 1972: 45-54) is the deep history. From Mabuse's *Adam and Eve* and Pol de Limbourg's *Fall and Expulsion from Paradise* through Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* in two versions, Memling's *Vanity*, Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris*, Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne for Charles II and Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love*, the convention persists. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements show the convention crossing media; Instagram filters and TikTok aesthetics extend it into digital self-surveillance. Mulvey's male gaze is the modern descendant of this tradition.

Counter-current one: hooks's oppositional gaze. "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). The oppositional gaze emerges from the historical denial: "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). It refuses capture: the black female spectator looks back. It produces alternative knowledge: the gaze "opens up the possibility of agency." Beyoncé's *Lemonade* visual album operationalises the oppositional gaze in cultural production. hooks presupposes Mulvey: the oppositional gaze is legible because the gaze it opposes is real.

Counter-current two: Haraway's situated knowledge. No vision is from nowhere; "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the move by which any standpoint masquerades as universal. The male gaze is one situated regime; recognising situatedness refuses the trick. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). "It matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12).

Counter-current three: Berger's pedagogy of demystification. Berger teaches the reader to read Memling's *Vanity* as the painter's enjoyment disguised as moral condemnation, or Tintoretto's mirror version of *Susannah and the Elders* as Susannah's enrolment as spectator of herself. He gives tools to refuse the conventions from within.

Limits of refusal. First, the counter-currents presuppose the gaze they contest. The oppositional gaze is legible because the prohibition is real; situated knowledge is necessary because the god trick is dominant; demystification is needed because the conventions are pervasive. The counter-currents do not abolish the gaze. Second, they operate inside particular regimes. hooks's oppositional gaze is most powerful in cinematic spectatorship; Haraway's situated knowledge in method; Berger's demystification in art-historical pedagogy. None abolishes the wider scopic regime. Third, Foucault's reminder that disciplinary vision is "more than the male gaze" implies counter-currents to the male gaze do not yet address the disciplinary gaze on schoolchildren, patients and criminals, or the medical gaze (Kember).

Synthesis through the exhibits. The Human Zoos at European and US expositions, the European nude tradition, the Calvin Klein advertisements, the Mercator Projection and obstetric ultrasound are five visual regimes. The male gaze operates centrally in the nude tradition and the advertisements; it intersects with the colonial gaze in the Human Zoos; with the medical gaze in obstetric ultrasound; with the cartographic god trick in Mercator. Counter-currents (oppositional gaze, situated knowledge, Berger's demystification, Gall-Peters and the "Upside Down" map) interrupt each regime where they can. Facial recognition extends the disciplinary regime into algorithmic forms that have not yet been adequately countered.

Critical limits. The counter-currents can be co-opted (the oppositional gaze sold as brand identity, situated knowledge reduced to identitarian closure, demystification consumed as scholarly fashion). A counter-current that refuses one regime can be re-enrolled into another.

The male gaze persists as the dominant convention of the European visual tradition Berger catalogued, the cinematic apparatus Mulvey theorised and the print advertising the lecture exhibits. hooks's oppositional gaze, Haraway's situated knowledge and Berger's demystification are the counter-currents that interrupt it. They presuppose the gaze; they operate inside particular regimes; they are necessary and not sufficient. The sociology of vision is the work of mapping these counter-currents and naming their limits.
