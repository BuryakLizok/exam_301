# L10 - Ways of Seeing: Vision, Knowledge and Power

## 1. Lecture overview

This lecture argues that vision is never innocent. To see is to occupy a position from which power flows, knowledge gets organised and social hierarchies are produced or contested. Drawing on Schroeder, Berger, Mulvey, hooks, Foucault and Haraway, the lecture shows that visual representations do not simply mirror a pre-existing reality; they construct it. From the colonial gaze of nineteenth-century Human Zoos to the panoptic gaze inside prisons, schools and hospitals; from the male gaze that converts women into objects of vision to the oppositional gaze that re-politicises black spectatorship; from the god trick of bird's-eye objectivity to Haraway's situated knowledge, vision is bound up with modernity, inequality, colonisation, slavery, gender and the discipline of bodies. The lecture's overarching question is "how is vision political?" and the answer is that every act of looking distributes light and shadow, includes some bodies as subjects and reduces others to objects, naturalises some perspectives as universal and renders others invisible.

## 2. Key thinkers and concepts

- **John Berger (*Ways of Seeing*, 1972):** Seeing comes before words. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Images embody a way of seeing; reproduction and photography transform art's social meaning. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). The surveyor/surveyed split inside women. Cultural mystification of the past.
- **Jonathan Schroeder (1998):** "To gaze implies more than to look at, it signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998).
- **Laura Mulvey (1975):** the male gaze in cinema, women as passive objects of a heterosexual male spectator's pleasure and control.
- **bell hooks (*Black Looks*, 1992):** the oppositional gaze. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life … There is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Enslaved black people denied the right to gaze; the oppositional gaze as agency: "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116).
- **Michel Foucault (*Discipline and Punish*, 1977):** the panoptic gaze. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]); "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable." Disciplinary power produces schoolchildren, patients, criminals as objects of knowledge.
- **Donna Haraway (*Simians, Cyborgs and Women*, 1991; *Staying with the Trouble*, 2016):** situated knowledge against the god trick. "The god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189). "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196). In *Staying with the Trouble* she adds "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12).
- **Sarah Kember (*Virtual Anxiety*, 1998):** medical scans render the pregnant woman invisible. "The body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]).

## 6. Common exam pitfalls

- Treating "the male gaze" as Mulvey's entire theory; pair it with Berger's surveyor/surveyed.
- Reducing all vision to gender; Foucault insists disciplinary vision is "more than the male gaze."
- Quoting Haraway as a relativist; she defends strong objectivity through acknowledged partiality.
- Forgetting the colonial/racial dimension (hooks, Human Zoos, Mercator).
- Treating images as neutral reflections; remember Berger, every image embodies a way of seeing.
- Missing the political stakes: vision produces, reproduces and can challenge inequality.

### 2025 Q12 / 2023 Q12 - Donna Haraway stated 'How we see the world is always a matter of power'. Explain and discuss its relevance for sociology today.

I read Haraway's claim as the keystone of a feminist epistemology that breaks with the modern dream of disembodied objectivity. To say that "how we see the world is always a matter of power" is to insist that vision is not the passive reception of given facts but an active, embodied, historically located practice. In this essay I unpack what Haraway means, place her in the wider lineage of theorists of vision and power, and argue that her position is more sociologically urgent today than when she first articulated it in 1988.

Haraway's situated knowledge begins as a critique of what she calls "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189). The bird's-eye view that scientists, mapmakers and policy experts claim, the view from above, with no body, no history, no interests, is in fact, she argues, the subjective view of the dominant unmarked group passed off as universal. Leonardo's 1502 map of Imola, used in the lecture, is the perfect emblem: a perspective no actual eye can occupy, yet presented as the truth of the city. For Haraway, "knowledge that is un-locatable is also knowledge that is irresponsible." Her positive alternative is that "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Strong objectivity is built not from pretended neutrality but from openly acknowledged partiality and from "the standpoints of the subjugated" which are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." Her famous formula, "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196), captures the paradox: bigger truth comes from owning where you are.

I situate this claim inside a longer conversation. Schroeder's reminder that "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998) tells us looking is never flat. Berger had already shown in *Ways of Seeing* that "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Mulvey's notion of the male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) named one specific scopic regime in which women appear as objects. Foucault's panoptic gaze, where "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]), described another, in which the disciplinary gaze produces schoolchildren, patients and criminals as analysable objects. hooks's oppositional gaze added the racial dimension, recalling that "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). Haraway gathers these strands into a meta-claim: every act of seeing is positioned, and every position is shot through with power.

Why does this matter for sociology today? First, the nineteenth-century Human Zoos that the lecture documents (the World Fairs and Expositions in France, the UK, Belgium, Spain and the US that "imported" colonised people for "exhibit") show how the imperial god trick was performed at industrial scale: European spectators positioned themselves as the unmarked observers and reduced colonised persons to ethnographic objects. The disciplinary gaze Foucault analyses still organises the institutional production of pupils, patients and prisoners. Second, the medical regime Kember examines extends the god trick into reproductive bodies: in obstetric ultrasound "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]), a particular technological view masquerades as transparent access to the foetus. Third, the cartographic order of Mercator alongside Gall-Peters shows that what counts as "the world" is a situated argument; the unmarked European centre of the Mercator presents itself as the world while concealing the projection that produces it. Fourth, Berger's surveyor/surveyed split (Berger, 1972: 46) is the everyday domestic operation of the god trick at the scale of gendered selves: women are taught to inhabit an external position from which they survey themselves continually.

I want to underline the relevance for sociological method itself. Haraway is not just doing critique; she is reforming how sociology should work. If we accept her claim, then survey research, ethnography and observational study must all declare their position. The interviewer's class, race, gender and history are not contaminants to be controlled away but constitutive of the knowledge produced. When I read a study of welfare claimants written from a Treasury standpoint, I am reading a situated knowledge masquerading as objective fact; when I read a study by claimants themselves I am reading a different and, on Haraway's argument, potentially stronger objectivity because its situatedness is owned. Berger makes the same point about art history: the "authoritative work" on Frans Hals he cites (Berger, 1972: 11) is presented as neutral expertise but in fact reproduces the standpoint of a "privileged minority … striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes."

The relevance also extends to the question of what counts as knowledge. Foucault's disciplinary gaze, which "enables us to see some kinds of subject (schoolchildren, patients, criminals) and to analyse them as objects", performs the god trick at institutional scale: a particular knowledge-producing apparatus appears as the natural way of seeing the population. Haraway extends her own argument in *Staying with the Trouble* by adding that "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12); the partial connections we use to compose knowledge are themselves consequential.

There are limits. Haraway's situated knowledge can be misread as relativism (every standpoint is just one among many) and used to defend reactionary "common-sense" views. Her actual position is sharper: situatedness plus accountability plus a preference for subjugated standpoints. There is also a risk of identitarian closure, where only members of a group can speak about that group; Haraway herself prefers webs of partial connection and coalition.

Overall, Haraway's statement is not a slogan but a research programme. It tells me, as a sociology student, that every dataset is also a gaze, every theory a position, every photograph a way of seeing. To take it seriously is to make sociology answerable for what it learns how to see. (732 words [VERIFY count])

### 2022 Q14 - Critically examine the links between vision, power and knowledge.

In this essay I argue that vision, power and knowledge form an inseparable triad in modern sociology, and that the lecture's central concept, ways of seeing, is the most productive way to grasp it. I move from Berger's account of seeing as culturally formed, through Foucault's disciplinary gaze, Mulvey's male gaze and hooks's oppositional gaze, to Haraway's situated knowledge, before pulling them together around the course's own examples.

Berger's account of seeing opens the problem. "Seeing comes before words … The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). I find this useful because it refuses the common-sense idea that vision is a window onto reality. For Berger, "every image embodies a way of seeing" (Berger, 1972: 10 [VERIFY PAGE?]); reproduction by camera and screen detaches images from their original context and circulates them inside new regimes of meaning. He shows that the European nude tradition encoded a particular knowledge: that women are "to-be-looked-at," that "men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47), that beauty is a competitive prize awarded to the woman judged most desirable by an external male spectator. The painting is not a record of what was; it is a production of how women were to be known. So vision already produces knowledge, and that knowledge already encodes power.

Foucault's panoptic gaze sharpens the link. In *Discipline and Punish* the Panopticon is the architectural diagram of modern power because it organises visibility itself: "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]); "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable." The inmate, never knowing when watched, internalises the inspector's gaze. Crucially, Foucault links this to knowledge: the disciplinary gaze "enables us to see some kinds of subject", schoolchildren, patients, criminals, "and to analyse them as objects." The clinic, the prison, the school and the army produce the knowledges that constitute their subjects. Power/knowledge is not a slogan but a procedure: a way of distributing light. Crucially Foucault adds that this disciplinary gaze "has infiltrated the others" so as to "bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, 1977: 316), giving us an infinitesimal distribution of power that vision makes possible. Bentham's Auto-Icon, the preserved body of the utilitarian theorist displayed at UCL, is the lecture's reminder that the architect of the panoptic principle is himself put on display.

The feminist critique of cinema extends Foucault's argument into gender. Mulvey's notion of the male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) identifies a scopic regime in which the camera is the eye of a heterosexual male, and women's bodies appear as objects of his pleasure and control. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split provides the interior mechanism: women learn to inhabit the gaze, to "watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). Together these accounts show that gender as social knowledge is produced through specific habits of looking. The lecture's Calvin Klein "Obsession" and "Obsession for Men" advertisements with Kate Moss literalise this: the woman appears, the heterosexual male spectator is hailed by the brand name, the same image is sold to both genders under the possessive grammar of the male gaze.

hooks complicates this by adding race. She recalls that in slavery "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); power was exercised by denying the right to gaze. Out of that trauma, she argues, an oppositional gaze emerged: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, 1992: 116). Vision becomes the site of both subjection and resistance; knowledge produced from below contests knowledge produced from above. The Human Zoos staged across European and US expositions are the colonial counterpart of the slave-owner's prohibition: colonised persons were forced to be visible as ethnographic spectacle while the European spectator's own position remained unmarked.

Haraway's situated knowledge gives the synthesis. There is no view from nowhere; "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the trick that dominant knowledges use to universalise their position. Real knowledge is partial, embodied, locatable, and "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). I read this as the meta-thesis that subsumes Foucault, Mulvey and hooks: vision constructs knowledge; knowledge constitutes power; therefore acknowledging where one sees from is an ethical and political act.

The course's own visual exhibits stage the triad concretely. Leonardo's 1502 *Map of Imola* presents a perspective no human eye can occupy and offers it as the truth of the city: Haraway's god trick in cartographic form. The Mercator Projection inflates Greenland and miniaturises Africa and presents this distortion as "the world"; the Gall-Peters counter-projection and the New Internationalist "World Turned Upside Down" map make the political conventions of orientation and proportion visible. Kember's analysis of obstetric ultrasound shows the medical gaze producing the foetus as visible while the pregnant woman exists "only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]). The detail of van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* with its convex mirror, used to accompany the situated-knowledge slide, makes the same point inside the picture: vision is always reflected from somewhere.

Critically, the triad has limits. Foucault's panopticon can over-determine subjects as docile; hooks's oppositional gaze reminds us that the surveyed look back. Haraway's emphasis on situatedness can collapse into identitarian closure unless paired with her insistence on partial connection. And vision is not the only sensory politics: hearing, smell and touch are also organised by power, and a fully sociological account would integrate them.

Still, the lecture's central insight holds. Vision, power and knowledge are mutually constitutive. Every photograph is a position; every dataset is a gaze; every map is an argument. Sociology's task is to make these positions visible, to refuse the god trick and to take responsibility for how it learns to see. (745 words [VERIFY count])

### 2021 Q14 - 'There are various ways of seeing the world, but they are all linked to questions of power.' Critically analyse.

The statement names what I take to be the most important single insight of the sociology of vision: that pluralism of perspectives does not dissolve power but instead expresses it. In this essay I unpack the claim, defend it against simple relativism, and explore where it leads using the course's own theorists and exhibits.

The lecture opens its cartographic argument with the Mercator and Gall-Peters projections side by side. Both are maps of the world; both are accurate in their own terms; yet they encode radically different politics. Mercator inflates the high latitudes (northern Europe and Canada bulge, Africa shrinks) and served European navigators sailing trade and colonial routes. Gall-Peters preserves relative area, restoring Africa, South America and South Asia to their true proportions. Two ways of seeing the same earth, both linked to questions of power: the imperial gaze that miniaturises the Global South, and the post-colonial counter-gaze that returns it to size. The New Internationalist "Upside Down" world map goes further by inverting north and south, exposing the political nature of the convention that puts Europe at the top. Maps, like all images, are ways of seeing tied to standpoints.

Berger gives the theoretical hinge. "The way we see things is affected by what we know or what we believe" (Berger, 1972: 8 [VERIFY PAGE?]); "every image embodies a way of seeing." But Berger does not stop at plurality. He asks whose interest plurality serves and whose it occludes. The cultural mystification of the past, he argues, is performed because "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, 1972: 11). When art is mystified (his example is the authoritative two-volume study of Frans Hals), the ruling class's past is naturalised as everyone's past. Multiple ways of seeing exist, but the dominant one masquerades as the only one. The phrase "all linked to questions of power" thus does not mean every way of seeing is equally powerful; it means every way of seeing has a location in a field of power.

Foucault deepens this. The panoptic gaze is not just one perspective among many; it is the perspective that knows it is a perspective and uses that knowledge to discipline others into self-monitoring. The disciplinary gaze produces particular subjects (schoolchildren, patients, prisoners) and analyses them as objects. Knowledge built from these gazes is then used to govern. Bentham's Auto-Icon, the preserved body of the utilitarian philosopher displayed at UCL, is a visual reminder that the gaze is itself displayed and disciplined. Multiple gazes exist (medical, juridical, pedagogical), but all are linked to power because all distribute light, visibility and knowability.

Mulvey's notion of the male gaze and Berger's surveyor/surveyed give a gendered case. Two people can stand in the same gallery and see the same Bronzino *Allegory of Time and Love*; one is positioned as the addressed male spectator, the other as the surveyed woman who learns from earliest childhood "to survey herself continually" (Berger, 1972: 46). Different ways of seeing, but both produced inside, and constitutive of, the patriarchal power relation. Berger shows that even within the European tradition the same image can be read as Venus and Cupid for moral cover or, as in Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne for Charles II, as the king's mistress whose nakedness signals "her submission to the owner's feelings or demands." hooks's oppositional gaze multiplies the plurality further: black female spectators develop a defiant counter-look that refuses the white male position cinema invites. Plurality of gazes is real, but each gaze has political coordinates.

Haraway's situated knowledge is the meta-statement of the claim under analysis. She insists that "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) must be refused, but she does not slide into "anything goes." Situated knowledge is rigorous because it owns its position and is therefore accountable. Her preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" is not because they are intrinsically virtuous but because they are less likely "to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." The plurality of ways of seeing, in other words, is asymmetrically distributed: dominant gazes pretend to universality, subjugated gazes know they are positioned.

I now critically test the claim against three exhibits from the course. First, the colonial gaze of Human Zoos. The "imported" colonised persons displayed at nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century expositions in France, the UK, Belgium, Spain and the US were not shown alongside the European spectator's own way of seeing; the spectator's position was unmarked, the colonised body was the only object. Two ways of seeing were in play (the European ethnographic and the colonised's own); only one was permitted to count as knowledge. Second, the medical gaze on the pregnant body. Kember's claim that the scanning process "eliminates the mother's body from view" so that "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]) exposes the power inside the medical way of seeing: the foetus appears as visible person while the pregnant woman is rendered ambient infrastructure. Third, Berger's contrast between the European nude tradition (the supine, spectator-addressed woman in Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders*, Memling's *Vanity*, Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris*) and "non-European traditions, in Indian art, Persian art, African art, Pre-Columbian art" where "nakedness is never supine in this way" (Berger, 1972: 53 [VERIFY PAGE?]) and figures are shown as "two people, the woman as active as the man." Two ways of seeing the human body, neither neutral, both political.

There are critical objections. First, the claim can sound deterministic, as if no neutral seeing is possible. I respond that "linked to power" does not mean "reducible to power"; it means we should ask the question. Second, it can ignore the materiality of vision (optics, neurology). I respond that the materiality of seeing is itself organised socially (eye-tests, glasses, photographic conventions, framing). Third, it can flatten resistance into mere "another perspective." I respond, with hooks, that resistance is real but always inside power.

The statement therefore holds. Plurality of ways of seeing is the condition of the modern visual field; power is what organises and weights that plurality. Sociology's task is to map the field, expose the god trick and amplify the subjugated gazes. (755 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 1 - "Men act and women appear" (Berger). Discuss.

I read Berger's aphorism, "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47), as the most compressed statement in modern visual sociology of how gender hierarchies are reproduced through looking. In this essay I unpack the line within Berger's wider argument, place it against Mulvey's male gaze and hooks's oppositional gaze, test it against the lecture's own exhibits, and weigh critical objections.

Berger develops the line across the middle of *Ways of Seeing* by contrasting the social presence of men and women. "A man's presence is dependent upon the promise of power which he embodies" (Berger, 1972: 45). The man's presence is outward, indicating what he can do to or for others. A woman's presence, by contrast, "expresses her own attitude to herself, and defines what can and cannot be done to her." She is born into "an allotted and confined space, into the keeping of men," and so her presence develops at the cost of "a woman's self being split into two." Berger names this the surveyor/surveyed split: from earliest childhood "she has been taught and persuaded to survey herself continually" (Berger, 1972: 46). The result, in his crystallised line, is that "men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47).

The European nude tradition is Berger's chief evidence. From Tintoretto's two versions of *Susannah and the Elders* (in one Susannah is spied upon by the Elders and "looks back at us looking at her"; in the other she joins her own spectators via a mirror) to Memling's *Vanity*, where the painter "put a mirror in her hand and called the painting *Vanity*, thus morally condemning the woman whose nakedness you had depicted for your own pleasure," to Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris* in which "Paris awards the apple to the woman he finds most beautiful" so that "Beauty becomes competitive," to Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne commissioned by Charles II that "shows her passively looking at the spectator staring at her naked … a sign of her submission to the owner's feelings or demands," to Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love* which "before it is anything else, … is a painting of sexual provocation" (Berger, 1972: 54): the painted woman is arranged for an unpainted male spectator standing outside the frame. Berger writes that to be nude in this tradition is "to be seen naked by others and yet not recognized for oneself" (Berger, 1972: 54 [VERIFY PAGE?]). The convention is not innocent. It encodes the bourgeois patriarchal order (the man as proprietor of the look, the woman as ornamental property) and circulates that order as taste, beauty and aesthetic judgement.

Mulvey extends Berger from the gallery to the cinema. Her notion of the male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) identifies a scopic regime in which Hollywood's camera operates as a heterosexual male spectator. Active narrative agency runs through the male protagonist; the woman halts narrative motion to "appear", the close-up on the leg, the lingering on the face, the slow tracking shot up the body. The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisement with Kate Moss reclining and its "Obsession for Men" companion campaign, both used in the lecture, literalise the formula: she appears, the brand grammar of "for men" hails the spectator-purchaser, the same body sells perfume to women and men under the possessive sign of the male gaze.

hooks complicates the diagnosis. The black female spectator has never been the addressed audience of Hollywood, and her relation to "men look at women" is doubled by race. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115); her oppositional gaze refuses the white-male position cinema invites and declares "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). Berger's aphorism therefore needs racial specification: the woman who appears in the European nude tradition is white; the black female body has been seen through a different scopic regime, most starkly in the Human Zoos staged at European and US expositions, where colonised women were displayed as ethnographic spectacle.

Foucault sharpens the institutional analysis. Disciplinary power "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316) that is not reducible to gender. The pregnant body Kember analyses through obstetric ultrasound is rendered medically "as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]); the surveyor here is not only the imagined male spectator inside the patient but the medical apparatus that detaches the foetus from her body as visible person. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split is multiplied: women appear before patriarchal spectators, medical apparatuses and the disciplinary gazes that classify them.

There are real objections. First, Berger universalises from European oil painting; he himself notes that "in other non-European traditions, in Indian art, Persian art, African art, Pre-Columbian art, nakedness is never supine in this way" and that there "the woman as active as the man, the actions of each absorbing the other" (Berger, 1972: 53 [VERIFY PAGE?]). Second, the binary erases queer and lesbian spectatorship and the gay male gaze. Third, hooks's reminder that the surveyor inside a black woman is both male and white makes the aphorism intersectional, not pristine.

Despite the complications, the core diagnosis holds. The surveyor/surveyed split, anchored in centuries of European visual culture from the medieval Adam-and-Eve cycles through Mabuse and Slevogt to Bronzino and Lely, is reproduced through the very conventions Berger catalogued. Berger's aphorism remains the indispensable starting point for analysing gendered vision, provided we situate it racially with hooks, cinematically with Mulvey and institutionally with Foucault and Kember. To take the line seriously is to refuse the convention by which one half of humanity acts and the other half merely appears. (757 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 2 - Critically assess Berger's argument in *Ways of Seeing*.

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* was published in 1972, the same year the BBC television series ran. In this essay I assess its argument on its own terms and through the related theorists the lecture brings into conversation with it, Mulvey, hooks, Foucault and Haraway. I find the book extraordinarily rich on the surveyor/surveyed split, on the photographic reproduction of images, and on the politics of taste, and in need of supplementation on race, institutional vision and method.

Berger's central thesis is that "seeing comes before words" but is never innocent: "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). Every image embodies a way of seeing, and so does every act of looking. The book is structured around two arguments. The first concerns the reproduction of images: mechanical reproduction (the camera, the printed plate, the television screen) detaches images from their original context, multiplies them, and circulates them inside new regimes of meaning. The second concerns the production of women as objects of vision in European oil painting: "men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). The surveyor inside the woman is male; she is "almost continually accompanied by her own image of herself" (Berger, 1972: 46).

The first argument is precise and well-supported. Berger writes that "the painter's way of seeing is reconstituted by the marks he makes on the canvas" but "photographs are not, as is often assumed, a mechanical record": "every time we look at a photograph, we are aware, however slightly, of the photographer selecting that sight from an infinity of other possible sights" (Berger, 1972: 10). Magritte's *The Treachery of Images* (1948) and *The Key of Dreams* are his early visual evidence: Magritte stages the gap between image and word ("Ceci n'est pas une pipe"; the pictured horse labelled "the door"). The Snellen eye-chart slide and the *voir/savoir* etymology reinforce the point: seeing and knowing are intertwined but never identical. Berger argued the European art tradition's apparent neutrality was a production: "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, 1972: 11). His example is the two-volume scholarly study of Frans Hals, which presents itself as authoritative art history while reproducing the standpoint of the ruling class whose ancestors the paintings glorify.

The second argument is supported by a careful tour through the nude tradition. Berger reads Mabuse's *Adam and Eve* (early sixteenth-century) alongside Pol de Limbourg's *Fall and Expulsion from Paradise* (early fifteenth-century) and Slevogt's *The Couple* to show how Genesis was illustrated and then transformed: in the medieval cycle Adam and Eve "became aware of being naked because, as a result of eating the apple, each saw the other differently" (Berger, 1972: 48), whereas in the Renaissance single panel "their shame is not so much in relation to one another as to the spectator." From there Berger moves to Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* (in one version Susannah "looks back at us looking at her"; in the other she "joins the spectators of herself" through a mirror), Memling's *Vanity* (whose moralising title masks the painter's enjoyment of the painted woman), Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris* (where "Beauty becomes competitive"), Lely's commissioned portrait of Nell Gwynne for Charles II, and Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love*. The cumulative argument is that the convention of the supine, spectator-addressed female nude is "always conventionalized" and that "the authority for its conventions derives from a certain tradition of art" (Berger, 1972: 53). Berger disagrees with Kenneth Clark's *The Nude*, which treats the nude as a "form of art": Berger insists the nude is a form of dress, in which the body is placed on display rather than revealed.

Mulvey extends the second argument from gallery to cinema (Mulvey, 1975), and the lecture's Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements show the convention persisting in twentieth-century perfume advertising. hooks complicates it with race: the woman who appears in Berger's tradition is white, and "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); the oppositional gaze refuses the white male position the convention invites. The lecture's Human Zoos slide deepens the racial dimension by reminding us that European spectatorship of colonised persons in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was itself a scopic regime entangled with conquest.

Where Berger needs supplementation. First, on institutional vision. Foucault's panoptic gaze, where "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]) and disciplinary power "distributes light" across schoolchildren, patients and criminals, names a scope of vision Berger did not theorise. Kember's analysis of obstetric ultrasound, in which "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]), shows the medical apparatus producing a different kind of surveyor/surveyed relation, one Berger's aesthetic critique cannot fully reach. Second, on method. Haraway's "god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) and the call to "become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190) push Berger's critique of mystification into a positive epistemological programme of situated knowledge. Berger names what is wrong with the unmarked European spectator; Haraway names what would be right.

Critical limits to Berger's argument on its own terms. The surveyor/surveyed split assumes a relatively unified European tradition; he himself qualifies this when he notes that "in other non-European traditions, in Indian art, Persian art, African art, Pre-Columbian art" (Berger, 1972: 53 [VERIFY PAGE?]) sexual imagery is structured differently. The binary erases queer and trans spectatorship. And Berger's pedagogy of slow looking and demystification leaves open the question of what an active counter-practice would look like, a gap hooks's oppositional gaze partially fills.

Despite these limits, *Ways of Seeing* remains the indispensable handbook for the sociology of vision. The book named, with precision and concrete visual evidence, the conventions by which the European visual order produces gendered, classed knowledge. Its critical lesson, refuse the apparent innocence of images, expose the conventions, ask whose look is encoded, remains the analytic the field requires. (758 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 3 - What does Haraway mean by the god trick, and why does she treat it as the central failure of Western epistemology?

The two parts of this question demand two answers. First, what does Haraway mean by "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189)? Second, why does she treat it as the central failure of Western epistemology rather than one error among many? In this essay I take the parts in turn, then sketch the positive programme (situated knowledge) that follows from her diagnosis, before testing the analysis on the lecture's own exhibits.

The god trick names the conjuring trick by which a particular standpoint (embodied, located, partial) presents itself as a view from nowhere. The conjurer's hands move; the audience attends to the apparent omniscience. The cartographer draws the Mercator projection, the anatomist diagrams the body, the ethnographer photographs the colonised subject, and each presents what they see as if no body, no history, no funding stream and no interest had selected it. Leonardo's 1502 map of Imola, used in the lecture, exemplifies the trick: a vertical view no human eye can occupy, presented as the truth of the city. Haraway writes that such "vision from nowhere" depends on "the unmarked positions of Man and White" being treated as universal. Knowledge that is locationless, she insists, is "irresponsible", incapable of being held to account for where it came from, who paid for it, whose interests it served. The detail of van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* (1434) with its convex mirror that the lecture uses alongside the situated-knowledge slide makes the same argument inside a single painting: vision is reflected from somewhere; the maker is present in the picture; there is no neutral view.

Why is this the central failure of Western epistemology, not a side error? Three reasons emerge from her 1988 essay collected in *Simians, Cyborgs and Women* (Haraway, 1991). First, the god trick is the move by which Western science took itself to be uniquely truth-producing. The dominant epistemic tradition has staked its claim on the disembodied observer: the rational mind that sees clearly because it sees from nowhere in particular. Haraway argues this self-image is false: every observer is somewhere, every instrument is built somewhere, every dataset is collected somewhere. The trick is constitutive of the tradition, not incidental.

Second, the god trick licenses the universalisation of dominant standpoints. The "human" of the rights tradition turns out to have been the European male propertied human; the unmarked spectator of Berger's European nude is the same figure. Each universal is a particular wearing a god trick. The error compounds because each new universal builds on the prior universal.

Third, the god trick disables ethics. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190) is her positive injunction. To be answerable requires owning the position from which one sees. A vision claiming to come from nowhere cannot be held responsible for what it does. Imperial geography and the nineteenth-century Human Zoos at European and US expositions used the apparent neutrality of "observation" to convert colonised persons into ethnographic specimens. Haraway's claim is that the god trick is therefore not a stylistic embarrassment but the central enabling fiction of Western epistemic violence.

The positive programme is situated knowledge. "The only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196). Strong objectivity is built not from pretended neutrality but from openly acknowledged partiality, with a structural preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" because they are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." A worker's eye on the factory sees what the manager's eye misses; the patient's eye on the clinic sees what the consultant overlooks. This is not relativism. It is the demand that vision become accountable. In *Staying with the Trouble* Haraway extends the point by insisting "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12); the partial connections through which knowledge is composed are themselves consequential, and the practice of "becoming-with" companion species (her racing pigeons in California, the situated cosmopolitical knottings she traces) is offered as a model for situated knowledge in action.

Tests against the course's exhibits. First, cartography. The Mercator projection presents itself as "the world" while inflating Europe and miniaturising Africa; the Gall-Peters projection corrects relative area; the New Internationalist "Upside Down" map inverts north and south. Each is a situated argument; only Mercator performs the god trick by appearing as natural. Second, the medical gaze. Kember's analysis of obstetric ultrasound shows the technological view that detaches the foetus from the pregnant woman appearing as transparent access while in fact operating a particular instrumental standpoint; "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]) is the god trick performed by sonography. Third, the disciplinary gaze. Foucault's panoptic principle, "Bentham laid down the principle that power should be visible and unverifiable", encodes a god-trick variant: the inmate cannot verify who occupies the tower, but the tower itself is presented as natural architecture. Disciplinary power "has infiltrated the others … making it possible to bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, 1977: 316) precisely by disguising itself as ordinary observation. Fourth, the male gaze. Mulvey shows the cinematic camera presenting itself as neutral observer while operating as heterosexual male; Berger's surveyor inside the woman (Berger, 1972: 46) is the internalised god trick at the scale of the gendered self.

Critical limits. Haraway's framework can be misread as licensing identitarian closure ("only insiders may speak"), which she explicitly refuses through her later vocabulary of "partial connection" and coalition (Haraway, 2016: 12). It can also be co-opted by reactionary appeals to lived experience untethered from analysis; her insistence on accountability resists this. And situated knowledge is a regulative ideal, not an algorithm; it tells us to ask the question, not how to settle it.

Haraway's diagnosis nonetheless holds. The god trick is the central failure because it is the foundation move on which other epistemic violences depend. To refuse it is to begin sociology properly: as a discipline that knows where it stands. (777 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 4 - Compare Mulvey's male gaze with hooks's oppositional gaze.

Mulvey and hooks both write about cinema and looking, and both treat the screen as a site where power is exercised through visual conventions. In this essay I compare their accounts on five axes (origin and object, scopic mechanism, theory of spectatorship, account of agency, and links to the wider lecture material) and argue that hooks's intervention does not so much refute Mulvey as racialise her diagnosis and supply the resistance her account lacked.

Origin and object. Mulvey's "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (Mulvey, 1975) emerges from a psychoanalytic-Marxist reading of classical Hollywood. Her object is the mainstream feature film's apparatus and the gendered spectatorial positions it engineers. hooks's *Black Looks: Race and Representation* (hooks, 1992) emerges from black feminist criticism intersected with film studies. Her object is at once Hollywood and the historical regime of looking under slavery. Where Mulvey theorises the camera, hooks theorises the spectator, and theorises the spectator she actually is. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115).

Scopic mechanism. Mulvey identifies a scopic regime she calls the male gaze: the Hollywood camera operates as a heterosexual male, positions the woman as object of looking, and routes identification through the male protagonist who looks. Active narrative agency runs male; the woman halts the story to "appear." The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements that the lecture exhibits, in which Kate Moss reclines and is offered to a heterosexual male purchaser, literalise the mechanism in print form. hooks accepts the basic diagnosis but insists it is racially specific. The male gaze Mulvey describes is also a white gaze. Hollywood addressed an imagined white audience and produced its woman-as-object inside a racial regime that excluded, stereotyped or fetishised black women. The nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Human Zoos at European and US expositions, where colonised persons were displayed as ethnographic spectacle, are the colonial ancestor of the Hollywood casting couch: the same scopic mechanism with race made explicit. hooks's mechanism is therefore double: race-and-gender, not gender-then-race.

Theory of spectatorship. Mulvey's classical account leaves little space for spectatorial agency. The apparatus positions the spectator; resistance is theoretically possible but under-developed in the 1975 essay. hooks turns the whole problem around. She begins from a black woman watching: from her own childhood discipline for "staring … hard intense direct looks" at adults, to enslaved black people punished for looking at white owners, "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). From this prohibition emerges, hooks argues, "an overwhelming longing to look, a rebellious desire, an oppositional gaze" (hooks, 1992: 116). The oppositional gaze refuses to internalise the prohibition. "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, 1992: 116). Where Mulvey describes how the apparatus captures the spectator, hooks describes how the spectator looks back.

Account of agency. This is the sharpest contrast. Mulvey's intervention is largely diagnostic; her cinema in the 1975 account remains a machine of capture. hooks's intervention is at once diagnostic and emancipatory. The oppositional gaze "opens up the possibility of agency" inside structures of domination. It is an epistemic act (producing alternative knowledge of self and world), a political act (refusing the regime that would deny it), and a creative practice (informing black feminist filmmaking, criticism and viewership). Mulvey theorises the trap; hooks theorises the practices that loosen it.

Links to the wider lecture material. Both authors sit inside the lecture's central claim that vision is bound up with power. Schroeder's reminder that "to gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998) names the structural superiority both Mulvey and hooks analyse. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split (Berger, 1972: 46) supplies the interior mechanism by which the male gaze becomes internal to the woman; hooks's oppositional gaze names the refusal of that internalisation. Foucault's disciplinary gaze, which "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316), is not reducible to gender but cuts across it; Mulvey's male gaze is one scopic regime among Foucault's many. Haraway's situated knowledge supplies the epistemological frame: Mulvey's camera and hooks's spectator are both somewhere in particular, and the god trick by which Hollywood pretends to be nowhere in particular is the move both authors expose. The Human Zoos slide gives the colonial counterpart Mulvey did not theorise and hooks centres.

Critical points. Mulvey's account has been faulted for heterosexism (no account of the gay male gaze or lesbian look), psychoanalytic universalism, and white default. hooks's framework foregrounds black female spectatorship and does not translate automatically to other marginalised positions. Both frameworks were articulated before the contemporary visual cultures the course's introductory material flags; both nonetheless name structures that persist.

Conclusion. Mulvey gives us the diagnostic vocabulary; hooks racialises the diagnosis and supplies the politics of refusal. Read together, they show that mainstream cinema operates a heterosexual white male gaze, that this gaze is internalised but contestable, and that the oppositional gaze is the name for vision that refuses to be captured. The sociology of cinematic vision needs both, and reads them through the wider theoretical scaffolding (Berger, Schroeder, Foucault, Haraway) the lecture provides. (763 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 5 - How does the panoptic gaze help us understand contemporary regimes of visibility?

Foucault's panoptic gaze is one of the most influential analyses of vision in modern sociology. In this essay I argue that the panoptic gaze remains indispensable for understanding contemporary regimes of visibility (the institutional, medical, scopic and gendered orders examined in the lecture) and I draw the analysis from *Discipline and Punish* through Berger, Kember, hooks and Haraway.

Foucault's account is the starting point. Bentham's Panopticon, an architectural diagram of cells around a central inspection tower, ensures that the inmate "is seen, but he does not see." The crucial sentence is "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]). Because the inmate cannot tell whether the inspector is watching at any given moment, he behaves as if always watched. Power becomes "visible and unverifiable." Disciplinary power, Foucault adds, "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" and "has infiltrated the others … making it possible to bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements" (Foucault, 1977: 316). The Panopticon is therefore not a building but a diagram, recurring across prisons, schools, hospitals, factories and armies. The lecture's exhibit of Bentham's Auto-Icon at UCL, the preserved body of the theorist himself put on display, makes the diagram material: the architect of panoptic visibility becomes its subject.

How does the panoptic gaze apply across the contemporary visual regimes the lecture flags? Four mechanisms.

First, asymmetric visibility. Inside the panoptic diagram the inmate is seen but does not see; inside the institutions Foucault analysed (the prison, the clinic, the school) the same asymmetry holds. Disciplinary power "enables us to see some kinds of subject (schoolchildren, patients, criminals) and to analyse them as objects." The inmate, pupil or patient cannot verify when the disciplinary gaze is engaged or how its records will be used. Berger's account of the European nude tradition stages a parallel asymmetry inside the visual field: the unpainted male spectator stands outside the frame "with his clothes still on" (Berger, 1972: 54) while the painted woman is seen, classified and consumed.

Second, internalisation of the gaze. Foucault's most consequential claim is that the panoptic subject internalises the inspector's position and disciplines themselves. Berger's surveyor/surveyed split is the same mechanism at the scale of the gendered self: women under patriarchy are "almost continually accompanied by [their] own image of [themselves]" (Berger, 1972: 46) and have been "taught and persuaded to survey [themselves] continually." The panoptic principle "visible and unverifiable" operates inside the woman: the male surveyor is a continuous presence whose actual occupation cannot be checked.

Third, normalisation. Foucault's disciplinary gaze produces docile subjects through continuous comparison against the norm. The lecture's slide on dominant ways of seeing as "normal" makes this point directly: because the knowledges and ways of seeing of dominant groups are "taken for granted as 'true', it is difficult to question them." Haraway's god trick names the same move: a particular standpoint is normalised as the universal view. The Mercator projection naturalises the European centre of the world; Leonardo's 1502 *Map of Imola* naturalises the vertical observer no human eye occupies.

Fourth, distribution of light. Foucault's "way of distributing light" maps onto the gendered, racialised and medical fields the lecture examines. Kember's analysis of obstetric ultrasound shows the medical apparatus distributing visibility: the foetus appears in the light; the pregnant woman "exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]). Some bodies are illuminated as objects of clinical knowledge; others are made structurally invisible. hooks's analysis of slavery shows the racial distribution of light: "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); the disciplinary gaze on black bodies has historically functioned to deny them the right of return-look.

Foucault is explicit that the disciplinary gaze "is not reducible to gender" and is "about more than the male gaze." Mulvey's male gaze names one scopic regime among the many disciplinary gazes Foucault inventories. The medical gaze on the pregnant body (Kember), the colonial gaze of Human Zoos at nineteenth-century European and US expositions, and the disciplinary gazes of the school and prison all instantiate the panoptic diagram while operating different distributions of light.

Berger's parallel diagnosis of mystification adds a complementary mechanism. The European art tradition uses apparent neutrality to disguise its political function: "a privileged minority is striving to invent a history which can retrospectively justify the role of the ruling classes" (Berger, 1972: 11). Berger's authoritative two-volume Frans Hals study performs a god trick that is also a disciplinary distribution of light: certain interpretations are illuminated as scholarship, others made invisible as ideology.

hooks's oppositional gaze names the counter-mechanism the panoptic diagram does not theorise. The look that "wants to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116) refuses the trap of visibility by becoming an active gaze rather than a passive surveyed body. Haraway's positive injunction, "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190), is the methodological version of the same counter-move.

Critical limits. Foucault's panopticon imagined one central watcher and one institutional setting; the contemporary regimes the lecture flags often distribute observation across multiple sites. Foucault under-theorised race; hooks must be added to read the racial distribution of light. And the panoptic subject is not pure inmate: counter-practices proliferate, from hooks's oppositional gaze to Haraway's situated knowledge to Berger's pedagogy of demystification.

Foucault nonetheless remains indispensable. "Visibility is a trap" is the founding diagnosis of modern regimes of visibility. Read alongside Berger on the gendered surveyor/surveyed, hooks on the racial denial of the right to look, Kember on the medical erasure of the pregnant woman, and Haraway on the god trick of universalised standpoints, the panoptic gaze is precisely the analytic the contemporary visual order requires. (786 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 6 - Is the gaze possession or recognition? Discuss.

The disjunctive in this question maps two tendencies in thinking the look in the lecture's theorists. The "gaze as possession" tendency runs Schroeder, Berger, Mulvey, Foucault: to gaze is to claim, classify, dominate. The "gaze as recognition" tendency runs through hooks's oppositional gaze and Haraway's situated knowledge: to look back, to acknowledge one's position, to demand answerability. In this essay I argue that the disjunctive is real but not exclusive (the same act of looking can be both) and that the sociological task is to map when looking tips toward possession and when it tips toward recognition.

The possession tendency. Schroeder's definition is the compact statement: "to gaze implies more than to look at, it signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998). To gaze is to position oneself as subject and to reduce the gazed-upon to object. Berger's analysis of the European nude tradition demonstrates the possessive structure: the painted woman is arranged for the unpainted male spectator-owner. Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne, commissioned by Charles II, "shows her passively looking at the spectator staring at her naked … a sign of her submission to the owner's feelings or demands" (Berger, 1972: 52); Memling's *Vanity* puts a mirror in the painted woman's hand so that the moralising title disguises the painter's enjoyment of his subject; Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love* is "before it is anything else, … a painting of sexual provocation" (Berger, 1972: 54). The Tintoretto *Susannah and the Elders* and its mirror version make the structure explicit: Susannah is spied upon by the Elders inside the painting and by the spectator outside it; nakedness is "to be seen naked by others and yet not recognized for oneself" (Berger, 1972: 54). The gaze possesses by converting a person into a property of vision.

Foucault sharpens the diagnosis institutionally. The Panopticon's gaze is a possession-by-knowledge: the inmate becomes an "object" to be analysed and classified. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]). The disciplinary gaze produces particular subjects (schoolchildren, patients, criminals) as objects to be analysed, classified, governed. Mulvey extends this to cinema: the male gaze possesses the woman's body as visual property; the camera does the spectator's work of acquisition. The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements that the lecture shows side by side make possession the explicit marketing grammar: the same Kate Moss image is sold to both genders under the sign "for men."

The colonial archive radicalises possession. The Human Zoos staged at nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century World Fairs in France, the UK, Belgium, Spain and the US converted colonised persons into spectacle and confirmation of imperial hierarchy. The imperial gaze does not merely look; it acquires. The Mercator Projection performs a cartographic possession: Europe enlarges, Africa shrinks, the projection naturalises its political proportions. Kember's medical scan possesses by extracting the foetus from the pregnant woman: "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]).

The recognition tendency. hooks's oppositional gaze names a politics of refused possession. "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). The look that refuses to be possessed is also the look that demands recognition. Under slavery "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); the oppositional gaze claims that right back. The "gaze" has "always been political" in hooks's life (hooks, 1992: 115); it has always been about the recognition denied. ### Mock 8 - Does Berger's claim that "seeing comes before words" still hold?

Berger opens *Ways of Seeing* with the claim that "seeing comes before words. The child looks and recognizes before it can speak" (Berger, 1972: 7). My conclusion is that it does, provided we read the claim as Berger meant it (developmental, phenomenological and epistemic) rather than as a metaphysical priority that survives every form of mediation.

Berger's claim in context. The opening of *Ways of Seeing* is precise. "Seeing comes before words. The child looks and recognizes before it can speak … It is seeing which establishes our place in the surrounding world; we explain that world with words, but words can never undo the fact that we are surrounded by it. Berger makes three claims at once: developmental (sight precedes speech), phenomenological (vision orients us before description), and epistemic (seeing and knowing never coincide). All three are grounded in Magritte's *The Key of Dreams* (Berger, 1972: 7), where the painted door is labelled "the wind" and the painted horse "the door": the gap between what we see and what we name is the gap Berger insists is constitutive. *The Treachery of Images* (1948) repeats the same lesson with its painted pipe and the inscription "Ceci n'est pas une pipe."

The neurological and developmental claim. The Snellen eye-chart slide and the *voir/savoir* etymology reinforce a piece of common observation: children orient by sight before speech, and language is laminated onto a prior visual world. Berger is not making a strong metaphysical claim about the priority of vision over all other senses; he is naming a developmental fact and using it to support a phenomenological one: that we are "surrounded" by the visible and that words "can never undo" the surrounding.

The phenomenological claim. The visible world structures our orientation before we articulate it. Vision "establishes our place in the surrounding world." The medical scan Kember analyses is a useful test: even when the pregnant woman is reduced to "a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]), the visual sonographic image still orients her, the clinician and the foetal-rights public before any explanatory caption. The image precedes its interpretation; the interpretation never fully exhausts the image. Berger's phenomenological claim survives.

The epistemic claim. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7) is the load-bearing line. Two readings. First, vision exceeds knowledge: there is always a residue of the visible we have not articulated. Second, knowledge exceeds vision: we always know more than we can show. Berger holds both. The reading of Berger's Frans Hals example (Berger, 1972: 11) demonstrates the second: scholarly knowledge of an artist's biography can mystify the visual record. The European nude tradition demonstrates the first: the painted woman is always more than her caption, but the caption (Venus, Vanity, Susannah, Nell Gwynne, the Allegory of Time and Love) channels her visibility into a structure of male spectatorship.

Mulvey's male gaze (Mulvey, 1975) extends Berger: the camera operates as a heterosexual male spectator whose look conditions the visible woman before any narrative is articulated. The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements show the same: the visual encounter precedes the brand grammar. Foucault's panoptic gaze adds an institutional twist: "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]) operates by making subjects visible before they have a chance to articulate themselves. The disciplinary gaze "distributes light" (Foucault, 1977: 316); the subject is illuminated as an object of analysis before being asked to speak. hooks's oppositional gaze is the reply: the spectator looks back before being given the words to describe her looking; "afraid to look, but fascinated by the gaze. Haraway's situated knowledge agrees with the priority of vision but adds the demand that the position from which one sees be acknowledged: "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). The priority of seeing makes the demand for accountability more, not less, urgent.

Tests against the visual exhibits. The Mercator Projection is registered as the world before the viewer is taught how it distorts; the Gall-Peters Projection and the New Internationalist "Upside Down" map work by re-presenting the visible before retraining the words. Leonardo's *Map of Imola* (1502) presents itself as the city before any explanation acknowledges the impossible perspective. Berger's "seeing comes before words" is, in this sense, why the god trick works: the visible is registered before the viewer can recover the question of whose position the visible was constructed for. The detail of van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* with the convex mirror is the same lesson inside one painting: the mirror shows the painter in the room before the painting's caption can name him.

Where Berger needs supplementation. Berger's claim assumed indexical photography and its referential anchor; he himself argued that "photographs are not, as is often assumed, a mechanical record" but a selected sight (Berger, 1972: 10). Reproduction and circulation of images, which Berger anatomised, change the conditions of "seeing before words": the visible we are surrounded by is partly composed of images that have travelled. The Calvin Klein advertisements only function because the visual conventions of the female nude Berger catalogued (Tintoretto, Memling, Lely, Bronzino) have been reproduced into the common visual vocabulary. Haraway's god trick adds the methodological point: a vision presenting itself as natural may be a situated knowledge masquerading as universal. Berger anticipated all this with "every image embodies a way of seeing" (Berger, 1972: 10).

Critical complications. Vision is not innocent of mediation, and Berger himself said so. The point is not that seeing is unmediated but that vision is constitutive of our orientation before words name our orientation. The priority is phenomenological and developmental, not metaphysical.

Berger's claim therefore holds. Seeing still comes before words; the relation between what we see and what we know is still never settled. The political question is which words we then have at our disposal, and that is precisely the question Mulvey, hooks, Foucault, Kember and Haraway answer for the lecture. (728 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 12 - Can a vision be sociological without being political?

The question asks whether a sociologically valid account of vision can be politically neutral, or whether the political is constitutive of sociological vision itself. My answer is that the political is constitutive (not as ideology but as the situatedness of every act of looking) and that "neutral" sociology of vision is a contradiction in terms. I argue this through Haraway, Foucault, Berger and hooks, then defend it against the objection that the position collapses into advocacy.

Haraway's foundational answer. Haraway's situated knowledge gives the strongest formulation. Every act of seeing is "from somewhere," from a body, from a history, from instruments built by named makers in named places. "The god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the pretence by which knowledge claims to escape its situation. Sociology that adopts the god trick is not less political; it is more political, because it imposes its standpoint as universal while denying that it has one. "We might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Answerability is a political concept. A vision that refuses answerability is the most political vision of all: it is power masquerading as neutrality.

This is the central reason "neutral" sociology of vision is impossible. The question is not whether the analyst's vision has a politics but whether the politics is acknowledged. Acknowledged politics is open to revision, critique and dialogue; denied politics is closed. Haraway's later work makes the same point through partial connection: "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12). The connections through which sociology composes its visions are political, and naming them is part of the work.

Foucault's institutional answer. Foucault makes the same point about institutional gazes. The medical, juridical and pedagogical gazes are not neutral observations of patients, criminals and pupils; they produce these subjects as objects of knowledge. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]). The disciplinary gaze "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316). Any sociology that takes the clinical case-record or the criminal mugshot as transparent data inherits the institution's politics. Sociology that wishes to be more than the institution's auxiliary must name the institutional production of its visible objects.

Berger's cultural answer. Berger refused the apparent neutrality of European visual culture. "The relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7). The European nude was not a record but a production for a male spectator-owner. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). His Frans Hals example (Berger, 1972: 11) is the methodological case: an "authoritative" two-volume art-historical study reproduces, by silence, the standpoint of the ruling class whose ancestors the paintings glorify. Sociology of art that treats Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love* as transparent depiction of "beauty" inherits the politics of the spectator-owner. Sociology that wishes to be sociological must reveal the position from which the image was made and the position from which it is now seen.

hooks's racial answer. hooks's oppositional gaze made the racial politics of looking explicit. "Slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115); "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). Sociology of cinema or art that takes the white spectator as the default audience reproduces, by silence, the racial politics of the visual regime. The political is not a contaminant to be controlled; it is the substance of the visual relation under analysis.

Tests across the course's exhibits. First, the Human Zoos. A "neutral" sociology of the nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century European and US expositions that catalogued attendance numbers without naming the colonial gaze would reproduce by silence the politics of the displays. The exhibits classified colonised persons as ethnographic objects; the sociology that describes them must name that classification. Second, the Mercator Projection. A sociology of cartography that took Mercator as "the world" reproduces the imperial standpoint Berger taught us to expose; the Gall-Peters Projection and the New Internationalist "Upside Down" map are situated alternatives. Third, obstetric ultrasound. A sociology of medical imaging that did not name the erasure of the pregnant woman Kember describes ("the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information", Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]) reproduces the medical gaze it claims to study. Fourth, the European nude. A sociology of art that treats Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* as transparent biblical illustration ignores Berger's reading of the Elders as the spectator's surrogate.

The objection. The strongest objection is that my claim collapses sociology into advocacy. If every account of vision is political, why prefer one account to another?

Haraway anticipates this. Situated knowledge is not relativism. It is a demand for "strong objectivity" built from acknowledged partiality, with structural preference for "the standpoints of the subjugated" because they are "least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge." A sociology that names its position is more rigorous than one that hides it. Disclosure enables critique; concealment forecloses it. A propagandist hides her standpoint; a situated sociologist names hers. The two are opposed.

A weaker version of the question asks whether sociology can be politically modest, describing without prescribing. Yes: sociology can be modest about specific policy recommendations while remaining politically situated. Describing the imperial gaze that organised the Human Zoos is sociological; prescribing how a museum should reorganise an ethnographic collection is policy. But the description itself is not politically neutral; it discloses the position of the analyst and the politics of the visual regime under study.

Critical limits. The position can over-politicise: not every visual choice is consequential. Some images are banal. The point is not that every detail carries political weight but that banality is not neutrality; even the family snapshot, as Berger noted, encodes a way of seeing (Berger, 1972: 10).

The answer therefore is no: a vision cannot be sociological without being political, because sociological vision is constitutively positioned, and positioning is political. The question is not whether to have a politics but whether to have one openly. Haraway's answerability, Foucault's institutional disclosure, Berger's demystification and hooks's oppositional naming are the modes by which sociology of vision becomes both political and rigorous. ### Mock 13 - "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault). Discuss.

Foucault's epigram "visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]) named the mechanism of the Panopticon. In this essay I argue that the epigram organises the lecture's account of vision more broadly: it names the panoptic gaze itself but also reads forward into Berger's surveyor/surveyed, Kember's medical gaze, hooks's racial counter-history and Haraway's god trick. I draw on these theorists to show that the trap of visibility is the lecture's most compressed diagnosis.

Foucault's trap precisely. Bentham's Panopticon makes the inmate continuously seeable from the central tower. Because the inmate cannot tell whether the inspector is watching, he behaves as if always watched. Power becomes "visible and unverifiable": the tower is visible, the inspector's actual occupation is not. The genius of the design, Foucault argues, is that the inmate internalises the gaze and becomes self-disciplining. The trap is not in being watched but in not knowing when. The disciplinary gaze "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316) and "has infiltrated the others … making it possible to bring the effects of power to the most minute and distant elements." The Panopticon is therefore a diagram of modern power, recurring across prisons, schools, hospitals and armies. Bentham's Auto-Icon, the preserved body of the utilitarian theorist displayed in the South Corridor of the Main Building at UCL, makes the diagram material: the architect of panoptic visibility becomes its subject.

Berger's surveyor/surveyed as panoptic trap. Berger described how women under patriarchy learn to inhabit their own visibility, watching themselves being looked at, accompanied by an internalised male image (Berger, 1972: 46). "She has been taught and persuaded to survey herself continually." The mechanism is the panoptic principle at the scale of the gendered self: the male surveyor is "visible and unverifiable" inside the woman, present as continuous structure without any particular male actually watching. Berger's tour of the European nude tradition (Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* in its mirror version, where Susannah joins her own spectators; Memling's *Vanity*, where the painted woman is given a mirror that turns her into a sight; Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne for Charles II, where her nakedness is "a sign of her submission to the owner's feelings or demands") catalogues the trap of visibility staged inside paintings. The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements that the lecture exhibits stage the same trap in print: the woman's visibility is sold as the brand's property.

Kember's medical gaze as panoptic trap. Kember's claim that the obstetric ultrasound "eliminates the mother's body from view" so that "the body of the woman exists only as a vehicle for scientific information" (Kember, 1998 [VERIFY YEAR?]) is the panoptic trap operating in the clinic. The pregnant woman is seen (her body imaged, her foetus made publicly visible) and yet not seen (her body rendered ambient infrastructure). Visibility is a trap because the visible is structured against her even as it depends on her. The lecture's slide headers summarise the diagnosis: "Woman as a problem for vision/knowledge"; "Woman as invisible."

hooks's racial supplement to the trap. Foucault under-theorised race. hooks reminds us that under slavery "white slave-owners … punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), and "slaves were denied their right to gaze." The trap of visibility was, for enslaved people, asymmetric in a particular way: they were forced into visibility as objects of disciplinary surveillance while denied the visibility that constitutes a subject. The oppositional gaze, "I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116), names the refusal of this asymmetric trap.

Haraway's god trick as enabling fiction of the trap. Haraway's god trick (Haraway, 1991: 189) names the move by which the panoptic apparatus presents itself as natural observation. The Panopticon disguises itself as architecture; the medical scan disguises itself as transparent access to the foetus; the European nude disguises itself as aesthetic appreciation. Each performs the god trick that lets the trap of visibility operate without acknowledgement. Haraway's positive injunction, "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190), names the counter-practice: to make the trap visible as a trap is to begin to escape it.

Other instances across the lecture. The Human Zoos at nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century World Fairs in France, the UK, Belgium, Spain and the US are the colonial form of the trap. Colonised persons were "imported" for "exhibit" as ethnographic objects; their visibility was the trap by which the imperial gaze constituted them. The Mercator Projection is the cartographic form of the trap: the world is made visible in a particular way that organises the visible against the Global South. Leonardo's 1502 *Map of Imola* and the convex mirror in van Eyck's *Arnolfini Marriage* are early visual instances of the same problematic; the mirror in van Eyck shows that the visible is reflected from somewhere, even when the painting pretends to a flat record. The "World Turned Upside Down" map exposes the political convention of orientation: visibility had been a trap precisely because we did not notice the convention by which north appeared up.

Critical limits. Foucault may overstate compliance. Counter-practices abound: hooks's oppositional gaze, Berger's pedagogy of demystification, Haraway's situated knowledge. Visibility traps, but visibility is also negotiated. The Gall-Peters Projection negotiates Mercator's trap; the "Upside Down" map negotiates the orientation trap; the oppositional gaze negotiates the racial trap. The diagnosis is not destiny.

The epigram nonetheless holds. "Visibility is a trap" reads through the panopticon, the surveyor inside the woman, the obstetric ultrasound, the colonial exposition and the cartographic projection. Foucault's diagnosis is the lecture's most compressed claim: vision distributes power, and the distribution is asymmetric. (758 words [VERIFY count])

### Mock 14 - How does the imperial gaze of the nineteenth-century Human Zoos help us read modernity's visual order?

The lecture pairs the Mercator Projection with Human Zoos to make a single argument: modernity's visual order is constitutively imperial. The Human Zoos in the lecture. The lecture's slide describes a series of World Fairs and Expositions held by major European countries (France, the UK, Belgium, Spain) and the US in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. As part of these events, "some colonial powers 'imported' people from the places that they had colonised, in order to 'exhibit' them." The colonial gaze converted colonised persons into spectacle. Visitors paid to look; the displayed persons were classified, photographed, measured. The expositions were where empire was visible to its metropolitan publics: the imperial economy presented as ethnographic theatre.

Schroeder's structural statement. "To gaze … signifies a … relation of power, in which the gazer is superior to the object of the gaze" (Schroeder, 1998). The Human Zoo is the relation in its most explicit form. The European spectator is constituted as the unmarked observer; the colonised person is constituted as the marked object. The architecture of the exposition (paths, viewing platforms, fencing) makes the asymmetry literal.

Foucault's disciplinary gaze. Foucault's account of disciplinary power illuminates the Human Zoo as a knowledge-producing apparatus. The disciplinary gaze "produces a way of seeing … a way of distributing light" (Foucault, 1977: 316) and "enables us to see some kinds of subject … and to analyse them as objects." The Human Zoo distributed light across colonised populations and analysed them as ethnographic objects. "Visibility is a trap" (Foucault, 1977: 200 [VERIFY YEAR?]) operates here in a particular form: the displayed persons were trapped in a visibility that constituted them as types rather than persons.

hooks's racial counter-history. hooks reminds us that the asymmetric distribution of the right to look has a long history. Under slavery "white slave-owners (men, women, and children) punished enslaved black people for looking" (hooks, 1992: 115); "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). The Human Zoo is the European-public counterpart: the imperial subject was given an unimpeded look at colonised persons whose own right to return the look was structurally denied. The oppositional gaze hooks theorises (hooks, 1992: 116) names the counter-practice that the colonial gaze attempted to foreclose. The Human Zoos and the prohibition of the slave's gaze are two faces of the same scopic regime.

Berger's reading of European visual culture. Berger's analysis of the European tradition shows that the imperial gaze is continuous with the gendered gaze. The painted woman in the European nude is arranged for the unpainted male spectator (Berger, 1972: 47); the displayed colonised person in the Human Zoo is arranged for the unmarked European spectator. Both are "to-be-looked-at"; both are produced as sights rather than recognised as persons. Berger's contrast with "non-European traditions, in Indian art, Persian art, African art, Pre-Columbian art" (Berger, 1972: 53 [VERIFY PAGE?]) deepens the point: the European convention of arranging bodies for an external spectator is itself a particular tradition, not a natural way of looking. The Human Zoo applies the convention to colonised peoples.

Haraway's god trick. The Human Zoo performed the god trick at exposition scale. The European visitor's standpoint was unmarked; the colonised person's standpoint was over-marked. The view from the path appeared as the natural human view; the displayed person was made to bear the visible difference. Haraway's claim that the god trick is "the subjective view of the dominant (unmarked) group passed off as universal" (Haraway, 1991: 189) reads exactly. Her injunction to "become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190) is the methodological demand the Human Zoo refuses.

The cartographic counterpart. The lecture pairs Human Zoos with the Mercator Projection. The same nineteenth-century European empires that drew Mercator's wall maps staged the Human Zoos. The map abstracts the territory; the zoo displays the inhabitants. Both inflate the metropolitan centre and miniaturise the colonised periphery. The Gall-Peters Projection and the New Internationalist "Upside Down" map are post-imperial counter-cartographies; the oppositional gaze hooks names is the post-imperial counter-spectatorship. The two counters are complementary.

Reading modernity through the Human Zoos. The Human Zoos help us read modernity as an order of vision. The lecture's central argument is that "vision is tied up with power relations" and "linked to … modernity, inequality and colonization, slavery." The Human Zoos are where modernity's vision was performed for the metropolitan public. They are the visual evidence that the colonial gaze and the disciplinary gaze of Foucault are not separate phenomena but instances of a single scopic regime. The European spectator who looked at the displayed colonised person at the 1897 exposition was the same subject who, under Berger's reading, looked at Cranach's *Judgement of Paris* in the gallery, and the same subject who, under Foucault's reading, looked through the inspection slit of the prison.

Critical limits. The continuity argument can flatten differences. The Human Zoo exhibited persons as spectacle; the disciplinary institutions Foucault analyses do not exhibit but enrol. The Mercator Projection abstracts territory rather than displaying bodies. The mechanisms differ in important ways. The point is not that all imperial vision is identical but that the imperial gaze persists as a structural pattern across distinct visual regimes.

The imperial gaze has not been dismantled by the end of the Human Zoos; it has been reorganised. The lecture's juxtaposition of Human Zoos with the Mercator Projection and with Foucault's disciplinary gaze tells us that the visual order of modernity is constitutively imperial, that the post-imperial counter-gazes (Gall-Peters, oppositional gaze, situated knowledge) are necessary, and that the sociological reading of any contemporary visual order must ask what kind of imperial gaze it inherits. ### Mock 15 - Counter-currents to the male gaze: hooks, Haraway and the limits of refusal.

The lecture's account of the male gaze is paired with the lecture's account of the oppositional gaze and situated knowledge. In this essay I argue that hooks's oppositional gaze and Haraway's situated knowledge name the principal counter-currents to the male gaze, that the counter-currents presuppose rather than refute the gaze they contest, and that the limits of refusal sharpen rather than weaken the sociological reading. I use the lecture's own theorists and exhibits as the test case.

Mulvey's actual claim. Mulvey's 1975 essay argued that classical Hollywood operated "a determining male gaze" through scopophilia and ego-identification. The camera was structurally heterosexual male; the woman appeared; identification routed through the male protagonist. The argument was about dominant cinema, not all cinema. The male gaze was always a hegemonic convention, not a metaphysical absolute. The serious question is whether the visible counter-currents in the lecture's wider theoretical lineage refute or confirm the diagnosis.

Berger's anchoring. Berger's analysis of the European nude tradition (Berger, 1972: 45-54) is the deep history of the male gaze. From Mabuse's *Adam and Eve* and Pol de Limbourg's *Fall and Expulsion from Paradise* (medieval-early Renaissance) through Tintoretto's *Susannah and the Elders* in its two versions, Memling's *Vanity*, Cranach's and Rubens's *Judgement of Paris*, Lely's portrait of Nell Gwynne for Charles II, and Bronzino's *Allegory of Time and Love*, the convention persists. "Men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47). The Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements that the lecture exhibits show the convention crossing media. Mulvey's cinematic male gaze is the modern descendant of a long European visual tradition.

Counter-current one: hooks's oppositional gaze. hooks's intervention is the sharpest counter to Mulvey. "The 'gaze' has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115); "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). The oppositional gaze emerges from the historical denial of the right to gaze: "slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). The counter-current works in two ways. First, it refuses to be captured by the male gaze: the black female spectator looks back rather than internalising the position of the addressed male. Second, it produces alternative knowledge of self and world: the oppositional gaze "opens up the possibility of agency" inside structures of domination. hooks's counter-current presupposes Mulvey's diagnosis: the oppositional gaze is legible as opposition because the gaze it opposes is real and dominant.

Counter-current two: Haraway's situated knowledge. Haraway's intervention is the methodological counter to the universalising tendency of Mulvey's "determining male gaze." Haraway insists that no vision is from nowhere; "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is the move by which any particular standpoint masquerades as universal. The male gaze, on Haraway's argument, is one situated visual regime; recognising its situatedness is the first step in refusing the trick. Haraway's positive programme, "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190), names the practice the male gaze refuses: the spectator who declares his position. In *Staying with the Trouble* she extends the same logic through "it matters what ideas we use to think other ideas (with)" (Haraway, 2016: 12); the situated counter-currents the lecture flags are themselves partial connections, not universal alternatives.

Counter-current three: Berger's pedagogy of demystification. Berger's own counter-current is a pedagogy of slow looking. By teaching the reader to read Memling's *Vanity* as the painter's enjoyment disguised as moral condemnation, or Tintoretto's mirror version of *Susannah and the Elders* as Susannah's enrolment as a spectator of herself, Berger gives readers tools to refuse the conventions from within. The pedagogical counter-current is less radical than hooks's oppositional gaze but works inside the European tradition rather than against it.

Limits of refusal. The counter-currents have limits. First, they presuppose the gaze they contest. The oppositional gaze is legible because the prohibition is real; situated knowledge is methodologically necessary because the god trick is dominant; demystification is needed because the conventions are pervasive. The counter-currents are necessary because the gaze persists; they do not abolish it. Second, they operate inside particular regimes. hooks's oppositional gaze is most powerful in cinematic spectatorship; Haraway's situated knowledge is most powerful as method; Berger's demystification is most powerful in art-historical pedagogy. None abolishes the wider scopic regime; each interrupts where it can. Third, Foucault's reminder that disciplinary vision is "more than the male gaze" (slide 20) implies that counter-currents to the male gaze do not yet address the disciplinary gaze on schoolchildren, patients and criminals or the medical gaze on the pregnant body Kember analyses. The wider disciplinary order remains.

Synthesis through the lecture's exhibits. The Human Zoos at European and US expositions, the European nude tradition Berger catalogues, the Calvin Klein "Obsession" advertisements, the Mercator Projection and obstetric ultrasound are five visual regimes the lecture exhibits. The male gaze operates centrally in the European nude tradition and the Calvin Klein advertisements; it intersects with the colonial gaze in the Human Zoos; it intersects with the medical gaze in obstetric ultrasound; it intersects with the cartographic god trick in the Mercator Projection. The counter-currents (oppositional gaze, situated knowledge, Berger's demystification, Gall-Peters and the "Upside Down" map) interrupt each regime where they can.

Critical limits. The counter-currents can be co-opted (the oppositional gaze sold as brand identity, situated knowledge reduced to identitarian closure, demystification consumed as scholarly fashion). The limits of refusal are partly conceptual and partly institutional: a counter-current that refuses one regime can be re-enrolled into another.

The male gaze persists as the dominant convention of the European visual tradition Berger catalogued, the cinematic apparatus Mulvey theorised and the print advertising the lecture exhibits. hooks's oppositional gaze, Haraway's situated knowledge and Berger's demystification are the counter-currents that interrupt it. They presuppose the gaze they contest; they operate inside particular regimes; they are necessary and not sufficient.
