# L15. Structuralism and Cultural Signs (Saussure, Barthes, Hall)

*SC301 cheat-sheet. Built from the lecture slides, Strinati Ch. 3 (Spring reading), and the teacher's revision summary.*

---

## §1. One-sentence summary

Structuralism and semiology argue that meaning in language and culture is not natural but produced by structured systems of signs (Saussure's signifier/signified), that popular-cultural signs work simultaneously at the levels of denotation, connotation and myth (Barthes), and that any serious account of cultural meaning has to be reworked, as Stuart Hall does, to bring back power, ideology, institutions and the active audience.

---

## §2. The five core moves

1. **Saussure's relational sign.** The sign = signifier (physical form: sound, mark, image, style) + signified (mental concept, not the thing). The relation is arbitrary; meaning arises from difference within a system (*langue*) rather than from things in the world. "In the linguistic sign, the signifier is the 'sound image'… and the signified is the concept of the object or idea which is being referred to" (Strinati, 1995: 83).
2. **From linguistics to a science of signs.** Saussure proposes semiology as "a science which studies the life of signs within society" (Strinati, 1995: 85). The langue/parole, synchronic/diachronic and paradigmatic/syntagmatic distinctions become tools for analysing fashion, food, advertising, film, news, anything culturally coded.
3. **Barthes's two-tier model.** Modern signs work simultaneously at denotation (literal, "what we see", apparently neutral) and connotation (cultural associations, values, emotions). The suit connotes professionalism/authority; the Jeep connotes adventure/masculinity.
4. **Myth as third-order signification.** Myth is "a system of communication… a mode of signification" (Barthes, 1972: 117). Its function is "to transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140), to make culturally specific, class-bound meanings appear timeless, obvious, natural. The Paris-Match cover of the naked Black soldier saluting the French flag is Barthes's emblematic case.
5. **Hall's reworking.** Structuralism is "extremely powerful at explaining how meaning is organised, but it is much less good at explaining power and inequality in concrete social contexts, media institutions… and audience interpretation and resistance" (teacher's summary). Hall's encoding/decoding model adds production, ideology and differential reception to the semiological frame.

**Key concepts defined tightly (consolidated from the slides):**

- **The sign.** The unit of meaning, made of signifier + signified, "the former and the latter existed before uniting and forming this third object, which is the sign" (Barthes, 1972: 122).
- **Signifier / signified.** Physical form vs. mental concept. Not the thing itself.
- **The arbitrariness of the sign.** There is "no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, 1995: 83).
- **Langue / parole.** System vs. use. "What is social from what is individual… what is essential from what is accessory" (Saussure, in Strinati, 1995: 82).
- **Synchronic / diachronic.** Snapshot of the system vs. history of changes.
- **Paradigmatic / syntagmatic axes.** Substitutable elements (paradigm) vs. combinable sequences (syntagm).
- **Denotation.** First-order, literal, "most obvious level… literal meaning" (lecture slide).
- **Connotation.** Second-order, cultural, "suggested or implied meaning" (lecture slide).
- **Myth.** Third-order, "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders" (lecture slide). "The very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140).
- **Mythologies.** Barthes's 1957 collection of short essays decoding wrestling, wine, soap, the Citroën DS, striptease, toys and the Paris-Match cover.
- **The death of the author.** Barthes's later move (Barthes, 1977): meaning is produced by readers, not fixed by writers' intentions.
- **Encoding / decoding (Hall).** Production encodes a "meaningful discourse" inside frameworks of knowledge, relations of production and technical infrastructure; audiences decode within their own frameworks. Three positions: dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, oppositional (Hall, 1980).
- **Polysemic text.** A text open to multiple readings.
- **Iconic / indexical / symbolic** (Peirce, on slide). Signs that resemble, are caused by, or are conventionally linked to what they signify.
- **The linguistic turn.** The broader twentieth-century move in social theory toward language as the model for understanding culture, meaning and power.

## §5. Critiques and limits (the Hall pivot)

- **Empirical validity.** Strinati: "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114). What evidence makes "roses = passion" rather than "roses = farewell" or "roses = sexual harassment"?
- **Neglect of production.** Semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117); cultural signs are also commodities.
- **Neglect of consumption / audience.** "Meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Hall's encoding/decoding is the corrective.
- **Ahistoricism.** Lévi-Straussian structuralism freezes the system synchronically and "reduces culture to a mental structure" (Strinati, 1995: 111), erasing class and history.
- **Crude theory of ideology.** Barthes reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, 1995: 117), which struggles with race, gender, colonial difference. Hall, feminist semiology and decolonial semiotics push back.
- **The "robbing myth of myth" problem.** Barthes himself: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth? All that is needed is to use it as the departure point for a third semiological chain, to take its signification as the first term of a second myth" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The chain can go on infinitely (irony, parody, the *Joe Chemo* counter-Camel slides, the Absolut Vodka counter-ads, DEATH cigarettes, Liquid Death, Dutch Barn vodka, culture jamming), which both opens space for resistance and threatens the diagnostic clarity of the original method.

## §6. Quick exam scaffolds

**The "do we need a theory of signs to understand capitalism?" scaffold (2023-style):**
1. State the question: is semiology necessary, or are political economy / class structure / commodity form sufficient?
2. Show what semiology adds (advertising, branding, cultural commodification, late-capitalist style cycles).
3. Show its limits (production, labour, class).
4. Argue for a Hall-style synthesis: semiology + Marxism + ideology theory.

**The "is X mythological?" scaffold (2022-style):**
1. Define myth as third-order signification that naturalises history.
2. Pick two or three contemporary examples.
3. Apply denotation -> connotation -> myth.
4. Critique: production, audience, polysemy, polyvalence, ideology.
5. Verdict: how convincing is the diagnosis?

**The "popular culture is ideological" scaffold (2021-style):**
1. Define Barthes's claim: myth is the form of bourgeois ideology because it transforms the historical into the natural.
2. Three concrete cases.
3. Strengths: it explains why power feels obvious.
4. Weaknesses: empirical validation, audience reception, reductive class theory.
5. Hall as a productive successor.

### 8.1 Past-year 2023 (Q7). Do we need a theory of signs and signification to understand capitalism?

I want to argue that we do need a theory of signs to understand capitalism, but only a theory reworked along Hall's lines. A pure semiology cannot grasp capitalism on its own, because capitalism is also a mode of production, a class structure and a circuit of accumulation. But a Marxism that ignores signs cannot explain why Hugo Boss sells professionalism, why Jeep can sell a 4x4 by attaching it to mountains, or why Liquid Death can sell still water by branding it like a heavy-metal band. The sign and the commodity have become inseparable, and any serious account of contemporary capitalism has to combine the two.

Begin with Saussure's account of the sign. Language is "not a transparent medium that reflects reality, but a structured system of signs, organised through difference and governed by rules that exist independently of individual intention" (lecture slide, citing Saussure, 1916). The signifier is the physical form (sound, mark, image, style); the signified is the mental concept. The relation between them is arbitrary, and meaning emerges from difference within a system, *langue*, rather than from things in the world. This is already politically useful, because it lets us see that "Frenchness", "bourgeois respectability" or "the eternal womanhood" of Barthes's photo of seventy novelists (Barthes, 1972: 56) are signs, not natural objects. The political work of capitalism partly consists in making such signs feel obvious.

Barthes's notion of myth makes this point sharper. Denotation is "the most obvious level… literal meaning"; connotation is the "cultural level… suggested or implied meaning"; myth is the "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders" (lecture slide). The function of myth is "to transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). The Hugo Boss suit denotes a garment; connotes professionalism and authority; mythologises bourgeois masculinity as the natural form of seriousness. The Jeep Compass advert denotes a 4x4 against a mountain horizon; connotes adventure and aspiration; mythologises consumption as escape. Without a theory of signs we cannot diagnose any of this; we can describe a brand's profits but not why its image works.

Capitalism in its late or platform form has made semiology more necessary. The "Styles in late capitalism" slide lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, asking what "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" they involve. The "Third order chains" slide makes the same point with DEATH cigarettes, Liquid Death water and the Dutch Barn vodka advert. Each is a brand that already parodies the form of branding. These signs only become legible once we treat them as semiological systems.

But, and this is where I follow Strinati and Hall, semiology on its own is not enough. Strinati notes that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117) and that "meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Barthes himself reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, 1995: 117) in a way that is theoretically thin: it does not specify which fraction of capital, which institutions, which audiences. The Paris-Match cover Barthes reads as "French imperiality" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126) is also produced by a magazine industry, sold to particular readerships, and decoded variously by Black and white French citizens. Race, gender and colonial difference exceed Barthes's binary class frame.

Hall's reworking is what makes semiology adequate to capitalism, and it is no accident that Hall reaches for Gramsci to do it. Gramsci's account of hegemony as "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12) supplies the political theory Barthes lacks. Hall's articulation theory turns semiological differences into hegemonic moves: a Jeep advert or a Hugo Boss image is not just a myth, it is a small contribution to the consent that capitalism needs. His encoding/decoding diagram (lecture slide, "Hall: building on structuralism?") restores the "media production and power" of the encoder and the "differential reception of the audience". A theory of signs is necessary because capitalism increasingly sells signs; a theory of capitalism is necessary because signs are produced by industries inside class relations. The honest synthesis is that we need both: Saussure to identify the structure, Barthes to read the myth, Hall and Gramsci to bring back power. The answer to the question is: yes, but only as one indispensable element in a wider critical sociology.

### 8.2 Past-year 2022 (Q5). Critically assess Barthes' argument that popular culture is "mythological".

Barthes's claim that popular culture is mythological is, in my view, both indispensable and over-stated. It is indispensable because he gives us the sharpest tool we have for seeing how popular-cultural signs naturalise specific historical arrangements. It is over-stated because, taken on its own, the argument under-determines questions of audience reception, institutional production and the polysemy of texts that Hall and Strinati raise.

Start with the argument itself. In *Mythologies*, Barthes describes myth as "a system of communication… a mode of signification" (Barthes, 1972: 117), and insists that "the very principle of myth" is that "it transforms history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). His most famous case is the *Paris-Match* cover, where "a young Negro in a French uniform is saluting, with his eyes uplifted, probably fixed on a fold of the tricolour" (Barthes, 1972: 125). Denotatively, this is a soldier saluting a flag. Connotatively, it is a "purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness". Mythically, it signifies "French imperiality", "France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The historical violence of French colonialism is laundered into a natural, obvious image of multi-racial loyalty. The wrestler's grimace, the wine bottle, the Citroën DS, the toys reviewed in *Mythologies* all work the same way. The lecture's third-order signification diagram captures the schema: denotative (literal), connotative (cultural), mythological (ideological worldview).

Applied to today's popular culture, this is enormously productive. The Hugo Boss suit advert turns class-coded clothing into "professionalism and authority"; the Jeep Compass advert turns a fuel-hungry 4x4 into "Upgrade your adventures". The O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign turns a corporate sponsorship into a national passion, and the "A Rose is a Rose?" slide spells out the chain: denotation = "a flower bush in the garden"; connotation = "the consumption of roses on special romantic occasions"; myth = "romance being commodified; being robbed of its innocent meaning". The Camel campaigns mythologise smoking as freedom, while *Joe Chemo* performs the third-order "robbery" Barthes invites: "since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, 1972: 135). DEATH cigarettes, Liquid Death water and the Dutch Barn vodka advert with Ricky Gervais push the recursion further, branding the parody of branding.

But the argument needs to be qualified on four fronts. First, validation: Strinati notes that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114). When Barthes says roses signify passion, why not farewell or irony? Second, production: semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117). A *Paris-Match* cover is also the output of a media industry inside a colonial state. Third, audience: Barthes treats the myth-consumer as essentially passive, taking "the signification for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model corrects this with dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings. The same Camel advert can mean cool to one viewer and a public-health crime scene to another, as *Joe Chemo* makes literal. Fourth, the theory of ideology is crude: Barthes reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, 1995: 117), which under-specifies race, gender and colonial power.

There is also a deeper issue Barthes himself acknowledges: once any signification can be the first term in a further chain, mythological readings multiply infinitely. Baudrillard pushes this development into the post-structuralist register, arguing that under late capitalism the sign cuts loose from any anterior referent and becomes pure simulacrum, "the generation by models of a real without origin or reality: a hyperreal" (Baudrillard, 1994: 1). Liquid Death and DEATH cigarettes look like Barthesian myths but read more cleanly as Baudrillardian sign-value: the brand simulates the parody of branding. The "Styles in late capitalism" slide lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, asking what "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" do as further significations. TikTok aesthetics and trend cycles iterate this in real time. This is theoretically generative but methodologically destabilising: at what point does the critic stop adding chains?

My assessment, then, is that the claim that popular culture is mythological is essentially correct as a diagnosis of a major mechanism of contemporary cultural power, and Barthes deserves credit for naming it. But it is incomplete. Without Hall on audiences, without political economy on production, without intersectional accounts of ideology, the mythological frame floats free of the social. Read alongside these, however, it remains one of the most powerful tools we have.

### 8.3 Past-year 2021 (Q5). What did Barthes mean when he argued that popular culture is ideological? How convincing do you find his approach?

When Barthes argues that popular culture is ideological, he is making a specific and quite technical claim, not just complaining about advertising. His claim is that the popular signs around us, magazine covers, wrestling matches, wine bottles, soap powders, cars, toys, operate at a third order of signification he calls myth, and that the function of myth is to convert the historically specific interests of a dominant class into apparently natural, timeless, obvious truths. Strinati paraphrases the political programme: semiology is "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature… the fundamental method of an ideological critique" (Barthes, in Strinati, 1995: 100). Or in Barthes's own words: "the very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140).

The mechanics matter. Saussure gives us the sign as signifier + signified, with an arbitrary relation between them and meaning arising from difference within *langue*. Barthes adds two levels. At denotation, a sign is literal: a photo of a Black soldier saluting a flag. At connotation, the sign acquires cultural associations: "Frenchness and militariness" (Barthes, 1972: 125). At the third order, myth, the connotation hardens into an ideological worldview: "French imperiality… France is a great Empire" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The historical truth of French colonial violence becomes the natural truth of a loyal multi-racial empire. Popular culture is ideological in Barthes's sense not because it lies, but because it makes specific historical arrangements feel like the way things are.

Take a few contemporary instances. The O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign denotes a national team in red roses; connotes athletic effort; mythologises a "natural" English sporting nationhood. The "A Rose is a Rose?" slide unpacks the chain: denotation as "a flower bush", connotation as romance commodified on Valentine's day, myth as romance "being robbed of its innocent meaning… the mythical signification is motivated; it is not arbitrary". The Camel "Smooth Character" campaigns mythologise smoking as masculine glamour. Barthes's seventy women novelists, each captioned with the number of her children, mythologise an "eternal statute of womanhood" where "women are on the earth to give children to men" (Barthes, 1972: 56). The *Joe Chemo* parody shows the chain can be robbed back: "since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The Absolut counter-ads run the same culture-jamming play.

How convincing is this? Quite convincing as a description of a real and important mechanism, but limited as a complete theory of popular culture, and limited as a theory of ideology in particular. Four caveats.

First, validation. Strinati's standing objection is that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114). The semiologist's eye is not obviously more reliable than another educated reader's.

Second, production. Cultural signs are produced inside industries. *Paris-Match* has shareholders, editors, image rights and a particular market. Strinati notes that "semiology… tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117). A serious analysis of capitalism's ideological power has to add political economy.

Third, audiences. Barthes pictures the consumer of myth as essentially passive, taking signification "for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model recognises that the same message can be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways. Black readers of the Paris-Match cover were not, on the whole, duped by French imperiality.

Fourth, the theory of ideology is too narrow. Barthes reduces myth to bourgeois class interest; Strinati calls this "fairly crude" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Race, gender, sexuality and colonial difference cut across class in ways Barthes's frame struggles to articulate. Feminist semiology and decolonial readings have stretched the toolkit further than Barthes himself.

So Barthes is right that popular culture is ideological in his precise sense; it naturalises history. He is half-right about how. The British cultural studies frame Williams supplies is the missing supplement here: if culture is "a whole way of life" (Williams, 1958: 6), then Barthes's individual mythological readings need to be embedded in the lived ordinary social context that gives each sign its weight. Plugged into Hall on encoding/decoding, into Williams on culture as a whole way of life, into political economy on production, and into feminist and decolonial readings on the texture of power, his approach remains one of the indispensable tools for analysing the cultural surface of contemporary capitalism. On its own, it is brilliant but partial. With these companions, it is essential.

### 8.4 Mock 1. The teacher's L15 summary contrasts "Culturalist - lived experience, historical context, and agency" with "Structuralist - the deep structures that organise meaning". Critically assess this contrast through Saussure, Barthes and Hall.

The teacher's summary frames the L15 lecture around a sharp pair: culturalism, which prioritises lived experience, historical context and agency, and structuralism, which prioritises the deep structures that organise meaning. The opposition is real, but the most useful sociological position does not pick one side; it works the contrast as a productive tension. Saussure and early Barthes sit at the structuralist pole; Williams and Thompson sit at the culturalist pole; Hall, characteristically, sutures the two. I want to argue that the structuralist pole gives us the analytical sharpness culturalism on its own lacks, and the culturalist pole gives the politics and the social texture that structuralism on its own evacuates.

Begin with the structuralist side. Saussure's claim is that language is "a structured system of signs, organised through difference and governed by rules that exist independently of individual intention" (lecture slide). Signifier and signified are inseparable but the relation between them is arbitrary, and meaning arises from position within a *langue*, not from things in the world. The lecture's traffic-lights image dramatises this nicely: red means "stop" because the system fixes the difference between red, amber and green, not because red is intrinsically stop-coloured. Strinati notes the strict relational logic: "there is no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, 1995: 83). Barthes extends the apparatus to images, fashion, food, advertising, film, news and popular culture, and adds the second and third orders: connotation and myth. The Hugo Boss suit, the Jeep Compass, the O2 "Wear the Rose" rose: all readable as positions within structured systems of difference.

The culturalist side is just as serious. Williams insists that culture is "a whole way of life" (Williams, 1958: 6), that "culture is ordinary" and that meaning is lived rather than abstractly coded. Thompson's emphasis is on agency and historical experience: working-class culture is a record of how people make, contest and remake their world. From this angle, Saussure looks too clean: real human beings do not live inside *langue*; they live inside particular households, workplaces, schools, neighbourhoods and traditions, and signs acquire weight from those settings.

So which is right? Both, on different problems. The structuralist eye is unbeatable for analysing the surface of media culture. When I want to decode the Hugo Boss advert, the Jeep advert, or the Paris-Match cover, "denotation -> connotation -> myth" is the apparatus that gets me precise readings. The teacher's summary makes the point: the model "can be applied beyond language but also in terms of images, fashion, food, advertising, film, news, and popular culture". Without it, criticism collapses into impressionistic taste, the failure mode of the Leavisite tradition.

But the culturalist eye is essential when I want to understand why a sign has weight here and not there. The Femininity-paradigm slide ("Obedient / Servile / Passive / Submissive…") is meaningful only because actual girls grow up inside actual households and schools that enforce it; the Jeep advert works because suburban driving feels suffocating; the "Wear the Rose" campaign mobilises real sporting affect because England's rugby culture is a lived inheritance. Williams reminds us that the sign sits inside what he calls a structure of feeling, the dense lived weather of an ordinary life. Strip the sign from that weather and you produce, as Strinati warns, a semiology that "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" and that loses the "social relationships in which" meaning is located (Strinati, 1995: 117).

Hall is the natural mediator. The teacher's summary names the move precisely: he "does not abandon structuralism. Instead, he reworks it, combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology." His encoding/decoding diagram, reproduced on the lecture slide titled "Hall: building on structuralism?", routes the relational sign back through production institutions and audience reception, that is, back through the culturalist field. The encoder works inside "frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, technical infrastructure"; the audience decodes inside its own analogous frameworks. The result is a model in which "deep structures that organise meaning" actually meet "lived experience, historical context, and agency".

My assessment, then, is that the culturalist/structuralist contrast in the teacher's summary is heuristically useful but methodologically misleading if treated as a forced choice. Saussure and Barthes give the apparatus; Williams and Thompson give the social ground; Hall integrates the two. For SC301 purposes the best position holds the apparatus in one hand and the way of life in the other. Neither survives alone.

### 8.5 Mock 2. The L15 deck insists that signs are not natural and stages this with the slide bullet "'…destroy the idea that signs are natural'" alongside the traffic-lights image. Why is the arbitrariness of the sign politically important for the analysis of popular culture?

The slide bullet "'…destroy the idea that signs are natural'" is the political hinge of the entire L15 lecture, and the traffic-lights image is its first dramatisation. Red signifies "stop" because the highway-code system fixes the difference between red, amber and green; nothing in the colour red is intrinsically stop-shaped. If the arbitrary-but-conventional sign is the rule, then any sign that feels natural is a politically charged achievement, not a fact of nature. That is why the principle matters for popular culture.

Begin with the Saussurean formula. The sign is signifier (physical form) plus signified (mental concept). The relation between them is arbitrary; "there is no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, 1995: 83). Meaning emerges from position within a *langue*, not from things in the world. The teacher's summary repeats the point: "Language is not a collection of names for things. It is a structured system of differences." Barthes's denotation / connotation / myth machinery presupposes the arbitrary sign.

The political payoff comes immediately. If the sign is arbitrary, then anything that presents itself as "just how things are" can be reopened. Three areas matter.

First, *gender*. The lecture's Femininity slide lists "Obedient / Servile / Passive / Submissive / Emotional / Sensitive / Private sphere / Reproductive / Child care / Domestic labour"; the Masculinity slide lists "Manly / Virile / Muscular / Heroic / Wise / Aggressive / Dominant / Sexually active / Successful / Public sphere". If these were natural, they would not vary across cultures and histories, but the lecture's fashion-history silhouette strip (1775 to 1952) shows even body shapes change diachronically. Dick Bruna's *The school*, where the boy wears blue and the girl wears red, looks intuitive but is an arbitrary fixing within a particular sign-system. Spender's *Man Made Language* extends the point below the level of image to language itself, arguing that "there are two fundamental categories, male and minus male" (Spender, 1980: 20). The arbitrariness of the sign licenses the feminist denaturalisation of gender.

Second, *commerce*. Brand connotation only works if audiences forget the arbitrariness. The Hugo Boss suit "connotes professionalism, success, authority" (teacher's summary); the Jeep Compass advert connotes "freedom, masculinity, status, aspiration" (teacher's summary). Strip the arbitrariness away and the suit is fabric; the Jeep is a fuel-hungry 4x4. The lecture's "A Rose is a Rose?" slide spells this out: roses denote "a flower bush in the garden" but the system has fixed them as romance, with the alternative connotations of "pain, rugby, England, Labour Party" available. Critical consumers re-open arbitrariness; brand managers depend on keeping it closed.

Third, *empire and race*. Barthes's Paris-Match cover does exactly the political work the lecture warns against: it takes the arbitrary cultural signifier of a saluting Black French soldier and transforms it into the "natural" sign of "French imperiality… that France is a great Empire" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The myth depends on the audience taking the signifier-signified pairing as natural. Ngũgĩ names the analogous operation in language: "the bullet was the means of the physical subjugation. Language was the means of the spiritual subjugation" (Ngũgĩ, 1986: 9). Decolonial semiotics, listed on the lecture's "Different approaches to semiotics" slide alongside Marxist, Feminist and Post-modernist approaches, is built on the principle that no imperial sign is innocent.

There is a methodological consequence. The lecture's third-order chain slides (DEATH cigarettes, Liquid Death, Dutch Barn vodka with Ricky Gervais, the Camel and *Joe Chemo* pair, the Absolut counter-ads "Absolut Liver Failure", "Absolut Impotence", "Absolut Drunk") show that re-arbitrarising a sign is one of the central moves of culture jamming. Barthes himself invites it: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The arbitrariness of the sign opens the door for resistance, not just for ideology.

The honest qualification: arbitrariness is a starting heuristic, not the final word. Iconic and indexical signs (the semiotics infographic slide) resist Saussure's strong claim; once a sign is mythologised, Barthes concedes that "mythical signification… is never arbitrary; it is always in part motivated" (Barthes, 1972: 136). The political agent of the motivation, capital, patriarchy, the colonial state, needs naming. Hall does this, and the analyst must follow him. But the foundational claim still does the political work the lecture says it does: it lets us treat any sign of popular culture as a denaturalisable object, and that is the precondition of any critical analysis at all.

### 8.6 Mock 3. The teacher's summary writes that the Saussurean "model can be applied beyond language but also in terms of images, fashion, food, advertising, film, news, and popular culture". How far can semiology travel across these registers, and where does it run into trouble?

The teacher's summary captures the bold promise of semiology in one sentence: the Saussurean model "can be applied beyond language but also in terms of images, fashion, food, advertising, film, news, and popular culture". My answer is that the model travels remarkably well across all of these, because each is a system of structured differences, but that the travel runs into specific kinds of trouble at the edges of production, audience and historicity. Semiology is a generous method whose limits are systematic.

Begin with the warrant. Saussure proposed semiology as "a science which studies the life of signs within society" (Strinati, 1995: 85), in which linguistics is only one branch. Barthes carries out the programme: in *Mythologies* he reads wrestling, wine, soap, the Citroën DS, the Paris-Match cover, toys and horoscopes. The L15 deck rehearses the full sweep. The Hugo Boss suit and Jeep Compass advert illustrate *advertising*; the O2 "Wear the Rose" campaign and the HSBC "creating wealth" advert add brand and finance; the fashion-history silhouette strip illustrates *fashion*; the *Joe Chemo* parody and the Absolut counter-ads illustrate culture jamming; the "Decolonize" graffiti illustrates *street culture*. Each is read using denotation, connotation and myth.

Three things make the travel work.

First, the model is *abstract enough to scale*. Signifier/signified, paradigm/syntagm and the three orders of signification are operators, not contents. They apply to any system of differences. A *Vogue* fashion spread, a McDonald's golden-arches logo, a *Bake Off* tent, a Taylor Swift album cover, an England football kit: all admit the same reading.

Second, the model *names a real social fact*. Popular culture really is saturated with signs that operate at more than one level. Brand managers know this; advertising creatives know this; the Camel campaign's mascot, settings and slogans are designed precisely as connotation-stacking devices. Saying that a Jeep advert connotes "freedom, masculinity, status, aspiration" (teacher's summary) is not an act of interpretive overreach; it is naming what the advert is for.

Third, the *examples cluster*. Brands lean on the same connotative paradigms across registers: masculinity-as-rugged in the Camel campaigns and the Jeep advert and the Marlboro Man; femininity-as-care in Bruna's *The school*, the women-with-children novelists photo, and the "clean girl" aesthetic on the styles-in-late-capitalism slide. Semiology lets us see the connections across registers.

But the travel hits trouble in five places.

First, *empirical validation*. Strinati's running objection is that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114). When the analyst tells me that roses signify passion, why not "pain, rugby, England, Labour Party" as the lecture's "A Rose is a Rose?" slide also allows? The semiologist's eye is unbeatable on the example but unreliable on the rule. Cross-register travel multiplies this problem: the same image moves through different audiences in different ways.

Second, *production*. Semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Advertising is made by agencies, film by studios, news by newsrooms, fashion by fashion houses. The Hugo Boss myth is not a free-floating cultural surface but a marketing output by a specific firm with a specific market segment. Travel without political economy gives only half the picture.

Third, *audiences*. The travel assumes the model decodes texts that audiences then absorb. But audiences are not blank screens. Hall's encoding/decoding model insists on dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings, and the lecture slide titled "Hall: building on structuralism?" marks the corrective. Cross-register travel runs out of the analyst's office quickly; my reading of *Bake Off* is not the audience's.

Fourth, *iconicity and indexicality*. Peirce's distinction between symbolic, iconic and indexical signs, reproduced on the semiotics infographic slide, presses on Saussure's strong claim that the sign is arbitrary. A photograph is iconic; smoke is indexical of fire. Travel into film and news, both photograph-saturated registers, has to soften the arbitrariness thesis.

Fifth, *historicity*. The fashion-history slide is the lecture's own corrective. What looks "natural" in one synchronic frame, the eighteenth-century silhouette, the 1950s housewife, the 2020s "clean girl", is a diachronic artefact. Lévi-Strauss's strong synchronic reading "freezes" culture (Strinati, 1995: 111-112) and loses this; Barthes is better but still under-historicised at the level of audience.

My answer: the model travels well across the registers the teacher names. It is the most generous transferable apparatus the sociology of culture has. But the travel works best when the analyst does not pretend the model has no edges. Add Hall on production and reception, add Williams on lived experience, add iconicity, and the travel is sound.

The teacher's two examples, the suit and the car, are the cleanest possible entry into Barthes's three-tier semiological apparatus. I will run the apparatus across each, then add two more brand cases from the L15 slides, then turn to the critique that makes the reading politically usable.

The myth makes the SUV feel inevitable.

Two more cases sharpen the pattern. The lecture's HSBC "Why should creating wealth overwhelm you" advert shows a woman in red against a financial skyline. Denotatively, a woman, a skyline, a slogan. Connotatively, wealth as inspiration, finance as glamour, the city as horizon. Mythically, global finance is mythologised as personal empowerment. The "A Rose is a Rose?" slide makes the worked version explicit: "myth = romance being commodified; being robbed of its innocent meaning… the mythical signification is motivated; it is not arbitrary".

The pattern is the same in each case: a quotidian object (suit, car, advert) is taken up as the signifier of a connotative set, and that set hardens into a worldview that feels natural. Saussure's arbitrary sign is the precondition (the suit could just be cloth); Barthes's three orders are the mechanism (denotation -> connotation -> myth); the brand is the agent. The teacher's summary's two short examples model the analytic move with exemplary economy.

But the analysis cannot stop at the myth. Strinati, the spring reading, presses three caveats that turn the analysis into something usable rather than something clever.

First, *production*. Semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117). The Hugo Boss suit and the Jeep Compass exist inside corporate marketing, supply chains, advertising budgets and segmented markets. The myth is not free-floating; it is bought. The Frankfurt diagnosis of the culture industry, "amusement under late capitalism is the prolongation of work" (Adorno and Horkheimer, 2002: 109), reminds us that the suit and the car are also commodities whose mythologies generate sales.

Second, *audience*. Hall's encoding/decoding model insists that audiences decode in dominant, negotiated and oppositional ways. The "Hall: building on structuralism?" slide makes this explicit: "Differential reception of the audience". I can see the Jeep advert as inspirational (dominant), as cliched (negotiated), or as evidence of consumer manipulation (oppositional). My students will not all read it the same way.

Third, *resistance*. Barthes invites the third-order robbery: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The lecture's *Joe Chemo* parody on the Camel myth, the Absolut counter-ads ("Absolut Liver Failure", "Absolut Impotence"), and the Dutch Barn vodka advert sold by Ricky Gervais all demonstrate the move. Each takes a brand myth as the first term of a new chain and turns the chain against the brand.

The synthesis. Brand advertising mythologises everyday objects by performing Barthes's three-tier operation: it strips the object of its arbitrariness, dresses it in connotation, and lets the connotation harden into the natural. Once we see the operation, the suit becomes cloth, the car becomes a machine, and the analyst (or culture jammer) can rob the myth back. Critical sociology is exactly this re-arbitrarising work.

### 8.8 Mock 5. The teacher's summary defines myth as "a way of making historically specific, culturally constructed meanings appear natural, timeless, and obvious". Discuss with reference to at least two of Barthes's worked examples on the slides and one contemporary case.

The teacher's compact definition is the heart of Barthes's project: myth is the operation that makes "historically specific, culturally constructed meanings appear natural, timeless, and obvious". The wording is almost a paraphrase of Barthes's own line that "the very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). To discuss the claim I will run two of Barthes's worked examples from the L15 slides, the Paris-Match cover and the seventy women novelists, then a contemporary case, the Hugo Boss / Jeep brand template (which fuses two slides), and then assess where the definition holds and where it strains.

Take the Paris-Match cover first. Barthes describes the scene precisely: "I am at the barber's, and a copy of Paris-Match is offered to me. On the cover, a young Negro in a French uniform is saluting, with his eyes uplifted, probably fixed on a fold of the tricolour" (Barthes, 1972: 125). *Denotatively*, a soldier saluting a flag. *Connotatively*, "a purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness" (Barthes, 1972: 125). *Mythically*, "French imperiality… France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag, and that there is no better answer to the detractors of an alleged colonialism than the zeal shown by this Negro in serving his so-called oppressors" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The teacher's three adjectives, natural, timeless, obvious, name exactly what the cover achieves. The specific history of French colonial recruitment, racial hierarchy and imperial violence is laundered into the obviousness of multi-racial military loyalty. Strinati glosses the operation: the soldier's "real history" is "placed in another system, that of the myth, which denies his history and culture" (Strinati, 1995: 105).

Now the seventy women novelists. The *Elle* feature reproduced in *Mythologies* shows women writers each captioned with their number of children. *Denotatively*, a magazine spread of working writers. *Connotatively*, women novelists who are *also* mothers, motherhood foregrounded. *Mythically*, what Barthes calls "the eternal statute of womanhood": "women are on the earth to give children to men… your freedom is a luxury, it is possible only if you first acknowledge the obligations of your nature" (Barthes, 1972: 56-58). The teacher's adjectives again hit: the spread makes a historically specific division of labour appear natural (women's nature is to mother), timeless (this is "eternal"), and obvious (no argument is offered, just numbers). Strinati paraphrases: "the role of women as mothers is made to appear natural and inevitable, the related connotation being that the power and dominance of men is equally natural and inevitable" (Strinati, 1995: 110).

Now the contemporary case. The Hugo Boss and Jeep slides run the same operation in a different register. The suit connotes "professionalism, success, authority" (teacher's summary) and mythologises bourgeois professional masculinity as the natural form of seriousness. The Jeep Compass advert connotes "freedom, masculinity, status, aspiration" (teacher's summary) and mythologises consumer mobility as freedom. McDonald's golden arches connote convenience and mythologise corporate fast food as natural urban refreshment. The teacher's three adjectives apply in each case: history converted into nature, the specific into the timeless, the contingent into the obvious.

So the definition does hold across cases that range from a 1950s magazine cover to a 1970s feminist target to a 21st-century branding template. That is exactly what we should want from a useful theoretical sentence: it pays off across registers.

But the definition strains in three places, and an honest essay says so.

First, *empirical validation*. Strinati's standing objection: "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114). The myth-reading is persuasive, not falsifiable. Why "French imperiality" rather than, say, "the obedient soldier as ambivalent figure of decolonisation"?

Second, *audience differentiation*. Barthes's myth-consumer "takes the signification for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142). Hall's encoding/decoding slide ("Hall: building on structuralism?") replaces this passive reader with dominant, negotiated and oppositional positions. Many Black readers of the Paris-Match cover did not in fact accept French imperiality.

Third, *the agent*. Barthes locates myth in "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, 1995: 117). The seventy-novelists case is patriarchal as much as bourgeois; the Paris-Match cover is colonial as much as bourgeois. The mythological agent has to be named more precisely than "the bourgeoisie".

The teacher's definition is therefore essentially right, and the apparatus delivers usable readings, but the analyst still has to specify which history, which actors, which audiences, in each case. With those specifications, the definition is one of the most productive sentences a sociology of culture has.

### 8.9 Mock 6. The L15 semiotics slide lists "Different approaches to semiotics: Marxist / Feminist / Decolonial / Post-modernist". Which of these four approaches is the most productive extension of Barthes for studying popular culture today?

The question of which is most productive today is a real one: each picks up a different limit of Barthes and extends the apparatus in a different direction. My answer is qualified: the Marxist extension, in its Hall-Gramscian form, is the most productive for the SC301 register, because it provides the political-economic and institutional ground the other three rely on. But each is indispensable for specific tasks, and the best practice combines them.

Begin with the Marxist extension. Barthes's project was already proto-Marxist, framed as "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature" (in Strinati, 1995: 100). The trouble is that this version of class is thin; Strinati calls it "fairly crude" (Strinati, 1995: 117). The decisive Marxist extension is Hall's, which routes semiology through Gramsci. Gramsci's hegemony, "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12), specifies which fraction of capital, which institutions and which alliances do the mythologising. Hall's encoding/decoding slide (lecture slide "Hall: building on structuralism?") restores production and reception. With Hall in hand, the Hugo Boss myth becomes a small hegemonic move by a particular fashion-corporate alliance; the Jeep Compass myth becomes a contribution to the consent automotive capital still needs in an age of climate crisis. This is semiology become political sociology.

The feminist extension is just as essential, and the L15 deck reaches for it directly. The Femininity-paradigm slide ("Obedient / Servile / Passive / Submissive…") and the Masculinity-paradigm slide ("Manly / Virile / Muscular / Heroic…") apply semiology to gender; Dick Bruna's *The school* is the worked case. Barthes's seventy women novelists, mythologising the "eternal statute of womanhood" where "women are on the earth to give children to men" (Barthes, 1972: 56), is feminist semiology *avant la lettre*. Spender's *Man Made Language* extends the analysis to language itself: "there are two fundamental categories, male and minus male" (Spender, 1980: 20). The feminist extension is unbeatable for gendered iconography in advertising, royal weddings, Instagram and the "clean girl" aesthetic as third-order myth.

The decolonial extension addresses the limit Barthes himself failed to cross. His Paris-Match reading names "French imperiality" but does so inside a class-bound theory of ideology. Decolonial semiotics, building on Fanon, Spivak and Ngũgĩ, redescribes the cultural surface of empire. Ngũgĩ's formula, "the bullet was the means of the physical subjugation. Language was the means of the spiritual subjugation" (Ngũgĩ, 1986: 9), is the linguistic turn read from the colonial periphery. The "Decolonize" graffiti on the L15 semiotics slide is the contemporary trace of this approach: a third-order signifier that robs the imperial myth of itself. For analysing public-monument debates, the cultural politics of museums, the global Black Lives Matter movement and the iconographies of contemporary anti-racist culture, the decolonial extension is the right tool.

The post-modernist extension takes the chain in the opposite direction. It accepts Barthes's invitation to keep robbing myth: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The lecture's third-order chains slide (DEATH cigarettes, Liquid Death, Dutch Barn vodka with Ricky Gervais) and its "Styles in late capitalism" slide (roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom, gamers, with the caption "knowingness, excess, parody, irony") are post-modernist semiology. Baudrillard's claim that the sign now circulates as "models of a real without origin or reality: a hyperreal" (Baudrillard, 1994: 1) is the limit case. The post-modernist extension is excellent at reading deepfakes, AI chatbots, streaming-algorithm aesthetics, content-moderation memes, brand authenticity ironies (OK Cola's deadpan slogans). It struggles, however, with political traction; if everything is simulacrum, where is the lever?

The verdict. All four extensions are productive, and the strongest reader uses all four. But the Marxist-Hallian extension is the most productive *as a base*: it specifies the institutions, the political economy and the reception structure that the other three need to land. Feminist extension layers gender onto that base; decolonial extension layers race and empire; post-modernist extension layers irony and recursion. On a SC301 essay, my preferred move is therefore Hall as base, then whichever of the other three the question demands.

### 8.10 Mock 7. The teacher's summary writes that Hall "does not abandon structuralism. Instead, he reworks it, combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology." Discuss what this combination gains over Barthes's mythology programme.

The teacher's summary is precise about Hall's move: he "does not abandon structuralism. Instead, he reworks it, combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology." The wording is important. Hall is not throwing Saussure and Barthes away; he is wiring their apparatus into a wider sociology so that it can do political work. The combination gains four things over Barthes's mythology programme, and an honest essay also names the residual limits.

Start with what Hall keeps. The encoding/decoding diagram reproduced on the L15 slide ("Hall: building on structuralism?") still has the relational sign at its centre: a "Visual / Aural - signifier" passes through "Signified" inside the production process. Hall takes the Saussurean machinery, signifier/signified, paradigm/syntagm, the three orders, as the *grammar* of cultural communication. He just stops pretending that grammar is a self-enclosed system.

Now the four gains.

First, *Marxism*. Barthes's class theory is thin: he gestures at "the bourgeoisie" (Strinati, 1995: 117) but never specifies which fractions or institutions do the mythologising. Hall's Marxism is Gramscian, importing hegemony as "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12). Read this way, a Hugo Boss advert is a small contribution to the consent that corporate capitalism needs; the Jeep Compass advert is a sign-trace of automotive capital's continuing hegemonic claim on personal autonomy. Hall later applied this directly to Thatcherism and to British racial politics.

Second, *ideology theory*. Barthes's myth is described as a "second-order semiological system" (Barthes, 1972: 117), but Barthes does not theorise ideology as such. Hall imports Althusserian and post-Althusserian ideology theory: ideology works through specific institutions (schools, families, media), through specific subject-positions, and through specific interpellations. The Femininity-paradigm slide on the L15 deck becomes legible as an ideological apparatus that interpellates female subjects, not just as a myth.

Third, *sociology*. Barthes is, characteristically, a literary critic with sociological ambitions; Hall is a sociologist with literary-critical resources. The encoding/decoding slide marks the move: encoding happens inside "frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, technical infrastructure"; decoding happens inside the audience's own analogous frameworks. The result is a sociology of meaning. Strinati's standing complaints, that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" and that "meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (Strinati, 1995: 117), are answered. Williams's broader culturalist insistence that culture is "a whole way of life" (Williams, 1958: 6) is incorporated.

Fourth, *audience activity*. Barthes's myth-consumer is passive: he "takes the signification for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142). Hall's three audience positions, dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, oppositional, restore agency to the reader. The same Camel "Smooth Character" advert can be read as cool (dominant), as kitsch (negotiated), or as a public-health crime scene (oppositional, which the *Joe Chemo* parody on the L15 slide makes literal). The Absolut "Absolut Liver Failure" counter-ad reads the Absolut myth oppositionally. Audiences are not just consumed by mythologies; they negotiate and resist.

The combined gain is large. Where Barthes gives a sharp diagnosis of a mechanism, Hall gives a usable sociology of how that mechanism actually works in production, in institutions and in reception. The L15 "Rethinking Modernity" map shows structuralism feeding into British Cultural Studies through exactly this combination.

But the combination is not without its own limits.

First, the encoder-decoder model leans toward broadcast-era media. The "Styles in late capitalism" slide names a contemporary environment, roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, gamers, in which encoders and decoders overlap and platform algorithms iterate signification in real time. TikTok and Instagram complicate the clean encode/decode binary.

Second, the model retains a structuralist residue. Post-structuralists (Foucault, the later Barthes himself in his "death of the author" move, Barthes, 1977) press on whether there is a stable meaning at all to decode. Hall's three-position model risks tidying that plurality.

Third, the Marxist base can underspecify race, gender and sexuality if it leans too hard on class. Hall himself supplies the corrective in his later race work, but the apparatus has to be pushed by intersectional theorists to land on all the registers of difference.

My verdict, then, is that the combination of semiology with Marxism, ideology theory and sociology is what makes structuralism socially serious, and that the teacher's summary sentence captures the right diagnosis. Hall's reworking is the move that turns Barthes from a brilliant diagnostician into the founding figure of a usable critical sociology of culture.

### 8.11 Mock 8. Explain what Saussure means by the arbitrariness of the sign, and assess its usefulness for analysing popular culture.

When Saussure argues that the sign is arbitrary, he is making the foundational move that allows cultural studies to exist at all. The claim is that the relation between signifier (the physical form: sound, mark, image) and signified (the mental concept) is not natural or motivated. "There is no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, 1995: 83). The English "dog" and the French "chien" point to the same concept because each language's system of differences fixes the link; nothing in the animal demands either sound. Meaning, in this picture, is not a property of things in the world. It is a property of the *langue*: the structured system of differences that allows us to say "dog" rather than "god".

The lecture slide on Saussure puts the point sharply: language is "a structured system of signs, organised through difference and governed by rules that exist independently of individual intention". This already implies a programme. If language works this way, perhaps fashion, food, advertising, film, news and popular culture work the same way. Saussure suggests as much: linguistics is a "sub-branch" of a general "science which studies the life of signs within society" (Strinati, 1995: 85). The whole project of semiology is licensed by this little phrase.

So what does arbitrariness do for the analysis of popular culture? Three things. First, it lets us *denaturalise* taken-for-granted signs. Red on traffic lights means "stop" not because red is intrinsically stop-coloured but because the highway-code system fixes the difference between red, amber and green. By extension, the Hugo Boss suit means "professional", the O2 rose means "passion" (also "pain, rugby, England, Labour Party"), Dick Bruna's blue-clothed boy means "boy" only by culturally specific fixings. Second, it makes *paradigmatic and syntagmatic analysis* possible: we can ask what other signifier could occupy the same slot and what the sequence does. The Femininity slide ("Obedient / Servile / Passive / Submissive…") and Masculinity slide ("Manly / Virile / Muscular / Heroic…") are paradigm lists; swapping any item changes the reading. The Jeep Compass advert's syntagm (sunset + mountains + 4x4 + slogan) builds the adventure myth out of sequenced elements. Third, it underpins Barthes. Without the arbitrariness of the sign, denotation, connotation and myth do not get off the ground; the point of "myth transforms history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140) is that signs that feel natural are arbitrary cultural inheritances.

The usefulness in concrete cases is striking. Dick Bruna's *The school* denotes two children and connotes gendered colour-coding, but the "blue/boy" link is an arbitrary fixing. Spender's *Man Made Language* extends this into a patriarchal account: language is "literally man made" and "still primarily under male control", so the apparently arbitrary signifiers of femininity are in fact motivated by male power (Spender, 1980: 12). The Femininity slide's paradigm reads as a Spender-style inventory of patriarchally fixed signifiers. The Camel campaigns connoted masculine glamour; arbitrariness allowed *Joe Chemo* to short-circuit the chain by reattaching the same cartoon camel to a chemo drip. The Absolut counter-ads work only because the bottle silhouette is an arbitrary signifier that can be re-signified. The lifestyle paradigm slide lists arbitrary signifiers that nonetheless feel obvious to those inside them.

But there are limits. Strinati and others have argued that even Saussure exaggerates arbitrariness. Some signs are iconic (resemble what they signify, like a photograph) or indexical (caused by what they signify, like smoke for fire). The semiotics infographic in the lecture slides distinguishes symbolic, iconic and indexical signs. Iconic signs are not arbitrary in the same way. Moreover, in culture proper Saussure's claim is already softened. Barthes notes that "mythical signification… is never arbitrary; it is always in part motivated" (Barthes, 1972: 136). Once a sign carries connotation and myth, it is no longer free-floating: the historical interests of class, gender and empire motivate which signifiers carry which mythologies.

The pragmatic upshot is that arbitrariness is a starting heuristic, not the final word. It lets us treat any cultural sign as potentially denaturalisable, which is enormous gain. But the analyst then has to ask the further question Hall presses: who motivated this specific signifier-signified pairing, in which institutional context, and how do real audiences decode it? Saussure opens the door; Barthes walks through it; Hall keeps it from swinging shut again. As a single foundational concept, however, the arbitrariness of the sign remains the most useful entry point that the sociology of culture has.

### 8.13 Mock 10. Compare Lévi-Strauss's structuralism with Barthes's semiology. Which is more useful for studying popular culture today?

Lévi-Strauss's structuralism and Barthes's semiology share an inheritance from Saussure and a commitment to reading culture as a system of signs rather than a window onto things. But they part company on universality, on history, and on politics, and the second of those differences is the one that matters most for studying popular culture today. My short answer: Barthes is more useful, but only when reworked.

Lévi-Strauss takes the Saussurean framework and turns it into a programme for finding universal mental structures behind diverse cultural surfaces. As Strinati puts it, Lévi-Strauss's "version of structuralism is concerned with uncovering the common structural principles underlying specific and historically variable cultures and myths. These structural principles involve the logical and universal characteristics of the human mind" (Strinati, 1995: 87). His method works through binary oppositions, nature/culture, raw/cooked, sacred/profane, and seeks the invariant transformations across myths. Totemism, Oedipus, the Iroquois owl-riddle: behind their empirical variety, "the same correlation between riddles and incest exists among peoples separated by history, geography, language and culture" (Lévi-Strauss, in Strinati, 1995: 92). The point is to display "the universality of human nature" (in Strinati, 1995: 92).

Barthes inherits the relational sign but turns it toward the *historical surface*. Semiology, on his account, is the "close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature" (in Strinati, 1995: 100). Where Lévi-Strauss reads myth to find the eternal mind, Barthes reads myth to expose how a specific class and a specific history get smuggled in under cover of "nature". The function of myth, he insists, is to "transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). The lecture's third-order signification diagram captures the schema: denotation, connotation, myth.

Three contrasts are decisive for our period.

First, *history*. Lévi-Strauss is, on Strinati's reading, fundamentally ahistorical: his "almost exclusive concern appears to be with synchronic analysis, uncovering the hidden and unconscious mental structure" (Strinati, 1995: 112). Barthes is unrelentingly historical: every myth he reads is dated, situated, classed. The British cultural studies frame radicalises this: if "culture is ordinary" and culture is "a whole way of life" (Williams, 1958: 6), then any structuralism that abstracts signs from lived historical experience misses what is most important about them. For contemporary popular culture, which churns through trend cycles, brand reinventions, viral moments, the Barthes-Williams historical sensitivity is far more usable than Lévi-Strauss's eternal-mind move.

Second, *politics*. Lévi-Strauss is not in any direct sense a critic of power. Barthes is: his semiology is meant as an "ideological critique" (in Strinati, 1995: 100), even if his theory of ideology remains thin. For a discipline that wants to understand brand capitalism, gendered representation, racialised iconography and political imagery, Barthes's bias is the right bias.

Third, *examples*. Lévi-Strauss works on pre-industrial myths and kinship systems. Barthes works on wrestling, wine, soap, the Citroën DS, magazine covers, toys, exactly the cultural surface that "popular culture today" still resembles. The lecture slides walk from Saussure's tree to Barthes's wrestler to a Hugo Boss advert to a Jeep advert to an O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign. The line of descent is from Barthes, not from Lévi-Strauss.

That said, Lévi-Strauss is not useless. His insistence that texts must be read in structural unity, not as isolated empirical items, survives in contemporary genre analysis. Eco's structuralist reading of the Bond novels (Strinati, 1995: 93-98) shows how an invariant scheme of moves accounts for popular success across the franchise. The Harry Potter fandom and the other contemporary lifestyle paradigms named on the "Styles in late capitalism" slide are saturated with comparable invariant narrative scaffolding. A Lévi-Straussian eye is good at seeing why these feel familiar.

But the deepest objections, empirical validation, audience, production, apply more harshly to Lévi-Strauss than to Barthes, because he claims more. Strinati: structuralism's "concept of an underlying and unconscious structure for its explanatory power is clearly liable to underestimate the significance of the role of the audience" (Strinati, 1995: 96). Hall's encoding/decoding model addresses this on the Barthes side; there is no obvious Lévi-Straussian equivalent.

My verdict, then, is that Barthes is the more useful starting point for studying popular culture today, because his historicity, his political edge and his examples are closer to our object. But Lévi-Strauss's eye for invariant narrative structure remains a useful supplement, and Hall's encoding/decoding model is the necessary third leg. All three, together, beat any one alone.

### 8.14 Mock 11. How convincing is Barthes's claim that the function of myth is "to transform history into nature"?

I find Barthes's claim that myth functions to transform history into nature highly convincing as a diagnosis of a specific, real and recurring mechanism in cultural life, but only partly convincing as a complete theory of how popular culture works. The diagnosis names something genuine; the theory under-specifies how the trick gets pulled off, who pulls it, and on whom.

Begin with the claim. In *Mythologies*, Barthes writes that "the very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). Myth is "a system of communication… a mode of signification… a type of speech" (Barthes, 1972: 117). It is a "second-order semiological system" that takes an existing sign (a photo of a Black soldier saluting a flag, say) and recruits it as the first term of a new chain whose ultimate effect is to make a culturally specific arrangement appear "natural and inevitable" (Strinati, 1995: 106). The Paris-Match cover that Barthes reads in the barber's shop is the textbook case: "All this is the meaning of the picture. But… I see very well what it signifies to me: that France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The historical specificity of French colonial domination disappears into the timelessness of "imperiality".

The claim is convincing first because it is empirically corroborated across almost any popular-cultural surface we choose. *Mythologies* set the template: the wrestler naturalises moral spectacle, the wine bottle naturalises Frenchness, the Citroën DS naturalises industrial modernity as magic, the Paris-Match cover naturalises empire. The lecture's contemporary slide pairs run the same pattern: the Hugo Boss advert naturalises bourgeois professional masculinity; the Jeep Compass advert naturalises the 4x4 as freedom; the O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign naturalises sport as English nationhood; the Camel campaigns naturalise smoking as glamour; the seventy-women-novelists photo naturalises "an eternal statute of womanhood" (Barthes, 1972: 56). In each case, what was historically constructed is dressed in the clothes of nature.

The claim is convincing second because it is methodologically usable. The lecture's third-order signification diagram makes the move teachable: denotation (most obvious, literal), connotation (cultural, suggested), myth (ideological worldview). Students can be given a Hugo Boss advert, a Jeep advert, the O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign, and run the three-order analysis. Barthes himself worked the same way across wrestling, wine, soap and toys.

But the claim is only partly convincing because it under-determines three things.

First, the *agent of mythification*. Barthes tends to attribute mythologies to "the bourgeoisie", a class which, he writes, "has obliterated its name in passing from reality to representation" (Barthes, 1972: 150). Strinati complains that the resulting theory of ideology is "fairly crude" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Gramsci's account of hegemony as the "'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12) gives the missing granularity: naturalisation is not the doing of a class as such but of a hegemonic bloc operating through specific institutions, alliances and "common sense". Whose specific interests, in which institutions, are doing the naturalising? Sometimes it is corporations; sometimes states; sometimes audiences themselves. The mythologising agent needs more granularity than "the bourgeoisie".

Second, the *mechanism on audiences*. Barthes pictures the consumer as passive, taking "the signification for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model (lecture slide) suggests this is too quick. Audiences decode within their own frameworks of knowledge, and produce dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings. Many viewers of the Paris-Match cover did not in fact accept the imperial myth.

Third, the *infinity problem*. Barthes himself acknowledges that "since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, 1972: 135). Third-order chains can be turned into fourth-order chains. The "Styles in late capitalism" slide asks what "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" do as further significations. If myth can always be robbed, the diagnostic clarity of "history -> nature" is destabilised: post-modern culture often *performs* its own historicity ironically while still feeling natural to its participants.

The honest assessment is therefore: yes, Barthes is right that one major function of popular-cultural signs is to transform history into nature, and his slogan names it cleanly. But the full explanation of how this happens, with what effects, and against what resistances requires Hall on encoding/decoding, political-economic accounts of production, and feminist and decolonial readings of which histories get naturalised. The slogan is a stage one diagnosis; the analysis takes more.

### 8.15 Mock 12. "Semiology tells us nothing about power." Discuss.

The provocation that semiology tells us nothing about power is too strong, but it has a real grain of truth, and it is exactly the grain that Stuart Hall's reworking is designed to handle. Semiology gives us a great deal about *one face* of power, the cultural-symbolic face by which ideologies naturalise themselves, and very little about other faces unless we add political economy and audience research to it. The honest answer is "semiology tells us about one register of power well, and is silent on others".

The case for the prosecution. Semiology, in its founding moments, looks like a method for decoding signs, not a theory of power. Saussure's project is to identify *langue* as a system of differences. Lévi-Strauss extends this to universal mental structures. Eco, reading Bond, uncovers "the invariant rules governing the narrative structure of these novels" (Strinati, 1995: 93). None of these moves names class, capital, the state, gender or race directly. Strinati's criticism is precisely that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117). If power lives in production relations and institutional control, semiology is missing power.

The case for the defence. Barthes's semiology, even in *Mythologies*, is explicitly a tool of ideological critique. He frames the project as "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature… the fundamental method of an ideological critique" (in Strinati, 1995: 100). When he reads the Paris-Match cover, what he sees is *imperial* power: "France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The "eternal statute of womanhood" he detects in the women-novelists-with-children photograph is *gendered* power: women "are on the earth to give children to men" (Barthes, 1972: 56). To say semiology tells us nothing about power is to ignore the fact that Barthes's whole project is to denaturalise the cultural surface of power.

The lecture's "Orders of Signification" slide makes the political stakes explicit: the third order is the "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders". Worldviews are political. The seventy women novelists, the Camel "Smooth Character" advert, the Hugo Boss suit, the Jeep Compass advert, the O2 rugby campaign, the HSBC "creating wealth" advert and the "Decolonize" graffiti read semiologically each become a small machine for naturalising specific power relations.

But the defence has to concede ground. Semiology on its own is weaker on three things power-analysts care about. First, *production*: who makes the signs, with what budgets, inside what corporate and state structures? The Camel advert is also a tobacco-industry supply chain inside US advertising regulation; a *Paris-Match* cover is also a newsroom inside a colonial state. Second, *audiences*: who decodes and how? Barthes assumes "the myth-consumer takes the signification for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model insists audiences can produce dominant, negotiated or oppositional readings (lecture slide "Hall: building on structuralism?"). Many Black readers of the Paris-Match cover read it oppositionally. Third, *institutional power*: police, courts, schools, platforms. Semiology has no direct purchase on these. Gramsci's frame is what fills the gap: hegemony, defined as the "'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life" (Gramsci, 1971: 12), routes power through exactly the cultural-symbolic register semiology reads, but anchors it in institutions and class alliances. The Frankfurt School's account of the culture industry, Habermas on the public sphere, Foucault on disciplinary power and Hall's Gramscian encoding/decoding circuit all add what semiology lacks.

So my position is this. The provocation is wrong as a flat claim: semiology gives us the sharpest tool we have for analysing how ideological power gets its naturalising work done at the level of signs. The *Joe Chemo* parody slides, the Absolut Vodka counter-ads ("Absolut Hangover", "Absolut Liver Failure", "Absolut Impotence", "Absolut Submission", "Absolut Drunk"), and the "robbing myth of myth" move (Barthes, 1972: 135) are themselves political acts of culture jamming. But the provocation is right as a warning against semiological imperialism. Power is also material, institutional and embodied. The synthesis is Hall: semiology + Marxism + ideology theory + sociology. Read like that, semiology tells us about half of power, which is half more than most disciplines manage. The other half we get from the rest of the sociology curriculum.

### 8.16 Mock 13. Critically assess Stuart Hall's reworking of structuralism.

Stuart Hall's reworking of structuralism is, in my view, the move that rescues semiology from being a clever but politically thin reading practice and turns it into a usable sociology of culture. The lecture slide titled "Hall: building on structuralism?" frames the question well: Hall does not abandon Saussure or Barthes; he keeps the signifier/signified machinery, but he wires it into media production, institutional power and audience reception. The teacher's summary makes the same point: "Structuralism is extremely powerful at explaining how meaning is organised, but it is much less good at explaining power and inequality in concrete social contexts, media institutions and production and audience interpretation and resistance… he reworks it, combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology" (teacher's summary).

To see why this matters, recall what Hall is reworking. Saussure gives us the relational sign and the system of differences. Barthes layers denotation, connotation and myth, with myth functioning to "transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). Strinati's critique is repeated: semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117) and neglects the social relationships in which signs are produced and consumed. On the consumption side, Barthes's "myth-consumer takes the signification for a system of facts" (Barthes, 1972: 142) is a passive picture.

Hall's encoding/decoding diagram (lecture slide) is the corrective. The slide shows a "Visual / Aural - signifier" passing through encoding inside frameworks of knowledge, relations of production and technical infrastructure, then through decoding inside the audience's own analogous frameworks. Two arrows mark the lecture's emphasis: "Media production and power" on the encoding side, "Differential reception of the audience" on the decoding side. The model recognises three audience positions, dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, oppositional, which Strinati implicitly invokes when he insists that audiences "play in arriving at interpretations of popular culture" (Strinati, 1995: 117).

What does Hall's reworking gain? Three things. First, it *re-historicises* meaning. Every encoded message sits inside a specific institutional moment with specific relations of production, which is also Williams's point when he insists that culture is "a whole way of life" (Williams, 1958: 6) rather than a self-enclosed sign-system. Hall sutures the semiological circuit to that wider materialist frame. Second, it *re-empowers* audiences. The same Hugo Boss suit advert can be decoded as aspirational professionalism (dominant), as ironic class display (negotiated), or as evidence of bourgeois masculinity's grip on the image of "success" (oppositional). The same Camel "Smooth Character" advert can be read as cool, as kitsch, or as the *Joe Chemo* counter-myth invites, as a public-health crime scene. Third, it brings *ideology* back into focus as a sociological process, not a metaphysical category. Gramsci is the key import here: hegemony as "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12) reframes Barthes's myth as a moment in a wider struggle for consent. Hall's later work on Thatcherism, on race and on the media show how encoded meanings struggle in real time inside political conjunctures.

But the reworking is not without its own limits.

First, the model can underspecify *how* differential reception actually happens. Saying audiences decode "within their frameworks of knowledge" is true but slightly tautological without empirical research into specific audiences. Critics have pushed reception studies to fill this in.

Second, Hall keeps a structuralist residue. The encoder still encodes a "meaningful discourse"; the audience decodes from it. Post-structuralist critics (Foucault, later Barthes himself) would press on whether there is a stable meaning at all to encode and decode, given the polysemy of texts. Barthes's "death of the author" (Barthes, 1977) and the polysemic text suggest meaning is always already plural. Hall's three-position model risks tidying that plurality.

Third, the model is best at *broadcast-era* media. Platform capitalism complicates this: the "Styles in late capitalism" slide points to a media environment where encoders and decoders overlap and TikTok aesthetics iterate signification in real time, partly outside the broadcast circuit Hall originally formalised.

Fourth, the reworking sometimes leans on Marxism in ways that may not fit every register of difference. Race, gender, sexuality and colonial power require more than class-and-ideology; intersectional and decolonial readings have extended Hall in productive directions.

My assessment is therefore strongly positive but qualified: Hall's reworking is the indispensable step that makes structuralism socially serious, but it needs further updating for platform media and intersectional power. As a hinge between Saussure-Barthes on the one side and contemporary cultural sociology on the other, it is unrivalled.

### 8.17 Mock 14. Why did the "linguistic turn" become so important to the sociology of media and culture in the 1960s-70s?

The linguistic turn became important to the sociology of media and culture in the 1960s and 1970s because it gave the discipline a usable, supple and politically charged set of tools precisely at the moment when the media environment was expanding and existing theoretical paradigms were running into trouble. Strinati's framing is useful here: structuralism and semiology "benefited from the increasing interest in theory taken by social science in the 1960s, and their reputation is said to owe something to their concern with societies which are increasingly inundated with popular culture" (Strinati, 1995: 80). The lecture's "Rethinking Modernity" map situates structuralism alongside Marxism, the Frankfurt School, British Cultural Studies and feminism in exactly this period.

Three pressures converged to make the linguistic turn attractive.

First, the *empirical pressure* of a saturated media environment. By the 1960s, advertising, television, magazine culture, popular music and film were everywhere. Existing approaches were ill-equipped. The Culture and Civilisation tradition (Arnold, Leavis) treated popular culture as a debasement of high culture; the early Frankfurt School treated it as the output of a manipulative culture industry; British Cultural Studies (Hoggart, Williams, Thompson) treated it sympathetically but lacked a precise method for decoding texts. Saussure's relational sign and Barthes's three orders of signification offered a method that could be applied at the level of the individual image or text, wrestling matches, wine, the Citroën DS, soap powders, the Paris-Match cover, without either dismissing it or simply documenting it.

Second, the *theoretical pressure* of a wider shift toward systems and structures. Lévi-Strauss in anthropology, Althusser in Marxism, Lacan in psychoanalysis, early Foucault in the history of ideas: each was, in different registers, moving away from the autonomous individual subject toward structures, languages and discourses that shape what subjects can think. As Strinati paraphrases Lévi-Strauss, "the so-called 'collective consciousness' would, in the final analysis, be no more than the expression… of certain time and space modalities of the universal laws which make up the unconscious activity of the mind" (in Strinati, 1995: 88-89). For media sociology, this was good news: it meant audiences and producers alike could be theorised as inside systems of signs they did not invent.

Third, the *political pressure* of 1968 and after. Strinati notes that Barthes's project was framed as "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature" (in Strinati, 1995: 100). To denaturalise the cultural surface, the flag, the family, the suit, the brand, the empire, was to do politics in a register the 1960s student left could recognise. The same period saw decolonisation make the politics of language acute. Ngũgĩ argues that "the bullet was the means of the physical subjugation. Language was the means of the spiritual subjugation" (Ngũgĩ, 1986: 9), which is the linguistic turn read from the colonial periphery: language as instrument of empire, not just as system of differences. *Mythologies* (1957) thus aged well into the late 1960s, and Stuart Hall's CCCS at Birmingham picked it up directly. The lecture's "Rethinking Modernity" diagram shows structuralism feeding both into British Cultural Studies and into post-structuralism (Foucault) and post-modernism (Lyotard, Baudrillard).

The fit with media and culture was particularly snug. Television and advertising had become the medium through which ideology now arrived, and television was an obviously *coded* medium: shot/reverse-shot, voice-over, music, brand, slogan, all readable as paradigmatic and syntagmatic choices. Hall's encoding/decoding model, formalised at Birmingham in the 1970s, used exactly this language. The linguistic turn delivered the vocabulary; media sociology delivered the institutional questions.

There were costs as well as gains. Empirical validation of semiological readings remained slippery: Strinati's complaint that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114) was already alive in the period. The reduction of ideology to "bourgeois ideology" struggled with race and gender. Audience reception was under-theorised until Hall rebuilt it.

But the underlying point holds: in a decade when media and culture were becoming the central terrain of social experience, the linguistic turn supplied a method for treating that terrain analytically. Without it, contemporary media sociology, from the analysis of advertising to encoding/decoding to studies of branding, platforms, identity politics and post-modern culture, would have lacked its central toolkit. The lecture's flowchart of theoretical schools shows structuralism feeding forward into post-structuralism and intersecting with cultural studies. That feeding-forward is precisely the legacy of the 1960s-70s turn, and it explains why the period was decisive.

### 8.18 Mock 15. What does Barthes's reading of the Paris-Match cover tell us about race, empire and cultural signs?

Barthes's reading of the Paris-Match cover is one of the foundational case studies of cultural semiology, and it tells us more about race, empire and cultural signs together than almost any other short passage in twentieth-century theory. It also exposes both the strengths and the limits of Barthes's frame on questions of racial power.

The scene is famous. "I am at the barber's, and a copy of Paris-Match is offered to me. On the cover, a young Negro in a French uniform is saluting, with his eyes uplifted, probably fixed on a fold of the tricolour" (Barthes, 1972: 125). Denotatively, that is what we see: a Black soldier saluting a flag. Connotatively, Barthes diagnoses "a purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness" (Barthes, 1972: 125). Mythically, the sign signifies "French imperiality", "that France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag, and that there is no better answer to the detractors of an alleged colonialism than the zeal shown by this Negro in serving his so-called oppressors" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126).

What does this tell us, first, about cultural signs in general? It tells us that signs work in layers and that the political work of culture happens at the third layer. The Black soldier is not "made" to mean French imperiality by anyone's individual intention; the meaning is built by the system. The soldier's specific history, colonial recruitment, racial hierarchy, the violence of empire, is what Barthes calls the "form" of the myth. That form has been "taken from one system, his real history" and "placed in another system, that of the myth, which denies his history and culture, and thus the real history of French colonial exploitation" (Strinati, 1995: 105). The cultural sign performs an act of historical theft.

What does this tell us about *empire*? It tells us that empire reproduces itself culturally not by openly defending colonisation but by displaying compliant racialised bodies in patriotic poses. The myth is not "France is right to colonise", that would be politically vulnerable. The myth is "look at this good Negro who salutes just like one of our boys" (Barthes, 1972: 134), a much harder target, because it presents itself as a simple human fact rather than a political argument. Ngũgĩ names the broader operation: "the bullet was the means of the physical subjugation. Language was the means of the spiritual subjugation" (Ngũgĩ, 1986: 9). The Paris-Match cover is the visual analogue of the linguistic move Ngũgĩ describes; the imperial sign-system does the spiritual subjugation through image rather than through grammar. Empire's strongest cultural defence is its capacity to make itself look like the absence of empire.

What does this tell us about *race*? Two things. First, race is mediated through cultural signs whose effects are not reducible to explicit racial language. The cover does not say "Black people support empire"; it shows it. The visual sign does political work an explicit statement could not. Second, the racialised subject is honoured as a "son" of France precisely on condition that his racialised history is erased: an "impoverishment of meaning" (Strinati, 1995: 105) by which the soldier's individual history is impoverished so that the imperial myth can be enriched.

But the reading also exposes the limits of Barthes's frame on race. Three should be flagged.

First, Barthes's theoretical agent is "the bourgeoisie" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Race in the colonial context is irreducible to class. The myth of imperiality serves a particular racial-colonial formation in which French citizenship was hierarchically organised by colour, and class is one but only one axis.

Second, audience differentiation. Barthes assumes the reader receives the myth as natural. Hall's encoding/decoding model insists Black French readers, anti-colonial activists and white Parisians decoded it differently. Oppositional readings were already alive in 1955.

Third, the legacy in our time. The lecture's HSBC advert with its woman-of-colour figure against a global financial skyline rehearses a related move: racial inclusion as visible alibi for corporate-financial power. The "Decolonize" graffiti, by contrast, performs the third-order "robbing myth of myth" (Barthes, 1972: 135) on the imperial template Barthes named.

Barthes's reading of the Paris-Match cover therefore tells us that race, empire and cultural signs interlock through myth's naturalising work; that the political defence of empire runs through aesthetics, not argument; and that the diagnosis still travels. To complete it, we need Hall on audiences and decolonial readings on the specifics of racial power. Barthes opens the door; the rest of the literature walks through.

### 8.19 Mock 16. Are audiences "myth-consumers" or active decoders? Discuss with reference to Barthes and Hall.

The honest sociological answer is that audiences are *both*, and the interesting empirical question is when and how each picture applies. Barthes's "myth-consumer" describes a real phenomenon, the moment of taking a coded sign as a natural fact, and Hall's "active decoder" describes another real phenomenon, the moment of recognising, negotiating or resisting the coding. The strongest theoretical position holds both at once.

Barthes's picture is sharp. The reader is allowed "to consume myth innocently" because "the signifier and the signified have, in his eyes, a natural relationship"; "the myth-consumer takes the signification for a system of facts: myth is read as a factual system, whereas it is but a semiological system" (Barthes, 1972: 142). This is a *phenomenology of reception*: the audience takes the soldier on the *Paris-Match* cover as just a fact about loyalty, not an artefact of imperial myth-making. The same logic applies when audiences see the Camel advert as just a cool image, the Hugo Boss advert as just a picture of a successful man, Coca-Cola Christmas ads as just festive warmth, or the seventy-women-novelists photo as just a record of working mothers.

The "myth-consumer" picture captures the phenomenon of cultural fluency without reflexivity. We are competent users of mythologies before we are critical readers of them. Wrestling matches register as drama before moral spectacle. The Camel campaigns feel like glamour before iconography of addiction. The Femininity slide's checklist feels like description before ideology. National anthem rituals at sports events feel like patriotism before manufactured belonging.

Hall's picture is the necessary corrective. The slide shows decoding happening "within frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, technical infrastructure", with the result that the same message can be read in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways. The audience is not blank; it brings class, race, gender, generation, subcultural position and political experience to the act of reading. Williams gives this the right ontological frame: culture as "a whole way of life" (Williams, 1958: 6) means audiences decode signs from within structures of feeling that are lived, ordinary and social rather than purely textual. Black readers of the Paris-Match cover did not, on the whole, accept French imperiality. The makers of *Joe Chemo* decoded Camel oppositionally years before public health agencies did. The "Absolut Liver Failure" and "Absolut Impotence" counter-ads do the same to the Absolut myth.

Strinati's critique sharpens Hall's point against Barthes. Semiology, he argues, "does not therefore take account of how people actually interpret myth, for if myths are so effectively mystifying, how can they be so easily demystified?" (Strinati, 1995: 107). If readers were really pure myth-consumers, no critical reading would ever surface. The fact that students in an SC301 seminar can sit and unpick the Hugo Boss advert, the Jeep advert, or the O2 "Wear the Rose" campaign on the lecture slides shows that the active-decoder picture is also true.

The synthesis is straightforward. The same audience moves between the two positions depending on context, mood, attention and competence. I see a Jeep Compass advert and feel the appeal of escape (myth-consumer); I then teach it on Monday morning and see the commodification of "adventure" (active decoder). The lecture's "Styles in late capitalism" slide lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, and notes that their cultures are saturated with "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" as further third-order significations. Post-modern audiences are routinely ironised consumers of their own mythologies.

There are limits on both sides. The myth-consumer picture risks condescension: it treats audiences as dupes, which is empirically false and politically demobilising. The active-decoder picture risks complacency: if every audience is always already critical, ideology has no work to do, which is also empirically false. The right position is in between, and the empirical research, reception studies in cultural studies after Hall, has spent fifty years filling in when each pole dominates and why.

My conclusion is therefore that Barthes is right that audiences sometimes consume myth as nature, Hall is right that audiences also actively decode within their own frameworks, and the live sociological project is to specify the conditions under which one mode prevails over the other. The lecture's contemporary lifestyle paradigm slide, roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, names a culture in which audiences are invited into both modes at once.

### 8.20 Mock 17. "In late capitalism, third-order chains of signification have no end." Discuss.

The slogan that in late capitalism third-order chains of signification have no end is half-right and half-misleading, and getting which half is which is the substance of the answer. The lecture's slide titled "Third order chains of signification in 'late capitalism'", featuring DEATH cigarettes branded with a skull, Liquid Death mountain water with its black-metal aesthetic, and a Dutch Barn vodka advert sold by Ricky Gervais ("Buying Dutch Barn will make a person rich & happy. And that person is Ricky Gervais"), is the visual joke that underwrites the slogan. Those products do not just sell themselves; they sell themselves *as* the parody of selling themselves. The myth is robbed before it even sets.

Begin with the theoretical warrant. Barthes himself invites the move: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth? All that is needed is to use it as the departure point for a third semiological chain, to take its signification as the first term of a second myth" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The lecture treats this passage explicitly under the slide "Robbing Myth of Myth: Third-Order Signification". And the next slide, "Styles and significations in late capitalism / post-modernity", lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, asking what "further third order significations… knowingness / excess / parody / irony" do in post-modern culture.

So the slogan is right that the *formal* possibility of further chains is open-ended. Once any sign can become the first term in a new mythological chain, there is no logical stopping point. Liquid Death takes the cigarette-style connotation of dangerous cool and applies it to *water*. DEATH cigarettes, with their skull branding and the disclaimer "TOBACCO SERIOUSLY DAMAGES HEALTH" baked into the same image, perform the same recursion. The Dutch Barn vodka advert mythologises the form of celebrity endorsement.

The slogan is right, secondly, about the *commercial* incentive for infinite recursion. The Absolut campaigns walk through one full cycle: "Absolut Spring", "Absolut Marilyn" mythologise the bottle as art; the counter-ads "Absolut Liver Failure", "Absolut Impotence" mythologise that myth as denial. Each iteration commodifies the previous critique.

But the slogan is misleading in three ways.

First, *not every audience experiences the chain as infinite*. Third-order recursion is highly visible to media-literate, often middle-class consumers fluent in irony. Many audiences experience the same brands as first-order myths. Apple's "Think Different" still works on viewers who do not pause to parse the slogan; the Camel advert still works on viewers who have never seen *Joe Chemo*. "No end" is true in principle and overstated in practice.

Second, *the chains terminate in something material*. Behind Liquid Death is a beverage company with shareholders and supply chains. Behind Camel is a tobacco industry inside US advertising regulation. Behind the Jeep Compass advert is a manufacturer, a dealer network, a price list. The recursive semiotics ride on a non-recursive political economy. Strinati's complaint that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117) applies with force.

Third, *the audience response is finite*. Hall's encoding/decoding model reminds us that recursion at the level of the sign does not automatically produce recursion at the level of the reader. Audiences get tired, exit categories, refuse the joke. TikTok trend cycles have life cycles: the "clean girl" of 2023 is not the "clean girl" of 2026; "looksmaxxers" rise and fall.

The deeper theoretical question is whether the infinite-chain image is descriptively accurate or ideologically convenient. Critics of post-modernism (including Jameson, who sits on the same "Rethinking Modernity" map as structuralism) argue that the sense of endless recursion is itself a feature of late capitalism: irony is the new sincerity, and unmasking is the new mask. Baudrillard pushes this further: under simulation, the sign no longer "refers" at all but circulates as "models of a real without origin or reality: a hyperreal" (Baudrillard, 1994: 1). Liquid Death and DEATH cigarettes are not third-order Barthesian myths so much as Baudrillardian simulacra, signs that have detached from any first-order referent. If so, then "the chain has no end" is itself a myth, a third-order signification that naturalises the impossibility of stepping outside.

My conclusion is that the slogan captures something real about late-capitalist signification but overstates it. The chains can keep going formally, do keep going commercially, but stop on the ground of production and on the ground of finite audiences. The most useful response to the slogan is to add Hall, political economy and trend-cycle empiricism, and to keep asking where each chain is actually attached.

### 8.21 Mock 18. How well does Barthes's semiology travel into feminist analyses of popular culture?

Barthes's semiology travels surprisingly well into feminist analyses of popular culture. The lecture's own examples, Dick Bruna's *The school* with its blue boy and red girl, the Femininity paradigm slide ("Obedient / Servile / Passive / Submissive / Emotional / Sensitive / Private sphere / Reproductive / Child care / Domestic labour") and the Masculinity slide ("Manly / Virile / Muscular / Heroic / Wise / Aggressive / Dominant / Sexually active / Successful / Public sphere"), point directly to the productive uses. But the travel is incomplete: Barthes's class-bound theory of ideology has to be extended to make feminist sense of gendered signs.

Begin with what Barthes gives feminism. In *Mythologies* he turns his eye to a photograph of seventy women novelists, each captioned with her number of children. Denotatively, women novelists. Connotatively, women novelists who are *also* mothers. Mythically, what Barthes calls the "eternal statute of womanhood": "women are on the earth to give children to men" (Barthes, 1972: 56). They may "write as much as they like" but cannot "depart from" their condition (Barthes, 1972: 56-57). The myth tells women that "your freedom is a luxury, it is possible only if you first acknowledge the obligations of your nature" (Barthes, 1972: 58).

This is feminist analysis avant la lettre. Strinati paraphrases: "the role of women as mothers is made to appear natural and inevitable, the related connotation being that the power and dominance of men is equally natural and inevitable" (Strinati, 1995: 110). The method is to denaturalise the gendered sign and re-historicise it. Spender's *Man Made Language* extends the analysis below the level of the image to language itself: language is "literally man made" and "still primarily under male control", and the "semantic rule which has been responsible for the manifestation of sexism in the language can be simply stated: there are two fundamental categories, male and minus male" (Spender, 1980: 12, 20). Read as semiology, Spender's patriarchal language is a Barthesian myth at the level of the linguistic sign itself.

The travel into contemporary work is direct. Dick Bruna's *The school* denotes two children, connotes the colour-coded binary, mythologises gender as a natural pair. The "clean girl" mythologises femininity as productive optimisation. Disney princesses operate as a semiotic system of feminine virtue; the Met Gala and royal weddings stage gendered iconography as natural spectacle. The Femininity slide's image of women doing laundry and kitchen work lays out the paradigmatic field of feminine connotations.

Masculinity gets the same treatment. The Camel "Smooth Character" campaigns mythologise masculinity as freedom; the Hugo Boss suit and the Jeep Compass work the same way. Eco's Bond reading maps the gendered moves: "Bond takes Woman… Bond, convalescing, enjoys Woman, whom he then loses" (Strinati, 1995: 95). The narrative scheme is built on gender as a binary opposition.

But three limits on the travel are real.

First, *Barthes's class-bound theory of ideology*. He reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, 1995: 117), which under-specifies patriarchy as a power formation that cuts across class. Feminist theorists from de Beauvoir onward had to do that work separately, and semiology needs the supplement. The "eternal statute of womanhood" is not just bourgeois; it operates across class, and it intersects with race and sexuality in ways Barthes does not theorise.

Second, *the active-decoder problem*. Barthes's myth-consumer is gender-neutral and passive. Feminist reception studies (after Hall) have shown that women audiences actively decode gendered media, sometimes resisting (queer and feminist re-readings of the Femininity-slide paradigm), sometimes pleasure-reading against the grain (the toys on the lecture's "Barthes - Toys" slide read as imaginative space rather than as gender training), sometimes oppositional. The Hall encoding/decoding model is therefore essential to make Barthes feminist-usable.

Third, *intersectionality*. Race, class, sexuality and disability cut through gender. The Femininity slide is implicitly white, middle-class, heterosexual. A "clean girl" sign produced by a Black creator travels through different mythological chains than a white one; the HSBC advert's woman-of-colour figure does different cultural work than the Hugo Boss model. Barthes does not theorise these intersections; feminist-of-colour scholarship has had to build them in.

My assessment is therefore that Barthes's semiology is *necessary* for feminist analyses of popular culture, because it provides the technical vocabulary for denaturalising gendered signs, and the lecture's own choice of examples shows how cleanly it applies. But it is not *sufficient*. To do feminist semiology well we need Hall on decoding, intersectional theorising on the textures of difference, and political economy on the industries that produce gendered images. Barthes opens the door; feminism walks through and remodels the building.

### 8.22 Mock 19. Is semiology a science or a form of literary criticism?

The honest answer is that semiology was conceived as a science, has often functioned as literary criticism, and is most defensible today as a *systematic interpretive method* whose status sits between the two. The interesting move is not to decide which label is correct but to see what each pressure brings out about the method's strengths and weaknesses.

The scientific pedigree is real. Saussure proposed semiology as "a science which studies the life of signs within society, shows what they are composed of, and discovers the laws which govern them" (Strinati, 1995: 85). The semiotics infographic lays out a quasi-formal taxonomy: symbolic / iconic / indexical signs, syntagm and paradigm, denotation and connotation. Barthes's early framing was equally scientific: semiology was "the fundamental method of an ideological critique" (in Strinati, 1995: 100). Hall's encoding/decoding model is presented with the diagrammatic precision of a flowchart.

But the practice of semiology has often looked more like literary criticism than like a science. Strinati's running complaint is sharp: "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (Strinati, 1995: 114). When Barthes claims that roses signify passion, the reader is asked to nod along. Why not "joke, farewell, or a platonic thank you"? "What evidence could a semiologist call upon to back up Barthes's interpretation?" (Strinati, 1995: 114). Williamson's reading of a tyre advert, the jetty "represents… strong and safe", is, on Strinati's analysis, "totally arbitrary" (Strinati, 1995: 115). The Leavisite tradition is the cautionary precedent here: Q. D. Leavis's *Fiction and the Reading Public* read mass-market novels in detail and concluded that they represented "an aspect of cultural disintegration of grave significance" (Leavis, 1932: 74), but the verdict rested on the critic's taste rather than on a method that could be checked by another reader. Semiology, on its weaker readings, risks the same fate: brilliant individual judgement dressed in technical vocabulary.

Three features make semiology look like criticism rather than science.

First, *the empirical validation problem*. A genuine science specifies its falsification conditions; semiology rarely does. The same sign admits multiple readings; the analyst picks one.

Second, *the polysemy concession*. Barthes argued that texts are polysemic, open to multiple interpretations. Strinati notes that this "hardly gets us very far" as a methodological position (Strinati, 1995: 114). A science needs ranking criteria; criticism survives on persuasive force.

Third, *audience and production blindness*. A semiology that ignores who made the sign and how audiences actually decode it is reading texts in a vacuum. That is criticism with social ambition rather than social science.

But it would be too quick to conclude that semiology is therefore "just" literary criticism. Three features keep it within reach of scientific method.

First, *the systematicity of the apparatus*. The signifier/signified distinction, the langue/parole distinction, the paradigmatic/syntagmatic axes, the three orders of signification, these are not impressionistic. They are repeatable analytical operations. Two readers trained in the apparatus can run them on the same Hugo Boss advert or Jeep Compass advert and produce overlapping, comparable analyses.

Second, *the structural cumulation*. Eco's structural reading of the Bond novels (Strinati, 1995: 93-98) generates predictions about narrative invariance that can be tested against other genre fiction. Hall's encoding/decoding model has generated decades of testable reception studies.

Third, *the integration into wider social science*. Once Hall reworks semiology to include encoding institutions and decoding audiences, the method becomes empirically tractable: you can study the production side ethnographically, the decoding side through audience research, and combine them with semiotic textual analysis. This is not bench science, but it is recognisable social science.

My own view is that the most useful position is the middle one: semiology is a *systematic interpretive method*. Its claims should be evaluated by criteria appropriate to interpretive social science: internal coherence, fit with the textual evidence, plausibility against alternative readings, productivity for further research, and consilience with what we know about production and reception. Judged by physics-style standards, it will always look like criticism; judged by the standards of qualitative social research, which it shares with ethnography and historical sociology, it is methodologically respectable.

The label "science" carries too much positivist baggage; the label "criticism" sells the method's rigour short. Semiology is best understood as one tool in the interpretive toolkit, indispensable for analysing the cultural surface of contemporary capitalism, and most defensible when used alongside encoding/decoding research, political economy and ethnography. That is neither pure science nor pure criticism, but it is good work.

### 8.23 Mock 20. What can semiology contribute to understanding the politics of national and imperial symbols (Barthes's Paris-Match cover, the O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign, the "Decolonize" graffiti on the semiotics slide)?

National and imperial symbols are the sharpest test case for semiology because they trade precisely on appearing natural and timeless rather than historically made. I want to argue that semiology contributes three indispensable things to their politics: a method for unpacking how they work, a vocabulary for naming the move from history to nature, and a route into the politics of counter-signs. The L15 slide that pairs the Paris-Match cover with "Wear the Rose" and the "Decolonize" graffiti is a working syllabus for that argument.

Start with the Paris-Match cover, which is Barthes's textbook case. The denotation is straightforward: a young Black soldier in French uniform salutes, his eyes raised, the tricolour presumably outside the frame. The connotation, on Barthes's reading, is "a purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness" (Barthes, 1972: 125). The myth, the third-order meaning, "is that France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag, and that there is no better answer to the detractors of an alleged colonialism than the zeal shown by this Negro in serving his so-called oppressors" (Barthes, 1972: 125-126). The image's political work is to "transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140): the Algerian war and the long arc of French colonial violence are blotted out by the apparently natural fact of a smiling colonial subject. Without the denotation / connotation / myth apparatus, the image looks innocent.

The "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign performs the same operation inside post-imperial English nationhood. The rose denotes a flower. It connotes Englishness, tradition, a pastoral and chivalric national past. The myth it produces is that England is a continuous community of taste, loyalty and sporting decency, and that the rose is the natural sign of belonging to it. Barthes's principle holds across the historical break: from imperial nation to post-imperial sporting nation, the work of the sign is to feel inevitable. The slide-bullet "destroy the idea that signs are natural" is the political hinge that the analyst can use here. The rose is conventional, not natural; it could have been the oak, the cup, the lion. Once that is visible, the political stakes of the choice become visible too: who counts as inside the rose, who is excluded from its pastoral, and which version of England the campaign mobilises.

The "Decolonize" graffiti is the third move and the most interesting. It is a counter-sign that operates on the mythic register the first two signs depend on. Barthes himself noted that "the best weapon against myth is perhaps to mythify it in its turn, and to produce an artificial myth" (Barthes, 1972: 135). The graffiti does not merely state a position; it intervenes in the sign-economy itself, marking a public monument or surface as a site of contested meaning. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and the wider conversation around statues and street names work in the same register: they make the naturalised signs of national history feel like signs again. This is semiology used reflexively.

Limits, however, matter. Strinati's reminder is that semiology on its own "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" and that "meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (Strinati, 1995: 117). Paris-Match is a magazine industry with readers and editors; the rugby campaign is a corporate sponsorship inside a televised sporting calendar; the graffiti is anonymous public speech with its own production conditions and audiences. A full account needs Hall's reworking, where encoding institutions and decoding audiences are restored, alongside Gramsci's account of hegemony as "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population" (Gramsci, 1971: 12) to dominant cultural leadership.

Semiology cannot replace political economy, audience research or the wider sociology of cultural production. What it does contribute is irreplaceable: it gives the analyst a method for showing exactly how the rose, the salute and the graffiti do their political work at the level of the sign. National and imperial symbols persist by appearing not to be signs at all. The L15 lecture's central instruction, "destroy the idea that signs are natural", is the first political act semiology asks of us. The Paris-Match cover, the "Wear the Rose" campaign and the "Decolonize" graffiti are the three sites where that act is most worth performing today.

### 8.24 Mock 21. Can popular culture be used to "rob myth of myth"? Discuss with reference to culture jamming, parody and irony.

Barthes's own answer to this question is more cautious than the slogan suggests. He writes that "the best weapon against myth is perhaps to mythify it in its turn, and to produce an artificial myth" (Barthes, 1972: 135), but he also warns that myth's mode is naturalisation, which makes direct critique inside its own register treacherously easy to recuperate. I want to argue that popular culture can rob myth of myth, but that the operation is unstable: parodic counter-signs tend to be re-mythologised by the very system they critique, and the work has to be done repeatedly.

Begin with the textbook cases of culture jamming. Adbusters's campaigns transformed corporate logos into their own critique: Joe Camel became Joe Chemo, the Calvin Klein "Obsession" advert was turned against itself, the Nike swoosh was rebranded as sweatshop emblem. Each operation works at exactly the second-order level Barthes identifies. The brand sign denotes a product; the parody preserves the denotation but inverts the connotation; the parodic myth replaces the corporate myth. Banksy operates in the same register. The Hugo Boss suit, in a culture-jamming reading, denotes a garment, connotes professionalism, and could have its mythological work, the naturalisation of bourgeois masculinity, exposed by a parodic remake. Strinati's principle stands: the function of myth is "to transform history into nature" (citing Barthes, 1972: 140), and culture jamming reverses the direction, denaturalising what was made to feel natural.

Parody and irony work less aggressively but on the same logic. *The Onion* and *The Daily Mash* report fictional news in the perfect house style of real news, denaturalising the conventions of journalism by exaggerating them. *Black Mirror* episodes such as "Fifteen Million Merits" stage a hyper-real version of media spectacle to make its current form feel constructed. *Severance* defamiliarises the office. *Adolescence* in another register defamiliarises crisis-television itself, refusing the genre's usual mythic closure. Each operates as an artificial myth, in Barthes's sense, that exposes the existing myth as a myth.

But there are real limits. The first is Strinati's polysemy concession (Strinati, 1995: 114): parody can be decoded as endorsement. The line between "this advert is ridiculous" and "this advert is now cooler because it knows it is ridiculous" is thin, and many viewers cross it without noticing. The DEATH cigarettes and Liquid Death water referenced in the L15 deck illustrate this: a brand that already parodies the form of branding can sell more product, not less. The second limit is the recuperation problem, which is structural to capitalist popular culture. Punk's countercultural style became Vivienne Westwood and then high-street fashion. Banksy's anti-art became gallery prices and Sotheby's auctions. Che Guevara's revolutionary iconography became t-shirts. Hall's encoding/decoding framework helps name this: yesterday's oppositional reading becomes today's preferred reading once the new sign is naturalised. The decolonial graffiti of one generation becomes the museum installation of the next.

The third limit is theoretical. Strinati notes that Barthes himself treats myth-busting as a literary-critical act rather than a political programme (Strinati, 1995: 114-117). Robbing myth of myth at the level of the text leaves the production conditions, the institutions and the political economy that generate the original myths fully intact. A truly counter-hegemonic operation, in Gramsci's vocabulary (Gramsci, 1971: 12), would need to combine semiotic intervention with a wider war of position across cultural institutions, media production and political organisation.

Where does this leave the question? Popular culture can rob myth of myth. The Adbusters campaigns, *The Onion*, Banksy's interventions, drag's defamiliarisation of gender, BLM-era reworkings of monumental iconography, all demonstrate the operation in working order. But the moment of demystification rarely lasts. Brands recuperate parody; oppositional readings become preferred ones; the artificial myth congeals into a new natural one. The honest answer is yes, briefly, repeatedly, and only when the semiotic operation is part of a wider hegemonic struggle. Barthes's caution stands: there is no single act of myth-busting that liberates popular culture once and for all. There is only the patient, repeated work of making signs feel like signs again.

### 8.25 Mock 22. "We need Saussure to identify the structure, Barthes to read the myth, and Hall to bring back power." Discuss.

The formula is broadly correct as a division of analytical labour, and it doubles nicely as a revision card. But I want to argue that it understates Hall's contribution, ignores Lévi-Strauss as the bridge between Saussure's linguistics and cultural analysis, and risks reducing semiology to a tidy three-step recipe. Each name in the formula does what it says, but each does more than the slogan allows.

Saussure first. The formula's account of him is accurate: he identifies the structure. The sign is signifier plus signified; the relation between them is arbitrary; meaning emerges from position within a *langue* rather than from things in the world (Saussure, 1916, cited in Strinati, 1995: 83). The traffic-lights image on the lecture deck stages this: red signifies stop because the highway-code system fixes a relational difference, not because red is intrinsically stop-coloured. Without Saussure the apparatus has no foundation. Strinati's gloss is precise: "there is no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, 1995: 83). The structural insight is the precondition for everything else.

Barthes second. The formula's account is also broadly right: he reads the myth. Barthes inherits Saussure but stretches the apparatus to images, fashion, food, advertising, film, news and popular culture. He adds the two further orders: connotation, the cultural level, and myth, the ideological level. He names the central operation of mythic signification, "to transform history into nature" (Barthes, 1972: 140). The Paris-Match cover, the wine, the steak, the wrestling, the seventy women novelists, the Citroën DS, all become legible once the denotation / connotation / myth machinery is applied. Without Barthes we can identify signs but cannot diagnose their political work.

Hall third, and here the formula does him a disservice. The line says he "brings back power", but Hall does considerably more than add a political moralism to a neutral toolkit. He rewrites the toolkit. First, he restores production through the encoder side of the encoding/decoding model: signs are made in institutions with technical infrastructure, frameworks of knowledge and relations of production. Second, he restores reception through the decoder side: preferred, negotiated and oppositional readings (Hall, 1980). Third, he integrates the model with Marxism via Gramsci's account of hegemony as "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group" (Gramsci, 1971: 12). Fourth, and crucially, he corrects Barthes's class reductionism by integrating race and the post-colonial: 'The Whites of Their Eyes' on the grammar of race in television, *Policing the Crisis* on the moral panic about mugging, the encoding/decoding diagram repurposed as a hegemony/counter-hegemony diagram. The teacher's summary makes the point exactly: Hall "does not abandon structuralism. Instead, he reworks it, combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology." That is not "bringing back power"; it is a reconstruction.

The formula has a fourth missing name: Lévi-Strauss. He is the natural bridge between Saussure's linguistics and Barthes's cultural analysis. He extends Saussurean structure beyond language into kinship, myth and culture, and gives the binary apparatus that Hall later turns to political use: West/Rest, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic. Strinati notes the cost of leaving him out: "structural analysis tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, 1995: 117), but structural analysis is also the apparatus that makes the binaries visible in the first place. Eco's structural reading of the Bond novels (Strinati, 1995: 93-98) is the worked example of how Lévi-Straussian structure produces testable predictions about narrative form.

There is also a fifth missing register: critique from outside the semiotic tradition. Williamson on advertising, Mulvey's male gaze (cross-link to L10), feminist film theory, decolonial semiotics, and the political-economic critiques developed by the Frankfurt School (cross-link to L12). The formula's strength is its tidiness; its weakness is that it makes semiology a closed shop, when the most productive contemporary work crosses the boundary.

As a revision-card slogan, the line works: Saussure for structure, Barthes for myth, Hall for power. As an account of what we actually need to analyse cultural signs in late capitalism, it understates Hall, omits Lévi-Strauss, and ignores the empirical and political registers that surround the tradition. The right formula is the sloganised one expanded: Saussure for the structural apparatus, Lévi-Strauss for the binary extension, Barthes for the mythic operation, Hall for the production-and-reception reconstruction, and the wider critical sociology for the political-economic ground.
