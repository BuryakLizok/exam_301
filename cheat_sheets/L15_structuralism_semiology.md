# L15 — Structuralism and Cultural Signs (Saussure, Barthes, Hall)

*SC301 cheat-sheet. Built from the lecture slides, Strinati Ch. 3 (Spring reading), and the teacher's revision summary.*

---

## §1. One-sentence summary

Structuralism and semiology argue that meaning in language and culture is not natural but produced by structured systems of signs (Saussure's signifier/signified), that popular-cultural signs work simultaneously at the levels of denotation, connotation and myth (Barthes), and that any serious account of cultural meaning has to be reworked — as Stuart Hall does — to bring back power, ideology, institutions and the active audience.

---

## §2. The five core moves

1. **Saussure's relational sign.** The sign = signifier (physical form: sound, mark, image, style) + signified (mental concept, not the thing). The relation is arbitrary; meaning arises from difference within a system (*langue*) rather than from things in the world. "In the linguistic sign, the signifier is the 'sound image'… and the signified is the concept of the object or idea which is being referred to" (Strinati, *An Introduction to Theories of Popular Culture*, p. 83).
2. **From linguistics to a science of signs.** Saussure proposes semiology as "a science which studies the life of signs within society" (Strinati, p. 85). The langue/parole, synchronic/diachronic and paradigmatic/syntagmatic distinctions become tools for analysing fashion, food, advertising, film, news — anything culturally coded.
3. **Barthes's two-tier model.** Modern signs work simultaneously at denotation (literal, "what we see", apparently neutral) and connotation (cultural associations, values, emotions). The suit connotes professionalism/authority; the Jeep connotes adventure/masculinity.
4. **Myth as third-order signification.** Myth is "a system of communication… a mode of signification" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 117). Its function is "to transform history into nature" (*Mythologies*, p. 140) — to make culturally specific, class-bound meanings appear timeless, obvious, natural. The Paris-Match cover of the naked Black soldier saluting the French flag is Barthes's emblematic case.
5. **Hall's reworking.** Structuralism is "extremely powerful at explaining how meaning is organised, but it is much less good at explaining power and inequality in concrete social contexts, media institutions… and audience interpretation and resistance" (teacher's summary). Hall's encoding/decoding model adds production, ideology and differential reception to the semiological frame.

---

## §3. Key concepts, defined tightly

- **The sign** — the unit of meaning, made of signifier + signified, "the former and the latter existed before uniting and forming this third object, which is the sign" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 122).
- **Signifier / signified** — physical form vs. mental concept. Not the thing itself.
- **The arbitrariness of the sign** — there is "no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, p. 83).
- **Langue / parole** — system vs. use; "what is social from what is individual… what is essential from what is accessory" (Saussure, in Strinati, p. 82).
- **Synchronic / diachronic** — snapshot of the system vs. history of changes.
- **Paradigmatic / syntagmatic axes** — substitutable elements (paradigm) vs. combinable sequences (syntagm). "A single element that when altered can change the overall meaning" (syntagm); "elements that can be altered and still retain the same meaning" (paradigm) (Independent semiotics infographic, lecture slides).
- **Denotation** — first-order, literal, "most obvious level… literal meaning" (lecture slides).
- **Connotation** — second-order, cultural, "suggested or implied meaning" (lecture slides).
- **Myth** — third-order, "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders" (lecture slides). "The very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 140).
- **Mythologies** — Barthes's 1957 collection of short essays decoding wrestling, wine, soap, the Citroën DS, striptease, toys and the Paris-Match cover.
- **The death of the author** — Barthes's later move: meaning is produced by readers, not fixed by writers' intentions.
- **Encoding / decoding (Hall)** — production encodes a "meaningful discourse" inside frameworks of knowledge, relations of production and technical infrastructure; audiences decode within their own frameworks. Three positions: dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, oppositional.
- **Polysemic text** — a text open to multiple readings.
- **Iconic / indexical / symbolic** (Peirce, on slide) — signs that resemble, are caused by, or are conventionally linked to what they signify.
- **The linguistic turn** — the broader twentieth-century move in social theory toward language as the model for understanding culture, meaning and power.

## §4. Worked examples (concrete, exam-ready)

- **Barthes's "wrestler"** — staged combat where bodies and gestures denote a fight but connote a moral spectacle of justice, suffering and defeat ("the bad guy" framed in the slide).
- **Barthes's "wine"** — denotes a fermented drink; connotes Frenchness, conviviality, classlessness; myth = wine as the "totem-drink" of the nation that erases its colonial production.
- **Barthes's "Citroën DS"** — denotes a 1955 car; connotes seamless modernity, aerodynamic perfection; myth = technology as quasi-religious magic.
- **The Paris-Match cover** — denotes a Black soldier saluting the French flag; connotes "a purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness"; myth = "French imperiality… that France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, pp. 125–126).
- **Apple's "Think Different"** — denotes a slogan; connotes creativity, individualism, rebellion; myth = consumption as self-actualisation.
- **Coca-Cola Christmas adverts** — denote a brand; connote family, warmth, snow, generosity; myth = a privatised, commodified Christmas presented as eternal tradition (the modern Santa partly invented by Coke's 1930s ads).
- **Met Gala costumes** — denote celebrity outfits; connote wealth, art-world status; myth = fashion as a "natural" expression of cultural hierarchy.
- **Royal weddings** — denote ceremonies; connote tradition, romance, nationhood; myth = the monarchy as timeless community-binding spectacle.
- **The Union Jack on Brexit** — denotes a flag; connotes sovereignty, history; myth = a "naturally" sovereign British people whose interests stand outside political construction.
- **Nike "Just Do It"** — denotes an imperative; connotes willpower, athleticism; myth = neoliberal self-improvement.
- **Charlie Brooker's *Black Mirror*** — a contemporary text that *robs myth of myth* (Barthes's third-order move) by staging the very signs of techno-utopia as horror.
- **Instagram aesthetics** — the "clean girl" or "looksmaxxer" feed (lecture slide on styles of late capitalism) denotes lifestyle content; connotes self-discipline, beauty, taste; myth = the optimised self as natural ideal.
- **TikTok trend cycles** — micro-trends denote moves and outfits; connote in-group belonging; myth = identity as endlessly performable through consumption.
- **Brand "essence" (Tesla = innovation)** — the car denotes a product; connotes the future; myth = green capitalism as planetary salvation.
- **Disney princesses as gendered signs** — denote characters; connote femininity, romance; myth = an "eternal statute of womanhood" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 56) where women are "on the earth to give children to men".
- **Cigarettes in Old Hollywood films / the Marlboro Man** — denote smoking; connote sophistication, rugged masculinity; myth = freedom and individualism, robbed of its myth by anti-smoking parodies like the *Joe Chemo* counter-ads on slides 32–33.
- **Sports kit changes (Nike vs Adidas)** — same denotative function (a football shirt) but connotative shift across brand codes; myth = sport as a "natural" arena of national identity and elite athleticism.
- **National anthem rituals at sporting events** — denote a song; connote unity; myth = the nation as an organic, pre-political community.

## §5. Critiques and limits (the Hall pivot)

- **Empirical validity.** Strinati: "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (p. 114). What evidence makes "roses = passion" rather than "roses = farewell" or "roses = sexual harassment"?
- **Neglect of production.** Semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (Strinati, p. 117); cultural signs are also commodities.
- **Neglect of consumption / audience.** "Meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (Strinati, p. 117). Hall's encoding/decoding is the corrective.
- **Ahistoricism.** Lévi-Straussian structuralism freezes the system synchronically and "reduces culture to a mental structure" (Strinati, p. 111), erasing class and history.
- **Crude theory of ideology.** Barthes reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, p. 117), which struggles with race, gender, colonial difference — Hall, feminist semiology and decolonial semiotics push back.
- **The "robbing myth of myth" problem.** Barthes himself: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth? All that is needed is to use it as the departure point for a third semiological chain, to take its signification as the first term of a second myth" (*Mythologies*, p. 135). The chain can go on infinitely — irony, parody, *Black Mirror*, culture jamming — which both opens space for resistance and threatens the diagnostic clarity of the original method.

## §6. Quick exam scaffolds

**The "is X mythological?" scaffold (2022-style):**
1. Define myth as third-order signification that naturalises history.
2. Pick two or three contemporary examples.
3. Apply denotation → connotation → myth.
4. Critique: production, audience, polysemy, polyvalence, ideology.
5. Verdict: how convincing is the diagnosis?

**The "do we need a theory of signs to understand capitalism?" scaffold (2023-style):**
1. State the question: is semiology necessary, or are political economy / class structure / commodity form sufficient?
2. Show what semiology adds (advertising, branding, cultural commodification, late-capitalist style cycles).
3. Show its limits (production, labour, class).
4. Argue for a Hall-style synthesis — semiology + Marxism + ideology theory.

**The "popular culture is ideological" scaffold (2021-style):**
1. Define Barthes's claim: myth is the form of bourgeois ideology because it transforms the historical into the natural.
2. Three concrete cases.
3. Strengths: it explains why power feels obvious.
4. Weaknesses: empirical validation, audience reception, reductive class theory.
5. Hall as a productive successor.

### 8.1 Past-Q 2023 (Q7) — Do we need a theory of signs and signification to understand capitalism?

I want to argue that we do need a theory of signs and signification to understand capitalism — but only a theory that has been reworked along Hall's lines. A pure semiology cannot grasp capitalism on its own, because capitalism is also a mode of production, a class structure and a circuit of accumulation. But a Marxism that ignores signs cannot explain why workers wear Nike, why my Instagram feed sells me Tesla, or why the brand "essence" of a soft drink can be sold for billions. The honest answer is that the sign and the commodity have become inseparable, and any serious account of contemporary capitalism therefore has to combine the two.

Begin with Saussure's account of the sign. Language is "not a transparent medium that reflects reality, but a structured system of signs, organised through difference and governed by rules that exist independently of individual intention" (lecture slides, citing Saussure's *Course in General Linguistics*, 1916). The signifier is the physical form (sound, mark, image, style); the signified is the mental concept. The relation between them is arbitrary, and meaning emerges from difference within a system — *langue* — rather than from things in the world. This is already politically useful, because it lets us see that "the British people" or "the free market" or "innovation" are signs, not natural objects. The political work of capitalism partly consists in making such signs feel obvious.

Barthes's notion of myth makes this point sharper. In *Mythologies*, he argues that signs work at two levels. Denotation is "the most obvious level… literal meaning"; connotation is the "cultural level… suggested or implied meaning"; myth is the "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders" (lecture slide, "Orders of Signification"). The function of myth is "to transform history into nature" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 140). Apple's "Think Different" denotes a slogan; connotes creativity and rebellion; mythologises consumer capitalism as self-realisation. The Citroën DS denotes a car; connotes seamless modernity; mythologises industrial production as magic. Coca-Cola's Christmas adverts denote a brand; connote warmth and family; mythologise a privatised Christmas as eternal tradition. Without a theory of signs we cannot diagnose any of this. We can describe Apple's profits but not why I want an iPhone.

Capitalism in its late or platform form has, if anything, made semiology more necessary. The lecture's slide on "Styles and significations in late capitalism / post-modernity" lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, asking what "further third order significations — knowingness, excess, parody, irony" they involve. TikTok trend cycles, Instagram aesthetics and brand "essence" (Tesla = innovation) only become legible once we treat them as semiological systems. Even Brexit — denotatively a referendum — is illegible without reading the Union Jack as a myth of sovereignty.

But — and this is where I follow Strinati and Hall — semiology on its own is not enough. Strinati notes that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117) and that "meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (p. 117). Barthes himself reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, p. 117) in a way that is theoretically thin: it does not specify which fraction of capital, which institutions, which audiences. The Paris-Match cover Barthes reads as "French imperiality" (*Mythologies*, p. 128) is also produced by a magazine industry, sold to particular readerships, and decoded variously by Black and white French citizens. Race, gender and colonial difference exceed Barthes's binary class frame.

Hall's reworking is what makes semiology adequate to capitalism. His encoding/decoding diagram (lecture slide, "Hall: building on structuralism?") restores the "media production and power" of the encoder and the "differential reception of the audience". A theory of signs is necessary because capitalism increasingly sells signs; a theory of capitalism is necessary because signs are produced by industries inside class relations. The honest synthesis is that we need both — Saussure to identify the structure, Barthes to read the myth, Hall to bring back power. The answer to the question is: yes, but only as one indispensable element in a wider critical sociology.

### 8.2 Past-Q 2022 (Q5) — Critically assess Barthes' argument that popular culture is "mythological".

Barthes's claim that popular culture is mythological is, in my view, both indispensable and over-stated. It is indispensable because he gives us the sharpest tool we have for seeing how popular-cultural signs naturalise specific historical arrangements. It is over-stated because, taken on its own, the argument under-determines questions of audience reception, institutional production and the polysemy of texts that Hall and Strinati raise.

Start with the argument itself. In *Mythologies*, Barthes describes myth as "a system of communication… a mode of signification" (p. 117), and insists that "the very principle of myth" is that "it transforms history into nature" (p. 140). His most famous case is the *Paris-Match* cover, where "a young Negro in a French uniform is saluting, with his eyes uplifted, probably fixed on a fold of the tricolour" (p. 125). Denotatively, this is a soldier saluting a flag. Connotatively, it is a "purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness". Mythically, it signifies "French imperiality" — "France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (pp. 125–126). The historical violence of French colonialism is laundered into a natural, obvious image of multi-racial loyalty. The wrestler's grimace, the wine bottle, the Citroën DS, the toys reviewed in *Mythologies* all work the same way. The lecture's third-order signification diagram captures the schema: denotative (literal), connotative (cultural), mythological (ideological worldview).

Applied to today's popular culture, this is enormously productive. Apple's "Think Different" turns commodity choice into rebellion. Coca-Cola's Christmas adverts turn corporate marketing into family tradition. Nike's "Just Do It" turns the structural impossibility of self-improvement under inequality into a problem of personal willpower. The Marlboro Man turned an addictive carcinogen into rugged masculine freedom — and the *Joe Chemo* parodies in the lecture slides perform exactly the third-order "robbery" Barthes invites when he writes that "since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (*Mythologies*, p. 135). Met Gala costumes, royal weddings, the Union Jack on Brexit, Tesla as "innovation", Disney princesses as gendered signs, even TikTok trend cycles all become legible as mythologies in this technical sense.

But the argument needs to be qualified on at least four fronts. First, the question of validation: Strinati notes that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (p. 114). When Barthes says roses signify passion, why not farewell, irony or sexual harassment? The semiologist's reading sometimes feels like literary criticism dressed as science. Second, production: Strinati objects that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117). A *Paris-Match* cover is also the output of a media industry inside a colonial state. Third, audience: Barthes treats "the myth-consumer" as essentially passive, taking "the signification for a system of facts" (*Mythologies*, p. 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model (lecture slide) corrects this by recognising "differential reception of the audience" with dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings. The same Disney princess can mean docile femininity to one viewer and an emancipation narrative to another. Fourth, the theory of ideology is crude: Barthes reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, p. 117), which under-specifies race, gender and colonial power.

There is also a deeper issue Barthes himself acknowledges in "Myth Today": once we accept that any signification can be the first term in a further chain, mythological readings can multiply infinitely. The lecture's slide on "Styles and significations in late capitalism" lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators and looksmaxxers, asking what "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" do as "further third order significations". Charlie Brooker's *Black Mirror* mythologises the mythologisers. This is theoretically generative but methodologically destabilising: at what point does the critic stop adding chains?

My assessment, then, is that the claim that popular culture is mythological is essentially correct as a diagnosis of a major mechanism of contemporary cultural power, and Barthes deserves credit for naming it. But it is incomplete. Without Hall on audiences, without political economy on production, without intersectional accounts of ideology, the mythological frame floats free of the social. Read alongside these, however, it remains one of the most powerful tools we have.

### 8.3 Past-Q 2021 (Q5) — What did Barthes mean when he argued that popular culture is ideological? How convincing do you find his approach?

When Barthes argues that popular culture is ideological, he is making a specific and quite technical claim, not just complaining about advertising. His claim is that the popular signs around us — magazine covers, wrestling matches, wine bottles, soap powders, cars, toys — operate at a third order of signification he calls myth, and that the function of myth is to convert the historically specific interests of a dominant class into apparently natural, timeless, obvious truths. Strinati paraphrases the political programme: semiology is "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature… the fundamental method of an ideological critique" (Barthes, in Strinati, p. 100). Or in Barthes's own words: "the very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (*Mythologies*, p. 140).

The mechanics matter. Saussure gives us the sign as signifier + signified, with an arbitrary relation between them and meaning arising from difference within *langue*. Barthes adds two levels. At denotation, a sign is literal: a photo of a Black soldier saluting a flag. At connotation, the sign acquires cultural associations: "Frenchness and militariness" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 125). At the third order, myth, the connotation hardens into an ideological worldview: "French imperiality… France is a great Empire" (pp. 125–126). The historical truth of French colonial violence becomes the natural truth of a loyal multi-racial empire. Popular culture is ideological in Barthes's sense not because it lies, but because it makes specific historical arrangements feel like the way things are.

Take a few contemporary instances. The Union Jack on a Brexit campaign denotes a flag, connotes sovereignty and Britishness, mythologises a "natural" sovereign people whose interests transcend politics. Nike's "Just Do It" denotes a slogan, connotes athleticism, mythologises neoliberal self-improvement as if structural inequality were just a personal will problem. Disney princesses denote characters, connote femininity, mythologise an "eternal statute of womanhood" where, as Barthes writes, "women are on the earth to give children to men" (*Mythologies*, p. 56). Coca-Cola's Christmas adverts mythologise a privatised, commodified Christmas as eternal tradition. The Marlboro Man mythologised addiction as freedom — and the *Joe Chemo* parody in the lecture's slide deck (slides 32–33) shows that the chain can be robbed back: "since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 135).

How convincing is this? Quite convincing as a description of a real and important mechanism, but limited as a complete theory of popular culture, and limited as a theory of ideology in particular. Four caveats.

First, validation. Strinati's standing objection is that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (p. 114). The semiologist's eye is not obviously more reliable than another educated reader's.

Second, production. Cultural signs are produced inside industries. *Paris-Match* has shareholders, editors, image rights and a particular market. Strinati notes that "semiology… tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117). A serious analysis of capitalism's ideological power has to add political economy.

Third, audiences. Barthes pictures the consumer of myth as essentially passive, taking signification "for a system of facts" (*Mythologies*, p. 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model — the lecture slide "Hall: building on structuralism?" — recognises that the same encoded message can be decoded in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways, depending on the audience's frameworks of knowledge. Black readers of the Paris-Match cover were not, on the whole, beautifully duped by French imperiality.

Fourth, the theory of ideology is too narrow. Barthes reduces myth to bourgeois class interest; Strinati calls this "fairly crude" (p. 117). Race, gender, sexuality and colonial difference cut across class in ways Barthes's frame struggles to articulate. Feminist semiology and decolonial readings have stretched the toolkit further than Barthes himself.

So Barthes is right that popular culture is ideological in his precise sense — it naturalises history. He is half-right about how. Plugged into Hall on encoding/decoding, into political economy on production, and into feminist and decolonial readings on the texture of power, his approach remains one of the indispensable tools for analysing the cultural surface of contemporary capitalism. On its own, it is brilliant but partial. With these companions, it is essential.

### 8.4 Mock 1 — Explain what Saussure means by the arbitrariness of the sign, and assess its usefulness for analysing popular culture.

When Saussure argues that the sign is arbitrary, he is making the foundational move that allows cultural studies to exist at all. The claim is that the relation between signifier (the physical form: sound, mark, image) and signified (the mental concept) is not natural or motivated. "There is no intrinsic, natural or essential reason why a particular concept should be linked with one sound image rather than another" (Strinati, p. 83). The English "dog" and the French "chien" point to the same concept because each language's system of differences fixes the link; nothing in the animal demands either sound. Meaning, in this picture, is not a property of things in the world. It is a property of the *langue*: the structured system of differences that allows us to say "dog" rather than "god".

The lecture slide on Saussure puts the point sharply: language is "a structured system of signs, organised through difference and governed by rules that exist independently of individual intention". This already implies a programme. If language works this way, perhaps fashion, food, advertising, film, news and popular culture work the same way. Saussure suggests as much: linguistics is a "sub-branch" of a general "science which studies the life of signs within society" (Strinati, p. 85). The whole project of semiology is licensed by this little phrase.

So what does arbitrariness do for the analysis of popular culture? Three things. First, it lets us *denaturalise* taken-for-granted signs. The colour red on traffic lights means "stop" not because red is intrinsically stop-coloured but because the highway-code system fixes the difference between red, amber and green. By extension, the suit means "professional", the rose means "passion", the Union Jack means "Britishness" only because of culturally and historically specific systems of signs. Recognising this is the first step in any critical reading. Second, it makes *paradigmatic and syntagmatic analysis* possible: we can ask what other signifier could occupy the same slot ("paradigm") and what the sequential combination of signs does ("syntagm"). A football shirt swapped from Nike to Adidas (paradigm) changes the connotation; a Coca-Cola advert sequencing snow, fireplace, family and bottle (syntagm) builds the Christmas myth. Third, it underpins Barthes's later work. Without the arbitrariness of the sign, denotation, connotation and myth do not get off the ground; the whole point of "myth transforms history into nature" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 140) is that signs that feel natural are in fact arbitrary cultural inheritances.

The usefulness in concrete cases is striking. Disney princesses denote characters and connote femininity, but the link between "princess" and "passive, obedient, romantic" is an arbitrary cultural fixing — feminist readings of Disney get traction precisely by treating those connotations as denaturalisable. The Marlboro Man denoted a cowboy and connoted masculine freedom; arbitrariness allowed *Joe Chemo* to short-circuit the chain. Apple's "Think Different" works only because the system of brand differentiation fixes Apple as "creative" against PC's "corporate". Instagram aesthetics, the "clean girl" feed, looksmaxxers and roadmen are systems of arbitrary signifiers that nonetheless feel obvious to those inside them.

But there are limits. Strinati and others have argued that even Saussure exaggerates arbitrariness. Some signs are iconic (resemble what they signify, like a photograph) or indexical (caused by what they signify, like smoke for fire). The semiotics infographic in the lecture slides distinguishes symbolic, iconic and indexical signs. Iconic signs are not arbitrary in the same way. Moreover, in culture proper Saussure's claim is already softened. Barthes notes that "mythical signification… is never arbitrary; it is always in part motivated" (*Mythologies*, p. 136). Once a sign carries connotation and myth, it is no longer free-floating: the historical interests of class, gender and empire motivate which signifiers carry which mythologies.

The pragmatic upshot is that arbitrariness is a starting heuristic, not the final word. It lets us treat any cultural sign as potentially denaturalisable, which is enormous gain. But the analyst then has to ask the further question Hall presses: who motivated this specific signifier-signified pairing, in which institutional context, and how do real audiences decode it? Saussure opens the door; Barthes walks through it; Hall keeps it from swinging shut again. As a single foundational concept, however, the arbitrariness of the sign remains the most useful entry point that the sociology of culture has.

### 8.5 Mock 2 — Using the techniques of semiology, analyse a contemporary advertising campaign of your choice.

I take Apple's long-running "Think Different" campaign as my object, because it lets me run the full semiological apparatus — signifier, signified, denotation, connotation, myth, paradigm and syntagm, and finally a Hall-style audience-and-production critique — across a single, well-known case.

The campaign launched in 1997 with a black-and-white television spot and print series featuring figures such as Einstein, Gandhi, Picasso, Martin Luther King and John Lennon, captioned with variants of the slogan "Think Different". My analysis treats each spot as a layered sign system.

At the level of *denotation*, what we see is straightforward: black-and-white archival photographs of cultural icons, the word-cluster "Think Different", and the Apple rainbow (later monochrome) logo. The lecture's "Orders of Signification" slide labels this first-order level "most obvious… literal meaning". Most viewers can describe what they see.

At the level of *connotation*, things get richer. Einstein's hair connotes genius; Gandhi's stillness connotes moral authority; King's oratory connotes courage. The shared monochrome treatment connotes historical seriousness and timelessness. "Different" connotes non-conformity, creativity, refusal. The Apple logo, juxtaposed with these figures, connotes inclusion in their company: to buy Apple is to be associated with rebellion-genius. The slide on "Denotation / Connotation" using the Hugo Boss image makes the analogous move (the suit not only denotes a garment, it connotes professionalism, success, authority). With Apple, the suit becomes the laptop.

At the level of *myth*, the campaign performs the operation Barthes describes: "the very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (*Mythologies*, p. 140). The myth here is that consumer choice is self-actualisation; that picking a brand is morally and creatively continuous with the work of Gandhi or King; that capitalism, far from constraining the rebel, is the rebel's natural medium. The myth flatters the consumer and dehistoricises the activists, who become not figures embedded in specific political struggles but free-floating icons of "thinking differently". This is third-order signification: "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders" (lecture slide).

A paradigmatic analysis sharpens the reading. Apple could have substituted business titans (paradigm rejected: too corporate) or sports stars (paradigm rejected: not "different" enough in 1997). The chosen paradigm — political and cultural dissidents — is precisely calibrated. Syntagmatically, the sequence of images, slogan and logo binds them: image of dissident → words "Think Different" → Apple logo. The dissident's prestige flows along the syntagm into the brand.

Now the Hall-style critique. First, *production*: this campaign was made by TBWA\Chiat\Day under direction from a returned Steve Jobs, aimed at rescuing Apple from near-bankruptcy. The institutional context is corporate self-rescue, not actual support for any of the named causes. Strinati's complaint about semiology — that it "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117) — would be a fatal omission if we stopped at myth. Second, *audience*: Hall's encoding/decoding model (lecture slide) tells me to ask what dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings the campaign enables. A dominant reading buys the brand-as-rebellion myth. A negotiated reading enjoys the aesthetic while half-recognising the irony. An oppositional reading, common in subsequent culture jamming, treats the ads as exhibit A for the absorption of dissent into capital. Third, *politics of representation*: the campaign reads the dissident archive selectively — King without the radical economic critique, Gandhi without colonial specificity. Decolonial and feminist semiotics would press on the racial and gender politics of who counts as iconically "different".

Finally, the third-order chain runs further. Subsequent parodies (the *Mac/PC* spoof versions, online memes, *Black Mirror*-style critique) take the Apple myth as the first term in a new chain — exactly Barthes's "use it as the departure point for a third semiological chain" (*Mythologies*, p. 135). The mythologisers are mythologised; brand "essence" itself becomes a meme.

What semiology gives me here is a way of seeing how an advertising campaign does cultural work that bare sales figures cannot reveal. What semiology cannot give me alone is the political economy of Apple's labour conditions or the actual demographics of who buys Macs and why. The honest reading therefore combines a Barthesian myth-analysis with Hall on encoding/decoding and a political-economic account of the brand. Together, the techniques of semiology let me say something precise about how "Think Different" sells sameness as difference.

### 8.6 Mock 3 — Compare Lévi-Strauss's structuralism with Barthes's semiology. Which is more useful for studying popular culture today?

Lévi-Strauss's structuralism and Barthes's semiology share an inheritance from Saussure and a commitment to reading culture as a system of signs rather than a window onto things. But they part company on universality, on history, and on politics — and the second of those differences is the one that matters most for studying popular culture today. My short answer: Barthes is more useful, but only when reworked.

Lévi-Strauss takes the Saussurean framework and turns it into a programme for finding universal mental structures behind diverse cultural surfaces. As Strinati puts it, Lévi-Strauss's "version of structuralism is concerned with uncovering the common structural principles underlying specific and historically variable cultures and myths. These structural principles involve the logical and universal characteristics of the human mind" (p. 87). His method works through binary oppositions — nature/culture, raw/cooked, sacred/profane — and seeks the invariant transformations across myths. Totemism, Oedipus, the Iroquois owl-riddle: behind their empirical variety, "the same correlation between riddles and incest exists among peoples separated by history, geography, language and culture" (Lévi-Strauss, in Strinati, p. 92). The point is to display "the universality of human nature" (in Strinati, p. 92).

Barthes inherits the relational sign but turns it toward the *historical surface*. Semiology, on his account, is the "close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature" (in Strinati, p. 100). Where Lévi-Strauss reads myth to find the eternal mind, Barthes reads myth to expose how a specific class and a specific history get smuggled in under cover of "nature". The function of myth, he insists, is to "transform history into nature" (*Mythologies*, p. 140). The lecture's third-order signification diagram captures the schema: denotation, connotation, myth.

Three contrasts are decisive for our period.

First, *history*. Lévi-Strauss is, on Strinati's reading, fundamentally ahistorical: his "almost exclusive concern appears to be with synchronic analysis, uncovering the hidden and unconscious mental structure" (p. 112). Barthes is unrelentingly historical: every myth he reads is dated, situated, classed. For contemporary popular culture — which churns through trend cycles, brand reinventions, viral moments — Barthes's historical sensitivity is far more usable.

Second, *politics*. Lévi-Strauss is not in any direct sense a critic of power. Barthes is: his semiology is meant as an "ideological critique" (in Strinati, p. 100), even if his theory of ideology remains thin. For a discipline that wants to understand brand capitalism, gendered representation, racialised iconography and political imagery, Barthes's bias is the right bias.

Third, *examples*. Lévi-Strauss works on pre-industrial myths and kinship systems. Barthes works on wrestling, wine, soap, the Citroën DS, magazine covers, toys — exactly the cultural surface that "popular culture today" still resembles. The lecture slides walk from Saussure's tree to Barthes's wrestler to a Hugo Boss advert to a Jeep advert to an O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign. The line of descent is from Barthes, not from Lévi-Strauss.

That said, Lévi-Strauss is not useless. His insistence that texts must be read in structural unity — not as isolated empirical items — survives in contemporary genre and narrative analysis. Eco's structuralist reading of the Bond novels (Strinati, pp. 93–98) shows how an invariant scheme of moves (M assigns Bond a task; villain appears; Bond and villain fence; woman appears; Bond takes woman; villain captures Bond; Bond beats villain) accounts for popular success across the franchise. Today's franchise media — Marvel, Disney+ serials, K-pop universes, the Harry Potter fandom (named on the lecture's "Styles in late capitalism" slide) — are saturated with this kind of invariant narrative scaffolding. A Lévi-Straussian eye is good at seeing why these feel familiar.

But the deepest objections — empirical validation, audience, production — apply more harshly to Lévi-Strauss than to Barthes, because he claims more. Strinati: structuralism's "concept of an underlying and unconscious structure for its explanatory power is clearly liable to underestimate the significance of the role of the audience" (p. 96). Hall's encoding/decoding model addresses this on the Barthes side; there is no obvious Lévi-Straussian equivalent.

My verdict, then, is that Barthes is the more useful starting point for studying popular culture today — because his historicity, his political edge and his examples are closer to our object. But Lévi-Strauss's eye for invariant narrative structure remains a useful supplement, and Hall's encoding/decoding model is the necessary third leg. All three, together, beat any one alone.

### 8.7 Mock 4 — How convincing is Barthes's claim that the function of myth is "to transform history into nature"?

I find Barthes's claim that myth functions to transform history into nature highly convincing as a diagnosis of a specific, real and recurring mechanism in cultural life — but only partly convincing as a complete theory of how popular culture works. The diagnosis names something genuine; the theory under-specifies how the trick gets pulled off, who pulls it, and on whom.

Begin with the claim. In *Mythologies*, Barthes writes that "the very principle of myth: it transforms history into nature" (p. 140). Myth is "a system of communication… a mode of signification… a type of speech" (p. 117). It is a "second-order semiological system" that takes an existing sign (a photo of a Black soldier saluting a flag, say) and recruits it as the first term of a new chain whose ultimate effect is to make a culturally specific arrangement appear "natural and inevitable" (Strinati, p. 106). The Paris-Match cover that Barthes reads in the barber's shop is the textbook case: "All this is the meaning of the picture. But… I see very well what it signifies to me: that France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (*Mythologies*, pp. 125–126). The historical specificity of French colonial domination disappears into the timelessness of "imperiality".

The claim is convincing first because it is empirically corroborated across almost any popular-cultural surface we choose. Coca-Cola's Christmas adverts naturalise a commercial Christmas. Royal weddings naturalise the monarchy as community ritual. The Union Jack on a Brexit poster naturalises the British nation as a pre-political fact. Nike's "Just Do It" naturalises self-improvement under inequality. Disney princesses naturalise "an eternal statute of womanhood" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 56). The Marlboro Man naturalised cigarettes as masculine freedom; the *Joe Chemo* parodies (lecture slides) show how the unmasking works by re-historicising the sign. National anthem rituals at sporting events naturalise the nation as an organic community. In each case, what was historically constructed (colonialism, monarchy, capital, patriarchy, addiction, the nation) is dressed in the clothes of nature.

The claim is convincing second because it is methodologically usable. The lecture's third-order signification diagram makes the move teachable: denotation (most obvious, literal), connotation (cultural, suggested), myth (ideological worldview). Students can be given a Hugo Boss advert, a Jeep advert, the O2 "Wear the Rose" rugby campaign, and run the three-order analysis. Barthes himself worked the same way across wrestling, wine, soap and toys.

But the claim is only partly convincing because it under-determines three things.

First, the *agent of mythification*. Barthes tends to attribute mythologies to "the bourgeoisie" — a class which, he writes, "has obliterated its name in passing from reality to representation" (*Mythologies*, p. 150). Strinati complains that the resulting theory of ideology is "fairly crude" (p. 117). Whose specific interests, in which institutions, are doing the naturalising? Sometimes it is corporations; sometimes states; sometimes audiences themselves. The mythologising agent needs more granularity than "the bourgeoisie".

Second, the *mechanism on audiences*. Barthes pictures the consumer as passive, taking "the signification for a system of facts" (*Mythologies*, p. 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model (lecture slide) suggests this is too quick. Audiences decode within their own frameworks of knowledge, and produce dominant, negotiated and oppositional readings. Many viewers of the Paris-Match cover did not in fact accept the imperial myth.

Third, the *infinity problem*. Barthes himself acknowledges that "since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth?" (*Mythologies*, p. 135). Third-order chains can be turned into fourth-order chains. The lecture's slide on "Styles and significations in late capitalism" lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, and asks what "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" do as further significations. If myth can always be robbed, the diagnostic clarity of "history → nature" is destabilised: post-modern culture often *performs* its own historicity ironically while still feeling natural to its participants.

The honest assessment is therefore: yes, Barthes is right that one major function of popular-cultural signs is to transform history into nature, and his slogan names it cleanly. But the full explanation of how this happens, with what effects, and against what resistances requires Hall on encoding/decoding, political-economic accounts of production, and feminist and decolonial readings of which histories get naturalised. The slogan is a stage one diagnosis; the analysis takes more.

### 8.8 Mock 5 — "Semiology tells us nothing about power." Discuss.

The provocation that semiology tells us nothing about power is too strong, but it has a real grain of truth, and it is exactly the grain that Stuart Hall's reworking is designed to handle. Semiology gives us a great deal about *one face* of power — the cultural-symbolic face by which ideologies naturalise themselves — and very little about other faces unless we add political economy and audience research to it. The honest answer is "semiology tells us about one register of power well, and is silent on others".

The case for the prosecution. Semiology, in its founding moments, looks like a method for decoding signs, not a theory of power. Saussure's project is to identify *langue* as a system of differences. Lévi-Strauss extends this to universal mental structures behind kinship and myth. Eco, in his structuralist reading of the Bond novels, uncovers "the invariant rules governing the narrative structure of these novels" (Strinati, p. 93). None of these moves names class, capital, the state, gender or race directly. Strinati's criticism of semiology is precisely that it "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117) and that "meaning is not a quality of the sign itself but of the social relationships in which it can be located" (p. 117). If power lives in production relations and institutional control, semiology missing those is missing power.

The case for the defence. Barthes's semiology, even in *Mythologies*, is explicitly a tool of ideological critique. He frames the project as "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature… the fundamental method of an ideological critique" (in Strinati, p. 100). When he reads the Paris-Match cover, what he sees is *imperial* power: "France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag" (*Mythologies*, pp. 125–126). The "eternal statute of womanhood" he detects in the women-novelists-with-children photograph is *gendered* power: women "are on the earth to give children to men" (*Mythologies*, p. 56). To say semiology tells us nothing about power is to ignore the fact that Barthes's whole project is to denaturalise the cultural surface of power.

The lecture's "Orders of Signification" slide makes the political stakes explicit: the third order is the "ideological level… worldview that is reinforced by other orders". Worldviews are political. Disney princesses, the Marlboro Man, the Union Jack on Brexit posters, royal weddings, Tesla as "innovation", the Coca-Cola Christmas advert, Nike's "Just Do It", Met Gala costumes — read semiologically, each becomes a small machine for naturalising specific power relations (gender, addiction, nationalism, monarchy, neoliberalism, capital).

But the defence has to concede ground. Semiology on its own is weaker on three things power-analysts care about. First, *production*: who makes the signs, with what budgets, inside what corporate and state structures? A Tesla ad is also a Tesla supply chain; a *Paris-Match* cover is also a newsroom inside a colonial state. Second, *audiences*: who decodes and how? Barthes assumes "the myth-consumer takes the signification for a system of facts" (*Mythologies*, p. 142). Hall's encoding/decoding model insists audiences can produce dominant, negotiated or oppositional readings (lecture slide "Hall: building on structuralism?"). Many Black readers of the Paris-Match cover read it oppositionally. Third, *institutional power*: police, courts, schools, platforms. Semiology has no direct purchase on these. The Frankfurt School's account of the culture industry, Habermas on the public sphere, Foucault on disciplinary power and Hall on the encoding/decoding circuit all add what semiology lacks.

So my position is this. The provocation is wrong as a flat claim — semiology gives us the sharpest tool we have for analysing how ideological power gets its naturalising work done at the level of signs. The Charlie Brooker *Black Mirror* counter-mythologisations, the *Joe Chemo* parodies, and the "robbing myth of myth" move (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 135) are themselves political. But the provocation is right as a warning against semiological imperialism. Power is also material, institutional and embodied. The synthesis is Hall: semiology + Marxism + ideology theory + sociology. Read like that, semiology tells us about half of power, which is half more than most disciplines manage. The other half we get from the rest of the sociology curriculum.

### 8.9 Mock 6 — Critically assess Stuart Hall's reworking of structuralism.

Stuart Hall's reworking of structuralism is, in my view, the move that rescues semiology from being a clever but politically thin reading practice and turns it into a usable sociology of culture. The lecture slide titled "Hall: building on structuralism?" frames the question well: Hall does not abandon Saussure or Barthes; he keeps the signifier/signified machinery, but he wires it into media production, institutional power and audience reception. The teacher's summary makes the same point: "Structuralism is extremely powerful at explaining how meaning is organised, but it is much less good at explaining power and inequality in concrete social contexts, media institutions and production and audience interpretation and resistance… he reworks it, combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology" (teacher's summary).

To see why this matters, recall what Hall is reworking. Saussure gives us the relational sign and the system of differences. Barthes layers denotation, connotation and myth onto it, with myth functioning to "transform history into nature" (*Mythologies*, p. 140). Lévi-Straussian structuralism finds invariant binary oppositions behind diverse cultural surfaces. Strinati's critique is repeated: semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117) and "neglects the social relationships in which signs are produced and consumed" (p. 117). On the production side, semiology has little to say about *Paris-Match*'s editorial economy. On the consumption side, Barthes's "myth-consumer takes the signification for a system of facts" (*Mythologies*, p. 142) is a passive picture.

Hall's encoding/decoding diagram (lecture slide) is the corrective. The slide shows a "Visual / Aural — signifier" passing into "encoding meaning structures 1" inside frameworks of knowledge, relations of production and technical infrastructure; then the "programme as meaningful discourse"; then "decoding meaning structures 2" inside the audience's own frameworks of knowledge, relations of production and technical infrastructure. Two arrows mark the lecture's emphasis: "Media production and power" on the encoding side, "Differential reception of the audience" on the decoding side. The model recognises three audience positions — dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, oppositional — which Strinati implicitly invokes when he insists that audiences "play in arriving at interpretations of popular culture" (p. 117).

What does Hall's reworking gain? Three things. First, it *re-historicises* meaning. Every encoded message sits inside a specific institutional moment with specific relations of production. Second, it *re-empowers* audiences. The same Disney princess can be decoded as docile femininity (dominant), as a guilty pleasure (negotiated), or as an object of feminist critique (oppositional). The same Union Jack on Brexit can be read as patriotic, as ironic, or as racially exclusionary. Third, it brings *ideology* back into focus as a sociological process, not a metaphysical category. Hall's later work on Thatcherism, on race and on the media show how encoded meanings struggle in real time inside political conjunctures.

But the reworking is not without its own limits.

First, the model can underspecify *how* differential reception actually happens. Saying audiences decode "within their frameworks of knowledge" is true but slightly tautological without empirical research into specific audiences. Critics have pushed reception studies to fill this in.

Second, Hall keeps a structuralist residue. The encoder still encodes a "meaningful discourse"; the audience decodes from it. Post-structuralist critics (Foucault, later Barthes himself) would press on whether there is a stable meaning at all to encode and decode, given the polysemy of texts. Barthes's "death of the author" and the polysemic text suggest meaning is always already plural. Hall's three-position model risks tidying that plurality.

Third, the model is best at *broadcast-era* media: a programme is encoded, sent through a channel, decoded by viewers. Platform capitalism complicates this. On TikTok, encoders and decoders are often the same people, trend cycles are co-produced, and "differential reception" is itself a piece of the next viral encoding. The lecture's slide on "Styles and significations in late capitalism" — roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers — points to a media environment where third-order signification proliferates in real time, partly outside the Hallian schema.

Fourth, the reworking sometimes leans on Marxism in ways that may not fit every register of difference. Race, gender, sexuality and colonial power require more than class-and-ideology; intersectional and decolonial readings have extended Hall in productive directions.

My assessment is therefore strongly positive but qualified: Hall's reworking is the indispensable step that makes structuralism socially serious, but it needs further updating for platform media and intersectional power. As a hinge between Saussure-Barthes on the one side and contemporary cultural sociology on the other, it is unrivalled.

### 8.10 Mock 7 — Why did the "linguistic turn" become so important to the sociology of media and culture in the 1960s–70s?

The linguistic turn became important to the sociology of media and culture in the 1960s and 1970s because it gave the discipline a usable, supple and politically charged set of tools precisely at the moment when the media environment was expanding and existing theoretical paradigms were running into trouble. Strinati's framing is useful here: structuralism and semiology "benefited from the increasing interest in theory taken by social science in the 1960s, and their reputation is said to owe something to their concern with societies which are increasingly inundated with popular culture" (p. 80). The lecture's "Rethinking Modernity" map situates structuralism alongside Marxism, the Frankfurt School, British Cultural Studies and feminism in exactly this period.

Three pressures converged to make the linguistic turn attractive.

First, the *empirical pressure* of a saturated media environment. By the 1960s, advertising, television, magazine culture, popular music and film were everywhere. Existing approaches were ill-equipped. The Culture and Civilisation tradition (Arnold, Leavis) treated popular culture as a debasement of high culture; the early Frankfurt School treated it as the output of a manipulative culture industry; British Cultural Studies (Hoggart, Williams, Thompson) treated it sympathetically but lacked a precise method for decoding texts. Saussure's relational sign and Barthes's three orders of signification offered a method that could be applied at the level of the individual image or text — wrestling matches, wine, the Citroën DS, soap powders, the Paris-Match cover — without either dismissing it or simply documenting it.

Second, the *theoretical pressure* of a wider shift toward systems and structures. Lévi-Strauss in anthropology, Althusser in Marxism, Lacan in psychoanalysis, early Foucault in the history of ideas: each was, in different registers, moving away from the autonomous individual subject toward structures, languages and discourses that shape what subjects can think. As Strinati paraphrases Lévi-Strauss, "the so-called 'collective consciousness' would, in the final analysis, be no more than the expression… of certain time and space modalities of the universal laws which make up the unconscious activity of the mind" (in Strinati, pp. 88–89). For media sociology, this was good news: it meant audiences and producers alike could be theorised as inside systems of signs they did not invent.

Third, the *political pressure* of 1968 and after. Strinati notes that Barthes's project was framed as "the close analysis of the processes of meaning by which the bourgeoisie converts its historical class-culture into universal nature" (in Strinati, p. 100). To denaturalise the cultural surface — the flag, the family, the suit, the brand, the empire — was to do politics in a register the 1960s student left could recognise. *Mythologies* (1957) thus aged well into the late 1960s, and Stuart Hall's CCCS at Birmingham picked it up directly. The lecture's "Rethinking Modernity" diagram shows structuralism feeding both into British Cultural Studies and into post-structuralism (Foucault) and post-modernism (Lyotard, Baudrillard).

The fit with media and culture was particularly snug. Television and advertising had become the medium through which ideology now arrived, and television was an obviously *coded* medium: shot/reverse-shot, voice-over, music, brand, slogan, all readable as paradigmatic and syntagmatic choices. Hall's encoding/decoding model, formalised at Birmingham in the 1970s, used exactly this language. The lecture's slide on Hall makes the move explicit: a "Visual / Aural — signifier" is encoded by an industry inside "frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, technical infrastructure" and decoded by an audience with its own analogous frameworks. The linguistic turn delivered the vocabulary; media sociology delivered the institutional questions.

There were costs as well as gains, of course, and the discipline noticed them at the time. Empirical validation of semiological readings remained slippery: Strinati's complaint that "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (p. 114) was already alive in the period. The reduction of ideology to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, p. 117) struggled with race and gender. Audience reception was under-theorised until Hall and his students rebuilt it.

But the underlying point holds: in a decade when media and culture were becoming the central terrain of social experience, the linguistic turn supplied a method for treating that terrain analytically. Without it, contemporary media sociology — from the analysis of advertising to encoding/decoding to studies of branding, platforms, identity politics and post-modern culture — would have lacked its central toolkit. The lecture's flowchart of theoretical schools shows structuralism feeding forward into post-structuralism and intersecting with cultural studies. That feeding-forward is precisely the legacy of the 1960s–70s turn, and it explains why the period was decisive.

### 8.11 Mock 8 — What does Barthes's reading of the Paris-Match cover tell us about race, empire and cultural signs?

Barthes's reading of the Paris-Match cover is one of the foundational case studies of cultural semiology, and it tells us more about race, empire and cultural signs together than almost any other short passage in twentieth-century theory. It also exposes both the strengths and the limits of Barthes's frame on questions of racial power.

The scene is famous. "I am at the barber's, and a copy of Paris-Match is offered to me. On the cover, a young Negro in a French uniform is saluting, with his eyes uplifted, probably fixed on a fold of the tricolour" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 125). Denotatively, that is what we see: a Black soldier saluting a flag. Connotatively, Barthes diagnoses "a purposeful mixture of Frenchness and militariness" (p. 125). Mythically, the sign signifies "French imperiality" — "that France is a great Empire, that all her sons, without any colour discrimination, faithfully serve under her flag, and that there is no better answer to the detractors of an alleged colonialism than the zeal shown by this Negro in serving his so-called oppressors" (pp. 125–126).

What does this tell us, first, about cultural signs in general? It tells us that signs work in layers and that the political work of culture happens at the third layer. The Black soldier is not "made" to mean French imperiality by anyone's individual intention; the meaning is built by the system. The soldier's specific history — colonial recruitment, racial hierarchy, the violence of empire — is what Barthes calls the "form" of the myth. That form has been "taken from one system, his real history" and "placed in another system, that of the myth, which denies his history and culture, and thus the real history of French colonial exploitation" (Strinati, p. 105). The cultural sign performs an act of historical theft.

What does this tell us about *empire*? It tells us that empire reproduces itself culturally not by openly defending colonisation but by displaying compliant racialised bodies in patriotic poses. The myth is not "France is right to colonise" — that would be politically vulnerable. The myth is "look at this good Negro who salutes just like one of our boys" (Barthes, *Mythologies*, p. 134) — a much harder target, because it presents itself as a simple human fact rather than a political argument. Empire's strongest cultural defence is its capacity to make itself look like the absence of empire.

What does this tell us about *race*? Two things. First, race is mediated through cultural signs whose effects are not reducible to explicit racial language. The cover does not say "Black people support empire". It shows it. The visual sign does political work that an explicit statement could not. Second, the analysis exposes a recurring move in racial myth-making: the racialised subject is honoured (as a "son" of France) precisely on condition that his racialised history is erased. This is what Strinati paraphrases as "impoverishment of meaning" (p. 105). The soldier's individual history is impoverished so that the imperial myth can be enriched.

But the reading also exposes the limits of Barthes's frame on race. Three should be flagged.

First, Barthes's theoretical agent is "the bourgeoisie" (Strinati, p. 117). Race in the colonial context is irreducible to class. The myth of imperiality serves a particular racial-colonial formation in which French citizenship was hierarchically organised by colour, and class is one but only one axis.

Second, audience differentiation. Barthes assumes the reader will receive the myth as natural. Hall's encoding/decoding model insists Black French readers, anti-colonial activists and white Parisians decoded the cover differently. Many oppositional readings were already alive in 1955. The myth was contested.

Third, the legacy in our time. Contemporary examples — Apple's "Think Different" with Gandhi and King, Coca-Cola's diverse Christmas adverts, the Union Jack on Brexit, royal weddings with carefully managed multi-racial guest lists — all rehearse the same move Barthes named: racial inclusion as the visible alibi for political continuity. Charlie Brooker's *Black Mirror* episodes that play with the iconography of state and corporate diversity perform Barthes's "robbing myth of myth" (*Mythologies*, p. 135) on exactly this template.

Barthes's reading of the Paris-Match cover therefore tells us that race, empire and cultural signs interlock through myth's naturalising work; that the political defence of empire runs through aesthetics, not argument; and that the diagnosis still travels. To complete it, we need Hall on audiences and decolonial readings on the specifics of racial power. Barthes opens the door; the rest of the literature walks through.

### 8.12 Mock 9 — Are audiences "myth-consumers" or active decoders? Discuss with reference to Barthes and Hall.

The honest sociological answer is that audiences are *both*, and the interesting empirical question is when and how each picture applies. Barthes's "myth-consumer" describes a real phenomenon — the moment of taking a coded sign as a natural fact — and Hall's "active decoder" describes another real phenomenon — the moment of recognising, negotiating or resisting the coding. The strongest theoretical position holds both at once.

Barthes's picture is sharp. In *Mythologies*, he writes that the reader is allowed "to consume myth innocently" because "he does not see it as a semiological system but as an inductive one"; "the signifier and the signified have, in his eyes, a natural relationship"; "the myth-consumer takes the signification for a system of facts: myth is read as a factual system, whereas it is but a semiological system" (p. 142). This is a particular *phenomenology of reception*: the audience takes the soldier on the *Paris-Match* cover as just a fact about loyalty, not as an artefact of imperial myth-making. The same logic applies when contemporary audiences see the Coca-Cola Christmas advert as just a heart-warming seasonal scene, the Marlboro Man as just a cool image of masculinity, or the Union Jack on a Brexit poster as just a flag.

The "myth-consumer" picture captures the genuine phenomenon of cultural fluency without reflexivity. We are competent users of mythologies long before we are critical readers of them. Disney princesses register as romantic before they register as gendered. Apple's "Think Different" feels like an invitation to creativity before it feels like brand positioning. The Marlboro Man feels like freedom before it feels like the engine of mass lung-cancer mortality. Barthes is right that one important mode of audiencing is the unreflective consumption of the natural-seeming.

Hall's picture is the necessary corrective. The lecture slide "Hall: building on structuralism?" shows decoding happening "within frameworks of knowledge, relations of production, technical infrastructure" on the audience side, with the result that the same encoded message can be read in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways. The teacher's summary captures Hall's logic: he was "deeply influenced by Saussure and Barthes" but reworked them by "combining semiology with Marxism, ideology theory, and sociology". The audience is not blank; it brings class, race, gender, generation, subcultural position, technical literacy and political experience to the act of reading. Black readers of the Paris-Match cover did not, on the whole, accept French imperiality. Working-class teenagers parodied the Marlboro Man long before public health did. *Black Mirror* viewers consume contemporary mythologies precisely as mythologies.

Strinati's critique sharpens Hall's point against Barthes. Semiology, he argues, "does not therefore take account of how people actually interpret myth, for if myths are so effectively mystifying, how can they be so easily demystified?" (p. 107). If readers were really pure myth-consumers, no critical reading would ever surface. The fact that students in an SC301 seminar can sit and unpick the Hugo Boss advert, the Jeep advert, or Apple's "Think Different" shows that the active-decoder picture is also true.

The synthesis is straightforward. The same audience moves between the two positions depending on context, mood, attention and competence. I watch a Coca-Cola Christmas advert and feel the warmth (myth-consumer); I then teach it on Monday morning and see the commodification of family (active decoder). Many TikTok and Instagram users hold both positions simultaneously — the "Styles in late capitalism" slide lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers and notes that their cultures are saturated with "knowingness, excess, parody, irony" as further third-order significations. Post-modern audiences are routinely ironised consumers of their own mythologies.

There are limits on both sides. The myth-consumer picture risks condescension: it treats audiences as dupes, which is empirically false and politically demobilising. The active-decoder picture risks complacency: if every audience is always already critical, ideology has no work to do, which is also empirically false. The right position is in between, and the empirical research — reception studies in cultural studies after Hall — has spent fifty years filling in when each pole dominates and why.

My conclusion is therefore that Barthes is right that audiences sometimes consume myth as nature, Hall is right that audiences also actively decode within their own frameworks, and the live sociological project is to specify the conditions under which one mode prevails over the other. The most powerful contemporary popular-cultural artefacts — the Marvel franchise, K-pop universes, the Harry Potter fandom, the Met Gala — work by inviting audiences into both modes at once.

### 8.13 Mock 10 — "In late capitalism, third-order chains of signification have no end." Discuss.

The slogan that in late capitalism third-order chains of signification have no end is half-right and half-misleading, and getting which half is which is the substance of the answer. The lecture's slide titled "Third order chains of signification in 'late capitalism'" — featuring DEATH cigarettes branded with a skull, Liquid Death mountain water with its black-metal aesthetic, and a Dutch Barn vodka advert sold by Ricky Gervais ("Buying Dutch Barn will make a person rich & happy. And that person is Ricky Gervais") — is the visual joke that underwrites the slogan. Those products do not just sell themselves; they sell themselves *as* the parody of selling themselves. The myth is robbed before it even sets.

Begin with the theoretical warrant. Barthes himself invites the move: "Since myth robs language of something, why not rob myth? All that is needed is to use it as the departure point for a third semiological chain, to take its signification as the first term of a second myth" (*Mythologies*, p. 135). The lecture treats this passage explicitly under the slide "Robbing Myth of Myth: Third-Order Signification". And the next slide, "Styles and significations in late capitalism / post-modernity", lists roadmen, clean girls, content creators, looksmaxxers, the Harry Potter fandom and gamers, asking what "further third order significations… knowingness / excess / parody / irony" do in post-modern culture.

So the slogan is right that the *formal* possibility of further chains is open-ended. Once any sign can become the first term in a new mythological chain, there is no logical stopping point. Liquid Death takes the Marlboro Man's connotation of dangerous cool and applies it to *water*. The mythologisation of mass-marketed water as deathly rebellion is a third-order signification stacked on a third-order signification. Tesla's brand "essence" — innovation, the future, planetary salvation — has already been ironised across TikTok ("just billionaire stuff"), and the irony is itself absorbed back into the brand. Charlie Brooker's *Black Mirror* mythologises the mythologisers; viewers screenshot Black Mirror moments to caption real news; the brand "Black Mirror" itself becomes a sign of cultured cynicism; and so on.

The slogan is right, secondly, about the *commercial* incentive for infinite recursion. Brand differentiation in saturated markets requires ever-more-baroque acts of signification. Apple positions itself against Microsoft; new brands position themselves against Apple; "anti-brand" brands position themselves against branding; Liquid Death sells you the joke about water-as-product. Each iteration commodifies the previous critique. The lecture's slide on "Styles in late capitalism" identifies precisely this dynamic with the lifestyle categories of the present.

But the slogan is misleading in three ways.

First, *not every audience experiences the chain as infinite*. The third-order recursion is highly visible to media-literate, urban, often middle-class consumers who are fluent in irony. Many audiences experience the same brands as first-order myths, full stop. The Marlboro Man still works in markets where the parody has not arrived. Coca-Cola's Christmas adverts still mythologise family Christmas naively for many viewers. "No end" is true in principle and overstated in practice.

Second, *the chains terminate in something material*. Behind Liquid Death is a beverage company with shareholders, supply chains and a marketing budget. Behind Tesla is a labour force. Behind Apple is a chip supply, a contract manufacturing relationship, a tax structure. The recursive semiotics ride on a non-recursive political economy. Strinati's complaint that semiology "tends to ignore the context of industrial production" (p. 117) applies with force here: the chains *of signs* can be infinite, but the chain *of commodity production* is finite, locatable and analysable.

Third, *the audience response is finite*. Hall's encoding/decoding model reminds us that recursion at the level of the sign does not automatically produce recursion at the level of the reader. Audiences get tired, exit categories, find new ones, refuse the joke. The "Styles in late capitalism" slide names categories that have a life cycle: the "clean girl" of 2023 is not the "clean girl" of 2026; "looksmaxxers" rise and fall; "roadmen" mutate. Trend cycles are not infinitely extendable in any one slot.

The deeper theoretical question is whether the infinite-chain image is descriptively accurate or ideologically convenient. Critics of post-modernism (including Jameson, who sits on the same "Rethinking Modernity" map as structuralism) argue that the sense of endless recursion is itself a feature of late capitalism: irony is the new sincerity, and unmasking is the new mask. If so, then "the chain has no end" is itself a myth — a third-order signification that naturalises the impossibility of stepping outside.

My conclusion is that the slogan captures something real about late-capitalist signification but overstates it. The chains can keep going formally, do keep going commercially, but stop on the ground of production and on the ground of finite audiences. The most useful response to the slogan is to add Hall, political economy and trend-cycle empiricism, and to keep asking where each chain is actually attached.

### 8.14 Mock 11 — How well does Barthes's semiology travel into feminist analyses of popular culture?

Barthes's semiology travels surprisingly well into feminist analyses of popular culture, and the lecture's own examples — the Dick Bruna "The school" book cover with the blue-clothed boy and red-clothed girl, the femininity slide of "Obedient / Servile / Passive / Submissive / Emotional / Sensitive / Private sphere / Reproductive / Child care / Domestic labour", and the masculinity slide of "Manly / Virile / Muscular / Heroic / Wise / Aggressive / Dominant / Sexually active / Successful / Public sphere" — point directly to the productive uses. But the travel is incomplete: Barthes's class-bound theory of ideology has to be extended to make full feminist sense of gendered signs.

Begin with what Barthes already gives feminism. In *Mythologies* he turns his analytical eye to a magazine photograph of seventy women novelists, each captioned with the number of her children. Denotatively, women novelists. Connotatively, women novelists who are *also* mothers. Mythically, what Barthes calls the "eternal statute of womanhood": "women are on the earth to give children to men" (*Mythologies*, p. 56). They may "write as much as they like" and "decorate their condition", but they cannot "depart from it" (pp. 56–57). The myth tells women that "your order is free on condition that it depends on his; your freedom is a luxury, it is possible only if you first acknowledge the obligations of your nature" (p. 58).

This is recognisably feminist analysis avant la lettre. Strinati paraphrases: "the role of women as mothers is made to appear natural and inevitable, the related connotation being that the power and dominance of men is equally natural and inevitable" (p. 110). The semiological method is to denaturalise the gendered sign — to take its naturalised "of course women are mothers" and re-historicise it as a culturally specific arrangement.

The travel into contemporary feminist work is direct. Disney princesses denote characters, connote femininity, mythologise gender as romance and domesticity. Instagram's "clean girl" aesthetic (lecture slide) denotes a look, connotes self-discipline and refinement, mythologises femininity as productive optimisation. TikTok's "tradwife" content denotes a lifestyle, connotes domestic contentment, mythologises a return to "natural" gender roles that are in fact a specific reactionary project. The lecture's femininity slide already lays out the paradigmatic field of feminine connotations; semiology lets us see those signifiers as cultural fixings, not natural facts.

Masculinity gets the same treatment. The masculinity slide lists virility, muscularity, aggression, dominance, public-sphere success. The Marlboro Man denotes a cowboy with a cigarette, connotes rugged independence, mythologises masculinity as freedom (and addiction as virility). Cigarettes in Old Hollywood films, the suit in the Hugo Boss advert (denotation/connotation slide), the Jeep advert ("Upgrade your adventures") and James Bond's brand assemblage all work the same way. Eco's structuralist Bond reading (Strinati, pp. 93–98) maps the gendered moves explicitly: "Bond takes Woman… Bond, convalescing, enjoys Woman, whom he then loses" (p. 95). The narrative scheme is built on gender as a binary opposition.

But three limits on the travel are real.

First, *Barthes's class-bound theory of ideology*. He reduces myth to "bourgeois ideology" (Strinati, p. 117), which under-specifies patriarchy as a power formation that cuts across class. Feminist theorists from de Beauvoir onward had to do that work separately, and semiology needs the supplement. The "eternal statute of womanhood" is not just bourgeois; it operates across class, and it intersects with race and sexuality in ways Barthes does not theorise.

Second, *the active-decoder problem*. Barthes's myth-consumer is gender-neutral and passive. Feminist reception studies (after Hall) have shown that women audiences actively decode gendered media — sometimes resisting (queer readings of Disney, ironic engagement with rom-coms), sometimes pleasure-reading against the grain (romance novels as imaginative space), sometimes oppositional. The Hall encoding/decoding model is therefore essential to make Barthes feminist-usable.

Third, *intersectionality*. Race, class, sexuality and disability cut through gender. The lecture's Femininity slide is implicitly white, middle-class, heterosexual. A Black "clean girl" sign on TikTok travels through different mythological chains than a white one; a working-class "tradwife" account does different cultural work than a middle-class one. Barthes does not theorise these intersections; feminist of colour scholarship has had to build them in.

My assessment is therefore that Barthes's semiology is *necessary* for feminist analyses of popular culture, because it provides the technical vocabulary for denaturalising gendered signs, and the lecture's own choice of examples shows how cleanly it applies. But it is not *sufficient*. To do feminist semiology well we need Hall on decoding, intersectional theorising on the textures of difference, and political economy on the industries that produce gendered images. Barthes opens the door; feminism walks through and remodels the building.

### 8.15 Mock 12 — Is semiology a science or a form of literary criticism?

The honest answer is that semiology was conceived as a science, has often functioned as literary criticism, and is most defensible today as a *systematic interpretive method* whose status sits between the two. The interesting move is not to decide which label is correct but to see what each pressure brings out about the method's strengths and weaknesses.

The scientific pedigree is real. Saussure himself proposed semiology as "a science which studies the life of signs within society, shows what they are composed of, and discovers the laws which govern them" (Strinati, p. 85). The Independent semiotics infographic in the lecture deck calls semiotics or semiology "the study of signs symbols and signification… the study of how meaning is created" and lays out a quasi-formal taxonomy: symbolic / iconic / indexical signs, syntagm and paradigm, denotation and connotation. Barthes's early framing was equally scientific: he wrote that semiology was for him "the fundamental method of an ideological critique" (in Strinati, p. 100), and *Elements of Semiology* (1964) reads like an attempt at a methodological manual. Hall's encoding/decoding model is presented in the lecture slides with the diagrammatic precision of a flowchart.

But the practice of semiology has often looked more like literary criticism than like a science. Strinati's running complaint is sharp: "Barthes does not attempt to indicate why his interpretation is to be preferred to others" (p. 114). When Barthes claims that roses signify passion, the reader is asked to nod along. Why not "joke, farewell, or a platonic thank you"? "What evidence could a semiologist call upon to back up Barthes's interpretation?" (Strinati, p. 114). Williamson's reading of a tyre advert — the jetty "represents… strong and safe" — is, on Strinati's analysis, "totally arbitrary" (p. 115).

Three features make semiology look like criticism rather than science.

First, *the empirical validation problem*. A genuine science specifies its falsification conditions. Semiology rarely does. The same sign admits multiple readings; the analyst picks one; the reader either agrees or doesn't.

Second, *the polysemy concession*. Barthes himself, in his later work, argued that texts are polysemic — open to multiple interpretations. Strinati notes that this "hardly gets us very far" as a methodological position (p. 114). If texts are open, why is *this* reading better than another? A science needs ranking criteria; criticism survives on persuasive force.

Third, *the audience and production blindness*. A semiology that ignores who made the sign, in what institutional conditions, and how audiences actually decode it is reading texts in a vacuum. That is criticism with social ambition rather than social science.

But it would be too quick to conclude that semiology is therefore "just" literary criticism. Three features keep it within reach of scientific method.

First, *the systematicity of the apparatus*. The signifier/signified distinction, the langue/parole distinction, the paradigmatic/syntagmatic axes, the three orders of signification — these are not impressionistic. They are repeatable analytical operations. Two readers trained in the apparatus can run them on the same Hugo Boss advert or Coca-Cola Christmas spot and produce overlapping, comparable analyses.

Second, *the structural cumulation*. Lévi-Strauss's reading of Oedipus and the Iroquois owl-riddle myth (Strinati, pp. 90–92) does aspire to test claims against new cases. Eco's structural reading of the Bond novels (Strinati, pp. 93–98) generates predictions about narrative invariance that can be tested against other genre fiction. Hall's encoding/decoding model has generated decades of testable reception studies.

Third, *the integration into wider social science*. Once Hall reworks semiology to include encoding institutions and decoding audiences, the method becomes empirically tractable: you can study the production side ethnographically, the decoding side through audience research, and combine them with semiotic textual analysis. This is not bench science, but it is recognisable social science.

My own view is that the most useful position is the middle one: semiology is a *systematic interpretive method*. Its claims should be evaluated by criteria appropriate to interpretive social science: internal coherence, fit with the textual evidence, plausibility against alternative readings, productivity for further research, and consilience with what we know about production and reception. Judged by physics-style standards, it will always look like literary criticism. Judged by the standards of qualitative social research — which it shares with ethnography, discourse analysis, and historical sociology — it is methodologically respectable.

The label "science" carries too much positivist baggage; the label "criticism" sells the method's rigour short. Semiology is best understood as one tool in the interpretive social-science toolkit, indispensable for analysing the cultural surface of contemporary capitalism, and most defensible when used alongside encoding/decoding research, political economy and ethnography. That is neither pure science nor pure criticism, but it is good work.
