# L13: Habermas and the Public Sphere (with Fraser's Critique)

## §1. Lecture Map

This lecture sits at the hinge between the Frankfurt School's pessimism about culture under capitalism (Adorno, Horkheimer) and a more democratically hopeful strand of critical theory. Marx provides the underlying claim that the ruling class controls the mental means of production and that base shapes superstructure. The first generation of Frankfurt thinkers radicalised this into the dialectic of enlightenment, the culture industry, standardisation, pseudo-individualisation, and a deep scepticism about revolutionary agency. Walter Benjamin partially reopens the question of agency through mechanical reproduction: art loses cult value and gains exhibition value, and new means of cultural production make new perceptions possible. Habermas, working in the second generation of the Frankfurt School, picks up this thread and develops the public sphere as a normative space in which democratisation through communication is at least conceivable.

Habermas's account is a two-part narrative: the emergence of a liberal bourgeois public sphere in the 17th and 18th centuries, and its eventual refeudalisation by the state and commercial private interests. The public sphere is a forum for rational and critical debate located between the state and civil society; it is where private persons assemble as a public body to discuss matters of common concern. Habermas formalises its discursive norms in the ideal speech situation. The lecture then turns to Nancy Fraser's "Rethinking the Public Sphere" (Fraser, 1992) as the central critical reconstruction: not a rejection but a reworking that takes inequality, plurality, and conflict seriously, and that introduces subaltern counterpublics. Contemporary application: print journalism, the Fourth Estate, the radical press and its decline, tabloidisation, press barons, social media, technofeudalism.

## §2. Key Concepts

- **The public sphere**: the realm of social life in which "something approaching public opinion can be formed" and access is "guaranteed to all citizens" (Habermas, 2006: 73). A space between the state and civil society where private persons assemble as a public body to discuss matters of general interest.
- **The bourgeois public sphere**: the historically specific form that emerged in 17th-18th century Britain, France, and Germany, premised on a capitalist economy, a reconstituted public state, and a bourgeois civil society. Coffeehouses, salons, periodicals, and the literary press were its institutional anchors.
- **Refeudalisation**: Habermas's term for the late-modern reversal in which large organisations and commercial interests strike deals with the state behind the scenes and stage public "openness" only as a kind of plebiscitary display, so that "the form of public opinion making is maintained, while the substance of the public sphere has long ago been undermined" (Habermas, 2006: 78).
- **The ideal speech situation**: the regulative norms of authentic deliberation. Competence to speak; freedom to question any assertion; freedom to introduce any assertion; freedom of expression; consensus reached by the most rational argument.
- **Communicative action**: action coordinated through reasons rather than through strategic calculation or coercion; the public sphere is its institutional home (Habermas, 1984).
- **The colonisation of the lifeworld**: the encroachment of system imperatives (money, administrative power) into domains that should be coordinated by communicative reason (Habermas, 1984).
- **The structural transformation**: the historical shift from a critical bourgeois public to a manipulated, consumerist "public", the title of Habermas's 1962 book (Habermas, 1989).
- **Publizität (publicity / making-public)**: the principle that proceedings be made public, which Habermas distinguishes from Öffentlichkeit (the public sphere proper), degraded today into "publicity" as PR (Habermas, 2006: 77).
- **Subaltern counterpublics**: Fraser's term for alternative discursive arenas formed by subordinated groups (feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics) in which they develop oppositional interpretations of their identities, interests, and needs (Fraser, 1992: 122-123).
- **Bracketing inequalities**: Habermas's claim that participants can "set aside" status differences to deliberate as equals; Fraser rejects this as "unrealistic and ideologically naïve" (Fraser, 1992: 119).
- **Strong publics / weak publics**: Fraser's distinction between publics whose discourse is decision-making (e.g., parliaments) and those whose discourse is opinion-forming (e.g., civil-society publics) (Fraser, 1992: 137).
- **Participatory parity**: Fraser's normative benchmark, the conditions under which all members of a polity can interact as peers (Fraser, 1992: 137).
- **The Fourth Estate**: the press as a fourth power, holding the executive, legislature, and judiciary accountable, a key institution of the public sphere in the liberal model.
- **Refeudalisation in cultural production**: McGuigan's extension of the thesis to the work of cultural producers themselves, under conditions of post-Fordist outsourcing, contract precarity, and the dominance of advertising-funded media (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183).
- **The market failure rationale**: Collins's argument that public-service intervention is justified where market provision fails to deliver culturally valued or politically necessary forms of broadcasting (Collins, 1993: 250, 258).
- **Mediated publicness**: Thompson's reformulation of public-sphere theory for an era of dispersed, screen-mediated communication rather than face-to-face dialogue (Thompson, 1993: 188).
- **Public service broadcasting and modern public life**: Scannell's argument that broadcasting democratises access to common cultural and political life (Scannell, 1989: 136).

## §4. Examples and Applications

- **17th-18th century coffeehouses**: Habermas's paradigmatic institutional site of the bourgeois public sphere. Private persons, gathered around the newspaper, formed "a public body" in which "something approaching public opinion can be formed" (Habermas, 2006: 73). Slide 8 of the SC301 deck depicts a London coffeehouse of the period to make the point visually.
- **The press in its critical phase**: per Habermas's encyclopedia article, the press transformed from "mere institutions for the publication of news into bearers and leaders of public opinion, weapons of party politics" (Habermas, 2006: 76, quoting Karl Bücher). Newspaper publishers became "dealers in public opinion" and the press became "a mediator and intensifier of public discussion".
- **The Fourth Estate**: the press as a fourth power holding executive, legislature, and judiciary to account, formalised by slide 8 and central to the liberal model.
- **Press freedom**: the institutional precondition of the public sphere's communicative function; the silenced-figure image on slide 9 makes the point graphically. Without freedom to publish, the ideal speech situation cannot be met.
- **The radical press** of the early 19th century. *The Poor Man's Guardian* (1831) was "established contrary to 'law,' to try the power of 'might' against 'right'" (slide 10), an example of a working-class counterpublic operating in defiance of stamp duty, anticipating Fraser's subaltern counterpublic concept (Fraser, 1992: 122-123) avant la lettre.
- **The decline of the radical press**: stamp duty repeal, prosecution, and competition from advertising-funded papers pushed working-class titles out (slide 11). McGuigan reads this as an early instance of the commercial logic that hollows out the public sphere from within rather than suppressing it from above (McGuigan, 1996: 4).
- **Advertising and the popular press**: once papers depended on advertising revenue they became responsive to advertiser-friendly content, entertainment, scandal, lifestyle, rather than to working-class agitation (slide 12).
- **Press barons and concentration of ownership**: Kevin Williams's *Get Me a Murder a Day!* (cited on slide 13) traces the 20th-century consolidation of British newspaper ownership. Concentrated ownership produces editorial alignment with proprietor interests, a structural pressure on the substance of the public sphere even when its form is maintained (Habermas, 2006: 77).
- **Tabloidisation in the late C20th** (slide 14): *The Sun*, *Daily Mail*, *Mirror*, *Express*. Journalism of scandal, celebrity, sport, and right-wing populism displaces political reporting, instantiating the structural transformation Habermas described (Habermas, 1989: 142, 232) and McGuigan extends (McGuigan, 1996: 180).
- **The refeudalisation of the public sphere** (slide 15, illustrated with a medieval feudal pyramid): Habermas's diagnosis that "large organizations strive for political compromises with the state and with one another, excluding the public sphere whenever possible. But at the same time the large organizations must assure themselves of at least plebiscitary support from the mass of the population through an apparent display of openness" (Habermas, 2006: 77).
- **"Planet Murdoch"** (slide 19): the SC301 slide deck shows a world map of News Corp interests across the UK, US, Eastern Europe, Asia, Australia, Latin America, and elsewhere (the UK Sun, News of the World, the Times, BSkyB, Sky News; the US New York Post, Wall Street Journal, Fox; STAR TV in Asia; Sky in Latin America and Italy). The map exemplifies the cross-national commercial refeudalisation Habermas warned about (Habermas, 2006: 77-78) and that McGuigan reads as a phantom public sphere (McGuigan, 1996: 180, 183).
- **"Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem"** (slide 18 of the deck): the contemporary update of Planet Murdoch, with the Ellisons (CBS, Paramount+, Pluto, TikTok), Zuckerberg (Meta), the Murdochs (Fox properties, Wall Street Journal, New York Post), the Sinclair family (185+ local news affiliates), Bezos (Washington Post, Prime Video, Twitch), Apple, Alphabet (Google, YouTube, Android, Gemini), Musk (X, xAI), and Altman (OpenAI/ChatGPT). The slide's argument is that concentration of the means of mass communication consolidates plebiscitary Publizität in the hands of a small ownership coalition; this is exactly the situation Habermas's refeudalisation thesis predicts.
- **Musk's "Violent Speech Policy"** (slide 22 image): Musk's announcement that the platform formerly called Twitter would offer "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach" by demonetising and burying disfavoured speech is the slide's chosen illustration of Fraser's critique that the formal openness of a "town square" can mask informal mechanisms of exclusion that systematically disadvantage subordinated groups (Fraser, 1992: 119).
- **Technofeudalism** (slide 19 in the SC301 deck): the slide juxtaposes Google, Netflix, Meta, Apple, and Amazon with medieval lords. Platforms extract rent rather than profit, and users are quasi-serfs producing data on platform-lords' land, a contemporary literalisation of Habermas's refeudalisation thesis (Habermas, 2006: 77).
- **The Californian Ideology** (slide 17): the libertarian-utopian promise that the internet would itself constitute a global public sphere; the slide collages the *Last Whole Earth Catalog*, an early Synchronet terminal, a "global village" globe-and-hands image, an Arab Spring cartoon, and a hand holding a phone showering social-media reactions. The thesis is that the promise was belied by concentrated platform ownership rather than fulfilled by global connectivity.
- **Feminist, Black, and queer publics** (the explicit examples on slide 22 and in the teacher's summary of Fraser): Fraser names these as the canonical subaltern counterpublics, "alternative discursive arenas formed by members of subordinated social groups in which they can develop oppositional interpretations of their identities, interests, and needs" (Fraser, 1992: 122-123). They serve a dual function: spaces of withdrawal and regroupment, and bases for agitation directed toward wider publics (Fraser, 1992: 123).
- **Domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work as bracketed-private** (Fraser, 1992: 111, 115): Fraser's specific cases of issues that the public/private distinction historically depoliticised; their political reclassification illustrates her thesis that "what counts as a matter of public concern is itself a political question, not a neutral or pre-given boundary".
- **Public service broadcasting as a third model** (slide 20, "Contemporary Relevance"; Scannell, 1989: 136; Collins, 1993: 250, 258): the BBC and comparable public-service institutions illustrate the slide's argument for "a third model of media ownership beyond state intervention and free-market economics". Scannell argues that public service broadcasting democratised access to a common cultural and political life (Scannell, 1989: 136). Collins argues that market provision systematically fails to deliver citizenship-oriented programming, so public-service intervention is justified on grounds of market failure as well as on grounds of cultural democracy (Collins, 1993: 250, 258).
- **Mediated publicness** (Thompson, 1993: 188): Thompson reformulates Habermas's dialogical model for an era of dispersed, screen-mediated communication. The reformulation matters because the contemporary public sphere is not a single conversation but an asymmetric system of broadcast, narrowcast, and platform-mediated address.
- **The phantom public sphere** (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183): McGuigan argues that "the public sphere" survives in late capitalism mainly as a regulative ideal whose actual conditions, concentrated ownership, advertising dependence, casualised cultural labour, are systematically obstructed by the very system that proclaims it.

## §5. Theory Application and Critical Synthesis

Habermas's value for contemporary media and cultural studies is twofold. First, it forces analysis of the institutional and material conditions under which something like public reason is possible: who owns the media, who has access, what regulatory environment sustains it. The slide deck (Lecture 13) explicitly notes that the concept "focuses our attention on the necessary material base required for genuinely public communication", important in an age of monopolised platforms (Habermas, 1989: 232). Second, it opens conceptual space for a third model of media ownership beyond state and market: public-service media as Scannell (1989: 136) and Collins (1993: 250, 258) defend.

The criticisms of Habermas summarised by the lecture are: (1) the principle of universal access masked structural exclusion in practice; (2) the model over-privileges the bourgeois variant and ignores popular and plebeian publics; (3) the refeudalisation thesis may simply be the continuation of capitalism rather than a new stage; (4) the model treats media audiences as passive consumers unless they reach the public sphere.

Fraser's reconstruction addresses (1) and (2) directly. Her arguments cluster into five claims: the bourgeois public sphere was structurally exclusionary, not accidentally so (Fraser, 1992: 115); bracketing inequalities is ideologically naïve because discursive norms of calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion themselves favour dominant groups (Fraser, 1992: 119); a single public sphere benefits dominant groups, so plurality through subaltern counterpublics, feminist, Black, queer publics, is a democratic necessity (Fraser, 1992: 122-123); the public/private distinction depoliticises issues that disproportionately affect women and subordinated groups, with domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work as her named examples (Fraser, 1992: 111); the "common good" rhetoric often masks particular dominant interests, and democratic deliberation is inherently conflictual, not consensual (Fraser, 1992: 137). Fraser thereby pluralises and politicises Habermas's model without abandoning the regulative idea of democratic communication.

Three further critical resources are necessary for a full synthesis. Thompson (1993: 188) reformulates the public sphere for the age of "mediated publicness", a dispersed, screen-mediated architecture rather than face-to-face conversation. McGuigan (1996: 4, 180, 183) argues that under late capitalist conditions the public sphere survives chiefly as a regulative idea, with its actual conditions, concentrated ownership, advertising dependence, casualised cultural labour, "phantom" rather than substantive. Scannell (1989: 136) and Collins (1993: 250, 258) defend public service broadcasting as the concrete institutional form in which Habermas's normative ideal can be partially realised, with Collins emphasising the market-failure rationale and Scannell emphasising the democratising character of broadcast access to a common cultural and political life.

Synthesis: a Habermas-after-Fraser framework, supplemented by Thompson, McGuigan, Scannell and Collins, keeps the normative core (rational deliberation, accountability, accessibility) while accepting (a) that publics are multiple, (b) that participatory parity must be a precondition not an output (Fraser, 1992: 137), (c) that the public/private line is itself a stake of contestation, and (d) that conflict among publics is generative rather than corrosive. Applied to platform capitalism: the "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach" formulation announced on Musk's platform (slide 22) is not a public sphere in any Habermasian sense because it lacks guaranteed access, equal voice, and freedom from owner caprice; it is, rather, an exemplary instance of demonstrative Publizität (Habermas, 2006: 77). Yet subaltern counterpublics in Fraser's structural sense, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), persist within and against the architectures of the platform economy, sustaining the possibility of the public sphere as critique even when the conditions of its substantive realisation are obstructed.

## §8. Full Mock Essays

### Essay 1: "The public sphere is a useful normative ideal but a bad description of how democracies actually work." Discuss.

I argue that the statement is broadly correct, but that the gap between the public sphere as ideal and as description is itself the analytical pay-off of Habermas's concept rather than its weakness. Habermas's account, as the lecture summarises, is a two-part narrative: the emergence of a liberal bourgeois public sphere in the 17th and 18th centuries, followed by its refeudalisation. The descriptive claim that there ever was a fully functioning bourgeois public sphere is dubious, and the descriptive claim that contemporary democracies operate through rational-critical deliberation is plainly false. Nonetheless, the normative idea, that legitimate political authority must justify itself before an informed reasoning public, remains indispensable.

Habermas's own definition is unambiguously normative: "by the public sphere we mean first of all a realm of our social life in which something approaching public opinion can be formed. Access is guaranteed to all citizens" (Habermas, 2006: 73). The conditional is doing the work in "something approaching" and "guaranteed". The historical chapters of *The Structural Transformation* describe 18th-century coffeehouses and the literary press (Habermas, 1989: 56); but Fraser is right to insist that the bourgeois public sphere was "never as inclusive, egalitarian, or disinterested as it claimed to be" (paraphrasing Fraser, 1992: 115). Women, the working class, and racialised minorities were structurally excluded; the apparent equality of coffeehouse conversation rested on a propertied, educated, male precondition.

Descriptively, contemporary democracies look even further from the model. The slide deck's "Planet Murdoch" map of News Corp interests (slide 19) and its more recent "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) document a configuration of concentrated cross-national ownership across the Murdochs, Zuckerberg, Bezos, the Ellisons, Alphabet, Musk, Apple, the Sinclair family and Altman's OpenAI that exemplifies what Habermas called demonstrative Publizität: "the form of public opinion making is maintained, while the substance of the public sphere has long ago been undermined" (Habermas, 2006: 78). Tabloidisation of the British press (slide 14, *The Sun*, *Daily Mail*, *Mirror*, *Express*) reinforces the diagnosis. The ideal speech situation, which requires that "the consensus of opinion be decided by the most rational argument" (SC301 Lecture 13 slides), is empirically rare in such conditions.

Yet the same examples show why the ideal remains useful. When critics object that Musk's "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach" formulation (slide 22) is not a public sphere in any defensible sense, they are doing so on Habermasian grounds, appealing to guaranteed access, equal voice, and reasoned justification. The Contemporary Relevance slide (slide 20) makes the point explicitly: the value of Habermas's concept is that it "focuses our attention on the necessary material base required for genuinely public communication" and "opens up the conceptual space for a third model of media ownership, one that is neither dominated by state intervention or free market economics". This third-model argument has been developed in detail by Scannell, who reads public service broadcasting as a genuinely democratising institution that made shared cultural and political life accessible "on terms unknown before" (Scannell, 1989: 136), and by Collins, who frames it in terms of market failure: market provision systematically under-supplies citizenship-oriented broadcasting, so public-service intervention is justified on economic as well as political grounds (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The ideal speech situation thus survives not as a snapshot of practice but as a regulative criterion by which practice is judged and as the rationale for an alternative institutional form.

Fraser sharpens this by insisting that the gap between ideal and reality is structural, not accidental, and that the response is to pluralise rather than abandon the model. Subaltern counterpublics, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics, provide both spaces of withdrawal and bases for agitation (Fraser, 1992: 122-123). The slide deck's own Considering Fraser's argument question, "What might be the 'subaltern counterpublics' for these groups?" (slide 23), invites exactly this pluralisation. These counterpublics are not failures of the public sphere; they are the public sphere properly conceived as multiple. Fraser's notion of participatory parity reframes the ideal: not "deliberation as if equal", but the institutional preconditions under which people can deliberate as peers (Fraser, 1992: 137). This avoids the descriptive overreach of the Habermasian model while preserving its normative bite.

A genuinely critical use of the concept therefore separates two questions. First, is the public sphere a fair description of any actually existing democracy? No: capitalism, racism, patriarchy, and concentrated ownership distort communication systematically, exactly as the criticisms summarised by the lecture suggest (universal access in principle, structural exclusion in practice). McGuigan goes further: the public sphere in late capitalism is largely a "phantom" public sphere, a regulative idea whose substantive conditions are systematically obstructed by the very economy that proclaims it (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183). Second, is the public sphere a useful normative ideal? Yes: it gives us criteria for evaluating media systems, identifies the necessary material base of public communication (the lecture's "Contemporary Relevance" slide), and opens conceptual space for a third model of media ownership beyond state and market (Scannell, 1989: 136; Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The statement is therefore correct as far as it goes, but understates the point: it is precisely because the public sphere is a bad description that it can still be a powerful critical ideal. To collapse the two registers, either dismissing Habermas because his history is partial, or defending him as a sociological realist, misses the work the concept is doing. With Fraser's amendments and Thompson's reformulation for an era of mediated rather than face-to-face publicness (Thompson, 1993: 188), the public sphere remains the most articulate normative benchmark we have for thinking about democratic communication.

### Essay 2: To what extent does Fraser's notion of subaltern counterpublics rescue or replace Habermas's public sphere?

Fraser's notion of subaltern counterpublics rescues more than it replaces, but the rescue is so thorough that it changes the shape of the project. I argue that Fraser preserves Habermas's regulative core (that democratic legitimacy depends on accessible, accountable communication) while replacing the singular bourgeois public sphere with a pluralistic, conflictual model in which participatory parity is the operative norm.

Habermas's classical formulation, as the lecture summarises, presents the public sphere as a forum for rational-critical debate between state and civil society in which "a body of private persons can assemble to discuss matters of public concern" (lecture summary, SC301 Habermas and the Public Sphere; cf. Habermas, 2006: 73). The ideal speech situation specifies discursive norms: competence to speak, freedom to question or introduce any assertion, freedom of expression, consensus through the most rational argument. Habermas himself acknowledged the historical limitations of the 18th-century bourgeois public sphere (Habermas, 1989: 87). Fraser's critique, however, argues that these limitations are not accidental: women, the working class, and racialised minorities were "structurally excluded, not accidentally marginalised" (paraphrasing Fraser, 1992: 115).

Fraser's central conceptual move is to introduce subaltern counterpublics: "parallel discursive arenas where members of subordinated social groups invent and circulate counter-discourses to formulate oppositional interpretations of their identities, interests, and needs" (Fraser, 1992: 123). These have a dual function: withdrawal and regroupment, and agitation directed at wider publics (Fraser, 1992: 123). Fraser's own examples, foregrounded in the teacher's summary of Lecture 13 and reiterated on slide 22 of the deck, are feminist publics, Black publics, and queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122). She makes the case concrete with reference to the kinds of issues that have been historically depoliticised by the public/private distinction: domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work, defined as "private" by dominant publics and so excluded from legitimate political debate (Fraser, 1992: 111). Feminist counterpublics have made each of these into a matter of public concern; this is the dual function in action, withdrawal into a space where shared experience can be articulated, then agitation that redraws the boundary of the political.

This is rescue because Fraser keeps the basic Habermasian wager: communication can in principle hold power to account, and the conditions of communication are themselves political stakes. She rejects "the radical antidemocratic move" of abandoning the public sphere altogether. But the rescue involves four substantial revisions. First, bracketing inequalities is ruled out as "unrealistic and ideologically naïve" (Fraser, 1992: 119). Discursive norms favouring "calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion" themselves disadvantage subordinated groups whose communicative practices may differ. Second, the singular public sphere is replaced by a plurality of publics, because in stratified societies a single public reflects dominant interests. Third, the public/private distinction is exposed as itself a political question: "what counts as a matter of public concern is itself a political question" (lecture summary). Fourth, the orientation to a "common good" is reframed: claims to universality often mask the particular interests of dominant groups, and democratic deliberation is inherently conflictual rather than consensual (Fraser, 1992: 137).

The case that this is replacement rather than rescue is strongest at the level of Habermas's foundational normative commitments. The ideal speech situation aims at rational consensus; Fraser's model expects ongoing contestation. Habermas's model assumes a single inclusive public is the regulative ideal; Fraser denies this. Yet Fraser preserves what is most defensible in Habermas: communicative access as a condition of legitimacy, the public-private distinction as a contestable object rather than a fact, and the necessity of an institutional material base for democratic communication. Her notion of participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137), peer interaction across structural inequalities, operates as a Habermasian regulative ideal under realistic conditions.

A challenge to Fraser's account, raised implicitly by the lecture's discussion of social-media ownership and control (slide 18, "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem"), is that the architectures of contemporary communication may not allow counterpublics to function as Fraser envisages. Thompson's reformulation is relevant here: in conditions of dispersed, screen-mediated publicness rather than face-to-face dialogue (Thompson, 1993: 188), the dual function of counterpublics, withdrawal and agitation, is harder to perform because the spaces of withdrawal are themselves owned by the same ownership coalition whose interests counterpublic agitation contests. McGuigan's "phantom public sphere" thesis (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183) reinforces the worry: counterpublic participation may be more spectral than substantive when its material conditions, access, ownership, distribution, remain in concentrated private hands.

On balance, Fraser rescues more than she replaces. She keeps the Habermasian motivation, democratic legitimacy through accessible, accountable communication, and the Habermasian methodology, institutional analysis of the conditions of public reason. She replaces the historical-empirical core (a singular bourgeois public converging on rational consensus) with a structural-pluralist one (multiple publics interacting under conditions of contestation). The net effect is a more empirically tractable and normatively defensible version of the project, one capable of analysing the contemporary configurations of ownership documented on slides 18 and 19 of the SC301 deck while keeping the regulative idea alive. Habermas remains the architect; Fraser is the engineer who rebuilt the load-bearing walls; Scannell (1989: 136) and Collins (1993: 250, 258) are the surveyors of the third institutional form, public-service broadcasting, in which the rebuilt edifice can be partially realised.

### Essay 3: Critically assess Habermas's refeudalisation thesis in light of contemporary platform capitalism.

Habermas's refeudalisation thesis holds up remarkably well when applied to contemporary social-media ownership and control, although the mechanisms have changed and the concept needs to be supplemented by Fraser's pluralism, McGuigan's "phantom public sphere", and the lecture's own discussion of technofeudalism. I argue that the thesis captures the structural logic of platform power but that its historical analogy needs careful handling.

Habermas's argument, summarised in 'The Public Sphere: An Encyclopedia Article', is that the 18th-century liberal model of rational-critical public discussion has been progressively undermined by "the interweaving of the public and private realms" in the social welfare state (Habermas, 2006: 77). Large organisations "strive for political compromises with the state and with one another, excluding the public sphere whenever possible. But at the same time the large organizations must assure themselves of at least plebiscitary support from the mass of the population through an apparent display of openness [demonstrative Publizität]" (Habermas, 2006: 77). Crucially, "the form of public opinion making is maintained, while the substance of the public sphere has long ago been undermined" (Habermas, 2006: 78; cf. Habermas, 1989: 232). The label "refeudalisation" gestures back to the medieval representative public sphere, in which power was displayed before the people rather than justified to them.

Applied to social-media capitalism, the thesis identifies several recognisable phenomena. First, the displacement of deliberation by display. The slide deck's image of Musk's "Violent Speech Policy" (slide 22), with the platform's announcement that disfavoured speech will be subject to "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach" and demonetised, is an exact instance of demonstrative Publizität: the form of openness is maintained while its substance, equal voice, is curated by ownership. Second, the concentration of ownership: the lecture's "Planet Murdoch" map (slide 19) maps News Corp interests across the UK, US, Eastern Europe, Asia, Latin America and Australasia, and the more recent "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) shows extreme concentration across legacy and digital media in the hands of the Ellisons, Zuckerberg, the Murdochs, the Sinclair family, Bezos, Apple, Musk, Alphabet, and Altman's OpenAI. This consolidates exactly the "private interests" Habermas warned about. Third, the politics-as-PR pattern: "in the form of 'publicity' it wins public prestige for people or affairs, thus making them worthy of acclamation in a climate of nonpublic opinion" (Habermas, 2006: 77).

The lecture's "Technofeudalism" slide (slide 19 of the deck) literalises Habermas's metaphor: a medieval scene with Google, Netflix, Meta, Apple, and Amazon as lords. Platforms extract rent rather than profit, users are quasi-serfs producing data on platform-lords' land, and political life is increasingly conducted in privatised enclosures. This directly maps the refeudalisation thesis onto contemporary capitalism.

However, three critical qualifications are necessary. First, as the lecture's criticisms of Habermas note (point three in the teacher's summary), refeudalisation may simply be "part of the continuation of capitalism" rather than a discrete historical reversal. The decline of the radical press in the 19th century (slide 11), the rise of advertising-funded popular newspapers (slide 12), press barons (slide 13, with Kevin Williams's *Get Me a Murder a Day!*), and tabloidisation (slide 14, *The Sun*, *Daily Mail*, *Mirror*, *Express*) were all earlier expressions of the same commercial logic. McGuigan makes this point sharply: the bourgeois public sphere was always partly "phantom", a regulative idea pursued under conditions of commercial concentration that systematically obstructed its realisation (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180). Contemporary platform capitalism is not a return to feudalism but the latest configuration of capitalist concentration in cultural production. The medieval metaphor risks obscuring the specifically capitalist drivers, advertising markets, network effects, data extraction, that McGuigan analyses (McGuigan, 1996: 183).

Second, Habermas's model treats media audiences as "passive consumers unless they have access to the Public Sphere", as the lecture summary's fourth criticism notes. This understates the participatory dimensions Thompson's "mediated publicness" framework captures: audiences are not merely receivers but participants in a dispersed system of address (Thompson, 1993: 188). Fraser's pluralisation is also necessary here: refeudalisation describes the dominant tendency, but the subaltern counterpublics she names, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), persist within and against platform infrastructures and exert political pressure on the boundary of what counts as public concern (Fraser, 1992: 111).

Third, the refeudalisation thesis tends to nostalgia for an 18th-century coffeehouse public that, as Fraser shows, was always exclusionary (Fraser, 1992: 115). To say the substance of the public sphere "has long ago been undermined" implies an earlier substance that was more inclusive than the historical record supports. A more defensible formulation: platform capitalism intensifies a structural tendency present from the start of bourgeois public communication, while creating new counterpublic possibilities that should be defended and enlarged.

Practically, the thesis supports the political demands flagged by the lecture's "Contemporary Relevance" slide (slide 20): a regulatory environment for genuinely public communication, attention to the necessary material base, and a third model of media ownership beyond state and market. Scannell defends public service broadcasting as the most developed institutional form of this third model: broadcasting that brings into being "a culture in common to whole populations" and admits "the unsorted everyday voices of ordinary listeners and viewers" into political life (Scannell, 1989: 136). Collins frames the same argument in terms of market failure: market provision systematically under-supplies citizenship-oriented broadcasting, so the public-service form is justified on economic as well as cultural-democratic grounds (Collins, 1993: 250, 258).

In sum, the refeudalisation thesis correctly diagnoses the structural logic of contemporary social-media capitalism (display rather than deliberation, owner caprice, plebiscitary acclamation, the substitution of PR for public reason) but should be deployed with Fraser's amendments (pluralism, the politicisation of the public/private line), McGuigan's qualifications (the phantom character of the bourgeois ideal from the outset), and Thompson's reformulation (mediated rather than face-to-face publicness). Scannell and Collins together specify the third-model institutional response. Counterpublics remain operative, the medieval metaphor should not obscure capitalist mechanisms, and the response is to enlarge the material conditions of public communication rather than to mourn a lost bourgeois Eden.

### Essay 4: "Bracketing inequalities is ideologically naïve" (Fraser). Evaluate this critique of Habermas with reference to the specific subaltern counterpublics Fraser names.

Fraser's critique of bracketing is the most empirically powerful element of her reconstruction of Habermas, and I argue it largely succeeds, although it stops short of fully replacing the Habermasian aspiration. I evaluate the critique with reference to the specific cases Fraser herself names, feminist counterpublics around domestic violence, reproductive rights and care work, and the parallel formations Fraser cites as Black publics and queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 111, 122-123).

Habermas's claim is that participants in the public sphere can set aside differences in status and power to deliberate as equals. The ideal speech situation specifies that "anybody with the competence to speak be allowed to take part" and that "the consensus of opinion be decided by the most rational argument" (SC301 Lecture 13 slides; cf. Habermas, 1989: 56). The 18th-century coffeehouse, depicted on slide 8 of the SC301 deck, is in Habermas's idealised account a space where the merchant, the writer, and the aristocrat met as private persons with arguments rather than as bearers of inherited rank.

Fraser's response is summarised by the lecture: she "rejects this as unrealistic and ideologically naïve. She argues that inequalities rooted in class, gender, race, and sexuality inevitably shape who can speak, who is heard, and whose arguments are taken seriously. Discursive norms that privilege particular styles of speech, such as calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion, often function to disadvantage subordinated groups whose communicative practices may differ" (lecture summary; cf. Fraser, 1992: 119). Two distinct claims sit inside this. First, an empirical claim: even where access is formally open, informal mechanisms reproduce exclusion. Second, a normative-conceptual claim: the discursive norms themselves are not neutral; they encode the communicative style of dominant groups and disadvantage those who deviate.

The first set of examples, the issues that Fraser cites as historically "bracketed-private", illustrates both claims. Domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work were, on the classical bourgeois public/private distinction, matters of "private" family life and so off-limits to public-sphere deliberation (Fraser, 1992: 111). Feminist counterpublics refused that classification. They insisted that what had been treated as private workplace and household matters were public political facts. The articulation took place in registers, testimony, narrative, shared experience, that the ideal of "calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion" (Fraser, 1992: 119) would marginalise. The shift in legal and policy frameworks that followed demonstrates Fraser's structural point: bracketing was not just a tactic that failed in practice but a normative posture that misrecognised what speech was doing under conditions of inequality.

The second set of examples, the parallel formations Fraser names as Black publics and queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), illustrates the same dynamics around race and sexuality. These publics function as subaltern counterpublics in Fraser's strict sense: spaces of withdrawal and regroupment for shared cultural experience, and bases for agitation directed at wider publics. Crucially, the discursive registers of these counterpublics, vernacular, performative, often refusing the protocols of professional rational-critical debate, are exactly the kind of communication that the "calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion" norm would code as illegitimate. Fraser's point lands again: bracketing race or sexuality means accepting a discursive frame that systematically devalues the modes by which subaltern publics articulate political claims.

Habermasian defences are available. One might say that Habermas's ideal is precisely the regulative ideal that the demands of these counterpublics appeal to: the demand that women, Black speakers, and queer speakers be heard as peers is itself a Habermasian demand. The critique is internal to the project rather than external to it. This is partially convincing. The ideal speech situation, properly understood, does not require participants to abandon their identities; it requires that no person be "prevented from exercising their rights to freedom of expression" (SC301 Lecture 13 slides). Yet Fraser's deeper point survives: even a properly understood ideal speech situation, if applied to a deeply unequal society, will reproduce inequality through the very norms it valorises (Fraser, 1992: 119). Bracketing is not just a tactic that fails in practice; it is a normative posture that misunderstands what speech is doing under conditions of inequality.

The constructive upshot is Fraser's notion of participatory parity as a precondition rather than a posture (Fraser, 1992: 137). Rather than asking participants to set aside their differences, participatory parity asks what social, economic, and discursive conditions must obtain so that participants can engage as peers. This shifts attention to material questions: ownership of the means of mass communication, which the slide deck's "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) makes vividly concrete, and the institutional design of the communicative architecture. McGuigan's "phantom public sphere" framing (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183) is relevant: participatory parity cannot be achieved within an architecture whose conditions of communication are structurally captured by concentrated private ownership.

In conclusion, the critique succeeds. Bracketing inequalities, even as a regulative ideal, naturalises the unequal conditions under which speech occurs. The cases Fraser herself names, domestic violence, reproductive rights and care work as bracketed-private (Fraser, 1992: 111), feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics as the counterpublic forms (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), demonstrate both the empirical failure of bracketing and the political productivity of refusing it. What survives of Habermas after Fraser is not the bracketing model but the demand that institutional conditions be built so that bracketing would not be required in the first place, and this demand, which Fraser names participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137), is itself the most defensible heir of the Habermasian project.

### Essay 5: Is the ideal speech situation a coherent regulative ideal or an unattainable fantasy? Discuss.

The ideal speech situation is a coherent regulative ideal, not an unattainable fantasy, provided we read it as a counterfactual norm rather than an empirical prediction. I argue that the five conditions Habermas specifies make sense as criteria for evaluating actual communication, that they survive Fraser's critique with modification, and that the public-service broadcasting tradition defended by Scannell (1989: 136) and Collins (1993: 250, 258) shows they are partially operationalisable as an institutional form.

The five conditions, as taught in the lecture, are: anybody with the competence to speak should be allowed to take part; anyone is allowed to question any assertion; anyone is allowed to introduce any assertion; no person is to be prevented from exercising freedom of expression; the consensus of opinion is to be decided by the most rational argument (SC301 Lecture 13 slides, *The Ideal Speech Situation*). Habermas does not claim that these conditions are routinely fulfilled. They are the conditions implicit in the very act of making a sincere claim: when I assert something as true, I presuppose that you could question it, that I would justify it by reasons rather than force, and that we could in principle agree.

Read this way, the ideal speech situation is a counterfactual or regulative ideal, in the Kantian sense. It functions like the concept of a perfectly frictionless surface in physics: nothing actual is perfectly frictionless, but the concept allows us to model and evaluate actual friction. Similarly, no actual conversation perfectly realises the five conditions; but they let us identify and criticise specific failures (coercion, censorship, asymmetric speaking rights, manipulation by rhetorical force rather than evidence, exclusion of relevant participants).

The fantasy objection has at least three forms. The first is empirical: actual discussions are full of power, interest, and manipulation. This is correct but does not undermine the ideal's regulative role. The second is conceptual: rationality itself is culturally specific, and any standard of "most rational argument" smuggles in particular norms. This is Fraser's worry. The lecture summary captures it: "Discursive norms that privilege particular styles of speech, such as calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion, often function to disadvantage subordinated groups whose communicative practices may differ" (Fraser, 1992: 119). The third is political: the demand for consensus suppresses legitimate dissent, especially from those whose grievances cannot be expressed in the discursive register of the dominant. McGuigan's "phantom public sphere" critique adds a fourth: the regulative ideal floats free of any institutional embodiment under conditions of concentrated commercial ownership (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180).

The first objection is the weakest because it confuses regulative ideals with empirical predictions. The second and third are more serious. Fraser's response is to pluralise the model: subaltern counterpublics, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics, develop their own discursive norms and challenge the norms of the dominant public from outside (Fraser, 1992: 122-123). The ideal speech situation, in a Fraserian reconstruction, applies not to a single comprehensive public but to the conditions of interaction across multiple publics, and the criterion of consensus is replaced by participatory parity and ongoing contestation (Fraser, 1992: 137).

Partial operationalisation is possible at the institutional level. Scannell's defence of public service broadcasting argues that the broadcasting tradition brought into being "a culture in common to whole populations" and admitted "the unsorted everyday voices of ordinary listeners and viewers" into political life on terms unprecedented in the older bourgeois public sphere (Scannell, 1989: 136). Public service broadcasting is not the ideal speech situation realised, but it is the most fully developed institutional embodiment of the ideal's underlying claims about access, equal voice, and reasoned address. Collins frames the case in terms of market failure: market provision systematically under-supplies the citizenship-oriented programming that the ideal would require, so public-service intervention is justified on economic as well as cultural-democratic grounds (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The Contemporary Relevance slide (slide 20) makes exactly this argument: the value of Habermas's concept is that it "opens up the conceptual space for a third model of media ownership, one that is neither dominated by state intervention or free market economics".

By contrast, the contemporary configuration of social-media ownership (slides 18 and 19) falls far short on every criterion of the ideal speech situation. The "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach" formulation on slide 22 is a sharp instance: access is not guaranteed, assertions are not equally questionable, freedom of expression is contingent on owner whim, and "consensus" is replaced by virality. The ideal speech situation lets us articulate exactly what is wrong with this configuration; its critical function depends on remaining a regulative ideal rather than collapsing into description.

The fantasy charge has bite when the ideal is misread as a prediction rather than as a norm. The conditions are presuppositions of speech-acts, not descriptions of empirical politics. They are violated all the time; but the very act of complaining about a violation appeals to them.

Fraser's critique is therefore best understood as modifying the ideal rather than abandoning it. Participatory parity, plurality of publics, recognition of varied communicative styles, refusal to bracket structural inequalities: these are corrections to the Habermasian model that preserve its regulative function while making it more empirically and politically tractable. Thompson's reformulation of the model for "mediated publicness" (Thompson, 1993: 188) is a further necessary correction: the ideal must be reframed for dispersed, screen-mediated communication rather than face-to-face conversation. The result is a richer normative apparatus: not "do all participants achieve consensus through rational argument?" but "do all relevant publics interact under conditions that allow peer participation and substantive contestation?"

In conclusion, the ideal speech situation is a coherent regulative ideal. It is unattainable in any pure form, but unattainability is part of what makes an ideal regulative. Its function is to provide criteria for identifying failures and to motivate institutional reform along the lines Scannell and Collins specify (Scannell, 1989: 136; Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The fantasy charge mistakes the genre of the claim. With Fraser's amendments (Fraser, 1992: 119, 137), Thompson's reformulation (Thompson, 1993: 188), and McGuigan's reminder that the ideal must be institutionally embodied to be more than phantom (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183), the ideal speech situation remains the most articulate norm we have for thinking about democratic communication.

### Essay 6: Compare and contrast Habermas's account of the public sphere with the Frankfurt School's culture-industry thesis.

Habermas's account of the public sphere and Adorno and Horkheimer's culture-industry thesis share a Frankfurt-School lineage and a Marxist starting point, but they differ sharply in their assessment of agency, communication, and the prospects for democratisation. I argue that the two should be read as complementary rather than rival accounts: the culture-industry thesis describes the mechanisms by which the public sphere is refeudalised, while Habermas's account preserves a normative horizon the culture-industry thesis abandons.

Both accounts begin from Marx's insight that the ruling class controls the mental means of production and that the base shapes the superstructure (lecture summary, *Context*). For Adorno and Horkheimer, writing in the *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, the culture industry standardises cultural products, produces "pseudo-individualisation" through superficial differentiation, and offers cathartic satisfactions that defuse rather than express discontent. Mass culture, on this account, is a system of integration: it absorbs the working class into capitalism by producing manageable desires and false subjectivities. The lecture's "Context" slide summarises this scepticism: "Sceptical on revolution, state-capitalism / fascism, reduces role of agency (perhaps some in the Bad Infinity, avant-garde)".

Habermas, working in the second generation of the Frankfurt School, is committed to the same critique of capitalist culture but resists the totalising pessimism. Where Adorno sees the culture industry as a sealed system of integration, Habermas locates an alternative communicative logic that survives in the public sphere as a space of rational-critical debate (Habermas, 1989: 56). Walter Benjamin's account of mechanical reproduction, which Habermas inherits, opens the door: "mass production gives possibility of different perceptions, new means of production so possible agency within culture" (lecture summary). The public sphere is the conceptual continuation of this opening.

The contrasts can be sharpened along three axes. First, agency: the culture-industry thesis minimises audience agency; Habermas, while criticised by the lecture's fourth point for treating recipients as "passive consumers unless they have access to the Public Sphere", nonetheless theorises a space of active citizen deliberation. Second, the assessment of mass media: for Adorno, radio and film are vehicles of the culture industry; for Habermas, "newspapers and magazines, radio and television are the media of the public sphere" (Habermas, 2006: 73). On Habermas's account they can serve either critical or integrative functions, depending on institutional design, a point Scannell develops directly with respect to public service broadcasting (Scannell, 1989: 136) and Collins with respect to the limits of market provision (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). Third, the normative horizon: Adorno offers immanent critique and aesthetic refusal but no positive political programme; Habermas offers the ideal speech situation and the demand for an institutional base.

Yet the two are not opposites. Habermas's refeudalisation thesis describes phenomena Adorno would recognise: "in the form of 'publicity' it wins public prestige for people or affairs, thus making them worthy of acclamation in a climate of nonpublic opinion" (Habermas, 2006: 77) is precisely the culture industry at work in the political domain. The tabloidisation of the late 20th-century press (slide 14), the consolidation of ownership under press barons (slide 13, with Kevin Williams's *Get Me a Murder a Day!*), the "Planet Murdoch" map (slide 19), the technofeudalism image of Google, Netflix, Meta, Apple and Amazon as medieval lords (slide 19 of the deck), and the "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) all instantiate culture-industry dynamics within what was once meant to be the public sphere. McGuigan reads this convergence directly: the public sphere in late capitalism is largely phantom, sustained as a regulative idea while its conditions are systematically obstructed by commercial concentration (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183). Habermas's diagnosis is in fact the culture-industry analysis applied to political communication, and McGuigan is the figure who has integrated the two readings most carefully.

The two accounts diverge on prognosis. Adorno is fundamentally pessimistic; only the avant-garde, with its refusal of standardisation, holds open a faint trace of negation. Habermas is cautiously optimistic: the institutional conditions of public communication can be reformed; deliberative institutions can be designed; a "third model of media ownership" beyond state and market is conceivable (slide 20, *Contemporary Relevance*; defended in detail by Scannell, 1989: 136 and Collins, 1993: 250, 258). Fraser's pluralisation, with the subaltern counterpublics she names, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), adds further empirical bite to Habermas's optimism by showing that culture-industry logics do not absorb all opposition.

A further point of contrast concerns rationality. Adorno is famously suspicious of instrumental rationality, which he sees as the deep logic of both the Enlightenment and the culture industry. Habermas's distinction between communicative and strategic action is a direct response: it preserves a non-instrumental form of rationality (oriented to mutual understanding rather than success) that can ground democratic legitimacy. Fraser then complicates this further: even communicative rationality, in its valorisation of "calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion" (Fraser, 1992: 119), can encode dominant cultural norms. The trajectory from Adorno through Habermas to Fraser is one of progressively more nuanced accounts of how reason and culture intersect.

For contemporary application, the two accounts can be combined. The slide deck's image of social-media ownership concentration (slide 18) is best read as a culture-industry analysis: standardised content, pseudo-individualised feeds, cathartic outrage cycles. But the persistence of Fraser's counterpublics, the parallel discursive arenas in which subordinated groups develop oppositional interpretations of their identities, interests, and needs (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), is best read in Habermasian-Fraserian terms: the public sphere is multiple, contested, and still capable of opposition. Thompson's notion of mediated publicness (Thompson, 1993: 188) bridges the two readings, registering both the dispersal of address that culture-industry analysis emphasises and the persistence of counterpublic agency that Fraser insists on.

In conclusion, Habermas and the culture-industry thesis describe the same phenomena from opposite normative orientations. Adorno emphasises domination; Habermas emphasises the conditions of possible democratisation. The culture industry diagnoses what refeudalisation looks like in cultural production; the public sphere supplies the normative criterion by which we know it is refeudalisation rather than emancipation. With Fraser's amendments and McGuigan's integrative reading, the public-sphere tradition becomes more empirically realistic and more politically tractable, while the culture-industry thesis remains indispensable as a description of the dominant tendency that the public sphere must contest.

### Essay 7: "Plurality is not a democratic deficit but a democratic necessity." Discuss with reference to Fraser.

Fraser's claim that plurality is a democratic necessity rather than a deficit is the most important reformulation of public-sphere theory of the late twentieth century. I argue that it succeeds against Habermas's singular bourgeois model, that the case is strengthened by the empirical record of subaltern counterpublics, and that it requires only one careful qualification: plurality is not the same as fragmentation.

Habermas's classical formulation, as the lecture notes, assumes a single comprehensive public sphere: "a body of private persons can assemble to discuss matters of public concern or common interest" (lecture summary). The implicit ideal is a unified discursive space oriented to consensus through the most rational argument. Fraser's response, summarised by the lecture, is that "in stratified societies, a single public sphere tends to reflect the perspectives and interests of those already in power, while marginalising countervailing viewpoints. For Fraser, plurality is not a democratic deficit but a democratic necessity. The expectation that all participants converge in one space on shared norms of discourse risks silencing oppositional voices rather than integrating them" (cf. Fraser, 1992).

Three arguments support Fraser's case. The first is structural. In any society with significant inequalities (class, gender, race, sexuality) the discursive norms that prevail in a single shared public will tend to reflect dominant group practices. Fraser identifies "calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion" as the marked styles of the bourgeois public (Fraser, 1992: 119), which disadvantage groups whose communicative repertoires differ. If the public sphere is singular, subordinated groups must either adopt these norms (at the cost of authenticity and political effectiveness) or be marginalised. Plurality opens the possibility of subaltern counterpublics, the feminist publics, Black publics, and queer publics that Fraser names (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), in which alternative discursive practices can develop. The second is functional. Counterpublics serve a dual function: spaces of withdrawal and regroupment, and bases for agitation directed toward wider publics (Fraser, 1992: 123). Without the former, subordinated groups cannot collectively articulate interests that have not yet been recognised by the dominant public. Without the latter, those interests cannot enter wider political contention. The third is constitutive. Democratic deliberation, Fraser argues, is inherently conflictual rather than consensual (Fraser, 1992: 137). Conflict requires plural sources of contestation; consensus prematurely closes the space of legitimate disagreement.

The cases Fraser herself uses bear out the argument. The classical bourgeois public/private distinction defined domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work as "private" matters of family and sexuality, off-limits to public-sphere deliberation (Fraser, 1992: 111). Feminist counterpublics refused that classification; they articulated each of these as a matter of public concern, and in doing so redrew the boundary of the political. This is the dual function in action: a space of withdrawal in which experiences could be collectively named, then agitation directed outward at the wider public sphere. Fraser's own naming of Black publics and queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122) extends the same structural argument to other axes of subordination.

Fraser's notion of participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137) is the normative criterion at the systemic level: the question is not whether each counterpublic is benign but whether the overall system of publics allows peer participation by all relevant groups. This is plurality with structure, not fragmentation.

The qualification worth adding concerns the architecture of contemporary communication. Thompson's "mediated publicness" thesis (Thompson, 1993: 188) registers that the inter-public communication Fraser's model presupposes is now conducted through dispersed, screen-mediated channels rather than face-to-face dialogue. McGuigan's "phantom public sphere" critique (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183) warns that the conditions under which counterpublics can address one another are themselves owned and shaped by the ownership coalition documented in the slide deck's "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) and "Planet Murdoch" map (slide 19). Plurality without participatory parity in the means of mass communication remains a phantom plurality.

Against this, defenders of Habermas might argue that the singular public sphere is itself the regulative ideal of inter-public communication: it is where claims from counterpublics get adjudicated. Fraser's response is that the singular public is itself shaped by dominant interests, so it cannot serve as a neutral arbiter. The alternative she sketches is a network of strong publics (deliberative-decision-making bodies) and weak publics (opinion-forming counterpublics), connected by ongoing contestation and procedural conditions of participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137).

The institutional implications are substantial. The Contemporary Relevance slide (slide 20) flags the demand for a third model of media ownership beyond state and market. Scannell defends public service broadcasting as the institutional form most able to support inter-public communication on terms of access and equal address (Scannell, 1989: 136). Collins frames the same defence in terms of market failure (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The challenge for democratic theory after Fraser is to design institutions that support both robust counterpublics and inter-public communication, against the centrifugal logics of concentrated commercial ownership.

In conclusion, Fraser's claim succeeds. Plurality is a democratic necessity because singular publics encode dominant interests, because subordinated groups need spaces of counter-discursive elaboration, and because democratic deliberation is constitutively conflictual. The cases Fraser names, the depoliticisation of domestic violence, reproductive rights and care work under the bourgeois public/private distinction (Fraser, 1992: 111), and the feminist, Black and queer publics that refuse that depoliticisation (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), vindicate the argument. The necessary qualification, that plurality must be institutionally supported to amount to more than phantom plurality, points to the next task: building infrastructures of the kind Scannell and Collins describe that can hold a plural system of publics together without flattening it into the dominant frame.

### Essay 9: "What counts as a matter of public concern is itself a political question" (Fraser). Discuss with reference to feminist critiques of the public/private divide.

Fraser's claim is the most politically consequential element of her critique of Habermas, and feminist theory provides its most powerful demonstration. I argue that the public/private divide is itself a political construct whose specific drawings systematically depoliticise issues that disproportionately affect women, that the cases Fraser herself names, domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work, vindicate the claim, and that the implications reach well beyond gender to other axes of inequality.

The Habermasian model, as the lecture summary explains, relies on a sharp separation between public matters of common concern and private matters of family, sexuality, and domestic life. The bourgeois public sphere of 18th-century coffeehouses, depicted on slide 8 of the SC301 deck, was constitutively organised around this division. Property, religion, and household authority were treated as private (outside the scope of public deliberation) while commerce, statecraft, and (selectively) civil society constituted the public. Habermas's later work modifies this, but the original model carries the assumption that the public/private line is given rather than constructed.

Fraser's response, summarised by the lecture, is that "this division has historically functioned to depoliticise issues that disproportionately affect women and other subordinated groups. By defining matters such as domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work as 'private,' dominant publics excluded them from legitimate political debate. Fraser insists that what counts as a matter of public concern is itself a political question, not a neutral or pre-given boundary" (Fraser, 1992: 111).

The three cases Fraser herself names make the claim concrete. First, domestic violence. The bourgeois public/private distinction defined "what happens in the home" as private. The reclassification of domestic violence as a public-policy matter, criminalised and addressed by state services, was the political achievement of feminist counterpublics over decades. The boundary moved, and it moved because it was contested. Second, reproductive rights. Whether reproductive access is a public-health matter (and therefore a state responsibility) or a private moral question (and therefore beyond state intervention except to restrict) is precisely a political question, and one whose answer has shifted with the strength and reach of feminist counterpublic agitation. Third, care work. The valorisation of unpaid caring labour (historically performed by women and treated as private "love") as economic and political input has been a continuing feminist project, evident in debates over parental leave, social-care funding, and the gendered impact of austerity.

Each example confirms Fraser's claim in two distinct senses. Empirically, the public/private boundary has moved over time, in response to organised political pressure, and the direction of movement has been broadly toward including issues affecting women. Conceptually, the boundary is not given by nature or by a-political reasoning; it is a stake of contestation. The very acts of arguing for inclusion and of resisting it are political acts, and the criteria for "public concern" are themselves shaped by which publics are heard.

The counterpublic dimension is crucial. The agitation that moved the boundary did not come from within the singular bourgeois public sphere; it came from the feminist publics, Black publics and queer publics Fraser names (Fraser, 1992: 122-123) and from analogous counterpublic formations. These performed the dual function Fraser specifies: spaces of withdrawal where shared experiences could be collectively named, and bases for agitation that addressed wider publics and demanded policy change (Fraser, 1992: 123).

The implications extend beyond gender. The public/private boundary similarly depoliticised racial discrimination, workplace conditions, and environmental harm. Each shift required counterpublic agitation to politicise what dominant publics treated as outside political deliberation. The principle Fraser articulates is general: "what counts as a matter of public concern is itself a political question, not a neutral or pre-given boundary" (Fraser, 1992: 111).

A possible objection is that Fraser's claim risks "everything is political" in a way that loses analytical traction. If every boundary is political, what is left to be private? Two responses. First, "the boundary is political" is not the same as "there is no boundary". Privacy as a value (bodily autonomy, freedom from surveillance, the integrity of intimate relationships) is itself politically constituted and politically defended. Fraser's point is that the location of the boundary is contestable, not that boundaries should be abolished. Second, recognising that the boundary is political does not collapse all distinctions; it requires that we make distinctions explicitly, with reasons, in conditions of participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137), rather than treating the existing boundary as natural.

A Habermasian defence might insist that the public/private distinction is operationally useful even if its specific contents are contested. This is partly right: deliberation needs to bound what is being deliberated about. But Habermas's original model treats the boundary as broadly given (Habermas, 1989: 87) rather than as itself a stake of deliberation, and that is what Fraser corrects. McGuigan's reading of the bourgeois public sphere as a phantom (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180) reinforces the correction: the specific drawings of the public/private line in the 18th-century bourgeois settlement were neither universal nor natural but historically and economically specific.

In conclusion, Fraser's claim is vindicated by the feminist record. The public/private boundary is itself a political question, and the specific drawings of that boundary have historically depoliticised issues that disproportionately affect women, the working class, and minoritised groups. The successful reclassification of domestic violence, reproductive rights, and care work as public concerns (Fraser, 1992: 111) demonstrates both the contingency of the boundary and the role of counterpublic agitation in moving it. The implication is not that everything is public, but that the location of the line must itself be subject to deliberation under conditions of participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137), exactly the more demanding ideal Fraser puts in place of Habermas's bracketed singular public.

### Essay 10: Critically evaluate the claim that the bourgeois public sphere was "never as inclusive, egalitarian, or disinterested as it claimed to be."

I argue that the claim is essentially correct, well supported by historical and feminist scholarship, and central to Fraser's reconstruction of the public-sphere tradition. The qualification is that Habermas himself acknowledged the historical limits of the bourgeois public sphere; the more substantive disagreement is about whether the exclusions were accidental or structural, and what follows for the model's normative use today.

Habermas's account, as the lecture summarises, treats the bourgeois public sphere as a "public forum for rational and critical debate" emerging "in the 17th and 18th centuries, particularly in Britain where there occurred a rapidly developing capitalist economy, a reconstituted public state, and the establishment of a bourgeois civil society." Its institutional sites were coffeehouses, salons, debating societies, and the literary and political periodical press. The principle was universal access; the practice, by the criticism even Habermas accepts, was selective.

Fraser's sharper version of the criticism, summarised by the lecture, is that "the bourgeois public sphere was never as inclusive, egalitarian, or disinterested as it claimed to be. Women, the working class, racialised minorities, and other subordinated groups were structurally excluded, not accidentally marginalised" (Fraser, 1992: 115).

Three dimensions of the critique are worth examining. The first is empirical. The London coffeehouses of the early 18th century, depicted on slide 8 of the SC301 deck, were overwhelmingly male spaces. Property qualifications restricted political participation; the institutions of the public sphere (universities, debating societies, learned journals) were structured around male, propertied, white participation. Working-class voices entered via the radical press (slide 10: *The Poor Man's Guardian*, 1831, was "established contrary to 'law,' to try the power of 'might' against 'right'"), but as slides 11 and 12 note, the radical press was suppressed and then displaced by advertising-funded popular newspapers. Colonised peoples were structurally outside the European bourgeois public sphere altogether.

The second is conceptual. Fraser's argument is not only that specific people were excluded but that the discursive norms of the bourgeois public sphere were themselves not neutral. "Discursive norms that privilege particular styles of speech, such as calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion, often function to disadvantage subordinated groups whose communicative practices may differ" (Fraser, 1992: 119). The valorisation of dispassion, abstract argument, and the suppression of personal experience encoded the communicative style of a particular class and gender as the universal standard. Even if more people had been formally admitted, the terms of admission would have continued to disadvantage those whose communicative practices differed.

The third is structural. The bourgeois public sphere was not accidentally exclusive; it was constitutively built on exclusions. The propertied gentleman who could afford the leisure and education for coffeehouse debate depended on the unpaid domestic labour of his wife and household servants, on the surplus extracted from colonial economies, and on the wage labour of the working class. The capacity to participate was materially produced by exclusions. This is the deepest point: claims to universality often masked the particular interests of dominant groups, exactly as Fraser argues: "claims presented as universal often mask the particular interests of dominant groups. Subordinated groups, by contrast, are frequently criticised for being 'self-interested' when they articulate their needs" (Fraser, 1992: 137). McGuigan reinforces the point: the bourgeois public sphere was always also a phantom public sphere, a regulative idea sustained in conditions that systematically obstructed its realisation (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180).

A Habermasian defence is available. Habermas's later work acknowledges historical exclusions explicitly and shifts the weight of the argument onto the normative ideal: the bourgeois public sphere matters because it produced the principle of universal accessibility, even if it failed to realise it. The principle then becomes a critical resource for expanding inclusion. The demand that the franchise be extended, that women be admitted to public life, that workers' voices be heard, that colonial subjects be granted political agency, is itself a Habermasian demand. On this reading, the claim that the bourgeois public sphere was not inclusive is correct as history but does not undermine its normative legacy.

This defence is partially convincing. The principle of accessibility, the demand for public justification, and the institutional infrastructure of the Fourth Estate are real legacies, and they have served as resources for inclusion movements. The history of expanded suffrage, civil rights, and minority recognition has been partly fought in Habermasian terms.

But Fraser's response is also forceful. The principle of universal accessibility is operationalised through specific discursive norms; if those norms continue to encode dominant styles, the formal extension of access does not produce real participatory parity. The history of women's entry into political life, for example, includes repeated experiences of being dismissed as "emotional" or "self-interested" when they articulate gendered grievances, a continuation of the bourgeois public sphere's discursive logic into formally inclusive institutions. The principle of accessibility cannot be detached from the form of communication it presupposes.

The contemporary stakes are visible in the slide deck's own examples. The "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) and the "Planet Murdoch" map (slide 19) document the structural conditions under which the bourgeois public sphere's discursive privilege is reproduced into the present. The subaltern counterpublics Fraser names, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), develop discursive registers, testimonial, vernacular, embodied, that challenge the privileging of "calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion" (Fraser, 1992: 119) and routinely meet with the same dismissals that the bourgeois public sphere applied to outsiders.

A historical refinement worth noting: the bourgeois public sphere was always interacting with plebeian counterpublics, even if Habermas's original account marginalised them. The slide deck's own attention to *The Poor Man's Guardian* (slide 10) acknowledges this; Fraser's pluralisation generalises it into a structural argument. There was never one bourgeois public sphere, but a plurality of competing publics, with the bourgeois sphere claiming a universality it did not possess (Fraser, 1992: 115).

The implication for using the public-sphere concept today is twofold. First, Habermas's normative core (accessible, accountable, reason-giving communication) survives, but only when paired with Fraser's amendments. Second, the historical account must be revised: the bourgeois public sphere was one public among others, claiming universality while resting on systematic exclusion. The concept is most useful when it identifies the conditions under which a plural system of publics could operate in conditions of participatory parity, not when it nostalgises an 18th-century moment that never existed in the idealised form.

In conclusion, the claim is essentially correct. The bourgeois public sphere was never as inclusive, egalitarian, or disinterested as it claimed to be; the exclusions were structural rather than accidental; the discursive norms it valorised were themselves not neutral; and its claims to universality masked particular interests. Habermas's normative legacy survives this critique, but only when reconstructed along Fraserian lines. The lesson for contemporary use is that public-sphere theory must be empirically honest about the conditions of communication and politically attentive to participatory parity across a plurality of publics.

### Essay 11: Critically assess McGuigan's claim that the contemporary public sphere is a "phantom" public sphere.

McGuigan's claim that the contemporary public sphere is largely a "phantom" public sphere (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183) is a powerful critical sharpening of Habermas's refeudalisation thesis. I argue that the phantom-public-sphere reading is substantially correct as a description of the dominant tendency, that it should be read in tandem with rather than against Habermas's original thesis, and that Fraser's pluralism and Scannell and Collins's defence of public service broadcasting together specify what would be required to make the public sphere substantive rather than phantom.

McGuigan's argument is that the public sphere in late capitalism survives chiefly as a regulative idea, a rhetoric of openness and accountability whose substantive conditions, access, equal voice, freedom from owner caprice, are systematically obstructed by the very economic and political arrangements that proclaim it. The phantom public sphere is one in which "the form of public opinion making is maintained, while the substance of the public sphere has long ago been undermined" (Habermas, 2006: 78). McGuigan generalises Habermas's diagnosis into a description of cultural production under conditions of advertising-funded media, concentrated ownership, post-Fordist casualisation of cultural labour, and the colonisation of communicative space by commercial calculation (McGuigan, 1996: 180, 183).

The empirical case for the phantom-public-sphere reading is documented across the SC301 slide deck. Slide 19 shows the "Planet Murdoch" map of News Corp interests across the UK, US, Eastern Europe, Asia, Latin America, and Australasia. Slide 18 shows the "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image: the Ellisons, Zuckerberg, the Murdochs, the Sinclair family, Bezos, Apple, Musk, Alphabet, and Altman's OpenAI. Slide 19 of the deck also shows the "Technofeudalism" image of Google, Netflix, Meta, Apple and Amazon as medieval lords. Slide 22 shows Musk's "Violent Speech Policy" announcement with the formula "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach". Each of these images instantiates the structural phenomenon McGuigan diagnoses: the form of openness maintained while the substance is curated by ownership.

The historical case is just as strong. The slide deck's account of the press, from the radical *Poor Man's Guardian* of 1831 (slide 10) through its decline (slide 11), advertising-funded popular newspapers (slide 12), press barons (slide 13, with Kevin Williams's *Get Me a Murder a Day!*), and late-C20th tabloidisation (slide 14), traces a trajectory in which the rhetorical claims of press freedom (slide 9) survive while the conditions of citizen-oriented journalism are systematically eroded by commercial concentration. This is McGuigan's argument in compressed form: the public sphere has been phantom for a long time, and the contemporary moment intensifies rather than originates the structure.

Three qualifications are necessary. First, the phantom-public-sphere reading is descriptively correct as a characterisation of the dominant tendency but should not be taken to deny that counterpublic agitation occurs. Fraser's pluralism is the corrective: the subaltern counterpublics she names, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), persist within and against the phantom architecture, performing the dual function of withdrawal and agitation (Fraser, 1992: 123). The public sphere is phantom in its dominant institutional form but not in its plural counterpublic life. The lecture's class 13 question, "What might be the 'subaltern counterpublics' for these groups?" (slide 23), invites exactly this pluralising correction.

Second, the phantom-public-sphere reading should be supplemented by Thompson's notion of "mediated publicness" (Thompson, 1993: 188). The architecture of contemporary publicness is not face-to-face but dispersed across screen-mediated channels. This complicates McGuigan's diagnosis: mediated publicness has powers as well as pathologies, and the phantom character of the institutional form does not exhaust the participatory dimensions Thompson registers. McGuigan's account is best read as an account of the institutional level, with Thompson's account complementing it at the level of practice.

Third, the phantom-public-sphere thesis needs a positive complement. McGuigan diagnoses the obstruction; Scannell and Collins specify the institutional form that would substantively realise the regulative idea. Scannell's defence of public service broadcasting argues that it brings into being "a culture in common to whole populations" and admits "the unsorted everyday voices of ordinary listeners and viewers" into political life on terms unprecedented in the older bourgeois public sphere (Scannell, 1989: 136). Collins frames the same defence in terms of market failure: market provision systematically under-supplies citizenship-oriented broadcasting, so the public-service form is justified on economic as well as cultural-democratic grounds (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The Contemporary Relevance slide (slide 20) names this directly: a third model of media ownership beyond state intervention and free-market economics. Without this third-model complement, the phantom-public-sphere thesis risks pessimism without remedy.

A potential objection: if the public sphere has always been phantom, what work does the concept still do? Two responses. First, the regulative role of the concept does not depend on its descriptive accuracy. The public sphere is most useful as a critical norm precisely when it is descriptively false; the gap between ideal and reality is the analytical fuel of public-sphere theory. McGuigan's intervention is to insist that we name the gap honestly rather than mistaking the rhetoric for the reality. Second, "phantom" is not "absent". McGuigan's argument is that the substantive conditions of public communication are obstructed, not that no public communication occurs. Counterpublic agitation, public service broadcasting, and the persistent demand for accountability all keep the regulative idea alive in the face of its phantom institutional form.

In conclusion, McGuigan's claim is substantially correct. The public sphere in contemporary cultural capitalism is largely a phantom public sphere, a rhetoric of openness maintained while its substantive conditions are systematically obstructed by concentrated ownership, advertising dependence, and the casualisation of cultural labour (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183). The diagnosis should be read in tandem with Habermas's original refeudalisation thesis (Habermas, 2006: 77), supplemented by Fraser's pluralism (Fraser, 1992: 122-123, 137) and Thompson's mediated-publicness reformulation (Thompson, 1993: 188), and complemented by Scannell and Collins's specification of public service broadcasting as the third-model institutional remedy (Scannell, 1989: 136; Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The phantom-public-sphere thesis is a critical sharpening of the structural transformation Habermas first diagnosed, and remains the most honest description available of the dominant tendency.

### Essay 12: "The structural transformation Habermas described in 1962 has only intensified under digital capitalism." Discuss.

The claim is broadly correct: the structural transformation Habermas described, the displacement of rational-critical public communication by commercialised, manipulated, and concentrated forms, has intensified under contemporary cultural capitalism, although the mechanisms have changed and counterpublic possibilities complicate the picture. Third, the third-model institutional response defended by Scannell (1989: 136) and Collins (1993: 250, 258) remains alive. The Contemporary Relevance slide (slide 20) identifies it as the political answer to intensification: public service broadcasting, neither state-controlled nor purely commercial, capable of bringing into being a culture in common and admitting ordinary voices into political life on terms the structural transformation otherwise denies. Scannell's argument is that public service broadcasting genuinely democratised access to common cultural and political life; Collins's complementary argument is that market provision systematically fails to deliver citizenship-oriented broadcasting, so public-service intervention is justified on economic as well as cultural-democratic grounds.

The political implications are several. First, the demand for the third model of media ownership becomes more urgent and more concrete as commercial concentration deepens. Second, the analysis must hold both levels at once: refeudalisation at the level of infrastructure (the slide deck's images of ownership), pluralisation at the level of publics (Fraser's counterpublic framework), mediated publicness as the architectural reformulation (Thompson), phantom character as the diagnosis (McGuigan), and the third-model institutional response (Scannell, Collins). Third, the slide deck's reminder that one criticism of Habermas is that refeudalisation may simply be "the continuation of capitalism" (lecture summary, third criticism) is well taken: the medieval metaphor of refeudalisation captures something real about the privatisation of what was meant to be public, but the specifically capitalist drivers remain in the foreground.

In conclusion, the claim is correct as far as it goes. The structural transformation has intensified under contemporary cultural capitalism in respect of infrastructure concentration, the substitution of Publizität for substantive openness, and the persistence of advertising-funded commercial logic across the trajectory the slide deck traces from the press through broadcasting to platforms (Habermas, 1989: 142, 232; Habermas, 2006: 77-78). It has not simply continued; counterpublics have persisted (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), the architecture of address has reorganised into mediated publicness (Thompson, 1993: 188), and the third-model institutional response remains an open political possibility (Scannell, 1989: 136; Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The most accurate description is intensification at the level of infrastructure, pluralisation at the level of publics, and a phantom public sphere as the result (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183).

### Essay 13: To what extent does Habermas's public sphere depend on a specifically bourgeois conception of rationality?

I argue that Habermas's public sphere depends substantially on a bourgeois conception of rationality but that the dependence is reformable rather than total: with Fraser's amendments and a more capacious account of communicative styles, the normative core (reason-giving, accountability, participatory parity) survives without the specifically bourgeois discursive trappings.

The bourgeois conception of rationality, as it operates in Habermas's classical formulation, has several features. Reasons are explicit and propositional. Argument is preferred over narrative, testimony, or affective appeal. Abstract claims are valorised over particular grievances. Dispassion is treated as a discursive virtue and emotion as a disqualification. Universality is the normative horizon: claims should be defensible to anyone, regardless of position. These features cluster in the discursive style of the 18th-century coffeehouse depicted on slide 8 of the SC301 deck, and in the editorial pages of broadsheet newspapers (Habermas, 1989: 56). They are not the only available styles of public speech, but in the bourgeois public sphere they were the standard against which other styles were judged.

Fraser's critique, summarised by the lecture, is precise on this point: "Discursive norms that privilege particular styles of speech, such as calm rationality, abstraction, and dispassion, often function to disadvantage subordinated groups whose communicative practices may differ" (Fraser, 1992: 119). The valorisation of these norms is not neutral; it encodes a particular cultural style, historically associated with educated bourgeois men, as the universal standard of legitimate communication. Groups whose communicative practices include narrative testimony, emotional register, vernacular humour, ritualised collective speech, or embodied performance are disadvantaged when they attempt to participate.

The historical evidence is clear. The very emergence of the bourgeois public sphere coincided with the exclusion of women from political conversation on the grounds that they were too emotional, too particular, too embodied to participate (Habermas, 1989: 87, registers the gendered specificity of the bourgeois settlement). Working-class radical traditions developed alternative styles (public oration, collective song, demonstration) that the bourgeois public sphere treated as mob action rather than legitimate political speech. The slide deck's *Poor Man's Guardian* image (slide 10), with its declaration that the paper was "established contrary to 'law,' to try the power of 'might' against 'right'", registers a different, more combative discursive style than that of the coffeehouse, and the subsequent slides (11, 12) trace how this counterpublic register was suppressed and then displaced. McGuigan's account of the structural transformation reads this trajectory as the suppression of plural communicative styles in favour of a commercially-mediated single register (McGuigan, 1996: 4).

Yet the dependence is not total. The Habermasian normative core can be separated from the bourgeois discursive style. The core consists of: communication oriented to mutual understanding rather than strategic manipulation; speakers presupposing the conditions of accountability (sincerity, truth, normative rightness); reasons offered and accepted on their merits rather than coerced; access for all relevant parties; equal voice; freedom from domination. None of these requires that "reason" take the specifically bourgeois form of calm propositional argument. A testimonial register can be accountable; an emotional register can be reason-giving; a vernacular style can presuppose conditions of communicative action.

Fraser's reformulation does the work of separating the normative core from the discursive style. Her notion of participatory parity (Fraser, 1992: 137) asks whether members of a polity can interact as peers under the conditions of communication that obtain. It does not require that they adopt a single discursive style. The subaltern counterpublics Fraser names, feminist publics, Black publics, queer publics (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), develop their own discursive practices, and the system of plural publics must allow for inter-public communication across style differences. The criterion of legitimacy shifts from "did everyone speak in calm rationality?" to "did the system of publics allow peer participation, recognition, and reasoned engagement across styles?"

Scannell's account of public service broadcasting offers a concrete instance of how the institutional public sphere can recognise communicative styles other than bourgeois rationality. Scannell argues that broadcasting brought "the unsorted everyday voices of ordinary listeners and viewers" into political life on terms the older bourgeois public sphere did not allow (Scannell, 1989: 136). This is not the abandonment of reason-giving; it is the recognition that reason can be given in vernacular as well as in propositional registers. Public service broadcasting is, in this respect, partly the institutional realisation of Fraser's reform: it admits stylistic plurality within an architecture of shared cultural and political life.

Thompson's notion of "mediated publicness" (Thompson, 1993: 188) makes the corresponding theoretical move. The contemporary public sphere is dispersed across a range of communicative architectures and stylistic registers, not gathered in a single conversational space; the bourgeois-rational coffeehouse style is one register among many. Thompson's framework allows the normative core of communicative action to be applied across the plural registers Fraser identifies rather than restricted to one historically specific style.

A countervailing concern is that abandoning the bourgeois-rational standard altogether risks accepting any speech as legitimate, including manipulation, coercive rhetoric, and bad-faith argument. This is the worry behind defences of "civility" and "balance." The Fraserian response is that the substantive criterion is participatory parity and respect for others' capacity to participate, not adherence to a particular style. Manipulation, coercion, and bad faith fail this test regardless of style; testimonial witness, emotional register, and vernacular humour pass it provided they respect interlocutors' parity.

In conclusion, Habermas's classical formulation depends substantially on a bourgeois conception of rationality, and this dependence has been a source of structural exclusion (Fraser, 1992: 115, 119). The dependence is reformable: separating the normative core (reason-giving, accountability, parity) from the specific discursive style (calm, abstract, dispassionate, propositional) yields a more defensible framework. Fraser's pluralisation (Fraser, 1992: 122-123, 137), Thompson's mediated-publicness reformulation (Thompson, 1993: 188), Scannell's defence of broadcasting as a register-expanding institution (Scannell, 1989: 136), and McGuigan's diagnosis of the phantom character of the bourgeois settlement (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180) together complete the reform. What survives is a normatively powerful, empirically tractable account of democratic communication that does not require participants to abandon their own communicative repertoires.

### Essay 14: Discuss the role of the press as a key institution of the public sphere, with reference to its rise, decline, and contemporary crisis.

The press has been the central institution of the public sphere in Habermas's account and remains so, even as its forms, ownership structures, and political economy have transformed. I argue that the press's trajectory tracked across the SC301 slide deck, from the radical *Poor Man's Guardian* of 1831 through advertising-funded popular newspapers, press-baron consolidation, late-C20th tabloidisation, and contemporary "Planet Murdoch" cross-national concentration, tracks the structural transformation of the public sphere, and that the analysis is best developed with Habermas, Fraser, McGuigan, Thompson, Scannell, and Collins held together.

Habermas's account of the press, as the lecture slides indicate, begins with the emergence of modern journalism alongside the bourgeois public sphere in the 17th and 18th centuries (Habermas, 1989: 56). The press transformed from "mere compilations of notices" into "bearers and leaders of public opinion, weapons of party politics" (Karl Bücher, quoted in Habermas, 2006: 76). Newspaper publishers became "dealers in public opinion", and the press became "an institution of the public itself, effective in the manner of a mediator and intensifier of public discussion, no longer a mere organ for the spreading of news but not yet the medium of a consumer culture" (Habermas, 2006: 76). The Fourth Estate doctrine (slide 8) formalised the press's constitutional role.

The lecture's slides on "The Emergence of the Press and Modern Journalism" (slide 7), "The Fourth Estate" (slide 8), and "The Notion of Press Freedom" (slide 9) track this rise. Press freedom is the institutional condition of the public sphere's communicative function: without freedom to publish, the conditions of the ideal speech situation cannot be met. The silenced-figure image on slide 9 makes the point graphically.

The radical press of the early 19th century, illustrated by *The Poor Man's Guardian* on slide 10 ("established contrary to 'law,' to try the power of 'might' against 'right'"), represents both the apex of the press as instrument of agitation and the precursor of Fraser's counterpublic concept (Fraser, 1992: 122-123). The slide deck's choice to foreground the *Poor Man's Guardian* registers exactly the kind of subaltern counterpublic press Fraser theorises.

The decline of the radical press (slide 11) was effected partly by repression (stamp duties, prosecution) and partly by competition from advertising-funded popular newspapers (slide 12). The political economy is straightforward: once newspapers depended on advertising revenue, they became responsive to advertiser-friendly content rather than to working-class political agitation. McGuigan reads this trajectory as the structural shape of capitalist cultural production: the commercial logic that hollows out the public sphere from within is more effective than direct repression (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180). The press's commercial transformation is the first major moment of structural transformation in Habermas's sense: "in the transition from the literary journalism of private individuals to the public services of the mass media, the public sphere was transformed by the influx of private interests" (Habermas, 2006: 76).

The 20th century continued this transformation through press barons and concentration of ownership. The slide deck's "Press Barons and Concentration of Ownership" (slide 13, citing Kevin Williams's *Get Me a Murder a Day! A History of Media and Communication in Britain*) and "Planet Murdoch" (slide 19) illustrate the structural pattern. The Murdoch map shows News Corp interests across the UK (the Sun, News of the World, the Sunday Times, the Times, TLS, BSkyB, Sky News, Thelondonpaper), the US (New York Post, Dow Jones, Wall Street Journal, TV Guide, Weekly Standard, MySpace, Fox Broadcasting, Fox News, 20th Century Fox), Eastern Europe (BTV Bulgaria, Imedi Television Georgia, B1 TV Romania, Fox Televizija Serbia), Asia (STAR TV, Tata Sky, Phoenix Television), Latin America (Sky Latin America, LAPTV), and Australasia. Concentrated ownership produces editorial alignment with proprietor interests and the displacement of public-sphere norms by commercial logic.

Tabloidisation in the late C20th (slide 14) pushed the transformation further. *The Sun*, *Daily Mail*, *Mirror*, and *Express* developed a journalism of scandal, celebrity, sport, and right-wing populism, displacing political reporting. The structural-transformation diagnosis applies: the form of public communication is maintained, but the substance is undermined (Habermas, 2006: 78; Habermas, 1989: 142, 232). McGuigan's reading is direct: the contemporary press is a phantom Fourth Estate (McGuigan, 1996: 180, 183).

The contemporary update is documented on slide 18, "Social media ownership and control", which shows the "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image. The configuration extends Planet Murdoch across the platform economy: the Ellisons (CBS, Paramount+), the Sinclair family (185+ local news affiliates), Zuckerberg (Meta), the Murdochs (Fox properties, Wall Street Journal, New York Post), Bezos (Washington Post, Prime Video), Apple, Musk (X, xAI), Alphabet (Google, YouTube), Altman (OpenAI/ChatGPT). The structural transformation has not slowed; it has accelerated and globalised, and the form of public communication is now sustained by an even smaller ownership coalition than the press-baron era.

Fraser's framework adds essential dimensions. The press's historic exclusions (women, working class, racialised minorities) were never accidental but structural (Fraser, 1992: 115). The counterpublic press, including the radical press whose paradigmatic British case the slide deck foregrounds in the *Poor Man's Guardian*, developed in parallel to mainstream journalism and produced political agitation in the dual function Fraser describes (Fraser, 1992: 123).

Thompson's account of "mediated publicness" (Thompson, 1993: 188) is the necessary supplement for understanding the contemporary phase. The architecture of news consumption is no longer concentrated in a daily paper; it is dispersed across screen-mediated channels and platform feeds. This complicates the Fourth Estate model in ways Habermas's original framework did not anticipate.

The lecture's "Contemporary Relevance of the Idea of the Public Sphere" slide (slide 20) identifies the political response: focus on the institutional and material conditions of the press, demand a regulatory environment for public-interest journalism, and conceptualise a third model of media ownership beyond state and market. Scannell defends public service broadcasting as the most developed institutional form of this third model (Scannell, 1989: 136); Collins frames the case in terms of market failure (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The combined Scannell-Collins case is that the citizenship-oriented functions of the press cannot be reliably delivered by commercial markets alone, so a public-service complement is necessary.

In conclusion, the press's role as a key institution of the public sphere is historically central, normatively indispensable, and structurally in crisis. The rise (Habermasian bourgeois public sphere; counterpublic radical press in Fraser's sense), the decline (commercial concentration, advertising dependence, tabloidisation, ownership concentration documented on slides 11-14 and 19), and the contemporary configuration (the "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image on slide 18) form a coherent trajectory analysable through Habermas's structural transformation (Habermas, 1989: 142, 232; Habermas, 2006: 76-78), Fraser's pluralisation (Fraser, 1992: 122-123), McGuigan's phantom-public-sphere diagnosis (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183), Thompson's mediated-publicness reformulation (Thompson, 1993: 188), and Scannell and Collins's defence of the third-model institutional response (Scannell, 1989: 136; Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The political stakes are concrete: without institutional investment in public-interest journalism along the lines Scannell and Collins specify, the material base of democratic communication continues to erode.

### Essay 15: Critically evaluate the case for public service broadcasting as a "third model" of media ownership beyond state and market, with reference to Scannell and Collins.

The case for public service broadcasting as a "third model" of media ownership beyond state and market is the political conclusion of the SC301 lecture's "Contemporary Relevance" slide (slide 20), and it is developed most carefully by Scannell (1989: 136) and Collins (1993: 250, 258). I argue that the case is substantially correct, that Scannell and Collins together provide complementary cultural-democratic and economic justifications, and that the third-model argument is the most concrete institutional response to the refeudalisation thesis Habermas first formulated and McGuigan extends.

The third-model argument is stated directly on slide 20: the value of Habermas's public sphere thesis is that it "opens up the conceptual space for a third model of media ownership, one that is neither dominated by state intervention or free market economics". The thought is that the binary choice between state ownership (with its tendency to political control) and market ownership (with its tendency to commercial capture and the demonstrative Publizität Habermas warns about, Habermas, 2006: 77) is a false dilemma. A third institutional form is possible, and public service broadcasting in the British and Western European tradition is the most fully developed historical instance.

Scannell's cultural-democratic case is that public service broadcasting genuinely transformed access to common cultural and political life. Broadcasting, on his account, brought into being "a culture in common to whole populations" and admitted "the unsorted everyday voices of ordinary listeners and viewers" into political life on terms unprecedented in the older bourgeois public sphere (Scannell, 1989: 136). This is more than the extension of the franchise to the airwaves; it is a reorganisation of the communicative architecture so that participation is open in registers other than the propertied bourgeois-rational style of the 18th-century coffeehouse. Scannell's argument intersects directly with Fraser's pluralist critique: public service broadcasting is the institutional form in which a plurality of communicative styles can be admitted to a shared public life (Fraser, 1992: 119, 122-123).

Collins's economic case is complementary: market provision systematically under-supplies citizenship-oriented broadcasting, so public-service intervention is justified on grounds of market failure as well as cultural democracy (Collins, 1993: 250, 258). The market-failure argument is structurally important because it answers the standard economic objection to public-service institutions, that they distort competitive markets, by showing that the relevant markets fail in the first place to deliver what democratic citizenship requires. Collins's case is therefore not merely a defence of a particular cultural form but a structural argument about the limits of commercial provision in the communications field.

The case is reinforced by the slide deck's documentation of what happens when market provision is left unchecked. The "Planet Murdoch" map (slide 19) shows cross-national commercial concentration of news. The "Trump's billionaire-ally media and personal-data ecosystem" image (slide 18) shows the contemporary platform extension of this concentration. The "Technofeudalism" slide (slide 19 of the deck) shows Google, Netflix, Meta, Apple and Amazon as medieval lords. Each image documents the structural condition that the third-model argument is the response to: commercial concentration produces demonstrative Publizität rather than substantive openness (Habermas, 2006: 77), and McGuigan's "phantom public sphere" framing captures the result (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183).

Three critical considerations are necessary. First, the third-model argument is not a defence of any particular national broadcasting institution as it stands; it is a defence of the institutional form. Particular instances are open to criticism on grounds of governance, programming policy, and political independence; the third-model argument is consistent with reform of particular institutions while defending the underlying form. Second, public service broadcasting is not a complete answer to the refeudalisation thesis. The configuration of social-media ownership documented on slides 18 and 19 exceeds the scope of broadcasting; the third-model argument needs extension into the digital and platform domains if it is to address the contemporary structural transformation. Third, the third-model argument needs to be reconciled with Fraser's pluralism. Public service broadcasting, in some of its historical forms, has been criticised for privileging a particular communicative register (bourgeois-rational, metropolitan, masculine) at the expense of the subaltern counterpublics Fraser names (Fraser, 1992: 122-123). Scannell's argument addresses this directly: broadcasting genuinely democratised access to common cultural and political life (Scannell, 1989: 136), but the realisation of this democratising potential has been uneven and contested.

Thompson's notion of "mediated publicness" (Thompson, 1993: 188) provides the theoretical resource for extending the third-model argument into the digital era. The contemporary public sphere is dispersed across screen-mediated channels rather than gathered in face-to-face conversation, and the third-model institutional response needs to be adapted to that dispersed architecture. The principle remains the same: an institutional form that is neither state-controlled nor commercially captured can provide the conditions for substantive rather than phantom public communication.

A potential objection: the third-model argument is institutionally conservative. It defends a 20th-century institutional form against the structural conditions of the 21st century. The response is that the third-model argument is not nostalgic but principled. The principle, that the substantive conditions of public communication require an institutional form that is neither state-controlled nor commercially captured, is general. The specific institutional realisation can and should be reformed for changed conditions; the principle remains. Scannell's argument is about the form, not just a particular instance; Collins's market-failure argument is structural, not historical.

In conclusion, the case for public service broadcasting as a third model of media ownership beyond state and market is substantially correct. Scannell's cultural-democratic case (Scannell, 1989: 136) and Collins's market-failure case (Collins, 1993: 250, 258) are complementary justifications for the same institutional form. The third-model argument is the most concrete institutional response to the structural transformation Habermas first diagnosed (Habermas, 1989: 142, 232; Habermas, 2006: 77-78), and to the phantom-public-sphere intensification McGuigan documents (McGuigan, 1996: 4, 180, 183). Fraser's pluralism (Fraser, 1992: 122-123) and Thompson's mediated-publicness reformulation (Thompson, 1993: 188) extend the argument into the plural, dispersed conditions of contemporary publicness. Together they specify the institutional form in which the regulative idea of the public sphere can be partially realised against the dominant tendencies the SC301 slide deck documents.
