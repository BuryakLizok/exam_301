# L09, Post-Colonial Theory

## 1. Core Argument

Post-colonial theory insists that colonialism is not a regrettable side-event in the rise of European modernity but its constitutive condition. My core argument runs through four moves. First, colonialism has three intertwined faces, settlement (the displacement of indigenous populations by the colonising power), economic exploitation (the appropriation of land, labour and resources tied tightly to the rise of capitalism) and unequal power relations (Lecture 9 slides). Howard French, in *Born in Blackness*, contends that we must not "ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that the point is not to "add Africa in" but to "invert the current view" (French, 2021, [VERIFY p.?]); the Portuguese trade of Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s makes the global texture of modernity inescapable. Second, the West is constituted discursively against the Rest. Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works through binary oppositions, "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277), and these binaries are not descriptions but hierarchies which the discourse "simplifies" (Hall, 1992: 280) into a single dichotomy. Third, Said's account of Orientalism shows that "the Orient is not only adjacent to Europe; it is also the place of Europe's greatest and richest and oldest colonies… and one of its deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87) and that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89). Following Foucault, Said treats Orientalism as a discourse that "manage[s], and even produce[s], the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Fourth, the colonised are denied subjectivity. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* registers the white child's hail, "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), that fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" extends this, the figure is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), not an empirical group, and Spivak's question of subaltern speech asks whether the oppressed can articulate themselves outside the categories of the coloniser. Enrique Dussel's intervention deepens these arguments: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Post-colonial theory, then, is a critique of modernity's myth of self-origination, of its binaries, of its epistemic violence, and of its denial of voice to those it makes peripheral.

## 2. Thinkers and Concepts

- **Edward Said (1935-2003)**, Born in Jerusalem under the British Mandate, raised in Egypt, taught in New York. *Orientalism* (1978). Key concepts: Orientalism, the Orient as Europe's contrasting Other, false binary divisions, discourse/power following Foucault, "flexible positional superiority" (Said, 1978: 26), the imperial archive.
- **Stuart Hall (1932-2014)**, Jamaican-British cultural theorist. *Formations of Modernity* (1992). Key concepts: the West and the Rest, the West as historical not geographical construct, discourse as "system of representation" (Hall, 1992: 277), binary oppositions, internal others (Jews, Eastern Europeans, women).
- **Frantz Fanon (1925-1961)**, Born in Martinique, French Antilles; psychiatrist, fought in French resistance and later in Algerian liberation. *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961). Key concepts: object/subject dialectic, the colonial gaze, the denial of subjectivity, the colonial wound, decolonisation as struggle for voice.
- **Chandra Mohanty (1955–)**, Born in Mumbai; teaches at Syracuse. *Under Western Eyes* (1988). Key concepts: the third-world woman, discursive colonisation, women as "the other of the other," refusal of universal sisterhood.
- **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (1942–)**, Indian-American literary theorist. *Can the Subaltern Speak?* (1988). Key concepts: the subaltern, epistemic violence, strategic essentialism, the impossibility of pure recovered voice.
- **Howard French**, *Born in Blackness* (2021). Argues Africa is central to making modern world; inversion of perspective.
- **Enrique Dussel (1934-2023)**, Argentine-Mexican liberation philosopher. *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993). Key concepts: the myth of modernity, the colonial wound, the colonial difference, trans-modernity, the fallacy of developmentalism, 1492 as birth of modernity.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

- Treating "post-colonial" as merely "after colonialism" temporally; it is rather a theoretical stance on the continuing presence of colonial structures, including in formally independent states.
- Reducing Said to "the West invented the East". Said is careful that "the Orient is not an inert fact of nature" but also that "it would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6). The Orient exists; Orientalism is the discourse about it.
- Treating Hall's binaries as descriptive of two real groups; they are productive fictions that organise power.
- Treating the subaltern as just "the poor". The lecture flags Spivak at the end (slide 30) as raising the question of who can speak for whom; the subaltern is the structurally voiceless, not simply the materially poor.
- Forgetting that Hall warns against treating "the West" as homogeneous; "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279), and the West also has its "internal others" such as Jews, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", and Western women represented as inferior to Western men (Hall, 1992: 280).
- Forgetting the symmetry: "the Rest" is just as internally differentiated as "the West", from the Middle East and Far East to Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia (Hall, 1992: 280).
- Forgetting Dussel's point that modernity has *two* faces: an emancipatory concept and a sacrificial myth, and the critique must hold both (Dussel, 1993: 75).
- Treating French's argument as "adding Africa". French's point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4).

## 7. Past-Year Exam Answers

### 2025 Q11, How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between 'the West and the Rest'?

The distinction between the West and the Rest is, on the surface, a piece of casual geography. Post-colonial theorists from Hall onwards have argued that it is in fact a discursive machine, and they have attacked it on four fronts: showing that the West is a historical not a geographical entity; revealing the binary logic that sustains it; exposing the power relations it conceals; and producing alternative cartographies that displace it.

The first move is Hall's. In *Formations of Modernity*, Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277). Geographically, Hall notes, Japan is east but counts as West; eastern Europe is in Europe but is unsure if it is in the West; Latin America is in the western hemisphere but is filed under the Third World. "The West is as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, 1992: 276). The West is a historical construct, a name for "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277), and therefore something that was made, can be unmade, and could have been otherwise.

The second move is to expose the binaries. Hall lays out the oppositional grammar: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (lecture slide 9; Hall, 1992: 277). Each pair is a hierarchy, not just a difference. The West is the positive term; the Rest is what the West is not. Said's account of Orientalism extends this to the Eastern variant: "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88). The Orient is timeless, mysterious, feminine, decadent. Hall's key sentence is that this discourse "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, 1992: 280). The discourse collapses internal heterogeneity on both sides.

The third move is to expose power. "The relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89). Drawing on Foucault, the discourse is the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). The slide-12 categories (Iraq, EU boundaries, immigration, Islam, "Arabia") are the contemporary effects. Dussel sharpens the point: the West is the self-appointed centre of "World History" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Brexit-era immigration politics and Trump-era "shithole" framings echo it.

The fourth move is to displace the cartography. Howard French inverts the standard map by showing the Portuguese used Indian cotton to buy African gold for European coinage in the 1500s (slide 4); there is no Europe without Africa and Asia. The Gall-Peters projection (slide 32) and the "World Turned Upside Down" map (slide 33) make Mercator's distortion visible. Mohanty's critique challenges the binary inside feminism: Western women's self-image as "educated, modern, … free to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) depends on a fabricated "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" counterpart (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Fanon shows what the binary feels like from inside: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325).

Concrete materials make the challenge specific. Hall's binary pairs name the operating code. The slide-16 stereotype-set shows what the code produces. Mohanty's "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) refuses homogenisation. Fanon's railway-carriage scene is what happens when the binary is enforced on a body, and Du Bois's L8 vocabulary clarifies its psychic form: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5) is the double consciousness the West/Rest binary installs inside the colonised subject. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and Rhodes Must Fall extend these challenges into contemporary politics.

These challenges are not symmetrical successes. The Mercator still organises classroom geography; the discourse Hall describes still simplifies; the stereotypes Said catalogues still circulate. But post-colonial theorists have made the West/Rest distinction visible; they have denaturalised it. Hall, Said, Fanon, Mohanty, French and Dussel together have replaced a description with an indictment.

### 2025 Q13, Outline and assess if Southern theory addresses inequalities in the Global South.

[VERIFY SOURCE: "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in the Lecture 9 slides or the L9 readings supplied (Ashcroft/Said, Hall, Dussel). The closest source-derived equivalent is Howard French's call to "invert the current view" of who makes modernity (slide 4), Dussel's claim that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History" against a periphery (Dussel, 1993: 65), and Hall's insistence that the West is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277). I therefore answer the question using these L9 materials, treating "Southern theory" as a label for the broader project of provincialising the West that Hall, French, Mohanty, Fanon and Dussel together articulate.]

Theorising from outside the metropole, which the question labels "Southern theory", is the critical effort to displace the European centre of social theory and take seriously the intellectual production of the formerly colonised world. I outline what this project looks like in L9 and assess how far it addresses inequalities within the Global South itself.

The outline begins with Hall. The West is a "historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277), a label for "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277). A theory written from the Rest must refuse both homogenisations and insist on the internal variety of "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280). French's *Born in Blackness* offers a concrete instance: Africa is a co-producer of modernity, and "the point is not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit shows that European capital accumulation depended on African and Indian inputs from the 1500s. Dussel pushes further: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65), and theory adequate to the periphery must be "trans-modern", a "co-realization … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76).

Whether such a project addresses inequalities *within* the South is mixed. The first reason for hope: theorising from the periphery lets Southern subjects take their own concerns as starting points. Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* is exemplary, a doctor in Martinique making his own colonial experience the object of theory. Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* makes non-Western women theorists rather than informants. Haraway's L10 framing supports this methodologically: "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196), and the "god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) is precisely the Eurocentric posture Southern theory refuses. Second, it refuses universalism. Mohanty's injunction that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) cuts against generic global feminism. Third, Dussel's trans-modernity refuses to make the South a mirror image of the North. Climate-justice critiques from the Global South and vaccine-inequality debates during COVID show this Southern theoretical voice in contemporary action.

On the cautious side, "the Global South" is itself a homogenising category. Mohanty's critique applies: collapsing vastly different societies into one figure repeats the operation Hall diagnoses for the Rest, which "can equally encompass the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280). Second, Fanon warns that the colonial gaze produces doubling: the post-colonial scholar trained in metropolitan categories may still be "dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Third, Dussel cautions that the emancipatory concept of modernity is intertwined with the "irrational myth" of sacrificial violence (Dussel, 1993: 75); a theory that asserts Southern virtue against Northern vice risks producing its own sacrificial myth.

The assessment, then, is mixed. Theory from the Rest is indispensable for putting Northern theory in its place; Hall, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel make the position of the Rest legible as a theoretical position. But the project does not by itself address inequalities within the South. The internal differentiations Hall flags, the gender hierarchies Mohanty insists on theorising within specific societies, and the doublings Fanon traces require continued analytic work. Dussel's trans-modernity is the clearest refusal of simple inversion: it asks not for a Southern modernity mirroring the Northern but for "incorporative solidarity" (Dussel, 1993: 76) across centre and periphery. Whether that solidarity has been built in practice is a separate question; the conceptual resources are there, but they remain unfinished.

### 2025 Q15, Critically discuss two aspects of the Gandhian view on technology.

[VERIFY SOURCE: Gandhi and his view on technology do not appear in the Lecture 9 slides or the L9 readings supplied (Ashcroft/Said, Hall, Dussel). The brief I am working to instructs me to treat this as an L9 question, but I cannot defend any specific Gandhian textual claim from L9 materials. I therefore answer the question using only those L9 materials that bear on the broader question of technology, modernity and colonisation, and I flag explicitly that any properly Gandhian claim would need a separate source check before deployment in the exam.]

The Gandhian critique of technology, on Gandhi's own writing, insists that technology is not neutral but ethically embedded and that scale and dependency are decisive moral questions. The L9 scaffolding for both aspects comes from Hall on the West/Rest binary, Said on how European culture "manage[s], and even produce[s], the Orient" through knowledge (Said, 1978: 88-9), French's inversion of the cartography (slide 4), and Dussel on modernity's rational kernel and sacrificial myth (Dussel, 1993: 75). I treat two aspects: technology as ethical-discursive embedding, and technology as a question of scale and dependency.

The first aspect, technology as ethical and discursive embedding rather than neutral instrument, draws on Hall's reminder that modernity is the property of "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277), with technological progress on the Western side of "progress/tradition, science/magic" (slide 9). Said sharpens this: the technical sciences are part of the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Technology is never just a tool; it is part of a discursive operation that distributes rationality unevenly. Gandhi's refusal of the instrumental view, insisting technologies embed a way of life, is consonant with this. Ngũgĩ's L4 analysis of language as colonial technology generalises the move: colonial languages were not neutral media but "weapons of conquest" embedded in "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37), and a technology that distributes rationality through its very form requires "decolonising the mind". A contemporary instance is the AI/ChatGPT bias toward English and Western data, which embeds linguistic hierarchies inside ostensibly universal tools.

The strength of this aspect is that it refuses the science/magic binary Hall identifies (Hall, 1992: 277). It denies that "Western" technology is neutral and "traditional" technology backward. The weakness is the one Hall flags: the Rest is "enormously" internally differentiated, "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280), and a Gandhian critique that universalises one form of non-Western practice risks repeating in inverted form the homogenising operation Hall criticises.

The second aspect is scale and dependency. French's slide-4 example, the Portuguese trading Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s, makes the point: industrial-scale European modernity depended from the start on extractive circuits linking Africa, Asia and Europe. Gandhi's preference for small-scale, locally embedded production was a refusal of this dependency. Dussel's "myth of modernity" lists among modernity's casualties "the colonized, the slave, the woman, the ecological destruction of the earth" (Dussel, 1993: 75); scale-critique of technology is in part critique of that destruction. The Belt and Road Initiative and contemporary climate-justice debates show how the dependency question continues.

The strength of scale-and-dependency critique is that it refuses Dussel's "fallacy of developmentalism", the thinking "that the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67). It opens space for "trans-modernity" as "incorporative solidarity" across centre and periphery (Dussel, 1993: 76). The weakness is Dussel's own caution: critique of Northern modernity must not become romantic celebration of a pre-modern past. "We do not propose a critique of reason as such; but we do accept their critique of a violent, coercive, genocidal reason" (Dussel, 1993: 75). A Gandhian scale-critique that simply rejected the rational kernel of modernity along with its sacrificial myth would be one-sided.

The two aspects together articulate a critique consonant with Hall on the West/Rest discourse, Said on knowledge and rule, French on global accumulation, and Dussel on modernity's two faces. The strongest Gandhian reading poses the questions, what does this technology do to bodies, communities, ecologies, and to the periphery, rather than supplying fixed answers about which technologies to use.

### 2024 Q10, What is postcolonial theory? In what ways is it a critique of modernity?

Postcolonial theory, as L9 assembles it, is the body of work, Said in *Orientalism* (1978), Hall in *Formations of Modernity* (1992), Mohanty in *Under Western Eyes* (1984), Fanon in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), French in *Born in Blackness* (2021), Dussel in his Frankfurt Lectures (1993), with Spivak flagged at slide 30, which insists that European colonialism is not an external chapter in the story of modernity but its inner architecture. I argue postcolonial theory critiques modernity on four registers: historical, discursive, subjective and epistemological.

Historically, postcolonial theory rewrites the periodisation of modernity. The conventional story, feudalism breaks down, Reformation, Enlightenment, industrial capitalism, runs internally to Europe. Dussel claims "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity" and that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). French makes the same move: Africa is a co-producer of modernity, and the point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit breaks the internally-European story. Hall's diagnosis of the West's tendency to treat itself "as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278) is itself an ideological act.

Discursively, postcolonial theory critiques the binaries through which modernity tells its story. Hall's West and Rest works by polarisation: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277). These are the simplifying grammar of a "system of representation" that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). The L1 vocabulary of bifurcation supplies the deeper diagnosis: Hall's West/Rest is itself a bifurcation in the Whiteheadian sense, "splitting into two branches" that "creates a gulf" (Whitehead, 1933: 217; Halewood, 2014), and modernity's habit of bifurcating fact from value, nature from culture, reason from magic is exactly the operation post-colonial theory exposes on the colonial axis. Said extends the critique eastward: "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Modernity is a comparative claim that requires a contrasting Other.

Subjectively, postcolonial theory tracks what modernity does to the colonised. Fanon captures the moment when liberal universal personhood collides with race: "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323) fixes him: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Modernity advertises itself as the kingdom of the autonomous subject but distributes subjectivity unequally. Mohanty shows the same logic on the gender axis: the average third-world woman is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) so that Western women appear as the modern subject.

Epistemologically, postcolonial theory attacks the production of knowledge. Said borrows Foucault: "without examining Orientalism as a discourse one cannot possibly understand the enormously systematic discipline by which European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Hall identifies four functions of the West-concept, classification, representation, comparison and evaluation, concluding "it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). Spivak (slide 30) extends this: sympathetic Western scholarship risks epistemic violence when it claims to "recover" voices that can only speak through Western categories.

Examples make the critique concrete. Said's slide-12 list, Iraq, EU boundaries, immigration, Islam, "Arabia", shows the discourse "still has effects today" (slide 12). The slide-16 stereotype-set, Orient as timeless, mysterious, feminine-passive-luxurious, immoral-decadent, is the recurring product. The Mercator projection is the cartographic form; Gall-Peters and the World Turned Upside Down (slides 32-33) are the counter-images. Mohanty's slides 19-23 show the same machinery in progressive Western feminism. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and the Rhodes Must Fall movement extend the critique into contemporary universities; repatriation debates over museum collections show the same logic in cultural institutions. Cultural references like *Black Panther* register postcolonial themes in popular form, while *Slumdog Millionaire* and white-saviour film tropes show the discourse Said and Mohanty diagnose continuing to operate. Vaccine inequality during COVID and the AI/ChatGPT bias toward English-language data show the modernity-as-cost picture in present operation. Postcolonial theory does not deny modernity emancipated some, Dussel concedes "the rational kernel" of the Enlightenment (Dussel, 1993: 75), but insists this was paid for in "an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence" (Dussel, 1993: 66). Its critique is not nostalgic but trans-modern: a modernity that finally accounts for its full cost.

### 2024 Q11, What is a Southern theory? What are the limitations of the concept?

[VERIFY SOURCE: As noted at 2025 Q13, "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in the Lecture 9 slides or the L9 readings supplied. I therefore answer the question using only the L9 materials' resources for theorising the non-Western, principally Hall on the West/Rest discourse, Said on Orientalism, French on inverting the cartography of modernity, Fanon on colonised subjectivity, Mohanty on the third-world woman, and Dussel on trans-modernity. The label "Southern theory" is treated as the question's framing for the broader project of theorising from outside the metropole.]

The question asks what a theory from the position of the formerly colonised would look like, and what limits attach. The L9 resources are Hall's West/Rest discourse, Said's Orientalism, French's *Born in Blackness*, Fanon's colonial subjectivity, Mohanty on Western feminism, and Dussel's trans-modernity. I sketch what these suggest and catalogue the limits.

The first step is negative. Hall's discourse works through homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation and naturalisation; it "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)" while doing the same to the Rest (Hall, 1992: 280). Theory from the Rest must refuse this in both directions. Said adds the Eastern axis: the Orient is "a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88), and theory from outside must refuse that distinction.

The second step is constructive. French models inversion of the cartography (slide 4). Dussel articulates the philosophical version: modernity is "constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65), and the task is "trans-modernity" as "co-realization … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races … Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76).

The third step is methodological. Mohanty insists that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Theory must be granular, not generic. Fanon's railway-carriage scene is methodological too: theory begins from a specific incident in a specific body. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and Global South climate-justice critiques are contemporary instances.

The concept faces serious limitations. First, "the South" risks the same homogenisation Hall criticises for the West. Hall warns "the Rest" "covers enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions" and "can equally encompass the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory of the South that bundled these together would reproduce, with inverted signs, the very simplification it refuses. Mohanty's parallel gender critique applies: collapsing women's experience across vastly different societies is "an essentially truncated" representation (Mohanty, 1984: 261).

Second, Hall reminds us "the West had its own internal 'others'": Jews ostracised, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", Western women represented as inferior to Western men (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory that treats the West as monolithic misses these internal differentiations and the cross-cutting solidarities they make possible. Said likewise treats Orientalism as an "uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7), not a uniform conspiracy.

Third, Fanon warns that standing in the periphery does not deliver an undistorted view. The colonial gaze produces doubling: the body is "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324). Theory from the periphery may still be written under that gaze. Gilroy's L8 figure of the Black Atlantic, "a transnational cultural and political space formed by slavery's middle passage" linking Africa, the Americas, Europe and the Caribbean as one circuit, is the productive corrective: it refuses to locate "the South" in any single geography and instead theorises diaspora and exchange across the imperial circuits Hall and French describe, blocking the simple opposition between a pure inside and pure outside of the West.

Fourth, Dussel cautions that the emancipatory concept of modernity is intertwined with its "irrational myth" of sacrificial violence (Dussel, 1993: 75-6). A theory that asserts Southern virtue against Northern vice would risk producing its own sacrificial myth. A generic "white saviour" inversion can replicate this in reverse.

The defence runs through Dussel's distinction between negation and trans-modernity. The point is not to replace the centre but to make space for "incorporative solidarity" across centre and periphery (Dussel, 1993: 76). Hall, French, Said, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel together do not deliver a finished theory of the South; they deliver the materials for one and flag in advance the homogenisations, doublings and inversions to which such a theory is prone.

### 2023 Q11, How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between "the West and the Rest"?

This question is materially identical to 2025 Q11; I pivot here on the historical-archive dimension. Post-colonial theorists have challenged the West/Rest distinction at three depths: as a discursive system (Hall, Said), as an experiential reality for the colonised (Fanon, Mohanty), and as a historical falsehood about who made the modern world (Dussel, French).

At the discursive depth, Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest demonstrates that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277). Hall identifies four functions of the concept: it classifies (West vs non-West), represents (a composite image), measures (a standard of comparison), and evaluates (developed = good = desirable). "In short, it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). The discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). The binaries, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, are the operating code of the discourse. Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation in the Eastern direction. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). The point is structural: the West cannot be the West without the Rest because its identity is *constituted* by contrast.

At the experiential depth, Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* tells us what the West/Rest binary feels like inside a body. The white child's "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323) is the discourse hailing the colonised; Fanon is fixed, dissected, given back his body "sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The West/Rest distinction is not just a sentence in a textbook; it is a wound on the flesh. Mohanty extends this analysis along the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) precisely by producing a "third world woman" who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The challenge is to refuse the binary in both its halves.

At the historical depth, Dussel and French rewrite the genealogy of modernity. Dussel argues that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates" (Dussel, 1993: 65) and that the Iberian conquest from 1492 is constitutive, not incidental. French's *Born in Blackness* makes Africa central: the point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit is the concrete evidence that European capital accumulation depended from the start on extractive global links. Dussel's seven-point myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) catalogues the mechanisms: modern civilisation understands itself as most developed; this obliges it to "develop" others; opposition justifies "the violence necessary to remove the obstacles to modernization"; the "suffering and sacrifices" are "inevitable and necessary". The L4 cross-link sharpens the epistemic side of this history: Ngũgĩ's "decolonising the mind" thesis names colonial languages as "weapons of conquest" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37), so the West/Rest binary is also a linguistic construct, embedded in the very media through which knowledge circulates.

Each register has a textual form in the L9 materials. Hall's discursive challenge runs through his four functions of the West/Rest concept (Hall, 1992: 277). Said's challenge runs through the slide-12 list of categories the Orientalist discourse still produces: Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia". Fanon and Mohanty's experiential challenge runs through the white-eye scene (Fanon, 1967: 323-5) and the third-world-woman figure (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Dussel and French's historical challenge runs through the 1492-as-birth claim and the inversion of the cartography (slide 4; Dussel, 1993: 65-7).

A balanced conclusion notes the limits. Hall reminds us that the discourse is "destructive" precisely because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280); that destructive force is not removed by being named. The Mercator projection on slide 31 is still the default classroom map. Said's slide-12 list of contemporary Orientalist effects names categories that the discourse still organises. Yet the post-colonial intellectual achievement registered in the L9 materials is real: the West/Rest binary can no longer be deployed innocently. Hall taught us its grammar; Said its archive; Fanon its embodiment; Mohanty its feminist version; Dussel its historical falsification; French its missing African heart. The challenge is now to convert this denaturalisation into structural change.

### 2023 Q13, Critically analyse Southern Theory as an alternative to European Modernity. What are its limits?

[VERIFY SOURCE: "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in L9 slides or readings; the answer below treats it as the question's label for the broader project of theorising modernity from outside the European centre, and uses Hall, Said, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel as the L9 resources for that project.]

European modernity, in its classical telling, is a story of self-generated progress radiating from a small corner of Europe to instruct the rest of humanity. A theory from the position of the Rest, what the question calls "Southern theory", refuses this story. It is both critique (European modernity is not what it says it is) and alternative (the formerly colonised world has been producing modernity on terms the Eurocentric story conceals).

The alternative comes in several voices. Hall's is foundational. The West is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277); the discourse functions through homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation and naturalisation; the four functions of the West-concept are classification, representation, comparison and evaluation, and "it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). Said adds the institutional account: Orientalism is "a corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 25, 88-9). French historicises: Africa is a co-producer of modernity, and the task is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). Dussel articulates the philosophical version: trans-modernity is "co-realization … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races … Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76).

The strengths are several. It punctures European self-congratulation by exposing dependency on the periphery from the start (French; Dussel, 1993: 65). It legitimates non-European intellectual labour: Fanon writes from the Antilles, Said from Palestine/Egypt/New York, Mohanty from Mumbai/Syracuse, Dussel from Argentina/Mexico. It refuses homogenisation in both directions (Hall, 1992: 279-80). It supplies methodological direction: Mohanty's principle that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) generalises into a refusal of generic theory. Haraway's L10 vocabulary makes the epistemological case: there is no view "from nowhere", and the "only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 189, 196), so theory from the position of the Rest is not parochial supplement but a precondition for non-irresponsible knowledge. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and Global South climate-justice critiques show this alternative in contemporary action.

The limits are real. First, "the South" risks the same homogenisation Hall criticises for the West. Hall is explicit that the Rest covers "enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions" (Hall, 1992: 280). Mohanty's parallel critique applies to any theory that bundles vastly different Southern lives into one figure (Mohanty, 1984: 261).

Second, Fanon warns the periphery is not automatically a transparent vantage point. The colonised subject is "dissected under white eyes" and finds the body "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324-5). Theory inside that doubling does not automatically escape it.

Third, Hall reminds us the West has "its own internal 'others'", Jews "frequently excluded and ostracized", Eastern Europeans "regarded as 'barbaric'", and Western women "represented as inferior to Western men" (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory that treats the West as homogeneous misses these cross-cutting axes.

Fourth, Dussel's caution about the "two faces" of modernity warns against simply inverting the value signs. "We do not deny the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment, only its irrational moment as sacrificial myth" (Dussel, 1993: 75). A theory of the South that retains the violent dualism while reversing the polarity would reproduce the "myth of modernity".

Fifth, Said warns that Orientalism is "produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7); a theory from the periphery is itself produced inside global asymmetries. Sixth, slide 30 ends on Spivak: a theory in the name of the periphery may, like the subaltern, struggle to register on available frames.

A balanced conclusion: a theory from the Rest is a necessary corrective, and Hall, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel open the theoretical space the discipline needs. But the alternative is not finished. It needs Hall's warning against homogenising both poles, Mohanty's specificity, Fanon's awareness of doubling, Dussel's refusal of simple inversion, and Said's awareness of how knowledge is produced inside power. The alternative is not Southern modernity as mirror image but Dussel's trans-modernity, a global conversation in which the periphery finally counts as theory.

### 2023 Q14, How are non-western models proposing a different relationship between modernity and technology? Discuss with examples.

[VERIFY SOURCE: The L9 materials do not contain a specific non-Western theorist of technology by name (no Gandhi, no buen vivir, no STS). I therefore answer the question by reading the L9 materials' resources for what they say about modernity, science, knowledge and the West/Rest binary, and treat "non-Western models of technology" as derivable from those resources rather than as separately attested in L9.]

The dominant story is that technology is the property of the West and part of the discourse that makes the West the West. Hall lists "science/magic" as one of the binary oppositions through which "the West and Modernity" are "created in terms of what they are not" (Hall, 1992: 277; slide 9). Said sharpens this: technical and scientific sciences are part of the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Technology is never neutral; it allocates rationality, agency and modernity asymmetrically. The non-Western model must begin by refusing the science/magic binary. Gandhi's critique of large-scale industrial technology and Connell's Southern theory both ground this refusal.

The first non-Western move, available from Hall and Said, denies that "Western" technology is rational and "non-Western" practice magical. Hall shows the West's claim to monopolise rationality is part of the discourse, not reality; the four functions of the West-concept include "criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked" (Hall, 1992: 277). A non-Western theory refuses this ranking and treats technologies as embedded practices organising bodies, communities and ecologies rather than evaluating them on one axis of "progress". Ngũgĩ's L4 analysis pushes this further by treating language itself as a technology: colonial European languages, "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37), were weapons of conquest, and "decolonising the mind" requires changing not merely tools but the medium in which technologies are conceived. The AI/ChatGPT bias toward English embeds linguistic hierarchies in apparently neutral systems.

The second move, from French and Dussel, insists the European technological story is a global story. The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4) shows European capital accumulation from the 1500s depended on Indian textiles, African gold and inter-continental circuits. French argues we "must not ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and the point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view". Dussel: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65). A non-Western theory refuses diffusionism; the techniques, materials and trade-circuits of "European" technology were globally constituted from the start. The Belt and Road Initiative shows this dependency continues.

The third move, Dussel's, reads the costs through the sacrificial myth. The seven-point myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) catalogues how modern civilisation justifies "the violence necessary to remove the obstacles to modernization" and renders "the colonized, the slave, the woman, the ecological destruction of the earth" as "participants in a process of redemptive sacrifice". A non-Western theory refuses the redemptive narrative and counts the costs. Climate-justice arguments from the Global South make this concrete.

The fourth move, also Dussel's, articulates "trans-modernity" as constructive alternative: "co-realization" between "center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Trans-modern technology incorporates technical knowledge but subordinates it to ethical, ecological and incorporative norms.

Critically, these moves face limits. First, Hall's warning against homogenising the Rest applies: "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280) do not share a single technological tradition. Second, Fanon (Fanon, 1967: 324-5) warns that theory from inside the colonial relation may still be written under the white gaze; the science/magic binary may have become internal to the colonised subject's self-understanding. Third, Dussel's two-faces argument cuts both ways: Northern modernity must be criticised for its sacrificial myth, but a non-Western theory must not simply reject the "rational kernel" of modern technical practice.

The strongest reading is neither wholesale rejection of modern technology nor celebration of authentic non-Western alternatives. It is a refusal of the science/magic binary (Hall), a re-historicisation of technology as global from the start (French), a counting of the costs (Dussel, 1993: 75), and a constructive articulation of trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76). Thinking about technology is thinking about modernity, and thinking about modernity is thinking about who counts.

### 2022 Q13, How does the concept of "the West" set itself up in distinction to "the Rest"?

The concept of "the West" sets itself up against "the Rest" through a discursive operation Hall describes in *Formations of Modernity*. I trace this in four steps, homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation, and naturalisation, and assess the political work it does.

Step one is homogenisation. The West is internally diverse (Germanic, Latin, Nordic, Mediterranean, Anglo-Saxon) and the Rest more so (China, India, the Islamic world, sub-Saharan Africa, indigenous Americas, Aboriginal Australasia). Yet the discourse "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, 1992: 280). Internal differences on both sides are collapsed.

Step two is polarisation. The discourse arranges itself through binary oppositions: rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic (slide 9; Hall, 1992: 277). Hall extends Saussurean logic to cultural identity: "national cultures acquire their strong sense of identity by contrasting themselves with other cultures" (Hall, 1992: 279). The West knows itself by *not being* the Rest.

Step three is hierarchisation. The first term of each pair carries positive value; the second negative. Hall is explicit: the discourse "provides criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked … 'the West' = developed = good = desirable; or the 'non-West' = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). The binary is not difference; it is hierarchy. It legitimates colonial intervention, civilising missions, and developmental aid as moral obligations to lift the Rest toward the West.

Step four is naturalisation. The discourse hides its constructed character. Hall insists "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277), but the discourse presents the West as a geographical fact. The Mercator projection visually naturalises Eurocentric geography. Teaching "Western civilisation" as a continuous tradition from Athens to Washington omits the Islamic transmission of Greek philosophy and treats the West as autochthonous.

Said extends the operation to the Eastern axis: "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Mohanty extends to gender: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities" against a "third world woman" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Morrison's L8 vocabulary names the underlying psychic mechanism: the production of racial outsiders is "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29), so the West's setting-itself-off against the Rest is identity-stabilisation through Othering, not neutral description.

The political work is extensive. The slide-5 list of British colonies (US states until 1776, Canada 1867, Australia 1900, Ireland 1922, India and Pakistan 1947, Ghana 1957, Jamaica 1962, Hong Kong 1997, Palestine and Iraq administered) makes the geographical reach concrete: these populations were "British subjects … under the authority of the government in London" (slide 5). The slide-6 list of French colonies (Vietnam, Cambodia, Algeria, Morocco, Ivory Coast, Cameroon, Gabon, Martinique, Syria, Lebanon) parallels this. Slide 18: "All the above legitimate French and British colonialism". Said's slide-12 list, Iraq, EU boundaries, immigration, Islam, "Arabia", names the contemporary effects. Brexit-era immigration politics and Trump-era "shithole" framings of the Global South show the discourse still operating. Hall: the discourse is "so destructive" because "it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280).

To set up the West against the Rest is to perform a sustained ideological operation that homogenises, polarises, hierarchises and naturalises difference, organising five centuries of colonialism. To undo the distinction is to refuse all four operations, recognising internal heterogeneity on both sides (Hall, 1992: 279-80), breaking the binary lockstep, refusing the hierarchy, and re-historicising what the discourse presents as natural geography (French, slide 4; Dussel, 1993: 65). Decolonising-the-curriculum debates, the Rhodes Must Fall movement and repatriation debates over museum collections are practical instances of this undoing, while Global South climate-justice critiques and the AI/ChatGPT bias toward English-language data show how the rationality binary continues to operate inside ostensibly universal infrastructures.

### 2022 Q15, Why is it important to examine social theory which emerges from beyond the West?

Examining social theory from beyond the West matters for four reasons L9 makes explicit: epistemic, political, methodological and ethical.

The epistemic reason is that the canonical Western tradition is partial. Hall reminds us the West is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277) and the standard Enlightenment account "treated the West as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278). That story is false: "the rise of the West is also a global story" (Hall, 1992: 278), and "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (Hall, 1992: 278). French makes the historical content explicit: Africa is "central to the making of the modern world" and the task is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). Without theory from beyond the West, we have not a global sociology but a parochial European one presented as universal.

The political reason is that knowledge is power. Said: Orientalism is "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25). The "corporate institution" of Orient-knowledge "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Producing knowledge from the periphery is itself political. Hall's four functions of the West-concept show how knowledge of "the Rest" was simultaneously ideology (Hall, 1992: 277). Du Bois's L8 figure of double consciousness, "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), names what the absence of theory beyond the West does to the periphery's self-understanding, while the "second sight" of those born "with a veil" (Du Bois, 1903: 4) names the critical resource such theory brings.

The methodological reason is that theory generated in one setting and applied uncritically to another distorts both. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" is exemplary: Western feminist scholarship without grounding in specific social formations produces "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized etc.)" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The remedy: "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Examining non-Western theory is required for empirical accuracy, not optional decoration.

The ethical reason is deepest. Dussel: modernity has two faces, an emancipatory rational kernel and "an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence" (Dussel, 1993: 66, 75). The myth's seven points list the victims, "the colonized, the slave, the woman, the ecological destruction of the earth" (Dussel, 1993: 75). The victims have a moral claim to be heard, and slide 30 poses "Who can speak for whom? Spivak". Examining theory from beyond the West is part of trans-modernity, where "the negated and victimized 'other-face' of modernity … must, in the first place, discover itself as innocent" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Climate-justice critiques from the Global South and vaccine-inequality debates during COVID are contemporary instances.

The L9 materials illustrate the case. Said wrote from Palestine, Egypt and New York; Mohanty from Mumbai; Fanon from Martinique and Paris; French to invert the European-internalist account (slide 4); Dussel from Argentina and Mexico (Dussel, 1993: 66-7). The L9 reading list is itself theory from beyond the West, and its availability is the precondition of the critique L9 mounts.

Objections must be addressed. The universalist objection says knowledge is knowledge regardless of where produced. The L9 reply is Hall's: the discourse functions as ideology, and ideologies are produced in particular places (Hall, 1992: 277). The relativist objection says non-Western theory is just one perspective. The L9 reply is Dussel's: the choice is not universalism vs relativism but modernity-as-myth vs trans-modernity-as-solidarity (Dussel, 1993: 76). The diluted-canon objection says broadening the syllabus dilutes quality. The empirical reply: Said, Hall, Fanon, Mohanty, French and Dussel are not diluting the canon; they are correcting it.

Examining social theory from beyond the West matters because epistemically it corrects partial knowledge (Hall, French), politically it refuses knowledge-as-domination (Said), methodologically it secures empirical accuracy (Mohanty), and ethically it acknowledges modernity's victims (Dussel, Fanon, Spivak). Decolonising-the-curriculum debates, the Rhodes Must Fall movement and the AI/ChatGPT bias toward English-language data make the stakes contemporary: when knowledge infrastructures embed Northern assumptions silently, sociology that does not look beyond the West cannot see itself. It is not a courtesy; it is a condition of intelligent sociology.

### 2021 Q13, Using at least two theorists, outline and assess the postcolonial critique of "the West".

I use Stuart Hall and Frantz Fanon as my two theorists, and assess the postcolonial critique of "the West" along three dimensions: discursive (Hall), experiential (Fanon), and political. I then weigh the strengths and limits.

Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest, set out in *Formations of Modernity* pp. 276-280, makes the critique discursive. The West, Hall argues, is "as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, 1992: 276), "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277). The concept performs four operations: classification (West/non-West), representation (composite image), comparison (measuring stick) and evaluation (hierarchical ranking). The discourse works through binary oppositions, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, and through simplifying logic that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). The West constituted itself by contrast: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). Its identity is relational, parasitic on the Rest it produced as Other.

Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* makes the critique experiential. Fanon takes the structures Hall describes and shows what they feel like in a human life. The railway-carriage scene, where a white child cries "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), turns the discourse into a hail. The Black subject is fixed: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am *fixed*" (Fanon, 1967: 325). His body is "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored…" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The white world bars him "from all participation. A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324). What Hall describes structurally Fanon registers viscerally: the West is a wound on the colonised body. The L8 lineage clarifies the conceptual genealogy: Du Bois's "double consciousness", the "sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), is the framework Fanon extends from the colour line in the United States to the colonial relation more broadly.

Together, Hall and Fanon constitute a powerful critique. The critique summarises in three claims. First, the West is a historical construct (Hall, 1992: 277). Second, the West constitutes itself by negation (Hall, 1992: 278, 280). Third, this constitution does material and psychic harm (Fanon, 1967: 323-5; Mohanty, 1984: 261). The critique extends to the Mercator projection as cartographic form, Said's slide-12 list (Iraq, EU boundaries, immigration, Islam, "Arabia") as contemporary effects, Mohanty's gender axis, and Spivak's question of who can speak. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates, the Rhodes Must Fall movement and repatriation debates over museum collections are contemporary instances of this critique in practice; Brexit-era immigration politics and Trump-era "shithole" framings of the Global South show the discourse still operating in mainstream public speech.

The strengths are considerable. The critique denaturalises a taken-for-granted category (Hall, 1992: 277); links discourse to power following Foucault (Said, 1978: 88-9); and supplies a vocabulary, Orientalism, West/Rest, the white gaze, the third-world-woman figure, that organises L9.

The limits are real. First, the critique can produce its own essentialism: speaking constantly of "the West" risks reifying it. Hall is alert: "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279), with "internal 'others'", Jews ostracised, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", Western women inferior to Western men (Hall, 1992: 280). Second, Hall's warning extends to the Rest: it covers "enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions" (Hall, 1992: 280). Third, Fanon's account requires reading the train scene alongside "since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The doubling is not static but a condition out of which a new subjectivity must be made. Fourth, Dussel's two-faces warning (Dussel, 1993: 75-6) cuts both ways: the critique must not become a sacrificial myth in reverse.

Hall and Fanon together produce one of L9's most important critical achievements. They give us a structural account of how the West constitutes itself against the Rest, and a phenomenological account of what that constitution does to a body. The critique remains indispensable but must be held with the cautions L9 flags: internal heterogeneity, specificity (Mohanty), the active making of new subjectivity (Fanon), and refusal of inverted essentialism (Dussel). The point is not to retire the critique but to deepen it.

### 2021 Q16, In what ways does 'Southern theory' take forward debates in 'Western' social theory?

[VERIFY SOURCE: "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in L9 slides or readings; the answer below treats it as the question's label for the broader project of theorising from outside the European centre, and uses Hall, Said, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel as the L9 resources.]

The question asks how theory from the periphery extends, rather than replaces, debates in the Western tradition. L9 supplies five lines: sociology of knowledge, theory of modernity, feminist theory, theories of empire and capitalism, and who can speak for whom.

In the sociology of knowledge, Said's *Orientalism* is the L9 exemplar. Foucault had analysed discourse internally to European institutions (asylum, clinic, prison); Said extends the analysis to the global discourse through which Europe constituted its non-European objects. Hall does parallel work for the West/Rest discourse, identifying four functions of the West-concept and concluding "it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277), extending classical theories of ideology. The L1 framing of bifurcation supplies a deeper register: Halewood's account of modernity as built on bifurcations, "splitting into two branches" that "creates a gulf" (Whitehead, 1933: 217; Halewood, 2014), is what Hall, Said and Mohanty extend onto the colonial axis, so "Southern theory" takes forward the very project of un-bifurcating modern thought that L1 opens.

In the theory of modernity, Hall and Dussel rewrite a debate largely settled internally to Europe. Hall criticises the story that "treated the West as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278) and insists "the rise of the West is also a global story" (Hall, 1992: 278). French historicises with the Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4). Dussel dates "the 'birth' of modernity" to 1492 (Dussel, 1993: 65). This is global re-grounding, with trans-modernity as the constructive extension (Dussel, 1993: 76).

In feminist theory, Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* takes forward debates Western feminism opened but could not complete: the "Third world woman" is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), and the corrective is to theorise "*within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). In theories of empire and capitalism, French's slide-4 inversion takes Marx's primitive-accumulation account forward; Dussel's claim that the standard genealogy occludes "the role of Spain and Portugal in the formation of the modern world system" (Dussel, 1993: 65) extends the historiography. In the question of who can speak, Fanon's railway-carriage scene takes forward Sartrean existentialism, slide 25 names Du Bois and Foucault, and slide 30's "Who can speak for whom? Spivak" opens an extension of recognition-debates Western theory had begun.

The extension argument has strategic and analytical merits. Strategically, it makes the case for non-Western theory more easily heard. Analytically, it captures the genuine relation: Said draws on Foucault; Hall extends post-Saussurean semiotics; Fanon writes alongside Sartre; Mohanty engages Western feminism; French and Dussel argue with Hegel and Habermas. None is alien import.

Caveats are needed. First, the extension thesis must not subordinate the periphery to the centre; theory from the Rest does its own work (Hall, 1992: 277-80; Dussel, 1993: 65). Second, some of what L9 does is rupture, not extension; the seven-point myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) breaks with Habermas. Third, Hall's warning against homogenising the West reminds us "Western theory" is not a single thing. Theory from the periphery is sometimes extension and sometimes rupture, and the discipline benefits from taking both moves seriously.

### Mock 1, "The Orient is an idea that has a history" (Said). Discuss.

I take Said's epigrammatic claim that the Orient is "an idea that has a history and a tradition of thought, imagery, and vocabulary that have given it reality and presence in and for the West" (Said, 1978: 87) as a doorway into three claims: that the Orient is an idea, that ideas have histories, and that the Orient's history is a Western history. I treat each in turn and end with a contemporary application.

The first claim, that the Orient is an idea, refuses the realist commonsense that the East is just a place. The Orient as Said writes about it is not the geography of Beirut, Cairo, Baghdad and Tehran but the discursive object that European philology, fiction, painting, opera and policy produced about that geography. "The Orient was almost a European invention, and had been since antiquity a place of romance, exotic beings, haunting memories and landscapes, remarkable experiences" (Said, 1978: 87). The Orient is therefore real in the constructivist sense: real as a system of representation, real in its political and material effects, but not a self-standing fact prior to representation. This is the Foucauldian move at the heart of *Orientalism*. The "corporate institution for dealing with the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25), universities, museums, ministries, archives, novels, produced its object as it described it.

The second claim, that the idea has a history, distinguishes Said from purely structuralist analysis. Said dates the modern crystallisation to the late eighteenth century, after Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 and the establishment of academic Oriental studies. Earlier European images, medieval crusader polemic, Marco Polo, the Ottoman threat to Vienna in 1683, fed into but did not yet constitute the Orientalist discourse that consolidates around Silvestre de Sacy, Ernest Renan and Edward Lane. The idea has a datable rise, an institutional habitat, a canon of texts.

The third claim, that the Orient's history is a Western history, is the political punch. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Hall's parallel about the West and the Rest generalises Said's claim eastwards: the West needs the Rest to define itself (Hall, 1992: 280). The Orient is therefore part of Europe's autobiography, not of Asia's.

Three qualifications. First, Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies to Said too; the Orientalist canon was not monolithic, and Said himself frames the discourse as "produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7). Second, the Orient writes back: Said writes from Jerusalem, Egypt and New York (slide 10). Third, the Orient is not the only Other: Mohanty's "average third world woman" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is a gendered cognate; Fanon's white-eye scene racialises a Black body in parallel (Fanon, 1967: 323-5). The L10 vocabulary of vision underlines the wider epistemic point. Berger's "the relation between what we see and what we know is never settled" (Berger, 1972: 7) and Haraway's "god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189) name the visual-epistemological form Said's Orient takes: an idea that has acquired the appearance of a view, but is in fact a particular Western situated knowledge passed off as the world.

The lecture itself provides the durable application. Slide 12 names the categories the Orientalist discourse still produces today: Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia". Slide 16 names the stereotype-set: the Orient as timeless, mysterious, feminine-passive-luxurious, immoral-decadent. Slide 18 records the political consequence: the discourse functioned to "legitimate French and British colonialism", and Said's quotation pinpoints the mechanism, the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Hall supplies the structural form.

Said's claim is therefore right and durable. The Orient is an idea, ideas have histories, and the Orient's history is part of Europe's history of constructing itself through Others. The contemporary reader takes the claim, holds it alongside Hall's structural grammar of the West and the Rest (Hall, 1992: 277-80), alongside Mohanty's gender inflection (Mohanty, 1984: 261-2), alongside Fanon's racial embodiment (Fanon, 1967: 323-5), alongside Spivak's question of who can speak (slide 30), and reads the world with sharper instruments than realist commonsense supplies. The Orient is a Western invention; whether the invention can be unmade is the project of postcolonial theory.

### Mock 2, Critically assess Said's argument in *Orientalism* in light of how the L9 lecture itself frames contemporary Orientalist effects.

Said's *Orientalism* (1978) argued that Western representations of the Orient have, since the late eighteenth century, formed a discourse, the "corporate institution for dealing with the Orient, dealing with it by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it" (Said, 1978: 25). I assess this argument using the L9 lecture's own list of contemporary Orientalist effects and the three qualifications Said himself enters in the Ashcroft excerpt.

The lecture frames the durability of Said's argument explicitly on slide 12. The "Orient" is a construction, "a legacy and a result of ways of thinking, acting, studying, trading, and dominating which still have effects today in how people in the West think about and act about people in that region", and the contemporary categories the discourse organises are Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, and "Arabia". This is the lecture's own claim that the Orientalism Said described in 1978 still has effects in the present. Slide 16 names the stereotype-set the discourse continues to produce: Orient as timeless, Orient as mysterious, Orient as feminine-passive-luxurious, Orient as immoral-decadent. The slide-13 "Tales of the Arabian Nights" image is the visual instance.

Said's claim that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89) describes the structural asymmetry the slide-12 categories enact: the West "thinks about" and "acts about" the region; the region is reported on, studied, mapped, intervened in, but rarely placed as the agent of its own representation. Said's stronger claim, that Orientalism functioned as the "enormously systematic discipline by which European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9), describes the institutional reach. Three Said-internal qualifications need entry. First, the Orient is not nothing. "It would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6); "there were, and are, cultures and nations whose location is in the East, and their lives, histories, and customs have a brute reality obviously greater than anything that could be said about them in the West" (Said, 1978: 25-6). The argument is about the discourse, not about whether the region exists. Second, the discourse is internally variegated: Orientalism "is produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7), and Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies to the Orientalist canon too. Third, the discourse is not only descriptive but productive: Orientalism "is, rather than expresses, a certain will or intention to understand, in some cases to control, manipulate, even to incorporate, what is a manifestly different (or alternative and novel) world" (Said, 1978: 26).

Two supplementations from the L9 reading set strengthen Said. Mohanty's parallel critique on the gender axis shows that the discourse Said describes has a feminine register: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) against the third-world-woman figure who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Fanon's white-eye scene shows what happens when the discourse is enforced on a colonised body: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Morrison's L8 analysis of Othering supplies the psychic motor: the production of racial outsiders is "a desperate attempt to confirm one's own self as normal" (Morrison, 2017: 29), which is exactly what Said's "surrogate and even underground self" does for European culture on the Eastern axis.

The L9 verdict on Said is therefore clear and durable. The slide-12 categories show that the discourse Said diagnosed continues to organise Western "thinking about and acting about" the Middle East and adjacent regions. Said's own qualifications, the Orient is not nothing, the discourse is internally variegated, the discourse is productive not just descriptive, keep the argument from becoming a conspiracy theory. Mohanty and Fanon supply the gender and racial supplementations. The Saidian diagnosis, as read in L9, is the indispensable starting point for any account of contemporary Orientalist effects.

### Mock 3, What does Fanon mean by the "white gaze", and why does it produce a doubled consciousness rather than simply an external constraint?

This is a two-part question. I take the parts in order from *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) and connect to Du Bois, Sartre and Hall.

On the first part, the white gaze is Fanon's name for the perceptual regime through which colonial racial discourse lands on a Black body. The paradigmatic scene is the railway carriage: a white child cries, "Look, a Negro!… Mama, see the Negro! I'm frightened!" (Fanon, 1967: 323). Fanon tries to laugh: "I made up my mind to laugh myself to tears, but laughter had become impossible" (Fanon, 1967: 323). The cry is interpellation that fixes Fanon into a pre-existing category. The white gaze is what Hall's West/Rest discourse does to a body: the binaries (rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body) deployed by an actual white child on an actual Black man, and his freedom to be anything other than "the Negro" collapses.

Three features specify the gaze. First, it is unidirectional: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Second, it produces its object: "My body was given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored… the corporeal schema crumbled, its place taken by a racial epidermal schema" (Fanon, 1967: 324). Third, the gaze is asymmetric: whiteness is invisible to itself; blackness is the entire content of how the Black subject is seen.

On the second part, why does this produce doubling rather than external constraint? An external constraint would say: I am free internally; I am only blocked externally. Fanon's analysis is sharper. The white gaze does not just block; it inhabits. The colonised subject becomes both subject (still thinking, feeling, suffering) and object (the dissected Negro) simultaneously, and these are not separable. "A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The Black man cannot simply will himself into being a man; he must also struggle against the gaze's interior occupation of his self-perception. He is alienated from his own body, which has been recoded.

This doubling has an explicit comparative cue. Slide 25 lists "four crucial points" (being an object; being a subject; the denial of subjectivity) and instructs: "Think about Du Bois and also Foucault, perhaps". Du Bois's L8 formulation of "double consciousness" is the directly imported template: "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), and "one ever feels his twoness, an American, a Negro; two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Fanon's "I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324) is the same structure under colonial conditions. Slide 24 adds Sartre: Fanon was "influenced by philosophy, most notably existentialism". The Sartrean gaze is the source; Fanon's contribution is to show that under colonialism the gaze is unequally distributed, the white world is "the only honourable one" and bars the colonised "from all participation" (Fanon, 1967: 324).

Why does the distinction matter? Because the response differs. To constraint, the response is to remove the obstacle. To doubling, the response must be more profound: to remake the relation between subject and racialised self-image. "Since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The making-known is the active creation of a new self the doubled colonial subject could not, by itself, have made. Slide 24 records that Fanon went on to *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), where this becomes the revolutionary anti-colonial subject.

The L9 conclusion (slide 30) connects this to Spivak's question of who can speak for whom. The Fanonian problem of doubling becomes the Spivakian problem of voice: how can a voice formed under the white gaze be heard on terms other than the gaze's? The two questions sit at the end of the lecture as the unresolved task that postcolonial theory inherits.

In summary, the white gaze lands the colonial discursive order on a Black body (Fanon, 1967: 323-5); it produces doubling rather than mere constraint because it inhabits the subject's own self-image (Fanon, 1967: 324); the comparative anchors the lecture provides are Du Bois on double consciousness and Sartre on the gaze (slides 24-25); and the response is the active production, through struggle, of a new subjectivity that no longer has to see itself through the gaze.

### Mock 4, Compare Said's Orientalism with Mohanty's "Third World Woman".

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and Chandra Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* (1988) both diagnose a homogenised non-Western Other that says more about its maker than its referent. They differ in axis, archive and political target. I compare them along these dimensions and assess what is gained by reading them together.

On axis, Said works the geographical-civilisational divide. The Orient is the East, primarily the Arab and Islamic Middle East, as seen by the West. Mohanty works gender within the broader colonial relation. The Third World Woman is the figure produced by Western scholarship and Western feminism about women in the periphery. The two binaries intersect: the veiled Muslim woman is at once Orientalist and gendered-Orientalist.

On archive, Said reads the nineteenth-century European Orientalist canon (Sacy, Renan, Lane, Burton, Flaubert, Nerval) supplemented by twentieth-century area studies. Mohanty reads late-twentieth-century Anglophone Western feminist scholarship and "women in development" anthologies. Said reads two centuries of imperial culture; Mohanty reads two decades of progressive feminist scholarship. Said's enemies are Orientalist; Mohanty's are well-meaning Western feminists. The analytic operation is similar.

On theoretical apparatus, Said draws Foucault's notion of discourse to explain how Orientalism functions as a "corporate institution… dealing with [the Orient] by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it, ruling over it" (Said, 1978: 25). Mohanty frames the Third World Woman as "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Both use the Foucauldian move that discourse produces its object. The L1 vocabulary of bifurcation makes the family resemblance explicit: each operates by "splitting into two branches" that "creates a gulf" (Whitehead, 1933: 217; Halewood, 2014), Said splitting Occident from Orient and Mohanty splitting Western feminist from third-world woman, so Said and Mohanty take the L1 critique of modern bifurcation and run it on the imperial and feminist axes respectively. Mohanty foregrounds who speaks for whom inside progressive scholarship in a way Said does not.

On political target, Said's enemy is Western imperial culture; the implication is decolonisation, political and intellectual. Mohanty's enemy is Western feminist universalism that subordinates Global South women under the figure of the helpless brown woman who needs saving. The "average third world woman" leads "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized etc.)" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Her political project is global feminist solidarity that respects difference without exoticising it.

Three things are gained by reading them together. First, gendered specification. Said's archive is mostly male and treats gendered tropes glancingly. Mohanty supplies the gender dimension; the Third World Woman is what Said's Orient looks like in its feminine register, a justification-figure for white-saviour campaigns and feminist universalism. Second, intra-progressive critique. Said critiques empire; Mohanty critiques solidarity-with-the-Third-World that does not check its premises. Third, the methodological injunction to specificity (Mohanty, 1984: 262).

Tensions are real. Said sometimes risks treating European culture as monolithic; Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies. Mohanty also risks homogenising Western feminism, though her targeted critique of "the assumption of women as an already constituted, coherent group with identical interests and desires" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is itself an explicit warning against homogenisation.

The L9 slides make the convergence visible. Slide 16's list of Orientalist stereotypes, the Orient as "feminine, passive, luxurious" and as "immoral, decadent", shows that the discourse Said diagnoses already has a feminine register. Mohanty's slides 19-23 show how that feminine register is operationalised in self-consciously progressive Western feminist scholarship: the "average third world woman" is rendered as Said's feminine-Orient figure made into a sociological subject. The slide-12 categories (Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia") are the territories where the Saidian discourse continues to organise Western thought; the slide-21 Western woman with her book and her coffee, in implicit contrast to the slide-20 "average third world woman" carrying wood through water, is what the same discourse looks like at the level of gendered self-representation.

In conclusion, Said and Mohanty are complementary diagnosticians of the constructed non-Western Other, Said on the civilisational axis (slides 10-18), Mohanty on the gender axis (slides 19-23). They share an analytic move (discourse produces its object: Said, 1978: 25 and Mohanty, 1984: 261) and an ethical injunction (refuse the homogenising figure: Said, 1978: 88-9 and Mohanty, 1984: 262). Read together, they give the L9 reader the conceptual instruments to dismantle both the imperial discourse and the well-meaning Western feminism whose solidarity reproduces the structure it opposes.

### Mock 5, Why does the L9 lecture end on Spivak's question "Who can speak for whom?", and what does the question add to Hall, Said, Fanon and Mohanty?

The lecture ends on slide 30 with the bullet "Who can speak for whom? Spivak". The placement is significant: Spivak is the question the lecture poses to its own arguments and to the reader. I read the question against the resources the lecture has assembled, Hall on the discourse of the West and the Rest, Said on Orientalism, Fanon on colonial subjectivity, Mohanty on the third-world woman, and Dussel on trans-modernity, and argue that Spivak's question identifies the unresolved problem the L9 framework inherits.

The question follows slide 30, which concludes the open task is "how can we develop ways of thinking which address the legacy of colonialism … without reducing post-colonial subjects to being the 'same'". The Spivak question is posed where the lecture has refused to homogenise the colonised.

Hall provides the structural setting. The discourse "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)" and "the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same" (Hall, 1992: 280). The discourse is "destructive" because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions" (Hall, 1992: 280). Spivak's question asks whether someone inside the homogenised "Rest" can speak in a way the discourse does not immediately re-homogenise.

Said provides the institutional setting. Orientalism is "the corporate institution for dealing with the Orient, dealing with it by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it" (Said, 1978: 25). Spivak's question asks whether someone the Orientalist institution has constructed as object can speak as subject without their speech being absorbed back into the corporate institution.

Fanon provides the phenomenological setting. The white-eye scene fixes Fanon as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Yet Fanon also writes: "Since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The making-known is exactly the act of speaking. Spivak's question asks whether the making-known can register on terms other than the gaze's.

Mohanty provides the parallel feminist setting. The figure of the "average third world woman" is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), and the methodological corrective is to theorise "*within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Spivak's question asks whether the women whom this homogenising figure was supposed to represent can speak inside Western feminist scholarship without being re-converted into the figure.

What Spivak's question adds to these four is the question of voice as a discursive event. Hall analyses the structure that produces "the Rest"; Said the institution that produces "the Orient"; Fanon the body that is produced as object under the gaze; Mohanty the homogenising figure produced inside Western feminism. Each tells us how the discourse constitutes its objects. Spivak's question asks the reciprocal: when those objects try to constitute themselves as speaking subjects, can the discourse hear them? The L9 lecture does not answer this question; it leaves it as the open task.

Dussel supplies the constructive setting for an answer. Trans-modernity is the project of "an incorporative solidarity … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76), and the "negated and victimized 'other-face' of modernity … must, in the first place, discover itself as innocent" (Dussel, 1993: 76). The Spivakian "speech" of the periphery, on Dussel's reading, is the act through which the periphery comes to judge modernity rather than be judged by it. Ngũgĩ's L4 thesis on language sharpens the practical question: if "decolonising the mind" requires breaking out of "the linguistic prison of English, French, and Portuguese" (Ngũgĩ, 1993: 37), then Spivak's question of whether the subaltern can speak is also a question about the medium of speech, and not only about the institutional reception of any utterance whose form is already preconfigured by the coloniser's language.

In conclusion, Spivak's question, posed at the lecture's closing slide, is the question the L9 framework leaves open. It asks whether the homogenised "Rest" (Hall), the Orientalist object (Said), the doubled colonial subject (Fanon), and the third-world-woman figure (Mohanty) can be reconstituted as a speaking position, and whether the trans-modern incorporation Dussel envisages is achievable. The question is not rhetorical, and the lecture explicitly refuses to close it.

The third move is to expose the modern map's distortion. Slide 31's Mercator is what most students have on their classroom wall. These slides are not just illustrations; they are the cartographic form of Hall's argument that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277) and that the binary is "destructive" because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280).

The cartographic argument connects to Said and Dussel. Dussel's claim that the standard genealogy occludes "the role of Spain and Portugal in the formation of the modern world system" (Dussel, 1993: 65) mirrors the cartographic occlusion: the Cantino Planisphere shows the global texture of early modernity which the Mercator suppresses.

In conclusion, the four cartographic slides function as an argument with four moves: denaturalise the modern map (Cantino, slides 2-4); make the imperial map visible (slides 5-8); expose the distortion of the dominant projection (slide 31); display alternative cartographies (slides 32-33). The argument is consonant with Hall's structural denaturalisation, Said's account of geographical entities as "man-made", and Dussel's rewriting of modernity from 1492. The L9 lecture treats maps not as neutral illustrations but as part of the discourse it analyses.

### Mock 7, How does the L9 framework treat the relation between "the West" and capitalism?

Capitalism is implicit in L9 at several points. Slide 7's definition of colonialism includes "the economic relationship. There is a direct link between capitalism and colonialism which benefits the colonizer". French's cotton-gold-coinage example shows the global texture of early-modern accumulation. Hall defines the West as "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277). Dussel locates modernity's "birth" at 1492 with the periphery as constitutive (Dussel, 1993: 65).

The first move rejects the separation of West and capitalism. Hall lists "capitalist" among the defining features of "the West" alongside "developed, industrialized, urbanized, … secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277). The West is the historical configuration in which industrialised capitalism, urbanisation, secularisation and modernity arose together. Anywhere that "shares these characteristics" belongs to the West "wherever it exists on a geographical map" (Hall, 1992: 277).

The second move rejects the separation of capitalism and colonialism. Slide 7's link is direct: capitalism and colonialism are intertwined, and the economic relationship "benefits the colonizer". French's slide-4 example is what this looks like in the 1500s: Indian cotton bought African gold to mint European coinage. European capital accumulation required Africa and Asia. The story in which European capitalism rises internally and then expands outward is an ideological inversion: capitalism was global from its first centuries. Belt and Road Initiative debates show this dependency continues.

The third move is Dussel's. Modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65). 1492 is the birth date, and "the role of Spain and Portugal in the formation of the modern world system" is the occluded content. The "fallacy of developmentalism", that "the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67), is the ideology that makes Western capitalism look universal rather than extractive. The L2 cross-reading reinforces the point: Dussel's seven-point myth and French's argument that Africa is "central to the making of the modern world" share the L2 theoretical block, so the West/capitalism nexus theorised here in L9 is continuous with the colonial-modernity argument L2 develops, not a separate domain.

The fourth move is Said's. Capitalism is part of "the corporate institution for dealing with the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25). Said names "even colonial bureaucracies and colonial styles" among supporting institutions, and lists "the scientist, the scholar, the missionary, the trader, or the soldier" as personnel of European ascendancy (Said, 1978: 26). The trader is alongside the soldier; Orientalist knowledge is not separate superstructure on top of economic base but part of the same "complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89).

L9 does not produce a free-standing theory of capitalism; it treats capitalism as one element of the West-Rest configuration. Dussel's seven-point myth (Dussel, 1993: 75) is about modernity in general; Hall's account is structural; Said's institutional. A fully theorised account would need pairing with the Marxist tradition the L9 readings draw on but do not centre.

The strength of L9's framework is that it refuses to separate cultural and economic. Slide 7's "direct link" is the simple form of more developed arguments in Hall, French, Said and Dussel. The reader refuses both purely cultural readings of postcolonial theory and purely economic readings of colonialism. The West is a configuration in which capitalism, colonialism, the discourse of modernity and the West/Rest binary are mutually constitutive.

L9 treats the West-capitalism nexus as inseparable. The West is "developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277) as a single configuration; capitalism and colonialism are directly linked (slide 7); the global texture of capital accumulation runs through Africa and Asia from the 1500s; Orientalist knowledge and imperial trade are part of the same institution; and modernity's birth at 1492 is the birth of a centre-periphery world-system (Dussel, 1993: 65).

Contemporary applications make this concrete. Climate-justice critiques from the Global South show that the ecological costs of capitalist modernity are still borne unequally by the formerly colonised. Vaccine inequality during COVID showed the same logic in pharmaceutical capitalism: Northern intellectual-property regimes restricted production where Southern need was greatest. Belt and Road Initiative debates over Chinese infrastructure investment show that the centre-periphery world-system Dussel describes is now multi-polar but still structured by capital flows. Repatriation debates over museum collections show that even the cultural infrastructure of Western capitalism continues to hold the loot of empire. L9 refuses to let capitalism be analysed separately from the discourse of the West.

### Mock 8, What is "post-colonial" in "post-colonial theory"? Is the "post" a date or a theoretical stance?

L9 is titled "Post-Colonial Theory" but does not gloss the prefix "post-". Yet the argument is plainly not that colonialism is over. "Post-colonial" in L9 is a theoretical stance, not a date.

The first reason is slide 30: "how can we develop ways of thinking which address the legacy of colonialism and its place in current society without reducing post-colonial subjects to being the 'same'". The phrasing presupposes the legacy persists. If colonialism were over, there would be no current post-colonial subject.

The second reason is Said's. Slide 12 names the categories the Orientalist discourse still organises: Iraq, EU boundaries, immigration, Islam, "Arabia". The "post" cannot mean "after the discourse"; it means "in the aftermath of the formal colonial relation, with the discourse still operating". Brexit-era immigration politics and Trump-era "shithole" framings are contemporary instances.

The third reason is Hall's. The West/Rest analysis is formally about the modern period, but its present-tense verbs make clear it is also about now. "This discourse, as a 'system of representation', represents the world as divided according to a simple dichotomy, the West/the Rest" (Hall, 1992: 280); "that is what makes the discourse of 'the West and the Rest' so destructive" (Hall, 1992: 280). It is a current operation.

The fourth reason is Fanon's. Fanon wrote in 1952, but the lecture deploys him as a contemporary resource: slides 26-29 work through the white-eye scene as still applicable. The racial-discursive operation is not confined to 1952 Martinique.

The fifth reason is Mohanty's. She wrote in 1984; slides 19-23 describe a Western feminist scholarly practice the lecture treats as still in operation. "The notion of 'Third world woman' is an effect of discourse" is on slide 19 in the present tense.

The sixth reason is Dussel's. Dussel dates the "birth" of modernity to 1492 and argues that the "myth of modernity" continues to operate (Dussel, 1993: 65, 75). The trans-modernity Dussel calls for in the future tense is "a project of political, economic, ecological, erotic, pedagogical, and religious liberation" (Dussel, 1993: 76); it is what comes after the myth, but the myth is current. The L1 vocabulary of bifurcation underscores the persistence: bifurcations, once installed, become invisible features of modern thought (Whitehead, 1933: 217; Halewood, 2014), and the West/Rest, Occident/Orient and modern/traditional splits are precisely such durable bifurcations, which is why "post-colonial" cannot be a date and must be a theoretical stance against their continuing operation.

The L9 framework therefore treats "post-colonial" as a theoretical stance with three components. First, it diagnoses the persistence of colonial structures of discourse, subjectivity and economy beyond the dates of formal independence (Said, Hall, Fanon, Mohanty). Second, it refuses to homogenise the colonised (slide 30: "they are not just the 'Other' of the West"). Third, it gestures toward a constructive alternative, French's inversion of the cartography (slide 4) and Dussel's trans-modernity (Dussel, 1993: 76), without claiming the alternative is yet achieved.

A purely temporal reading of "post-colonial" would miss all three components. It would treat 1947 (India), 1957 (Ghana), 1962 (Jamaica) and 1997 (Hong Kong) as the dates after which colonial analysis ceases to apply. The L9 materials reject this reading both explicitly (slide 30) and implicitly (the present-tense verbs of Hall, Said, Mohanty and Dussel).

Contemporary instances anchor the point. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and the Rhodes Must Fall movement insist that the colonial residue persists inside formally independent post-colonial universities. Repatriation debates over museum collections show the same residue in cultural institutions. Climate-justice critiques from the Global South make visible the post-colonial unevenness of ecological harm, and vaccine inequality during COVID showed that post-colonial here means "still structured by global hierarchies even after the formal end of empire". The AI/ChatGPT bias toward English-language data shows how the post-colonial discursive operation now runs through Western-built knowledge infrastructures.

"Post-colonial" in the L9 framework is a theoretical stance on the continuing presence of colonial structures, not a date. The position is supported by every author the lecture quotes. The student must signal this clearly: "post-colonial" is not "after-colonial" but "still inside-colonial-aftermath".

### Mock 10, How do the L9 readings handle internal heterogeneity, on both sides of the West/Rest binary?

The L9 lecture's central diagnostic is binary: West vs Rest, Occident vs Orient, modern subject vs colonised object, Western woman vs third-world woman. A serious risk is that the critique reproduces the binary by treating each pole as homogeneous. The L9 readings handle this risk explicitly.

Hall is the most direct. After laying out the binary, Hall enters two qualifications on page 280. First, "the West has always contained many internal differences, between different nations, between Eastern and Western Europe, between the Germanic Northern and the Latin Southern cultures, between the Nordic, Iberian and Mediterranean peoples". Attitudes "varied widely, as they still do between, for example, the British, the Spanish, the French and the German" (Hall, 1992: 279-80). Second, the West "had its own internal 'others'": Jews "frequently excluded and ostracized", Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", "western women … represented as inferior to western men" (Hall, 1992: 280).

Hall does the same for the Rest. It "covers enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions, for example, between the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia. It can equally encompass the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280). Hall converts the warning into method: simplification "is precisely what this discourse itself does" (Hall, 1992: 280). The binary is destructive because it simplifies; the critique must refuse simplification on both sides.

Said handles heterogeneity in three moves. First, "it would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6); cultures in the East have "a brute reality obviously greater than anything that could be said about them in the West". The Orient is varied; Orientalism is the discourse that homogenises it. Second, the American understanding is "considerably less dense" than the European (Said, 1978: 24). Third, Orientalism "is produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power, … power political … power intellectual … power cultural … power moral" (Said, 1978: 26-7).

Fanon handles heterogeneity through specificity. The white-eye scene is a particular incident in a particular place, not a universal racial situation. Slide 24 records Fanon's specific biography: born in the French Antilles in 1925, educated in Martinique and Paris, influenced by Sartrean existentialism. Slide 26 notes "the language used by Fanon, reflects the culture/society of the time".

Mohanty's whole argument is about internal heterogeneity within "Third World women". She challenges "the assumption of women as an already constituted, coherent group with identical interests and desires, regardless of class, ethnic or racial location or contradictions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), and converts this into method: "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Mohanty's intervention is structurally a defence of heterogeneity against feminist universalism.

Dussel handles heterogeneity within the West by foregrounding Spain and Portugal. The standard genealogy occludes "the role of Spain and Portugal in the formation of the modern world system" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Slides 5 and 6 list British and French colonies separately, and slide 4 foregrounds Portuguese trade circuits; L9 refuses to treat European colonialism as one undifferentiated phenomenon. Haraway's L10 epistemology converts this into method: there is no view "from nowhere", and "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 189, 196), so situated knowledge requires both halves of the binary to be theorised in their internal heterogeneity, since any view that homogenises is by definition the "god-trick" L10 forbids.

This handling has consequences for the binary itself. The binary's "destructive" character comes from simplification (Hall, 1992: 280). A critique that treats either pole as monolithic reproduces the destructive operation it claims to oppose. Decolonising-the-curriculum debates and Rhodes Must Fall need to hold both halves: the binary is the diagnostic frame, the qualifications protect against inverted essentialism.

L9 handles internal heterogeneity through Hall's double qualification, Said's insistence on the Orient's brute reality and the uneven exchange, Fanon's specificity, Mohanty's anti-universalist method, and Dussel's foregrounding of Spain and Portugal. Contemporary instances make the cost of forgetting these qualifications visible: Brexit-era immigration politics framed Eastern Europeans and non-European migrants under the same Othering grammar Hall warned against; Trump-era "shithole" framings of the Global South homogenised vastly different societies; AI/ChatGPT bias toward English-language data treats one part of the West's archive as the universal record. The binary is a diagnostic frame, not an ontological claim. The student must hold the binary and the qualifications together.
