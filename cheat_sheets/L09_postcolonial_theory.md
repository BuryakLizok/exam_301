# L09 — Post-Colonial Theory

## 1. Core Argument

Post-colonial theory insists that colonialism is not a regrettable side-event in the rise of European modernity but its constitutive condition. My core argument runs through four moves. First, colonialism has three intertwined faces — settlement (the displacement of indigenous populations by the colonising power), economic exploitation (the appropriation of land, labour and resources tied tightly to the rise of capitalism) and unequal power relations (Lecture 9 slides). Howard French, in *Born in Blackness*, contends that we must not "ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that the point is not to "add Africa in" but to "invert the current view" (French, 2021, [VERIFY p.?]); the Portuguese trade of Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s makes the global texture of modernity inescapable. Second, the West is constituted discursively against the Rest. Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works through binary oppositions — "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277) — and these binaries are not descriptions but hierarchies which the discourse "simplifies" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280) into a single dichotomy. Third, Said's account of Orientalism shows that "the Orient is not only adjacent to Europe; it is also the place of Europe's greatest and richest and oldest colonies… and one of its deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 87) and that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). Following Foucault, Said treats Orientalism as a discourse that "manage[s] — and even produce[s] — the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 88-9). Fourth, the colonised are denied subjectivity. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* registers the white child's hail — "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323) — that fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 325). Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" extends this — the figure is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261), not an empirical group — and Spivak's question of subaltern speech asks whether the oppressed can articulate themselves outside the categories of the coloniser. Enrique Dussel's intervention deepens these arguments: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 65). Post-colonial theory, then, is a critique of modernity's myth of self-origination, of its binaries, of its epistemic violence, and of its denial of voice to those it makes peripheral.

## 2. Thinkers and Concepts

- **Edward Said (1935-2003)** — Born in Jerusalem under the British Mandate, raised in Egypt, taught in New York. *Orientalism* (1978). Key concepts: Orientalism, the Orient as Europe's contrasting Other, false binary divisions, discourse/power following Foucault, "flexible positional superiority" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 26 of Ashcroft reader), the imperial archive.
- **Stuart Hall (1932-2014)** — Jamaican-British cultural theorist. *Formations of Modernity* (1992). Key concepts: the West and the Rest, the West as historical not geographical construct, discourse as "system of representation" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277), binary oppositions, internal others (Jews, Eastern Europeans, women).
- **Frantz Fanon (1925-1961)** — Born in Martinique, French Antilles; psychiatrist, fought in French resistance and later in Algerian liberation. *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961). Key concepts: object/subject dialectic, the colonial gaze, the denial of subjectivity, the colonial wound, decolonisation as struggle for voice.
- **Chandra Mohanty (1955–)** — Born in Mumbai; teaches at Syracuse. *Under Western Eyes* (1988). Key concepts: the third-world woman, discursive colonisation, women as "the other of the other," refusal of universal sisterhood.
- **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (1942–)** — Indian-American literary theorist. *Can the Subaltern Speak?* (1988). Key concepts: the subaltern, epistemic violence, strategic essentialism, the impossibility of pure recovered voice.
- **Howard French** — *Born in Blackness* (2021). Argues Africa is central to making modern world; inversion of perspective.
- **Enrique Dussel (1934-2023)** — Argentine-Mexican liberation philosopher. *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993). Key concepts: the myth of modernity, the colonial wound, the colonial difference, trans-modernity, the fallacy of developmentalism, 1492 as birth of modernity.
- **Raewyn Connell** — Australian sociologist. *Southern Theory* (2007). Key concepts: Southern theory, the metropole/periphery in sociology, the imperial archive of social science, decolonising knowledge.
- **Shmuel Eisenstadt** — Israeli sociologist. *Multiple Modernities* (2000). Key concepts: multiple modernities, civilisational variants, refusal of unilinear convergence.
- **Mohandas Gandhi (1869-1948)** — *Hind Swaraj* (1909). Key concepts: appropriate technology, swadeshi, technology as ethical practice, critique of industrial civilisation, the spinning wheel as counter-symbol.
- **Homi Bhabha** — Indian-British theorist. *The Location of Culture* (1994). Key concepts: hybridity, ambivalence, mimicry, the third space.
- **Paul Gilroy** — Black British theorist. *The Black Atlantic* (1993), *After Empire* (2004). Key concepts: planetary humanism, conviviality, post-colonial melancholia.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

- Treating "post-colonial" as merely "after colonialism" temporally — it is rather a theoretical stance on the continuing presence of colonial structures, including in formally independent states.
- Reducing Said to "the West invented the East" — Said is careful that "the Orient is not an inert fact of nature" but also that "it would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, *Orientalism*, pp. 25-6 in Ashcroft reader). The Orient exists; Orientalism is the discourse about it.
- Treating Hall's binaries as descriptive of two real groups — they are productive fictions that organise power.
- Treating the subaltern as just "the poor" — Spivak's subaltern is the structurally voiceless, particularly the subaltern woman.
- Treating Southern theory as anti-Western — Connell wants knowledge from the South taken seriously, not Western theory expelled.
- Treating multiple modernities as cultural relativism — Eisenstadt holds that modernity is real and global, but its institutional forms are plural.
- Treating Gandhi as anti-technology — he was anti-industrial, not anti-tool; the *charkha* is technology, ethically chosen.
- Forgetting that post-colonial theory is itself contested by Marxist critics (Ahmad, Dirlik) who argue it culturalises what is fundamentally about capital accumulation.
- Forgetting Dussel's point that modernity has *two* faces: an emancipatory concept and a sacrificial myth — critique must hold both.

## 7. Past-Year Exam Answers

### 2024 Q10 — What is postcolonial theory? In what ways is it a critique of modernity?

Postcolonial theory is the body of work that emerged from the late 1970s — Said, Spivak, Bhabha, Hall, Fanon read retrospectively, Mohanty, Connell, Dussel — which insists that European colonialism is not an external chapter in the story of modernity but its inner architecture. I will argue that postcolonial theory critiques modernity on four registers: historical, discursive, subjective and epistemological.

Historically, postcolonial theory rewrites the periodisation of modernity. The conventional sociological story — feudalism breaks down, the Reformation, the Enlightenment, industrial capitalism — runs internally to Europe. Dussel, by contrast, claims that "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity" and that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 65). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* makes the same move from a different angle: African gold, African labour and African slaves underwrite European capital accumulation from the 1500s onwards. The East India Company's revenue extraction in Bengal financed British industrialisation; the cotton processed in Manchester was grown by enslaved labour in the Americas. Stripping these connections out — what Hall calls the West's tendency to treat itself "as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 278) — is itself an ideological act.

Discursively, postcolonial theory critiques the binaries through which modernity tells its story. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works by polarisation: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). These are not descriptions but the simplifying grammar of a "system of representation" that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). Said's account of Orientalism develops the same critique on the Eastern axis: the Orient was "one of [Europe's] deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 87), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). Modernity in this telling is not just a description of European societies; it is a comparative claim that requires a contrasting Other.

Subjectively, postcolonial theory tracks what modernity does to the colonised. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity captures the moment when the modern liberal claim of universal personhood collides with race: the white child's call — "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323) — fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 325). Modernity, which advertises itself as the kingdom of the autonomous subject, in fact distributes subjectivity unequally; the colonised is granted the body but not the mind, the spectacle but not the gaze. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" shows the same logic on the gender axis — the average third-world woman is rendered "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261) so that Western women can appear as the modern subject.

Epistemologically, postcolonial theory attacks the production of knowledge itself. Said borrows Foucault to argue that "without examining Orientalism as a discourse one cannot possibly understand the enormously systematic discipline by which European culture was able to manage — and even produce — the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, *Orientalism*, pp. 88-9). The imperial archive — the museum, the gazetteer, the colonial census, the anthropological monograph — is part of how modernity knows the world. Spivak's question of subaltern speech extends this critique into the present: even sympathetic Western scholarship risks epistemic violence when it claims to "recover" subaltern voices that can only speak through Western categories.

Examples make the critique concrete. The British Museum's continuing custody of the Benin bronzes, looted in 1897, is modernity's epistemic violence in material form. The Rhodes Must Fall campaign at the University of Cape Town and Oxford targets exactly this: the entanglement of imperial extraction and modern knowledge. Vaccine apartheid in COVID, where Global North states blocked the TRIPS waiver, demonstrates that the colonial difference still organises the global distribution of life chances. Western media commentary in early 2022 that contrasted "civilised" Ukrainian refugees with "barbaric" Middle Eastern ones reproduced Hall's binaries verbatim. Postcolonial theory does not deny that modernity emancipated some — Dussel concedes "the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 75) — but it insists that this emancipation was paid for in genocides, expropriations and erasures whose ghosts continue to haunt the present. Its critique of modernity is therefore not nostalgic but trans-modern: it asks for a modernity that finally accounts for its full cost.

### 2024 Q11 — What is a Southern theory? What are the limitations of the concept?

Southern theory, as Raewyn Connell formulates it in her 2007 book of the same name, is a project of provincialising Northern social theory. The argument has three steps. First, the canonical sociological tradition — Marx, Weber, Durkheim, the Chicago school, Parsons, Bourdieu — was produced in the imperial metropole and read the world through metropolitan eyes. Second, the periphery generated its own knowledge — Indian sociology, African ubuntu philosophy, Latin American dependency theory, Aboriginal jurisprudence, Islamic ethics — that has been systematically ignored or downgraded. Third, the task is not merely to "add" Southern thinkers to a Northern syllabus but to invert the centre-periphery relation so that the South becomes a site of theory production, not just a field of data extraction.

Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis is a close cousin. Modernity, he argues, is real and global but not unilinear: Japanese modernity, Indian modernity, Iranian modernity each combine industrialism, bureaucracy and mass politics with distinctive civilisational substrates. Dussel's trans-modernity pushes further, calling for "the co-realization of that which it is impossible for modernity to accomplish by itself: that is, of an incorporative solidarity… between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 76). Gandhi on appropriate technology — the spinning wheel as alternative to the steel mill — and the Bandung Conference of 1955 are practical anticipations of Southern theory.

The concept does important work. It defamiliarises what Northern theorists treat as universal. Durkheim's account of the division of labour is plainly a description of nineteenth-century France presented as a general law of social evolution. Weber's "Protestant ethic" privileges one particular European trajectory. The teaching of "classical theory" in sociology departments around the world still tends to treat European white men as the source of theory and Africans, Indians and Latin Americans as the source of cases. Southern theory legitimates Indian sociologists like Veena Das, African theorists like Achille Mbembe, and Latin American liberation thinkers as theorists, not informants.

But the concept faces serious limitations. First, "the South" is itself a homogenising category. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies here: collapsing Brazil, Bangladesh, Botswana and Bolivia into a single epistemic location risks the very violence Southern theory protests. The cosmopolitan elites of São Paulo and the indigenous communities of the Amazon are both "Southern" in geography but their relation to global capital, knowledge production and modernity itself is vastly different. Second, the global-South/global-North geography has become unstable. China's Belt and Road Initiative, the rise of Indian capital, Brazilian agribusiness — these are Southern actors that reproduce Northern patterns of extraction. The South-South solidarity imagined at Bandung in 1955 is fractured by new sub-imperialisms. Third, there is a risk of nativism. The Modi government in India invokes the language of decolonisation while persecuting Muslims and Dalits; Hindu nationalism wears Southern theory's clothes for authoritarian ends. Fourth, Southern theory shares with Said's Orientalism the danger of inverted essentialism: if the West is described in monolithic terms — Cartesian, instrumental, dualist — then the South ends up being defined as everything the West is not, reinscribing the very binary Hall taught us to suspect. Fifth, Connell's project, like much Anglophone postcolonial theory, is produced in English-language Northern universities; the very dissemination of Southern theory depends on infrastructures of the North. Sixth, there are Marxist critics — Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber — who argue that focusing on the geography of theory occludes the more fundamental question of class and capital, which run across both hemispheres.

A defender of Southern theory can answer some of these. Connell does not claim that all knowledge from the South is liberatory or that all knowledge from the North is corrupt; she argues for symmetrical seriousness. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities does not collapse difference but proliferates it. Dussel's trans-modernity explicitly tries to avoid both the universalism of Northern theory and the relativism of pure Southern particularism. Concrete examples — the African Union's pushback against IMF structural adjustment, the Latin American "pink tide" governments' attempts to articulate post-neoliberal political economy, the Grameen Bank's microfinance model — show Southern theory generating practical political innovation. Yet the limitations are real. Southern theory works best when it is granular — when it talks about particular Southern thinkers, particular Southern struggles, particular Southern epistemic traditions — and weakest when it deploys "the South" as a homogeneous counter-pole to "the North." In that mode it risks repeating, with the signs reversed, the Eurocentrism it set out to displace.

### 2025 Q11 — How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between 'the West and the Rest'?

The distinction between the West and the Rest is, on the surface, a piece of casual geography. Post-colonial theorists from Hall onwards have argued that it is in fact a discursive machine, and they have attacked it on four fronts: showing that the West is a historical not a geographical entity; revealing the binary logic that sustains it; exposing the power relations it conceals; and producing alternative cartographies that displace it.

The first move is Hall's. In *Formations of Modernity*, Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Geographically, Hall notes, Japan is east but counts as West; eastern Europe is in Europe but is unsure if it is in the West; Latin America is in the western hemisphere but is filed under the Third World. "The West is as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 276). The West is a historical construct — a name for "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277) — and therefore something that was made, can be unmade, and could have been otherwise.

The second move is to expose the binaries. Hall lays out the oppositional grammar: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (lecture slide 9; Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Each pair is a hierarchy, not just a difference. The West is the positive term; the Rest is what the West is not. Said's account of Orientalism extends this to the Eastern variant: "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 88). The Orient is timeless, mysterious, feminine, decadent. Hall's key sentence is that this discourse "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). The discourse collapses internal heterogeneity on both sides.

The third move is to expose power. "The relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). For Said, drawing on Foucault, the West/Rest discourse is not innocent description; it is "Orientalism as a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 25 in Ashcroft). The British Museum's Benin bronzes, the East India Company's tax registers, the colonial census of India, the anthropological monograph on "savage" societies — all are technologies through which the West produced, managed and ruled the Rest. Dussel sharpens the point: the West is not just a name for one civilisation among others but for the self-appointed centre of "World History," with the periphery serving as raw material for its self-image (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 65).

The fourth move is to displace the cartography. Howard French inverts the standard map by showing that "the Portuguese use cotton from India to buy gold in Africa to use as coinage in Europe (in the 1500s!)" (lecture slide 4) — there is no Europe without Africa and Asia. The Gall-Peters and "World Turned Upside Down" projections shown in the lecture make the Mercator's distortion visible. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" challenges the West/Rest binary inside feminism, showing how Western women's self-image as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261) depends on a fabricated third-world woman. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity shows what the binary feels like from inside: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 325).

Concrete contemporary instances make the challenge urgent. Western media in 2022 framed Ukraine as "civilised, European" against Russia as "barbaric," and contrasted blue-eyed Ukrainian refugees with "less relatable" Iraqi and Afghan ones — Hall's binaries in real time. Vaccine apartheid in COVID showed the West/Rest distinction governing access to life itself. Rhodes Must Fall and "decolonise the curriculum" target the discursive machinery in the universities. The Bandung Conference of 1955 is the classic counter-cartography from below, and the African Union's resistance to IMF structural adjustment is its contemporary descendant.

A balanced assessment must acknowledge that these challenges are not symmetrical successes. The Mercator projection still hangs on classroom walls. The British Museum still holds the bronzes. "Civilised vs barbaric" still organises international politics. But post-colonial theorists have made the West/Rest distinction visible as a distinction — they have denaturalised it. We can no longer treat it as innocent geography. Hall, Said, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel together have replaced a description with an indictment.

### 2025 Q13 — Outline and assess if Southern theory addresses inequalities in the Global South.

Southern theory, in Raewyn Connell's formulation, is a critical project that aims to displace the metropolitan dominance of Northern sociological theory and to take seriously the intellectual production of the Global South. I will outline the project's main claims, then assess how far it actually addresses the inequalities that scar the South itself.

The outline runs as follows. Connell argues that the canonical European tradition was generated in conditions of imperial dominance and reflects metropolitan preoccupations. Marx's account of capitalism takes the British factory as its paradigm; Durkheim's anomie diagnoses a French nineteenth-century crisis; Weber's rationalisation thesis is European to its core. Yet these are taught as universal sociology. Meanwhile, theories generated in the South — Latin American dependency theory (Prebisch, Cardoso, Frank), African post-colonial state theory (Mahmood Mamdani, Achille Mbembe), Indian subaltern studies (Ranajit Guha, Partha Chatterjee), Aboriginal Australian sovereignty thinkers, Islamic political economy — are filed under "area studies" or "anthropology" rather than read as theory. Southern theory demands intellectual equality. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis is a close ally, arguing that "modernity" is plural and that civilisational matrices shape different modernity paths. Dussel's trans-modernity, Gandhi's appropriate technology, the Bandung Conference of 1955 and the African Union's ongoing critique of IMF conditionality are practical companions.

The assessment of whether Southern theory addresses inequalities *within* the Global South is more ambivalent. There are five reasons to be hopeful and five to be cautious.

On the optimistic side: first, Southern theory enables Southern intellectuals to take their own concerns as theoretical starting points rather than imported ones. The Grameen Bank's microfinance, Brazilian participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, the Indian Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act all emerged from Southern intellectual cultures that took the rural poor as central subjects of theory. Second, it provides language for analysing intra-Southern phenomena — Chinese investment in Africa, Indian capital in Bangladesh, Brazilian agribusiness in Paraguay — that Northern theory could only call "development." Third, dependency theory, a paradigmatic Southern contribution, gave Latin Americans the analytical tools to explain why decades of post-independence growth still left them peripheral. Fourth, multiple modernities pluralises political imagination: Iranian modernity, Japanese modernity, Indian modernity become possible objects rather than failures of Northern modernity. Fifth, Southern theory animates the decolonise-the-curriculum movement that is reshaping universities from Cape Town to Cambridge, opening curricula to Black, indigenous and Southern voices.

On the cautious side: first, "the Global South" is itself a homogenising category. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies: the lived realities of São Paulo's elites and Bolivian indigenous women are not the same. Second, much Southern theory is produced in English in Northern universities — Spivak at Columbia, Bhabha at Harvard, Mbembe at Duke — and travels through Northern publishing infrastructures. The Indian peasant whose voice subaltern studies wanted to recover does not write Routledge books. Third, Southern theory can be co-opted by nationalist authoritarianism. Hindu nationalism under Modi mobilises "decolonisation" to attack Muslims and Dalits, weaponising the language of intellectual decolonisation for sectarian ends. Fourth, the new Southern capitalisms — China, India, Brazil — reproduce the extractive patterns of Northern colonialism inside the South. The Belt and Road Initiative's debt-trap critique echoes 1970s dependency theory but with Beijing as creditor. Southern theory has been slow to develop the conceptual tools to analyse this. Fifth, Marxist critics like Vivek Chibber argue that Southern theory's emphasis on cultural difference obscures capital's universal logic and disarms class politics. The Bangladeshi garment worker, on this reading, shares more with the Manchester mill worker of 1850 than with the Dhaka professor who theorises her.

The assessment, then, is mixed. Southern theory is indispensable for putting Northern theory in its place and for legitimating Southern intellectual production. It has produced concrete political and economic experiments. But it does not, by itself, address inequalities within the South. The Adivasi displaced by a dam in Madhya Pradesh, the rural woman in northern Nigeria denied schooling, the Bolivian indigenous communities whose lithium feeds Northern batteries — their inequalities are produced both by global structures and by Southern elites. Southern theory has a tendency to focus on the North-South axis and to bracket the class, caste, gender and ethnic stratifications that organise Southern societies. A truly decolonial Southern theory needs Mohanty's intersectional warning alongside Connell's geographical one. It needs Dussel's trans-modernity, which explicitly refuses to make the South a mirror image of the North. Whether such a Southern theory exists fully in 2026 is debatable. The seeds are present in subaltern studies, in liberation theology, in indigenous jurisprudence, in feminist work like Mohanty's. But the project remains unfinished, and the global academy's structural inequalities still skew its development.

### 2025 Q15 — Critically discuss two aspects of the Gandhian view on technology.

Gandhi's view of technology, set out most fully in *Hind Swaraj* (1909) and lived out across forty years of political practice, is one of the most distinctive non-Western contributions to the philosophy of modernity. I will discuss two aspects in particular: technology as ethical and embodied practice rather than instrument, and technology as a question of scale and dignity, captured in the icon of the *charkha* (spinning wheel). I will then assess each critically.

The first aspect is technology as ethical practice. For Western modernity from Bacon onwards, technology is instrumental: a means to a separately chosen end. The plough, the steam engine, the algorithm — these are neutral tools, and whatever moral content they have lies in how humans use them. Gandhi rejects this view. Technology, for him, embeds an entire ethical world. The factory does not just produce cloth; it produces a particular kind of human being — one disciplined by the clock, alienated from the rhythms of nature, separated from the product of their labour. Industrial technology, even when used "for good," carries with it a metaphysical commitment to speed, mastery, and extraction. The hand-loom, by contrast, embeds a different ethical world: it links body, breath and material, it preserves the dignity of the labourer, it situates the worker in a community of craft. Gandhi's *Hind Swaraj* therefore opposes railways, machinery and mass production not because he believes humans should be unable to weave or travel but because the particular technologies of British industrial modernity carry the colonial relation inside them. The cotton mills of Manchester ran on cotton picked by enslaved labour in the Americas and woven into cloth that destroyed the Bengali handloom industry. Gandhi's *swadeshi* (self-reliance) movement asked Indians to spin their own thread, weave their own cloth, and thereby refuse the embedded ethics of the imperial supply chain.

Critically, this aspect has both strengths and weaknesses. Its strength is that it anticipates by half a century what STS scholarship and Latour have argued more recently: that technologies are never neutral, that they carry social relations in their design. The smartphone is not just a tool; it is a particular form of attention, sociality and surveillance. The drone is not just a flying camera; it is a particular form of warfare. Gandhi's intuition is now mainstream in the philosophy of technology. The weakness is that the ethical-embedment argument can slide into nostalgia. The handloom is not automatically more ethical than the power loom; village India in 1909 was not a paradise of dignified craft but a caste-stratified, patriarchal hierarchy in which Dalit weavers were despised. B. R. Ambedkar's critique of Gandhi runs hard here: the village is not the answer for those whom the village oppressed.

The second aspect is technology as a question of scale and dignity. Gandhi famously contrasted the *charkha* (spinning wheel) with the steel plant. Nehru, the modernising prime minister, called the great dams and steel plants "temples of modern India." Gandhi answered with the spinning wheel: small, local, controllable, distributed, intelligible. Technology, for Gandhi, should be on a human scale — small enough that the person using it understands it, owns it, and is not owned by it. This anticipates E. F. Schumacher's "appropriate technology" (or "intermediate technology") movement of the 1970s, and the contemporary critiques of platform capitalism. The Belt and Road Initiative, large-scale agribusiness in Brazil, hydroelectric mega-projects in India that displace millions — all are instances of the Nehruvian, not Gandhian, road. The Grameen Bank's microfinance, locally rooted credit cooperatives, and small solar systems for off-grid villages are nearer the Gandhian sensibility.

Critically, the scale-and-dignity argument has its own strengths and weaknesses. Its strength is its prophetic accuracy. The ecological costs of mega-scale technology — the Amazon deforestation crisis, climate breakdown, the resource demands of digital infrastructure — vindicate Gandhi's warning that mastery has its price. His critique of consumerism — "the world has enough for everyone's need but not for everyone's greed" — echoes through every contemporary degrowth movement. Its weakness is that "appropriate technology" can become an excuse for under-investing in the South. Vaccine apartheid during COVID illustrated this: when the Global South needed mRNA technology fast, "appropriate technology" was no answer. Insulin, antiretrovirals, fertilisers and electricity grids cannot all be made on a spinning wheel. Decolonising technology means having access to the most advanced tools, not retreating from them. The Hindu-nationalist invocation of "Indian wisdom" against "Western science" — which has, under Modi, included claims about ancient Indian aviation and plastic surgery — shows how Gandhian critique can be debased.

The two aspects together give us a partial but powerful view. Gandhi's ethical and scalar critique of technology was an early and prescient non-Western philosophy of modernity, and it continues to animate appropriate-technology, degrowth, food sovereignty and digital-commons movements globally. But the critique needs to be held alongside the Ambedkarite warning that not every Gandhian village is a paradise, and the developmentalist warning that some Western technologies — vaccines, antibiotics, contraceptives, semiconductors — are non-negotiable for Southern dignity. The strongest reading is one that takes Gandhi as offering critical *questions* about technology — about what it does to bodies, communities, and ecologies — rather than fixed answers about which technologies to use.

### 2023 Q11 — How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between "the West and the Rest"?

This question is materially identical to 2025 Q11 above; I write a distinct answer here pivoting on the historical-archive dimension. Post-colonial theorists from the 1970s onwards have challenged the West/Rest distinction at three depths: as a discursive system (Hall, Said), as an experiential reality for the colonised (Fanon, Mohanty), and as a historical falsehood about who made the modern world (Dussel, French).

At the discursive depth, Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest demonstrates that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Hall identifies four functions of the concept: it classifies (West vs non-West), represents (a composite image), measures (a standard of comparison), and evaluates (developed = good = desirable). "In short, it functions as an ideology" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). The discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). The binaries — rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic — are the operating code of the discourse. Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation in the Eastern direction. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). The point is structural: the West cannot be the West without the Rest because its identity is *constituted* by contrast.

At the experiential depth, Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* tells us what the West/Rest binary feels like inside a body. The white child's "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323) is the discourse hailing the colonised; Fanon is fixed, dissected, given back his body "sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). The West/Rest distinction is not just a sentence in a textbook; it is a wound on the flesh. Mohanty extends this analysis along the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261) precisely by producing a "third world woman" who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261). The challenge is to refuse the binary in both its halves.

At the historical depth, Dussel and French rewrite the genealogy of modernity. Dussel argues that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 65) and that the Iberian conquest of the New World from 1492 is constitutive, not incidental. French's *Born in Blackness* makes Africa central: "He argues that we must not ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that "the point is not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (lecture slide 4). The standard narrative — Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution — that Habermas inherits from Hegel and that omits Spain, Portugal, the slave trade and the colonised periphery is not just incomplete; it is a Eurocentric distortion. The imperial archive — the museum, the gazetteer, the colonial map — is part of the discourse and part of the falsification.

Each register has its political form. Hall's discursive challenge animates the "decolonise the curriculum" movement — Why Is My Curriculum White? — and Rhodes Must Fall. Said's challenge animates museum repatriation debates: the British Museum's Benin bronzes, the Parthenon marbles, the Quai Branly's African collections. Fanon and Mohanty's experiential challenge animates Black Lives Matter and post-colonial feminism. Dussel and French's historical challenge animates the 1619 Project, the reparations debate and the reconsideration of British imperial history in school curricula. Each of these contemporary movements is, in the strict sense, a post-colonial challenge to the West/Rest binary in action.

A balanced conclusion notes the limits. The Mercator projection still organises classroom geography. The Bretton Woods institutions still organise global finance. Western media in 2022 reverted instantly to "civilised vs barbaric" framing for Ukraine. Vaccine apartheid showed that the discourse retains material force in life-and-death distribution. Yet the post-colonial intellectual achievement is real: the West/Rest binary can no longer be deployed innocently. Hall taught us its grammar, Said its archive, Fanon its embodiment, Mohanty its feminist version, Dussel its historical falsification, and French its missing African heart. The challenge is now to convert this denaturalisation into structural change.

### 2023 Q13 — Critically analyse Southern Theory as an alternative to European Modernity. What are its limits?

European modernity, in its classical telling, is a story of self-generated progress — the Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, industrial revolution, parliamentary democracy — that radiates outward from a small north-western corner of Europe to instruct the rest of humanity. Southern theory, as Connell articulates it, refuses this story. It is both a critique (European modernity is not what it says it is) and an alternative (the Global South has been producing modernity all along, on its own terms). I will analyse the alternative and then catalogue its limits.

The alternative comes in several voices. Connell's Southern theory itself argues that "Western theory ignores knowledge from the Global South" and demands that the sociological canon be re-read with this exclusion in mind. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis holds that modernity is real but plural: Japanese modernity, Indian modernity, Iranian modernity each combine institutional rationalisation with civilisational substrates that produce distinctive social forms. Gandhi on appropriate technology offers a normative alternative — the spinning wheel against the steel plant, swadeshi against industrial dependency. Dussel's trans-modernity offers a philosophical alternative — "the co-realization of that which it is impossible for modernity to accomplish by itself: that is, of an incorporative solidarity… between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 76). Howard French historicises the alternative: African gold and labour built European modernity, so the South was never the receiver but always a co-producer.

The strengths of this alternative are several. It punctures the self-congratulation of European modernity. It legitimates Southern intellectual labour. It explains why classical sociology never quite fits Southern societies — Durkheim's anomie, Weber's iron cage, even Marx's English working class are not the keys to understanding rural Bihar or the Bolivian altiplano. It generates political experimentation: the Bandung Conference of 1955, the African Union's resistance to IMF structural adjustment, the Latin American "pink tide," indigenous-led environmental movements in Ecuador and Bolivia. It opens space for non-Western traditions — Confucian, Islamic, Hindu, indigenous — to be considered as theoretical resources rather than ethnographic curiosities.

The limits, however, are real. First, "Southern theory" risks a new homogeneity. The South is enormous and internally fractured. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies here: collapsing the world's diversities into a single Southern voice repeats the simplifying violence that Hall's "the West and the Rest" performed in the other direction. Second, multiple modernities can become culturalist apology. Eisenstadt's framework risks treating every authoritarianism that wears local clothes as "another modernity." Hindu nationalism under Modi, Erdogan's Turkey, Putin's Russia, the Saudi monarchy: are these multiple modernities or are they ordinary authoritarianisms with local accents? The framework's relativism can disable critique.

Third, Southern theory can be co-opted. Modi's BJP uses the language of decolonisation to legitimate Hindu majoritarianism. The Chinese Communist Party uses Southern theory to defend its surveillance state as a non-Western modernity. Belt and Road infrastructure investment is framed as "South-South cooperation" even when it reproduces extractive patterns. Fourth, the alternative depends materially on Northern infrastructure. Connell's *Southern Theory* is published by Polity Press in Cambridge. The papers of Southern scholars are indexed in Northern citation databases. Even the critique of the metropole travels via the metropole. Fifth, Marxist critics — Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber, Neil Lazarus — argue that focusing on the geography of theory obscures the universal logic of capital. The Bangladeshi garment worker and the British call-centre worker face the same world market; treating their experiences as incommensurable Southern and Northern modernities risks disarming class solidarity.

Sixth, Southern theory tends to focus on the macro North-South relation and to neglect intra-Southern stratifications — caste, ethnicity, gender, urban-rural. Liberation theology and feminist Southern theorists like Maria Lugones and Veronica Gago do this better than Connell's original frame. Seventh, there is a danger of nostalgia. Gandhi on appropriate technology is brilliant but the village is not automatically good. Ambedkar's critique of Gandhi — that the village is the *site* of caste oppression — still stands. Eighth, the "alternative modernities" register can paper over how thoroughly globalised the South already is. The Indian middle class consumes the same Netflix shows, drives the same SUVs and chases the same MBA credentials as its Northern counterparts. Treating India as an "alternative modernity" can be a way of not noticing how Northern it already is.

A balanced conclusion: Southern theory is a necessary corrective to a Eurocentric sociology, and Eisenstadt, Dussel, Gandhi and Connell together open theoretical space that the discipline desperately needs. But Southern theory is not a finished alternative. It needs Mohanty's intersectional warning, Ambedkar's caste critique, Marxist class analysis and Bhabha's hybridity together to avoid becoming a new orthodoxy. The alternative to European modernity is not Southern modernity but trans-modernity in Dussel's sense — a global conversation in which the South finally counts as theory.

### 2023 Q14 — How are non-western models proposing a different relationship between modernity and technology? Discuss with examples.

The dominant Western model of the modernity-technology relation, inherited from Bacon, Descartes and the Industrial Revolution, treats technology as the instrumental means by which humanity masters nature and accelerates history. Non-Western models challenge this in several distinct registers: ethical, scalar, ecological, and democratic. I will discuss three — Gandhi on appropriate technology, Chinese state technology, and indigenous and Latin American buen vivir — with examples, and then assess the broader question.

Gandhi on appropriate technology is the most theoretically developed non-Western alternative. *Hind Swaraj* (1909) argues that industrial technology embeds an entire ethical world: the factory produces alienated labour, the railway produces hurried lives, the steam engine produces colonial extraction. The *charkha* (spinning wheel) is the counter-symbol: small, local, controllable, dignifying. Swadeshi (self-reliance) movements in early twentieth-century India organised around hand-spun *khadi* cloth, both as economic resistance to the Lancashire cotton mills and as ethical practice. The point is not that Indians should be primitive but that the *choice* of technology is a moral choice about what kind of human being and what kind of community one wants to build. E. F. Schumacher's "Small is Beautiful" (1973) and the appropriate technology movement carry this forward; contemporary degrowth, food sovereignty (La Via Campesina) and digital commons movements are descendants.

Chinese state technology offers a very different non-Western alternative: not anti-industrial but state-directed and tied to a different political theory. The Belt and Road Initiative, the rapid build-out of solar and high-speed rail, and the social credit system together represent a model where technology is mobilised by a developmental state for collective goals rather than purely commercial ones. From a Western liberal perspective this is alarming; from a Southern theory perspective it complicates the binary, showing that technological modernity does not require Anglo-American liberal individualism. Example: China's installation of more solar capacity than the rest of the world combined, and the BRI's infrastructure investments across Asia and Africa, demonstrate a different state-technology relation. Critics, including post-colonial scholars, note that this is also a new sub-imperialism and that ethnic minorities (Uyghurs) face surveillance modernity at its sharpest. So this is not a liberatory alternative, but it is a non-Western one.

Indigenous and Latin American buen vivir (Quechua *sumak kawsay*, Aymara *suma qamaña*) offers a third model. Buen vivir is enshrined in the Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutions and proposes a relation to technology mediated by ecological and spiritual obligations to *Pachamama* (Mother Earth). Technologies that extract irreversibly from the earth — open-pit mining, lithium extraction for batteries, mega-dams — are evaluated negatively even when they produce growth. Indigenous-led environmental movements have stopped pipelines (Standing Rock in the US), forced consultation regimes on mining companies, and produced legal innovations like rights of nature. Example: Ecuador's 2008 constitution gave nature legal personhood; this is technological-modernity-rethought from the ground of indigenous cosmology.

Dussel's trans-modernity provides a philosophical frame for these alternatives. It is "the co-realization of that which it is impossible for modernity to accomplish by itself" — political, economic, ecological, erotic, pedagogical and religious liberation across centre and periphery (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 76). Trans-modern technology is not anti-modern; it incorporates Northern technical knowledge but subordinates it to ethical, ecological and democratic norms developed elsewhere. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis frames the more general claim: there are many technology-modernity relations, and the unilinear Northern story is one option among several.

Critically, these alternatives have limits. Gandhi's appropriate technology cannot make insulin. Chinese state technology produces surveillance and Uyghur internment camps. Buen vivir constitutional provisions in Ecuador have been routinely overridden by extractive governments needing oil revenues. Hindu nationalism's invocation of "Indian science" includes embarrassing claims about ancient flying machines that should warn us about how easily decolonisation rhetoric can degrade into pseudoscience.

The serious question is whether these non-Western models offer ways to combine the genuine benefits of modern technology — vaccines, antibiotics, contraception, electricity, telecommunications — with a relationship to nature and community that is not extractive and not alienated. The answer in 2026 is partial yes. The Grameen Bank's microfinance, mobile banking innovations like M-Pesa in Kenya, the spread of solar in rural Africa via small distributed systems, India's Aadhaar-enabled welfare delivery (with all its problems), the indigenous-led "rights of nature" movement, and the BRICS Bank as alternative to the World Bank all gesture toward a different modernity-technology relation. None is sufficient on its own. Together they suggest that the Western instrumental model is not the only available grammar for the technological future. The critical post-colonial point — Gandhi's, Dussel's, Eisenstadt's, Connell's — is that thinking about technology is also thinking about modernity, and that thinking about modernity is also thinking about who counts.

### 2022 Q13 — How does the concept of "the West" set itself up in distinction to "the Rest"?

The concept of "the West" sets itself up against "the Rest" through a discursive operation that Hall describes with unusual clarity in *Formations of Modernity*. I will trace this operation in four steps — homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation, and naturalisation — and then assess the political work it does.

Step one is homogenisation. The West is internally diverse — Germanic, Latin, Nordic, Mediterranean, Anglo-Saxon — and the Rest is even more so — China, India, the Islamic world, sub-Saharan Africa, indigenous Americas, Aboriginal Australasia. Yet the discourse of the West and the Rest "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). The internal differences of the West are collapsed in favour of its shared character; the internal differences of the Rest are collapsed in favour of their shared difference from the West.

Step two is polarisation. The discourse arranges itself through binary oppositions: rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic (lecture slide 9; Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). Saussure taught us that meaning works through difference — "night" means what it does because it is opposite to "day." Hall extends this to cultural identity: "national cultures acquire their strong sense of identity by contrasting themselves with other cultures" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 279). The West knows itself by *not being* the Rest. The Rest is the negative pole that makes the positive pole legible.

Step three is hierarchisation. The binaries are not symmetrical. The first term of each pair carries positive value; the second carries negative value. The West is rational, cultured, intellectual, progressive, scientific; the Rest is irrational, savage, embodied, traditional, magical. Hall is explicit: the discourse "provides criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked and around which powerful positive and negative feelings cluster… 'the West' = developed = good = desirable; or the 'non-West' = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277). The West/Rest binary is not difference; it is hierarchy. It legitimates colonial intervention, civilising missions, and developmental aid as moral obligations to lift the Rest toward the West.

Step four is naturalisation. The discourse hides its own constructed character. Hall insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277), but the discourse presents the West as a geographical fact, an obvious self-evident location on the map. The Mercator projection visually naturalises Eurocentric geography. The university teaching of "Western civilisation" naturalises the West as a continuous tradition from Athens to Washington via Jerusalem, Rome, Florence, London and Paris — a fictional genealogy that omits the Islamic transmission of Greek philosophy and treats the West as autochthonous.

Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation on the Eastern axis. "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 88). The Orient is constructed as Europe's "deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 87), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). Mohanty extends this to the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities" against a "third world woman" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261) who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261).

The political work of this discursive operation is extensive. It legitimated the East India Company's rule in Bengal as the gift of order to the irrational. It legitimated French colonisation in Algeria as the *mission civilisatrice*. It legitimated American intervention in the Middle East as the export of democracy. It legitimates contemporary media framings — "civilised" Ukrainians versus "barbaric" Russians and the absent Afghan refugee; "white saviour" NGO campaigns; the moral asymmetry between Israeli and Palestinian deaths in international coverage. Hall's point is that the discourse is "so destructive — it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280).

To set up the West against the Rest is therefore not merely to draw a line; it is to perform a sustained ideological operation that homogenises, polarises, hierarchises and naturalises difference, and that has organised five centuries of colonialism, racism and global inequality. To undo the West/Rest distinction is to refuse all four operations — to recognise internal heterogeneity on both sides, to break the binary lockstep, to refuse the implicit hierarchy, and to re-historicise what the discourse presents as natural geography.

### 2022 Q15 — Why is it important to examine social theory which emerges from beyond the West?

Examining social theory from beyond the West matters for at least four reasons: epistemic, political, methodological and ethical. I will take each in turn, drawing on Connell, Eisenstadt, Dussel, Gandhi and Mohanty, and then assess the case for and against.

The epistemic reason is that the canonical Western sociological tradition is partial. Marx, Weber and Durkheim wrote about nineteenth-century Europe and generalised their findings into universal laws. Marx's account of capitalism takes the English factory as paradigm; Durkheim's account of solidarity takes industrial France; Weber's rationalisation thesis takes Protestant Northern Europe. When we try to use these theories to understand contemporary India or West Africa or indigenous Mexico, they fit awkwardly at best. Connell's Southern theory argues that Indian sociology (Veena Das, Partha Chatterjee), African theory (Mahmood Mamdani, Achille Mbembe), Latin American dependency theory (Cardoso, Frank) and indigenous jurisprudence have produced concepts that classical sociology lacks — concepts of communal land, of subaltern politics, of post-colonial state, of cosmovision. Without these we do not have a global sociology; we have a parochial European sociology dressed up as universal.

The political reason is that knowledge production is itself power. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest functions as ideology; Said's account of Orientalism shows that European knowledge of the East was "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 25 in Ashcroft). If knowledge is power, then producing knowledge from the periphery is part of political emancipation. Bandung in 1955, the non-aligned movement, dependency theory's challenge to modernisation theory, the African Union's recent push back against IMF structural adjustment, indigenous-led "rights of nature" constitutionalism in Ecuador and Bolivia — these are political projects that depend on theoretical work done outside the metropole. The decolonise-the-curriculum movement in UK universities applies the same logic to higher education itself.

The methodological reason is that theory generated in one setting and applied uncritically to another distorts both. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" is exemplary: Western feminist theory, applied to South Asian or African women without grounding in their actual lives, produces "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world'" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261). The remedy is to theorise from the ground: "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 262). Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis applies the same methodological principle to modernity itself: there are many institutional combinations, and treating one trajectory as universal misdescribes the others. Methodologically, then, examining non-Western theory is not optional decoration; it is required for empirical accuracy.

The ethical reason is the deepest. Dussel's trans-modernity argues that modernity has two faces: an emancipatory concept and a sacrificial myth. The myth has produced genocides — the Americas after 1492, the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the Belgian Congo, partition violence in 1947, the famines under British rule in India. The victims of modernity have a moral claim to be heard. Spivak's question of subaltern speech captures the ethical stakes: even when we want to listen, the categories through which we listen may silence those we want to hear. Examining non-Western social theory is an attempt to extend the categories — to make theoretical space for voices that the canonical tradition cannot accommodate. Dussel writes that the "negated and victimized 'other-face' of modernity… must, in the first place, discover itself as innocent, as the 'innocent victim' of a ritual sacrifice, who, in the process of discovering itself as innocent may now judge modernity as guilty" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 76). Examining non-Western theory is part of that ethical recovery.

Concrete examples sharpen the case. The Grameen Bank's microfinance was developed by Bangladeshi economist Muhammad Yunus and has been imitated globally — a Southern theoretical innovation that travelled. Latin American dependency theory has reshaped global development economics. Indian subaltern studies has influenced historiography worldwide. Indigenous-led environmental movements have produced concepts (rights of nature, buen vivir, the seventh-generation principle) that are now central to climate ethics. Gandhi on appropriate technology has animated degrowth and sustainable-development thinking. Without these non-Western contributions, contemporary social theory would be massively poorer.

There are objections to address. First, the objection from universalism: knowledge is knowledge regardless of where it is produced; geography is not epistemology. Reply: this is true in principle but false in practice, because the global academy structurally over-represents Northern voices and citations. Second, the objection from quality: not all non-Western theory is good. Reply: of course not; nor is all Western theory good. The principle of equal seriousness does not entail equal validity for each particular claim. Third, the Marxist objection: focusing on theory's geography obscures class. Reply: a properly Southern Marxism (Walter Rodney, Samir Amin, José Carlos Mariátegui) shows that class and geography are not alternatives but co-constituents.

In conclusion, examining non-Western social theory matters because epistemically it corrects partial knowledge, politically it underwrites emancipatory projects, methodologically it secures accuracy, and ethically it acknowledges the victims of modernity. It is not a courtesy extended to the South; it is a condition of sociological intelligence about the world we actually live in.

### 2021 Q13 — Using at least two theorists, outline and assess the postcolonial critique of "the West".

I will use Stuart Hall and Frantz Fanon as my two theorists, and I will assess the postcolonial critique of "the West" along three dimensions: discursive (Hall), experiential (Fanon), and political. I will then weigh the strengths and limits of the critique.

Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest, set out in *Formations of Modernity* pp. 276-280, makes the critique discursive. The West, Hall argues, is "as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 276). It is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 277), and it functions as ideology. The concept performs four operations: classification (West/non-West), representation (a composite image), comparison (a measuring stick) and evaluation (a hierarchical ranking). The discourse works through binary oppositions — rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic — and through a simplifying logic that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). Crucially, Hall shows that the West constituted itself partly by contrast: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 278). The West cannot be the West alone; its identity is relational, parasitic on the Rest it produced as Other.

Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* makes the critique experiential. Fanon takes the discursive structures Hall describes and shows what they feel like in a human life. The famous scene on the train, where a white child cries "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323), turns the discourse into a hail. The Black colonised subject is fixed by the white gaze: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am *fixed*" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 325). His body is "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored…" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). The white world bars him "from all participation. A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). What Hall describes structurally Fanon registers viscerally: the West is not just an idea, it is a wound on the colonised body.

Together, Hall and Fanon constitute a powerful critique. Hall provides the structural anatomy; Fanon provides the lived testimony. The critique can be summarised in three claims. First, the West is a historical construct, not a natural fact. Second, the West constitutes itself by negation — by producing and contrasting itself with the Rest. Third, this constitution does material and psychic harm to those positioned as the Rest. The critique extends naturally to contemporary phenomena: museum repatriation debates over the Benin bronzes; Rhodes Must Fall; the Western media's "civilised vs barbaric" framing of Ukraine 2022; vaccine apartheid during COVID; the "white saviour" critique of NGO interventions; and Mohanty's parallel critique of "Third World Woman."

Assessing the critique requires acknowledging both strengths and limits. The strengths are considerable. The critique denaturalises a category that had been taken for granted; it links discourse to power following Said and Foucault; it provides intellectual resources for political movements; it has reshaped curricula, museums and media. It has supplied a vocabulary — Orientalism, the colonial wound, decolonising knowledge, epistemic violence, the imperial archive — that has travelled globally.

The limits are also real. First, the critique can produce its own essentialism: by speaking constantly of "the West" the critique risks reifying the very entity it wants to dissolve, treating it as a coherent agent rather than a contested space. Hall is alert to this — "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 279) — but his readers often forget. Second, the critique can be culturalist, focusing on representation at the expense of political economy. Marxist post-colonial critics (Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber) argue that the Bangladeshi garment worker and the British call-centre worker face the same global capitalism, and that focusing on cultural difference disarms class solidarity. Third, Fanon's account of colonial subjectivity, while powerful, is sometimes read as static — as if every Black subject in every setting experiences the white gaze identically. Fanon himself, who was also a revolutionary in Algeria, did not believe this; the *Wretched of the Earth* is precisely about agency and refusal. Fourth, the critique is now sometimes co-opted by authoritarian nationalisms — Hindu nationalism, Putin's Russia, Erdoğan's Turkey — that wear decolonisation's clothes for sectarian ends.

A balanced conclusion: Hall and Fanon together produce one of the most important critical achievements of twentieth-century social theory. They have changed how we read the news, how we walk through museums, how we teach the canon, and how we think about ourselves. The critique remains indispensable. But it needs to be held with care — with class analysis, with attention to internal heterogeneity, with awareness of how easily critique can be appropriated. The point is not to retire the critique but to deepen it.

### 2021 Q16 — In what ways does 'Southern theory' take forward debates in 'Western' social theory?

Southern theory, in Connell's formulation, is sometimes presented as an alternative to Western social theory. I will argue that it is better understood as taking forward debates that Western social theory itself began but could not complete. Southern theory advances Western theory in five areas: the sociology of knowledge, the theory of modernity, feminist theory, theories of capitalism, and ecological theory.

In the sociology of knowledge, Western theory had already produced Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia*, Bourdieu's account of the academic field, Foucault's archaeology of knowledge, and Latour's actor-network theory. Each tried to show that knowledge is shaped by its conditions of production. But Western theory rarely turned this insight on itself; it generally exempted the canonical sociological tradition from its own sociology of knowledge. Connell's Southern theory completes the gesture. It asks: what does it mean that the sociological canon was produced overwhelmingly by white men in imperial metropoles between 1830 and 1930? It points out that classical theory's empirical universe was Europe; its informants were European; its problems were European. The honest extension of Bourdieu's reflexive sociology is to recognise the imperial conditions of sociology itself. Subaltern studies (Guha, Chatterjee) and Mbembe's African post-colonial theory take forward Foucault's analysis of power-knowledge by extending it to the imperial archive.

In the theory of modernity, Weber's account of rationalisation, Habermas's account of the public sphere, Giddens's account of disembedding, Beck's account of risk society — all tried to characterise the distinctive features of modernity. But they all treated modernity as a European achievement that then radiated outward. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis takes the debate forward by pluralising it: Japanese modernity, Indian modernity, Iranian modernity are not failed approximations of European modernity but distinctive combinations. Dussel's trans-modernity goes further, asking what a modernity that fully acknowledged its colonial costs would look like. These are not rejections of Habermas; they are corrections that Habermas could not produce from within his own framework.

In feminist theory, second-wave Western feminism produced massive theoretical achievements — Beauvoir's *Second Sex*, Friedan, Millett, Firestone, MacKinnon. But, as Mohanty showed, it tended to treat its own historical particularity as universal. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" — the figure as "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261) — extends second-wave feminism into a genuinely global feminism. The insistence that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 262) takes forward feminist theory's own commitment to intersectionality before that word was widely used. Black feminist theory (Crenshaw, hooks, Hill Collins) does similar work from within the Western academy but with similar Southern-theoretical inflections.

In theories of capitalism, Marx had identified primitive accumulation and the role of colonial plunder but his successors in the European Marxist tradition tended to treat colonialism as an addendum. Dependency theory (Prebisch, Cardoso, Frank, Wallerstein) takes Marx forward by making the world-system rather than the nation the unit of analysis. Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* extends Marx into the African context. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* completes the historiographical task. Together they take forward the Marxist debate by showing that capitalism is constitutively a world-system, not a European phenomenon with global extensions.

In ecological theory, Western environmentalism (Carson, Schumacher, the Brundtland Report) had identified ecological limits to growth but tended to frame ecological politics in technocratic terms. Southern theory contributions — Vandana Shiva's eco-feminism, indigenous buen vivir constitutionalism in Ecuador and Bolivia, Gandhi on appropriate technology, the rights-of-nature movement — take ecological theory forward by linking it to cosmovisions of human-nature relations that Western theory had largely abandoned. The Standing Rock protests in the US, the indigenous-led opposition to Amazon deforestation, the climate-justice movement's centring of Global South vulnerability — these are political enactments of theoretical work done outside the metropole.

The argument that Southern theory takes Western theory forward, rather than replacing it, has both strategic and analytical merits. Strategically, it makes the case for Southern theory more easily heard in Northern institutions: Southern theory does not ask sociology to abandon its tradition but to extend it. Analytically, it captures the genuine relation: most Southern theorists were trained in Western institutions, read Marx and Weber and Foucault, and are engaged in serious dialogue with the canon. Spivak teaches at Columbia; Bhabha at Harvard; Mbembe and Mahmood Mamdani are deeply read in Hegel, Marx and Foucault. Southern theory is not an alien import; it is post-colonial extension of debates the Western tradition started but could not finish from within.

Caveats are needed. First, taking Western theory forward is not the only mode in which Southern theory operates; some Southern theory genuinely breaks with Western frameworks (indigenous epistemologies that refuse the subject-object distinction, for instance). Second, framing Southern theory as extension can risk subordinating it to the Western canon; Connell would reject this. Third, Western theory's own internal critics (Marxists, feminists, Foucauldians) had already opened many of the debates Southern theory deepens. A balanced view holds that Southern theory and Western theory are in genuine dialogue, that Southern theory is sometimes extension and sometimes rupture, and that the global academy benefits from taking both moves seriously. The Bandung Conference's intellectual descendants — from Frantz Fanon in Algeria to Walter Rodney in Tanzania to the contemporary Latin American decolonial school — are not visitors to Western theory; they are co-authors of contemporary social thought.

### Mock 1 — To what extent is Said's account of Orientalism still relevant in 2026?

To-what-extent. Said's *Orientalism* was published in 1978 and the question is whether the analysis still illuminates the world nearly half a century later. I will argue that it remains substantially relevant, with three qualifications.

The core argument is intact. Said's account of Orientalism holds that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89), and that European culture "gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). The basic structure — Western identity constituted through contrast with an Other coded as exotic, irrational, decadent and feminine — survives in contemporary discourse. The Western media's framing of the Iraq War (2003) as an attempt to bring democracy to people who could not produce it themselves was textbook Orientalism. The framing of Ukraine 2022 as "civilised" against "barbaric" Russians, with simultaneous neglect of equally civilised Afghan and Iraqi refugees, demonstrated that the binary structure is alive. Hindu-Muslim politics in Modi's India, Israeli-Palestinian framings in Western coverage, the persistent figure of the "veiled Muslim woman" in French laïcité debates — all reproduce the structure Said anatomised.

Concrete examples make the persistence visible. The Benin bronzes, looted in 1897, are still mostly in European museums; the British Museum continues to resist repatriation. The Quai Branly in Paris was constructed in 2006 to display African and Oceanic art in ways that scholars critiqued as neo-Orientalist. Hollywood's *Aladdin* remake reproduced Orientalist tropes about the "Arabian" world. Counter-terrorism scholarship and policy continue to deploy the trope of an Islamic world that does not produce modernity from within. Said's analysis still describes how a major fraction of Western intellectual and political production handles the Middle East and the Muslim world.

But three qualifications must be entered. First, the "Orient" Said wrote about was primarily the Arab and Islamic Middle East; he was less interested in East Asia or South Asia. The rise of China, in particular, has produced new forms of Western discourse that do not fit the Orientalist mould as Said described it. "Yellow peril" tropes have re-emerged but they are mixed with Western fascination with Chinese efficiency and admiration for Chinese economic success in ways the *Orientalism* framework cannot fully predict.

Second, the post-9/11 securitisation of Muslim populations has produced a more aggressive and more bureaucratic Orientalism than Said described. The imperial archive of nineteenth-century philology has been replaced by surveillance databases, no-fly lists and Prevent-style counter-extremism programmes. The discourse has hardened. Talal Asad and Mahmood Mamdani have extended Said's framework to capture this securitisation.

Third, there are now significant counter-currents. Arabic-language journalism, Al Jazeera, the rise of Arab and Muslim scholars in Western universities, the Pakistani novel in English (Kamila Shamsie, Mohsin Hamid), Egyptian film, Iranian cinema — these constitute Orient-speak from within, refusing the silence Said anatomised. Said's pessimism about Western Orientalist hegemony may need to be tempered by recognition of this Southern intellectual and cultural production. Mohanty's parallel critique of "Third World Woman" has been substantially answered by global feminist movements that refuse the figure.

To what extent, then, is Said relevant in 2026? Substantially but not completely. The discursive structure he diagnosed still organises mainstream Western coverage of the Muslim world, museum holdings, military intervention rhetoric, and cultural production. The Ukraine 2022 framing, vaccine apartheid during COVID, and the "civilised vs barbaric" sorting of refugees all reproduce his analysis. But the world he wrote about has changed: China is now a centre, not a periphery; Arab and Muslim voices speak back in global publishing; counter-terrorism has hardened the Orientalist machinery into surveillance bureaucracy. Said's framework needs supplementation — from Mohanty on gender, from Mamdani and Asad on securitisation, from Connell on Southern intellectual production — but it remains the indispensable starting point. The student of contemporary international relations who has not read *Orientalism* will miss the deep structure of much that they read in *The Economist* or *The New York Times*. The student who reads only Said, however, will miss the new forms the discourse has taken since 1978.

### Mock 2 — Single-author: Critically discuss Fanon's account of colonial subjectivity.

Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) is one of the founding texts of post-colonial theory, and his account of colonial subjectivity is its heart. I will outline the account in three movements — the colonial gaze, the doubling of subject and object, and the path to reclaimed voice — and then assess its strengths and limits.

The first movement is the colonial gaze. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity begins in a famous scene on a French train. A white child sees Fanon, a Black man, and cries: "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323). The cry escalates: "Look, a Negro!… Mama, see the Negro! I'm frightened!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323). Fanon tries to laugh: "I made up my mind to laugh myself to tears, but laughter had become impossible" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323). The gaze fixes him. He is no longer a fellow passenger, a doctor, a writer; he is the Negro. The white gaze has the power to define what Fanon is. The discourse of the West and the Rest, which Hall describes structurally, here lands on a particular body in a particular moment. The colonised subject is hailed into existence as object.

The second movement is the doubling. Under the colonial gaze, Fanon becomes both subject and object simultaneously. "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am *fixed*" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 325). He retains consciousness — he is still a subject thinking, feeling, suffering — but his consciousness is now the consciousness of an object. "My body was given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored…" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). He is asked to behave like a man but is permitted only to behave like a Black man: "The white world, the only honourable one, barred me from all participation. A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). The doubling produces a kind of internal exile. The colonised subject is alienated from his own body, which has been recoded by the gaze. This anticipates W. E. B. Du Bois's "double consciousness" — the sense of always seeing oneself through the eyes of others. Fanon's doubling extends Du Bois into the registers of psychoanalysis and existentialism (Fanon was reading Sartre).

The third movement is the path to voice. Fanon does not stop at description. The denial of subjectivity demands a struggle to reclaim it: "Since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). In his later work, especially *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), this making-known becomes revolutionary struggle. The colonised subject reclaims itself through political action, including violence, which Fanon analyses unflinchingly as a moment of self-recovery. Gayatri Spivak's question of subaltern speech is in dialogue with this: can the colonised speak in a way that does not just repeat the categories of the coloniser? Fanon's answer is that reclaimed voice is possible but only through struggle and only on the far side of the colonial wound.

Critically, the account has enormous strengths. It is one of the very few mid-twentieth-century theorisations of subjectivity that takes race as constitutive rather than incidental. It translates discursive analysis (Hall, Said) into embodied experience. It anticipates contemporary affect theory, psychoanalytic studies of race (Frank Wilderson, Sylvia Wynter), and Afro-pessimism. It has been read globally — by Black liberation movements in the US, by post-colonial activists in Algeria, by Aboriginal Australian writers, by Steve Biko in South Africa, by Edward Said himself. Its rhetorical force — the alternation between analysis and lived voice — is unique.

The limits are also real. First, Fanon's account is gendered. The colonised subject of *Black Skin, White Masks* is masculine; the colonial wound is described primarily from a male position. Black feminist critics (bell hooks, Hortense Spillers) have shown that the colonial gaze does different work on Black women and that Fanon's framework needs to be extended. Second, his account is sometimes essentialist, treating the white gaze and the Black colonised subject as fixed positions. Bhabha's reading of Fanon recovers a more dynamic, hybridised Fanon, but the original text can be read in a static mode. Third, his commitment to revolutionary violence in *The Wretched of the Earth* is controversial. Hannah Arendt argued that violence consumes politics rather than founding it. Even sympathetic readers acknowledge that the Algerian War, which Fanon supported, produced post-independence regimes that were authoritarian and corrupt. Fourth, the psychoanalytic frame Fanon uses is itself European; the question of whether to use Freud and Lacan to theorise colonial subjectivity is contested by indigenous theorists who want their own conceptual vocabularies.

A balanced assessment: Fanon's account of colonial subjectivity remains one of the indispensable theorisations of how power lands on bodies. It connects Hall's discursive analysis to lived experience, it anticipates contemporary critical race theory, and it has been politically generative for liberation movements globally. But it needs to be read with gender, hybridity, post-violence politics, and indigenous epistemologies as supplementary considerations. The contemporary student reads Fanon not as a finished theory but as a launching point — a precise diagnosis of one face of colonial subjectivity that needs to be extended, complicated and dialogically engaged.

### Mock 3 — Comparison: Compare Said and Hall on how the West constructs its Other.

Edward Said and Stuart Hall are the two great anatomists of how the West constructs its Other. Their projects are deeply related but differ in scope, archive and method. I will compare them along four axes — geography of focus, theoretical apparatus, archive of analysis, and political conclusion — and assess what is gained from reading them together.

Geographically, Said's *Orientalism* focuses tightly on the Arab and Islamic Middle East. His Orient is Beirut, Cairo, Baghdad, Jerusalem, Tehran. His theorists are nineteenth-century French and British Orientalists — Sacy, Renan, Lane. His novelists are Flaubert, Nerval, Conrad. Hall's *Formations of Modernity* is broader. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest treats the Rest as a generic category — sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas, Asia, the Pacific — and follows European contact with the New World from 1492. Said is a microscope on the Orient; Hall is a panorama of the Rest.

Theoretically, both deploy Foucault. Said adopts Foucault's notion of discourse explicitly: "I have found it useful here to employ Michel Foucault's notion of a discourse" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 25 in Ashcroft). Orientalism is "the corporate institution for dealing with the Orient — dealing with it by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 25 in Ashcroft). Hall draws on Foucault too but also on Saussure's semiotics — meaning operates through difference, so the West/Rest binary is structurally akin to night/day. Hall's analysis is more semiotically grounded; Said's is more historically textured.

In archive, Said works textually. He reads the Orientalist library — Lane's *Modern Egyptians*, Renan's racial philology, Burton's travel writing, Flaubert's *Voyage en Egypte*. Hall is more sociological, drawing on historiography of European expansion (Roberts, Mann), early-modern travel literature, and broader processes of European state formation. Said's evidence is texts; Hall's evidence is structures of representation across a wider cultural-political field. Said is closer to literary criticism; Hall is closer to cultural studies of the long durée.

Politically, both arrive at structurally similar conclusions but with different inflections. Said: "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89). Hall: the discourse of the West and the Rest "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 280). Both insist on the constructed character of the binary and on its destructive simplification. But Said is more focused on the will to dominate ("a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient"), while Hall is more focused on the ideological function of the discourse as ranking system ("it functions as an ideology").

Reading them together yields several gains. First, Hall provides the structural grammar; Said provides the textual specificity. The student who reads only Hall has an apparatus but few concrete examples; the student who reads only Said has rich textual analysis but a narrower geographical scope. Together they give us both the diagram and the artefacts. Second, Hall historicises Said. Said's Orientalism rises in the late eighteenth century with the imperial European expansion; Hall traces the longer history back to 1492, situating Orientalism within a broader discourse of the West and the Rest that predates and exceeds it. Third, Hall's attention to internal heterogeneity — "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, *Formations of Modernity*, p. 279) — supplements Said's sometimes-monolithic treatment of "European culture." Said himself acknowledged this complication in his later work (*Culture and Imperialism*, 1993).

There are also tensions. Hall's structuralist semiotics treats the West/Rest binary as functioning like language — a system of differences. Said's Foucauldian historicism treats Orientalism as a contingent institution with a datable rise. The two analyses can both be right but they answer different questions: Hall asks how the binary works as meaning; Said asks how it was produced and reproduced over two centuries.

Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" extends both. Mohanty agrees with Hall and Said about the homogenising operation but inflects it along the gender axis. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity tells us what the operation does to a particular Black body. Dussel's trans-modernity philosophises the binary into a question about the future of modernity itself.

Concrete examples illuminate the convergence. The British Museum's Benin bronzes are at once a Saidian object (a particular looted artefact) and a Hallian one (an instance of how the West collects the Rest). The Ukraine 2022 "civilised vs barbaric" framing is Said and Hall simultaneously. The decolonise-the-curriculum movement targets both the Saidian textual canon and the Hallian structural curriculum.

In conclusion, Said and Hall offer complementary, not competing, anatomies of how the West constructs its Other. Said is the philologist of Orientalism; Hall is the cartographer of the West and the Rest. Read together, they give the student a more complete map of the discursive operation. The criticism of each from the other's vantage point sharpens both: Hall can be too structural without Said's textual richness; Said can be too monolithic without Hall's internal heterogeneity. The contemporary post-colonial reader holds them in productive tension.

### Mock 4 — Discuss with examples: How do contemporary museum repatriation debates exemplify post-colonial critique?

Museum repatriation debates — the Benin bronzes, the Parthenon marbles, the Maqdala treasures, the Quai Branly's collection, the Berlin Humboldt Forum's holdings — have become flashpoints for post-colonial critique. I will discuss four ways in which they exemplify the critique: as instances of the imperial archive, as discursive operations of the West and the Rest, as contemporary expressions of the colonial wound, and as occasions for trans-modern repair.

The first dimension is the imperial archive. Said's account of Orientalism identifies the imperial archive — the museum, the gazetteer, the colonial monograph — as part of how Western knowledge produced its Other. Museum collections are the imperial archive made physical. The British Museum's Benin bronzes, looted in the 1897 British punitive expedition against Benin City, are exemplary. They are now displayed in London, organised by Western curators, captioned in English, and made available primarily to European and tourist publics. The Beninese people from whose cultural heritage they were taken have been functionally excluded from custody, interpretation and access for over a century. This is exactly Said's claim about the Orient being "in a sense, on display in the museum" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 26 in Ashcroft). The Maqdala treasures (taken from Ethiopia in 1868), the Asante gold regalia (taken from Ghana in 1874), the Aboriginal Australian remains held in European institutions — each is a node in the imperial archive.

The second dimension is the West/Rest discursive operation. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest classifies, represents, compares and evaluates. Museums are extraordinarily efficient at performing all four operations. They classify objects as "European" (in the National Gallery) or "ethnographic" (in the British Museum); they represent civilisations through composite images; they invite visitors to compare "us" with "them"; they evaluate (the Greek marbles are "high culture," the African masks are "primitive art"). The very architecture of the nineteenth-century museum, with its evolutionary arrangement of cases from "primitive" to "advanced," materialised the hierarchies Hall describes. Even contemporary museums that have moved beyond this overt evolutionism often retain the underlying classification — European masters in the painting galleries, African and Pacific objects in the ethnographic galleries.

The third dimension is the colonial wound. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity makes the colonial relation embodied. Repatriation debates make the embodiment visible. When Nigerian curators describe the Benin bronzes' continued residence in London as an ongoing wound, when Ethiopian campaigners speak of the Maqdala treasures as an injury sustained by Ethiopian identity, when Greek officials describe the Parthenon marbles as separated from the body of Greek civilisation — they are speaking the language of the colonial wound. The objects are not just objects; they are pieces of a body that has been dissected. The bronzes were sacred objects in Benin ritual life; their removal was not the gain of an art collection but the dismemberment of a culture.

The fourth dimension is the question of trans-modern repair. Dussel's trans-modernity asks what a post-colonial modernity that takes responsibility for its sacrificial cost would look like. Repatriation is a concrete answer in a particular domain. The German government's 2022 agreement to return the Benin bronzes; the Quai Branly's partial returns to Benin and Senegal; the discussions over the Hoa Hakananai'a moai with Rapa Nui — these are early experiments in what Dussel calls "incorporative solidarity" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 76). Repatriation is not just transfer of objects; it is acknowledgement that the imperial archive was a wrong that requires repair. Crucially, repatriation also raises questions about who can speak on behalf of the originating community — Spivak's question of subaltern speech, applied to cultural heritage. Who in Benin speaks for the bronzes? The Oba? The Nigerian federal state? Beninese diaspora? Local communities?

Counter-arguments are heard. The British Museum's traditional defence is that universal museums benefit all humanity by gathering world cultures in one accessible location; that repatriated objects might be at risk in their countries of origin; that long custody has created its own kind of legitimacy. From a post-colonial perspective, each of these defences reads as a continuation of the original discursive operation. "Universal" turns out to be Western universal; "at risk" turns out to be a paternalist judgement; "long custody" turns out to be the legitimation of theft by duration.

The political stakes are high. Rhodes Must Fall at Cape Town and Oxford targeted statues as artefacts; the decolonise-the-curriculum movement targets the syllabus as artefact; repatriation targets the museum collection as artefact. Each is a refusal of the imperial archive. Each draws on the same theoretical resources — Said on the museum and the discourse, Hall on the West/Rest structure, Fanon on embodied injury, Spivak on voice, Dussel on trans-modern repair.

In conclusion, museum repatriation debates exemplify post-colonial critique in its most public, contested and tangible form. They show Said's, Hall's, Fanon's, Spivak's and Dussel's frameworks operating simultaneously on the same set of objects. They demonstrate that the colonial wound is not a metaphor but a continuing material condition. They show, finally, that post-colonial critique is not just an academic exercise but a programme of political and cultural redress with concrete consequences — for museums, governments, communities and the future shape of global cultural heritage.

### Mock 5 — To what extent does Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" still hold?

Chandra Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* (1988) argued that "the notion of 'Third world woman' is an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261), produced in Western and some Western-feminist approaches to the world, rather than describing any real empirical group. The question is whether the critique still holds nearly four decades on. I will argue that it substantially does, with three significant modifications.

The core claim is durable. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" identified the figure of a homogeneous "average third world woman" leading "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized etc.)" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261). This figure, Mohanty showed, was the implicit counterpart to a Western feminist self-representation as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 261). The figure persists. White-saviour NGO campaigns — Kony 2012, certain Save the Children advertising, much voluntourism — still mobilise the figure of the helpless brown woman. Microfinance campaigns by the Grameen Bank and its imitators sometimes deploy the figure to justify lending. The 2010s discourse around female genital cutting often performed the figure. Mohanty's diagnosis still describes a major fraction of Western humanitarian, development and feminist discourse.

The methodological injunction is also durable. Mohanty's claim that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, *Under Western Eyes*, p. 262) is now mainstream in feminist research methods. Intersectionality (Crenshaw), feminist epistemology of standpoint (Harding), and global feminist research practice all embed the requirement that gender be analysed within local political, economic and cultural specificity. The Mohanty injunction is one of the operating principles of contemporary feminist scholarship.

Three modifications need to be entered. First, the geography has shifted. Mohanty wrote in a moment when "Third World" still functioned as a coherent category — the non-aligned post-colonial periphery. By 2026, the term has been substantially displaced by "Global South" or "Majority World," and the internal stratifications of the Global South have become much more visible. The Bangladeshi garment worker, the Indian software engineer, the Saudi princess and the Bolivian peasant are not in the same situation. Mohanty's frame applied to "Third World Woman" needs more disaggregation than the 1988 essay allowed. Mohanty herself has updated the framework in subsequent work, particularly *Feminism Without Borders* (2003).

Second, Southern feminist voices have multiplied. In 1988, the figure of the Third World Woman was largely spoken about; by 2026 she speaks back in many languages and through many platforms. Black African feminist scholarship (Oyèrónk Oyewúmí, Pumla Dineo Gqola), South Asian feminism (Nivedita Menon, Kavita Krishnan, Uma Chakravarti), Latin American feminism (Maria Lugones, Veronica Gago, Rita Segato), Arab feminism (Lila Abu-Lughod, Saba Mahmood) — these are theoretical and political forces that the 1988 essay could not have predicted. The Western feminist monopoly on theorising the Third World Woman has been substantially broken. Spivak's question of subaltern speech finds at least partial answers in this proliferation of Southern feminist theorists.

Third, the discourse has become more contested but also more sophisticated. White-saviour NGO campaigns now provoke immediate critique on social media; the "white saviour industrial complex" essay by Teju Cole became a touchstone; the figure of the rescued Muslim woman in the early 2000s justified war (Afghanistan, Iraq) but also produced a robust counter-discourse from Muslim feminist scholars who refused both the figure of victim and the figure of rescuer. The discourse Mohanty diagnosed has hardened in places (security-state framings of Muslim women) and softened in others (mainstream global feminism's growing reflexivity).

Concrete contemporary examples sharpen the assessment. The 2014 Boko Haram kidnappings produced the #BringBackOurGirls campaign, which Mohanty-influenced scholars critiqued for reproducing the helpless African girl trope even as it sought to advocate. The 2021 Taliban return in Afghanistan produced a Western discourse about "saving Afghan women" that Afghan feminists themselves criticised for ignoring how twenty years of NATO presence had produced ambiguous gains. Indian feminist responses to the 2012 Delhi rape case ran into Western framings of "Indian culture" as uniquely patriarchal, which Indian feminists rejected as Orientalist. The #MeToo movement has been received and reshaped differently across the Global South in ways that complicate any singular global feminist narrative.

To what extent, then, does Mohanty's critique hold? Substantially. The figure of the homogenised Third World Woman still operates in Western humanitarian, development and security discourses. The methodological injunction to specificity is canonical in contemporary feminist research. But the world has moved: Southern feminist voices are louder, the Third World category has been displaced by more granular maps, and the discourse Mohanty diagnosed has become more contested. The 2026 reader takes Mohanty's critique as essential starting equipment but needs to update its application — extending it through Crenshaw's intersectionality, through Spivak's subaltern speech, through indigenous feminisms, and through the new Southern feminist theorists who write back. The essay of 1988 is not the last word; it is the indispensable first word of a global feminist conversation that is still developing.

### Mock 6 — Single-author: Critically assess Dussel's claim that 1492 is the birth-date of modernity.

Enrique Dussel's claim that "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66) is one of the most provocative interventions in post-colonial theory. I will outline the claim, situate it against rival periodisations, and assess its strengths and weaknesses.

The claim is part of Dussel's broader argument that modernity is constituted dialectically by Europe and its non-European Other. "Modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 65). The conquest of the Americas, beginning with Columbus's voyage of 1492 and the Spanish conquest of Mexico under Cortés in 1519-21, is the moment at which Europe acquired an outside it could constitute as inferior. Before 1492, Dussel argues, Europe was peripheral to a more powerful Islamic world. After 1492, Europe positioned itself as centre of a world history of which it imagined itself the apex.

This challenges the standard European periodisation. Habermas, following Hegel, identifies the constitutive events of modernity as "the Reformation, the Enlightenment, and the French Revolution" (cited in Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 74). Weber locates modernity in the Protestant work ethic of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Marx locates it in the industrial revolution of the late eighteenth. Anthony Giddens locates it in the post-1700 European institutional complex of capitalism, industrialism, surveillance and military power. All of these periodisations have one thing in common: they treat modernity as a development internal to Europe. Dussel's 1492 thesis disrupts this internalism. Modernity, on his view, is constitutively a relation with the Americas, with Africa, with the colonised Other.

The strengths of the 1492 thesis are several. First, it accounts for the immense flows of silver from Potosí and gold from Africa that funded European state-building and the rise of capitalism — flows that the standard periodisation tends to treat as background rather than constitutive. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* makes this connection vividly: "the Portuguese use cotton from India to buy gold in Africa to use as coinage in Europe" (lecture slide 4). The European wealth that funded the Reformation and the Enlightenment was already, by Dussel's argument, partly colonial wealth. Second, it accounts for Spain and Portugal. Hegel and Habermas effectively write Iberia out of modernity's history; Spain becomes, in Hegel's words, a region whose destiny "is decided elsewhere" (Hegel, cited in Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 74). Dussel restores Iberia as the pioneer of the modernity that Northern Europe would later inherit. Third, it accounts for the genocidal dimension of modernity. The conquest of the Americas killed perhaps 90% of the indigenous population through war, disease and exploitation. If modernity began with the Reformation, this mass death is incidental; if modernity began in 1492, the genocide is constitutive. Dussel's seven-point summary of "the myth of modernity" — including that "the praxis of modernity must, in the last instance, have recourse to the violence necessary to remove the obstacles to modernization" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 75) — depends on this constitutive linkage. Fourth, it explains the structural inequality of the contemporary global system better than the internalist accounts can: if modernity was always centre-and-periphery, then the persistence of the centre-periphery relation in 2026 is not an aberration but the original logic.

The weaknesses are also real. First, "birth" is too singular a metaphor for a process. The world after 1492 was not yet the world of industrial capitalism, parliamentary democracy, mass print, mass science. Many features of "modernity" as conventionally identified emerged centuries later. Dussel can respond that 1492 is the moment when the basic structure (centre-periphery, colonial extraction, sacrificial myth) was put in place, even if the institutional contents developed later. Second, the 1492 thesis privileges the Americas over other colonised regions. African slavery in the trans-Atlantic system was central, but it was already centuries old in 1492. Asian trade networks predated European involvement by millennia. The 1492 framing risks making the Americas the only birthplace, when Africa and Asia were also constitutive sites. Third, Dussel's framing is influenced by his location in Latin American liberation philosophy and may underweight the specificity of African, Indian and East Asian experiences of modernity. Fourth, the "myth of modernity" with its seven points can be read as too neat — a stylised diagram rather than a finely textured history. Fifth, Marxist critics might argue that capitalism's logic is older or younger than 1492 depending on how one defines it, and that the periodisation by colonial event obscures the deeper logic of capital.

Examples can ground the assessment. The silver of Potosí (mined from 1545) funded Hapsburg Spain, the Thirty Years War, and indirectly the rise of the Dutch and English maritime empires; this is Dussel's case. The Iberian Reconquest's model — broken treaties, mass conversion under threat, confiscation of land, repartition to officers — became the template for the conquest of Mexico under Cortés; this is also Dussel's case. The British East India Company's plunder of Bengal in the 1750s and 60s, however, did not need the 1492 framework to be analysed; older logics of mercantile capitalism and state violence already operated. The trans-Atlantic slave trade's origins in fifteenth-century Portugal predate 1492; the African dimension of modernity does not start with Columbus. So the 1492 thesis is partly right and partly an Iberian-centred bias.

A balanced conclusion: Dussel's 1492 thesis is a powerful and necessary corrective to Eurocentric internalist periodisations of modernity. It restores Iberia, the Americas and the colonial relation to the constitutive history of modernity. It refuses the moral comfort of separating modernity's emancipatory concept from its sacrificial myth. But the thesis is best held alongside other periodisations rather than as a sole replacement: the slave trade, the Mughal-British encounter, the Opium Wars, the Berlin Conference of 1884-85 are all also constitutive moments. Modernity has many birthdays, not one. Dussel gives us the indispensable Latin American date; we need also to honour the African dates, the Asian dates and the indigenous dates, before we can say that modernity has finally accounted for all its origins.

### Mock 7 — Discuss with examples: How is post-colonial theory relevant to contemporary international relations?

Post-colonial theory was developed primarily in literary, cultural and sociological registers, but it has become indispensable for understanding contemporary international relations. I will discuss four ways in which it illuminates current world politics — the persistence of the West/Rest binary in diplomatic discourse, the colonial wound in trade and finance, the rise of new sub-imperialisms, and the politics of voice in international institutions — with examples throughout.

The first relevance is the persistence of the West/Rest binary. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest still organises Western diplomatic and media language. The Ukraine 2022 framing of "civilised European" Ukrainians against "barbaric" Russian invaders, while simultaneously coding Afghan and Iraqi refugees as less worthy of sympathy, was Hall's binary in real time. The persistent Western framing of China as "alien" and "authoritarian" — even as Western states tolerate authoritarian allies in the Gulf — reproduces the same operation. Said's account of Orientalism applies sharply to Western diplomatic discourse on the Middle East: the assumption that the region cannot produce stable democracy from within, that intervention is therefore civilising, that local actors lack rationality. The 2003 invasion of Iraq was justified through textbook Orientalist tropes; the post-2014 framing of Iran's nuclear programme deploys the same. Mohanty's parallel critique applies to the figure of the "veiled Muslim woman" deployed to justify intervention in Afghanistan and to legitimate French laïcité.

The second relevance is the colonial wound in trade and finance. The contemporary international financial system is the institutional descendant of colonial relations. Bretton Woods (1944) was designed by the Allies in their colonial moment; the IMF and World Bank have repeatedly imposed structural adjustment on African and Latin American economies in ways that critics from dependency theory onwards have analysed as neo-colonial. The TRIPS regime in the WTO globalises Western intellectual property norms, with consequences vivid during COVID: vaccine apartheid showed the global distribution of life chances mapping onto the colonial difference. Wealthy states hoarded mRNA vaccine doses while blocking the TRIPS waiver that would have allowed Indian and South African manufacturers to produce generics. Dussel's "myth of modernity" — the assumption that Northern technologies and norms must be unilaterally followed — operates fully in this regime. The African Union's recent calls for reform of the global financial architecture, the BRICS Bank as alternative to the World Bank, and the proposed African Continental Free Trade Area are post-colonial counter-projects.

The third relevance is the rise of new sub-imperialisms. The Belt and Road Initiative makes China a major creditor across Africa, Central Asia and parts of Europe; debt-trap diplomacy critiques echo the colonial logic but with Beijing as the centre. Indian capital in Bangladesh and Nepal, Brazilian agribusiness in Paraguay, South African retail in southern Africa — these are intra-Southern extractive patterns that complicate the simple North-South binary of classical dependency theory. Connell's Southern theory needs updating to address them: the South contains its own centres and peripheries. Hindu nationalism under Modi pursues a regional Hindu hegemony that wears the language of decolonisation while practising colonial-style exclusion of Muslims and ethnic minorities. Post-colonial theory provides the diagnostic vocabulary for these new patterns even when the patterns are produced by formerly colonised actors.

The fourth relevance is the politics of voice in international institutions. Spivak's question of subaltern speech illuminates the structure of the United Nations Security Council, where the five permanent members (US, UK, France, Russia, China) are former colonial or current major powers and African and Latin American states have only rotating non-permanent seats. The voice of the Global South in international decision-making is structurally constrained. Calls for UN reform — African demands for permanent Security Council seats, Brazilian and Indian aspirations — are post-colonial demands for representation. The 2024-25 debates over Israel-Palestine and the International Criminal Court have shown the gap between Northern and Southern perceptions of international law's legitimacy. Northern selective application of international law to Russia (Ukraine) but not to Israel (Gaza) has been read across the Global South as confirming what Hall's discourse already predicted: the West treats "us" and "them" by different rules.

Concrete examples beyond those already mentioned: the Bandung Conference of 1955 as the canonical post-colonial international moment; the New International Economic Order proposed by the G77 in 1974 as a post-colonial economic project; the Rhodes Must Fall movement crossing from Cape Town to Oxford as global decolonial circulation; vaccine apartheid as Dussel's myth of modernity in COVID form; the 2022 Western media binaries on Ukraine refugees as Hall's discourse in operation; the Belt and Road as new centre-periphery; the African Union's December 2024 calls for global financial reform.

Critically, post-colonial theory needs supplementation for IR. It tends to neglect quantitative analysis of trade and finance, which is essential for understanding the global economy. It tends to be culturalist where political economy is needed. It tends to under-theorise the state, which is the unit of analysis in classical IR. Constructivist IR theorists (Onuf, Wendt) have begun integrating post-colonial insights without losing analytical rigour; English School theorists (Linklater, Hurrell) have engaged with post-colonial critiques of international society; critical IR scholars (Robert Cox, Robbie Shilliam) have produced a post-colonial IR canon. The student of IR in 2026 cannot understand Ukraine, Gaza, Taiwan, Sahel coups, BRICS expansion or the climate negotiations without post-colonial theoretical resources.

In conclusion, post-colonial theory is not just relevant to contemporary international relations; it is indispensable. The structures it diagnoses — the West/Rest binary, the colonial wound, the imperial archive, the politics of voice — organise contemporary world politics in visible and consequential ways. The IR student who reads only Morgenthau and Waltz will miss most of what is actually happening. The student who reads Said, Hall, Fanon, Spivak, Connell, Dussel and Mohanty alongside the IR canon will see the contemporary world more clearly.

### Mock 8 — To what extent is the concept of hybridity (Bhabha) a useful supplement to Said and Hall?

Homi Bhabha's concept of hybridity, developed in *The Location of Culture* (1994), proposes that colonial and post-colonial cultures are not pure either/or but messy in-between. The question is whether hybridity supplements Said and Hall productively, or whether it dilutes the political force of their critique. I will argue that hybridity is a substantial but limited supplement.

The case for hybridity as supplement rests on three claims. First, both Said and Hall sometimes risk monolithic treatment of "the West" and "the Rest." Said's *Orientalism* can be read as treating European culture as a unified Orientalising machine; Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest treats the two terms as if they were stable poles. Bhabha's hybridity insists that the colonial encounter produces mixed forms: the colonial subject who speaks the master's language with subversive accent; the colonial administrator who adopts local dress and food; the post-colonial city that is neither Indian nor British but something new. Bhabha's "third space" — the in-between zone where new identities are negotiated — captures phenomena Said and Hall sometimes miss.

Second, hybridity better captures contemporary diasporic and globalised experience. The British-Pakistani novelist writing in English, the Nigerian-American afrobeats artist, the Indian engineer in Silicon Valley, the Chinese-Caribbean filmmaker — these are not products of either pure "Western" or pure "Eastern" culture but of hybrid formation. Salman Rushdie's *Midnight's Children* (1981), Hanif Kureishi's *The Buddha of Suburbia* (1990), Zadie Smith's *White Teeth* (2000), Kamila Shamsie's *Burnt Shadows* (2009), and contemporary writers like Akwaeke Emezi articulate hybrid identities that Said's framework, focused on the asymmetric domination of Orient by Occident, was not designed to analyse.

Third, hybridity opens space for agency. Said's account of Orientalism can read deterministically, as if the colonised subject can only be produced by the discourse. Bhabha's mimicry — the colonial subject who imitates the coloniser but with a difference that destabilises the original — recovers agency from inside the colonial relation. The Indian clerk in the British colonial bureaucracy who internalises British forms but uses them to argue for independence; the African nationalist who deploys European political theory against European empire; the post-colonial state that adopts UN sovereignty norms to resist intervention — each is a mimic in Bhabha's sense, and each exhibits the agency that pure discursive determination cannot accommodate. Fanon, in *Black Skin, White Masks*, also gestured toward this — "to make myself known" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324) — but Bhabha theorises it more systematically.

But the case against hybridity as adequate supplement also has force. First, hybridity can depoliticise. If everyone is hybrid, then there are no clear positions of oppression and no clear stakes for political action. The Bangladeshi garment worker is hybrid in some sense (she wears Western-style jeans under her sari and uses an Android phone) but her hybridity is structured by a global capitalist hierarchy that hybridity-talk can obscure. Marxist critics (Aijaz Ahmad) have argued that Bhabha's hybridity is a culturalist celebration of cosmopolitan elites that ignores the harsh asymmetries Said and Hall correctly emphasise.

Second, hybridity can flatten power. Said's insistence that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, *Orientalism*, p. 89) is the political core of his analysis. Hybridity, by emphasising mixture, can blunt the force of domination. The Indian writer in English is hybrid, yes, but the global English-language publishing industry is still centred in London and New York, and prestige flows in one direction.

Third, hybridity can romanticise pain. Diasporic in-betweenness is sometimes painful — the second-generation migrant who feels neither at home nor abroad, the post-colonial intellectual who critiques the West from within Western universities. Bhabha sometimes celebrates this in-betweenness as creative; for many of those who live it, it is also distressing.

Fourth, hybridity is not equally available to all. The Pakistani diaspora author in London is hybrid in a way that the Pakistani peasant in rural Sindh is not. The post-colonial elite is more hybrid (in Bhabha's celebratory sense) than the post-colonial poor. Spivak's question of subaltern speech reminds us that hybridity-talk can serve as the metropole's way of attending to those who are most like itself and ignoring those who are not.

The synthesis I want to defend: hybridity supplements Said and Hall productively in three areas — agency, contemporary diasporic experience, and internal heterogeneity of "West" and "Rest" — but it does not replace them. The fundamental Saidian and Hallian insight that the West constructs itself as Other against the Rest, that this construction is asymmetrical, and that it organises material and discursive power, remains the essential framework. Hybridity makes the picture more complex; it does not invalidate the picture. The contemporary post-colonial reader benefits from holding Said's structural diagnosis, Hall's discursive grammar, Fanon's embodied testimony, Mohanty's gender inflection, Spivak's subaltern question and Bhabha's hybridity-and-mimicry together, applying each to the phenomena it illuminates best.

Concrete examples make the synthesis live. The British Museum's Benin bronzes are a Saidian object (imperial archive) but their digital reproduction on contemporary platforms is hybrid (Beninese curators reach global audiences through Western technologies). The Ukraine 2022 media coverage was a Hallian binary (civilised vs barbaric) but the Ukrainian refugee in Berlin who speaks Ukrainian, German and English and uses TikTok is a hybrid subject. Rhodes Must Fall is a Fanonian struggle for recognition, but the student leaders' use of Twitter to globalise the campaign is Bhabhaian mimicry of metropolitan modes turned against the metropole. Each theory illuminates a face of the phenomenon. Hybridity is a real supplement to Said and Hall, valuable in particular registers, but not a replacement for the structural analysis they pioneered.

### Mock 9 — Comparison: Compare Fanon and Spivak on the voice of the colonised.

Frantz Fanon and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak are the two great twentieth-century theorists of colonial and post-colonial voice. Their projects are intimately connected — Spivak read Fanon closely and engages him directly — but differ in moment, method and conclusion. I will compare them on four axes: starting point, theoretical apparatus, conclusion about voice, and political implication.

The starting point differs significantly. Fanon writes in the late 1940s and early 1950s as a Black colonised man encountering French racism in Paris and Lyon. His starting scene is the white child's hail: "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 323). Fanon's question begins from his own embodied experience of being fixed by the colonial gaze. Spivak writes in the late 1980s from the position of a postcolonial intellectual in the American academy reading subaltern studies, French theory and Indian historiography. Her starting question, "Can the subaltern speak?", is a methodological question about historiography — specifically about whether the figure of Bhubaneswari Bhaduri, who committed suicide in 1926 while menstruating to prevent her death being read as illicit-romantic rather than political, can be retrieved as a speaking subject by later scholarship. Fanon begins from his own body; Spivak begins from another's silence.

Theoretically, Fanon's apparatus is existentialist-psychoanalytic. He reads Sartre, Hegel, Freud and Lacan; his account of colonial subjectivity uses categories of the gaze, recognition, alienation, and the desire to be recognised. The colonial relation is staged as a failed dialectic of recognition: the white master refuses recognition to the Black slave, leaving the slave fixed as object. Spivak's apparatus is deconstructive-feminist. She reads Derrida, Foucault, Deleuze and Marx; her account of the subaltern uses categories of representation (vertretung as proxy and darstellung as portrait), epistemic violence, strategic essentialism, and the limits of what discourse can recover. Where Fanon is psychic and embodied, Spivak is discursive and epistemological.

The conclusion about voice differs most sharply. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity ends not in silence but in struggle. "Since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks*, p. 324). In *The Wretched of the Earth*, this making-known becomes revolutionary action: anti-colonial struggle, sometimes violent, that produces a new colonised subject in the act of struggle itself. Fanon's answer to the question "can the colonised speak?" is yes, through struggle and only through struggle. Spivak's answer is famously more pessimistic. The subaltern, in her precise sense — the most structurally voiceless, particularly the subaltern woman who is silenced both by colonial and patriarchal hierarchies — cannot speak in a way that is heard on the terms of the speaker. Even sympathetic Western recovery (subaltern studies, postcolonial feminism) risks ventriloquising. Bhubaneswari Bhaduri's careful staging of her death as not-romantic was, by Spivak's reading, misread; even her body's signal of menstruation was overwritten by the family narrative. Spivak's bleaker conclusion is that the subaltern cannot speak, not because the subaltern has nothing to say, but because the available discursive frames cannot register what she says.

Politically, the implications diverge. Fanon's framework licences revolutionary politics. The Algerian War of Independence, which Fanon supported and theorised, becomes the model: violence as decolonising act, struggle as the production of post-colonial subjectivity. Spivak's framework licences a more cautious intellectual practice: persistent critique, strategic use of essentialism for political purposes while remaining aware of its limits, the slow patient work of dismantling epistemic violence. Fanon goes to the maquis; Spivak goes to the seminar room.

Yet they are deeply connected. Spivak explicitly draws on Fanon, particularly his analysis of the woman in Algerian struggle and his account of the colonised subject as fixed by the gaze. Both diagnose the discursive structures that produce colonial silencing. Both insist that the question of voice is political, not merely literary. Both refuse the easy assumption that "we" (the metropolitan readership) can simply recover "their" voice through sympathetic listening. Both connect voice to power.

The complementarity is also instructive. Fanon helps us understand what happens to bodies under colonial discourse; Spivak helps us understand what happens to archives. Fanon helps us understand revolutionary moments of voice; Spivak helps us understand the persistent moments where voice does not arrive. Fanon is necessary when colonialism is acute and present (Algeria 1954-62, Mozambique 1960s-70s, Palestine ongoing); Spivak is necessary when colonialism has become subtler, when the post-colonial state has emerged but the subaltern within it is still silenced (the Adivasi displaced by a dam, the Dalit woman raped in a North Indian village, the indigenous community whose territory is taken for lithium).

Critically, both have faced challenges. Fanon's account is gendered male; the colonial subject in *Black Skin, White Masks* is a man, and Black feminist critics have shown that the colonial gaze does different work on Black women. Fanon's later work tried to address this but unevenly. Spivak's pessimism about voice has been criticised as paralysing political action. Bhabha and others have argued for hybridity and mimicry as resources for voice that Spivak underweights. Indian feminists like Nivedita Menon have shown subaltern women speaking in collective political movements (the Self-Employed Women's Association, for instance) in ways Spivak's framework did not anticipate.

A synthesised reading uses both. The contemporary post-colonial reader takes Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity as the embodied register: this is what colonialism does to a body under the gaze. The reader takes Spivak's question of subaltern speech as the epistemic register: this is what colonialism does to archives and discourses. Where the two converge — for example, on the colonised woman whose body is gazed at and whose voice is unarchived — they together produce a powerful theorisation that neither alone could achieve. The Indian feminist movement, the Black Lives Matter movement, the indigenous rights movements of the Americas all need both Fanon's revolutionary insistence on voice through struggle and Spivak's patient, suspicious epistemology of representation. They are not rival frameworks; they are necessary co-readings of the post-colonial condition.

### Mock 10 — Discuss with examples: How does post-colonial theory reframe the history of capitalism?

Standard Western histories of capitalism — from Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) through Marx's *Capital* (1867) to contemporary economic history — tend to centre Europe. The rise of capitalism is told as a story of European institutional innovation: enclosures in England, mercantile finance in Holland, machinery in Manchester, joint-stock companies in London. Post-colonial theory reframes this history by insisting on the colonial constitution of capitalism. I will discuss four aspects of the reframing — primitive accumulation, the slave trade, the colonial market, and the racial structure of labour — with examples throughout.

The first aspect is primitive accumulation. Marx himself had identified "primitive accumulation" as the initial stock of capital that made capitalist production possible, and he had named colonial plunder, the Atlantic slave trade and the conquest of the Americas as crucial sources. But the European Marxist tradition that inherited *Capital* tended to treat these as historical preconditions rather than constitutive structures, and to focus on factory production in Manchester and Birmingham. Post-colonial theory restores primitive accumulation to centrality. Eric Williams's *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) argued that the British abolition of slavery in 1833 came only after Caribbean sugar had funded the industrial revolution. C. L. R. James's *The Black Jacobins* (1938) placed the Haitian Revolution at the heart of the modern political imagination. Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) traced how five centuries of European trade with Africa systematically extracted wealth and capacity from the continent. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* (2021) makes Africa central to the making of modern capitalism: African gold mined and traded by African polities became European coinage; African labour, free and enslaved, produced the commodities (sugar, cotton, coffee, tobacco) that built European consumer demand. Dussel's argument that "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity" (Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, p. 66) applies particularly to capitalism: Iberian silver from Potosí flooded into European money supply from 1545, transforming the conditions of European trade.

The second aspect is the slave trade. The trans-Atlantic slave trade is no longer treatable as background. Estimates vary but at least twelve million Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas between 1500 and 1870; perhaps two million died in the Middle Passage. The plantations they worked produced the sugar, cotton, tobacco and coffee that built European industrial demand. The cotton mills of Manchester ran on slave-grown cotton until the US Civil War. The triangular trade — European manufactures to Africa, African slaves to the Americas, American commodities to Europe — was an integrated capitalist system that no honest history can sideline. Eric Williams, C. L. R. James, Walter Rodney, Howard French and W. E. B. Du Bois (*Black Reconstruction*, 1935) form a tradition that has now been substantially absorbed into mainstream economic history (Sven Beckert's *Empire of Cotton*, Edward Baptist's *The Half Has Never Been Told*). Post-colonial theory is the intellectual force that drove this absorption.

The third aspect is the colonial market. European industrial production from the late eighteenth century required not just inputs (cotton, sugar, indigo) but also outputs — colonial markets for finished goods. The British destruction of the Indian textile industry through the East India Company's policies in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries opened Indian markets to Lancashire cloth. Gandhi's swadeshi movement and the symbol of the *charkha* (spinning wheel) were a direct response to this colonial market-making a century later. Dussel's analysis is acute: European "civil society" needs "to seek outside itself new consumers… to find ways to move about among other peoples that are inferior to it with respect to the resources that it has in abundance, or, in general, its industry" (Hegel, cited in Dussel, *Eurocentrism and Modernity*, pp. 73-4). The colonial market was not an afterthought; it was the demand-side complement to industrial supply.

The fourth aspect is the racial structure of labour. Post-colonial theory has shown that capitalism's labour markets have always been racially structured. The plantation, the mine, the colonial railway and the post-colonial Free Trade Zone are not racially neutral institutions; they sort workers into racialised categories with different wages, rights and life chances. Cedric Robinson's *Black Marxism* (1983) coined "racial capitalism" to capture this; Du Bois had anticipated it. Today, the global supply chain — from cobalt mining in the DRC to lithium extraction in Bolivia, from garment work in Bangladesh to data labelling in Kenya — retains a racial structure that classical accounts of capitalism miss. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity helps us understand why: capitalism does not just exploit labour, it produces racialised subjects whose exploitation is naturalised.

Concrete examples sharpen the reframing. The East India Company's revenue extraction from Bengal in the 1750s-60s — the Bengal famine of 1770 killed perhaps ten million people while the Company exported revenue to London — was a moment of colonial capital accumulation that funded subsequent British industrialisation. The opium trade with China in the 1830s-40s, the Opium Wars and the imposition of unequal treaties, financed British India's deficits. The Berlin Conference of 1884-85 partitioned Africa for European capital. The post-1945 World Bank and IMF have managed the post-colonial economic order in ways that critics have analysed as neo-colonial. The 21st-century lithium triangle (Bolivia, Chile, Argentina) supplies batteries to Northern green-transition projects, raising fresh questions about extractivism. Vaccine apartheid during COVID was capitalism's racial structure made visible on the most basic biopolitical level.

Critically, the post-colonial reframing of capitalism has limits. It can over-emphasise extraction and under-theorise the productive transformations capitalism also enabled (industrial productivity, agricultural intensification, urbanisation). It can underweight the agency of colonised actors in shaping capitalism (Indian merchants, African traders, Chinese diasporas were not just victims but participants). It can sometimes collapse capitalism into colonialism, missing the specific logic of capital accumulation that classical Marxist analysis tracks. The strongest post-colonial reframing — Robinson's racial capitalism, Beckert's empire-of-cotton history, Patnaik and Patnaik's analysis of imperial drain — holds the colonial and capitalist registers in productive tension.

In conclusion, post-colonial theory reframes the history of capitalism by making colonial relations constitutive rather than incidental. It restores primitive accumulation to the centre, foregrounds the slave trade, theorises colonial markets, and analyses the racial structure of labour. It has substantially transformed economic history and is now influencing development economics, global political economy and contemporary debates about reparations and global tax justice. The student in 2026 cannot understand contemporary global capitalism without these resources. The reframing is unfinished — debates continue about how much weight to give colonial extraction versus European institutional innovation — but the basic insight is now mainstream: capitalism was, from its beginning, a world-system in which colonial relations were not external but internal.
