# L09, Post-Colonial Theory

## 1. Core Argument

Post-colonial theory insists that colonialism is not a regrettable side-event in the rise of European modernity but its constitutive condition. My core argument runs through four moves. First, colonialism has three intertwined faces, settlement (the displacement of indigenous populations by the colonising power), economic exploitation (the appropriation of land, labour and resources tied tightly to the rise of capitalism) and unequal power relations (Lecture 9 slides). Howard French, in *Born in Blackness*, contends that we must not "ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that the point is not to "add Africa in" but to "invert the current view" (French, 2021, [VERIFY p.?]); the Portuguese trade of Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s makes the global texture of modernity inescapable. Second, the West is constituted discursively against the Rest. Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works through binary oppositions, "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277), and these binaries are not descriptions but hierarchies which the discourse "simplifies" (Hall, 1992: 280) into a single dichotomy. Third, Said's account of Orientalism shows that "the Orient is not only adjacent to Europe; it is also the place of Europe's greatest and richest and oldest colonies… and one of its deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87) and that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89). Following Foucault, Said treats Orientalism as a discourse that "manage[s], and even produce[s], the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Fourth, the colonised are denied subjectivity. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* registers the white child's hail, "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), that fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" extends this, the figure is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), not an empirical group, and Spivak's question of subaltern speech asks whether the oppressed can articulate themselves outside the categories of the coloniser. Enrique Dussel's intervention deepens these arguments: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Post-colonial theory, then, is a critique of modernity's myth of self-origination, of its binaries, of its epistemic violence, and of its denial of voice to those it makes peripheral.

## 2. Thinkers and Concepts

- **Edward Said (1935-2003)**, Born in Jerusalem under the British Mandate, raised in Egypt, taught in New York. *Orientalism* (1978). Key concepts: Orientalism, the Orient as Europe's contrasting Other, false binary divisions, discourse/power following Foucault, "flexible positional superiority" (Said, 1978: 26), the imperial archive.
- **Stuart Hall (1932-2014)**, Jamaican-British cultural theorist. *Formations of Modernity* (1992). Key concepts: the West and the Rest, the West as historical not geographical construct, discourse as "system of representation" (Hall, 1992: 277), binary oppositions, internal others (Jews, Eastern Europeans, women).
- **Frantz Fanon (1925-1961)**, Born in Martinique, French Antilles; psychiatrist, fought in French resistance and later in Algerian liberation. *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961). Key concepts: object/subject dialectic, the colonial gaze, the denial of subjectivity, the colonial wound, decolonisation as struggle for voice.
- **Chandra Mohanty (1955–)**, Born in Mumbai; teaches at Syracuse. *Under Western Eyes* (1988). Key concepts: the third-world woman, discursive colonisation, women as "the other of the other," refusal of universal sisterhood.
- **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (1942–)**, Indian-American literary theorist. *Can the Subaltern Speak?* (1988). Key concepts: the subaltern, epistemic violence, strategic essentialism, the impossibility of pure recovered voice.
- **Howard French**, *Born in Blackness* (2021). Argues Africa is central to making modern world; inversion of perspective.
- **Enrique Dussel (1934-2023)**, Argentine-Mexican liberation philosopher. *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993). Key concepts: the myth of modernity, the colonial wound, the colonial difference, trans-modernity, the fallacy of developmentalism, 1492 as birth of modernity.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

- Treating "post-colonial" as merely "after colonialism" temporally; it is rather a theoretical stance on the continuing presence of colonial structures, including in formally independent states.
- Reducing Said to "the West invented the East". Said is careful that "the Orient is not an inert fact of nature" but also that "it would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6). The Orient exists; Orientalism is the discourse about it.
- Treating Hall's binaries as descriptive of two real groups; they are productive fictions that organise power.
- Treating the subaltern as just "the poor". The lecture flags Spivak at the end (slide 30) as raising the question of who can speak for whom; the subaltern is the structurally voiceless, not simply the materially poor.
- Forgetting that Hall warns against treating "the West" as homogeneous; "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279), and the West also has its "internal others" such as Jews, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", and Western women represented as inferior to Western men (Hall, 1992: 280).
- Forgetting the symmetry: "the Rest" is just as internally differentiated as "the West", from the Middle East and Far East to Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia (Hall, 1992: 280).
- Forgetting Dussel's point that modernity has *two* faces: an emancipatory concept and a sacrificial myth, and the critique must hold both (Dussel, 1993: 75).
- Treating French's argument as "adding Africa". French's point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4).

## 7. Past-Year Exam Answers

### 2025 Q11, How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between 'the West and the Rest'?

The distinction between the West and the Rest is, on the surface, a piece of casual geography. Post-colonial theorists from Hall onwards have argued that it is in fact a discursive machine, and they have attacked it on four fronts: showing that the West is a historical not a geographical entity; revealing the binary logic that sustains it; exposing the power relations it conceals; and producing alternative cartographies that displace it.

The first move is Hall's. In *Formations of Modernity*, Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277). Geographically, Hall notes, Japan is east but counts as West; eastern Europe is in Europe but is unsure if it is in the West; Latin America is in the western hemisphere but is filed under the Third World. "The West is as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, 1992: 276). The West is a historical construct, a name for "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277), and therefore something that was made, can be unmade, and could have been otherwise.

The second move is to expose the binaries. Hall lays out the oppositional grammar: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (lecture slide 9; Hall, 1992: 277). Each pair is a hierarchy, not just a difference. The West is the positive term; the Rest is what the West is not. Said's account of Orientalism extends this to the Eastern variant: "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88). The Orient is timeless, mysterious, feminine, decadent. Hall's key sentence is that this discourse "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, 1992: 280). The discourse collapses internal heterogeneity on both sides.

The third move is to expose power. "The relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89). For Said, drawing on Foucault, the West/Rest discourse is not innocent description; it is the "corporate institution for dealing with the Orient" through which "European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9). The categories on Said's slide 12, Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia", are not neutral descriptions but the contemporary effects of a long Western "way of thinking, acting, studying, trading, and dominating". Dussel sharpens the point: the West is not just a name for one civilisation among others but for the self-appointed centre of "World History", with the periphery serving as raw material for its self-image (Dussel, 1993: 65).

The fourth move is to displace the cartography. Howard French inverts the standard map by showing that the Portuguese used cotton from India to buy gold in Africa to use as coinage in Europe in the 1500s (slide 4); there is no Europe without Africa and Asia. The Gall-Peters projection (slide 32) and the "World Turned Upside Down" map (slide 33) make the Mercator's distortion visible by restoring proportional area and by reversing the north-up convention. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" challenges the West/Rest binary inside feminism, showing how Western women's self-image as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) depends on a fabricated third-world counterpart who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity shows what the binary feels like from inside: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325).

Concrete materials make the challenge specific. Hall's enumeration of the binary pairs, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, names the operating code (Hall, 1992: 277). Said's list of Orientalist stereotypes, the Orient as timeless, mysterious, feminine-passive-luxurious, immoral-decadent (slide 16), shows what the operating code produces on the Eastern axis. Mohanty's careful sentence that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) is the methodological refusal of the homogenising move. Fanon's white-eye scene on the train (Fanon, 1967: 323) is what happens when the binary is enforced on a body.

A balanced assessment must acknowledge that these challenges are not symmetrical successes. The Mercator projection still organises classroom geography. The discourse Hall describes still simplifies. The Orientalist stereotypes Said catalogues still circulate. But post-colonial theorists have made the West/Rest distinction visible as a distinction; they have denaturalised it. Hall, Said, Fanon, Mohanty, French and Dussel together have replaced a description with an indictment.

### 2025 Q13, Outline and assess if Southern theory addresses inequalities in the Global South.

[VERIFY SOURCE: "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in the Lecture 9 slides or the L9 readings supplied (Ashcroft/Said, Hall, Dussel). The closest source-derived equivalent is Howard French's call to "invert the current view" of who makes modernity (slide 4), Dussel's claim that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History" against a periphery (Dussel, 1993: 65), and Hall's insistence that the West is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277). I therefore answer the question using these L9 materials, treating "Southern theory" as a label for the broader project of provincialising the West that Hall, French, Mohanty, Fanon and Dussel together articulate.]

The project of theorising from outside the metropole, which the question labels "Southern theory", is a critical effort to displace the European centre of social theory and to take seriously the intellectual production of the formerly colonised world. I will outline what this project looks like inside the L9 materials and then assess how far it actually addresses inequalities within the Global South itself.

The outline begins with Hall. The West, for Hall, is a "historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277), a label for "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277). The West appears unified because its differences are bracketed; the Rest appears unified because its differences are bracketed (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory written from the Rest would have to refuse both homogenisations and insist on the internal variety of "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* provides a concrete instance: Africa is not an addition to the story of modernity but a co-producer of it, and "the point is not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4) shows that European capital accumulation depended on African and Indian inputs from the 1500s onwards. Dussel pushes this further. Modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65), and a theory adequate to the periphery must therefore be "trans-modern", a "co-realization … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76).

The assessment of whether such a project addresses inequalities *within* the Global South is mixed. The first reason for hope is that theorising from the periphery enables Southern subjects to take their own concerns as theoretical starting points. Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* is exemplary: a doctor in the French Antilles makes his own colonial experience the object of theory rather than data for European psychiatry. Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* makes Indian and other non-Western women theorists rather than informants. The second reason is that this project provides language for refusing the universalism that erases periphery-specific oppressions. Mohanty's injunction that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) cuts against generic global feminism and demands attention to particular Southern social formations. The third reason is that it pluralises political imagination: Dussel's trans-modernity is precisely a refusal to make the South a mirror image of the North.

On the cautious side, the first concern is that "the Global South" is itself a homogenising category. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies: collapsing the lives of women in vastly different societies into a single figure repeats the homogenising operation Hall diagnoses for the Rest. Hall warns that the Rest "covers enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions" and "can equally encompass the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280). A Southern theory that simply inverts the binary without disaggregating the Rest reproduces the very discursive simplification Hall criticises. The second concern is Fanon's warning that the colonial gaze produces doubling, not just exclusion: the post-colonial scholar trained in metropolitan categories may not have escaped what Fanon calls being "dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes" (Fanon, 1967: 325) even when speaking from the periphery. The third concern is Dussel's own caution that the emancipatory "concept" of modernity is intertwined with the "irrational myth" of sacrificial violence (Dussel, 1993: 75); a theory of the South that simply asserts Southern virtue against Northern vice risks producing its own sacrificial myth.

The assessment, then, is mixed. A theory written from the perspective of the Rest is indispensable for putting Northern theory in its place; Hall, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel together have made the position of the Rest legible as a theoretical position rather than as a residual fact. But the project does not by itself address inequalities within the South. The internal differentiations Hall flags (Hall, 1992: 280), the gender hierarchies Mohanty insists on theorising "*within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262), and the doublings Fanon traces in the colonial subject require continued analytic work. Dussel's trans-modernity is the most explicit attempt within the L9 materials to refuse a simple inversion: it asks not for a Southern modernity that mirrors the Northern one but for an "incorporative solidarity" (Dussel, 1993: 76) across centre and periphery. Whether that solidarity has been built in practice is a separate question; the conceptual resources are there in the L9 readings, but they remain unfinished.

### 2025 Q15, Critically discuss two aspects of the Gandhian view on technology.

[VERIFY SOURCE: Gandhi and his view on technology do not appear in the Lecture 9 slides or the L9 readings supplied (Ashcroft/Said, Hall, Dussel). The brief I am working to instructs me to treat this as an L9 question, but I cannot defend any specific Gandhian textual claim from L9 materials. I therefore answer the question using only those L9 materials that bear on the broader question of technology, modernity and colonisation, and I flag explicitly that any properly Gandhian claim would need a separate source check before deployment in the exam.]

The question asks about Gandhian technology critique. Within the L9 materials, the relevant scaffolding for an answer comes from Hall's analysis of the West/Rest binary, from Said's account of how European culture "manage[s], and even produce[s], the Orient" through institutionally located knowledge (Said, 1978: 88-9), from Howard French's inversion of the cartography of modernity (slide 4), and from Dussel's argument that modernity has both a rational kernel and a sacrificial myth (Dussel, 1993: 75). I will treat the question as one about two aspects of any non-Western critique of technological modernity that the L9 framework allows: technology as ethical-discursive embedding rather than neutral instrument, and technology as a question of scale and dependency. The label "Gandhian" is preserved as the question demands; the substantive content is what L9 sustains.

The first aspect is technology as ethical and discursive embedding rather than neutral instrument. The dominant Western story, Hall reminds us, treats modernity as the property of "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277), and locates technological progress squarely on the "Western" side of the binary "progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277; slide 9). Said's analysis sharpens this: the technical sciences are part of the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Technology, on this analysis, is never just a tool; it is always also part of a discursive operation that distributes rationality, agency and modernity unevenly. A Gandhian-style critique that refuses the instrumental view of technology and insists that technologies embed a way of life is therefore consonant with what Hall and Said say about the broader discourse of modernity.

Critically, the strength of this aspect is that it refuses the West/Rest split between science and magic, between rational instrument and traditional craft, that Hall identifies (Hall, 1992: 277). It denies that "Western" technology is neutral and that "traditional" technology is backward. The weakness is the one Hall himself flags about the Rest more generally: it is "enormously" internally differentiated, "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280), and one cannot assume that the technical practices of one part of the Rest stand for the whole. A Gandhian critique that universalises one form of non-Western practice risks repeating in inverted form the homogenising operation Hall criticises.

The second aspect is technology as a question of scale and dependency. Howard French's slide-4 example, the Portuguese trade of Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s, makes the point in sharp historical form: industrial-scale European modernity depended from the start on extractive circuits linking Africa, Asia and Europe (slide 4). A critique of large-scale industrial technology can be read as a critique of those extractive circuits. Dussel's articulation of the "myth of modernity" lists, among the casualties of modernity's sacrificial logic, "the colonized, the slave, the woman, the ecological destruction of the earth" (Dussel, 1993: 75); a scale-and-dependency critique of technology is in part a critique of that destruction.

Critically, the strength of the scale-and-dependency aspect is that it refuses the unilinear "development" picture that Dussel calls the "fallacy of developmentalism", the thinking "that the path of Europe's modern development must be followed unilaterally by every other culture" (Dussel, 1993: 67). It opens conceptual space for what Dussel calls a "trans-modernity" of "incorporative solidarity … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76). The weakness is the same risk Dussel himself acknowledges: critique of Northern modernity must not become romantic celebration of the periphery's pre-modern past. Dussel is explicit that "we do not propose a critique of reason as such; but we do accept their critique of a violent, coercive, genocidal reason" (Dussel, 1993: 75). A Gandhian-style scale critique that simply rejected the rational kernel of modernity along with its sacrificial myth would, on Dussel's terms, be one-sided.

The two aspects together, on the L9 reading, articulate a critique of technological modernity that is consonant with Hall on the West/Rest discourse, with Said on the entanglement of knowledge and rule, with French on the global texture of early-modern accumulation, and with Dussel on the two-faced character of modernity. The strongest reading takes Gandhian critique as posing the questions, what does this technology do to bodies, communities, ecologies, and to the periphery, rather than as supplying fixed answers about which technologies to use.

### 2024 Q10, What is postcolonial theory? In what ways is it a critique of modernity?

Postcolonial theory, as the L9 materials assemble it, is the body of work, Said in *Orientalism* (1978), Hall in *Formations of Modernity* (1992), Mohanty in *Under Western Eyes* (1984), Fanon in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) read retrospectively, French in *Born in Blackness* (2021), Dussel in his Frankfurt Lectures (1993), with Spivak's question flagged at the lecture's conclusion (slide 30), which insists that European colonialism is not an external chapter in the story of modernity but its inner architecture. I will argue that postcolonial theory in the L9 framing critiques modernity on four registers: historical, discursive, subjective and epistemological.

Historically, postcolonial theory rewrites the periodisation of modernity. The conventional sociological story, feudalism breaks down, the Reformation, the Enlightenment, industrial capitalism, runs internally to Europe. Dussel, by contrast, claims that "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity" and that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* makes the same move from a different angle: Africa is not an addendum to modernity but a co-producer of it, and the point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit, where Indian cotton bought African gold which became European coinage in the 1500s (slide 4), is the kind of evidence that breaks the internally-European story. Stripping these connections out, what Hall calls the West's tendency to treat itself "as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278), is itself an ideological act.

Discursively, postcolonial theory critiques the binaries through which modernity tells its story. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works by polarisation: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277). These are not descriptions but the simplifying grammar of a "system of representation" that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). Said's account of Orientalism develops the same critique on the Eastern axis: the Orient was "one of [Europe's] deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Modernity in this telling is not just a description of European societies; it is a comparative claim that requires a contrasting Other.

Subjectively, postcolonial theory tracks what modernity does to the colonised. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity captures the moment when the modern liberal claim of universal personhood collides with race: the white child's call, "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Modernity, which advertises itself as the kingdom of the autonomous subject, in fact distributes subjectivity unequally; the colonised is granted the body but not the mind, the spectacle but not the gaze. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" shows the same logic on the gender axis, the average third-world woman is rendered "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) so that Western women can appear as the modern subject.

Epistemologically, postcolonial theory attacks the production of knowledge itself. Said borrows Foucault to argue that "without examining Orientalism as a discourse one cannot possibly understand the enormously systematic discipline by which European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Hall, on the same logic, lists four functions of the West/Rest concept, classification, representation, comparison and evaluation, and concludes that "it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). Spivak's question of subaltern speech, flagged in the lecture's concluding slide (slide 30), extends this critique into the present: even sympathetic Western scholarship risks epistemic violence when it claims to "recover" voices that can only speak through Western categories.

Examples drawn from the lecture make the critique concrete. Said's own slide-12 list, the categories of Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, and "Arabia", shows that the Orientalist discourse "still has effects today in how people in the West think about and act about people in that region" (slide 12). The stereotype-set in slide 16, the Orient as timeless, mysterious, feminine-passive-luxurious, immoral-decadent, is the recurring product of the discourse. The Mercator projection on slide 31 is the cartographic form of the same operation; the Gall-Peters projection (slide 32) and the "World Turned Upside Down" map (slide 33) are the counter-images that make the distortion visible. Mohanty's three-step demonstration on slides 19-23 of how Western feminist scholarship constructs the "average third world woman" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) shows the same machinery operating in self-consciously progressive Western writing. Postcolonial theory does not deny that modernity emancipated some, Dussel concedes "the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment" (Dussel, 1993: 75), but it insists that this emancipation was paid for in what Dussel calls "an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence" (Dussel, 1993: 66). Its critique of modernity is therefore not nostalgic but trans-modern: it asks for a modernity that finally accounts for its full cost.

### 2024 Q11, What is a Southern theory? What are the limitations of the concept?

[VERIFY SOURCE: As noted at 2025 Q13, "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in the Lecture 9 slides or the L9 readings supplied. I therefore answer the question using only the L9 materials' resources for theorising the non-Western, principally Hall on the West/Rest discourse, Said on Orientalism, French on inverting the cartography of modernity, Fanon on colonised subjectivity, Mohanty on the third-world woman, and Dussel on trans-modernity. The label "Southern theory" is treated as the question's framing for the broader project of theorising from outside the metropole.]

The question asks what a theory written from the position of the formerly colonised would look like, and what limits attach to such a project. Within the L9 materials, the relevant resources are Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest, Said's account of Orientalism, French's *Born in Blackness* (slide 4), Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity, Mohanty's critique of Western feminist scholarship, and Dussel's trans-modernity. I will sketch what these resources suggest a theory from the periphery looks like, and then catalogue the limitations the L9 materials themselves flag.

The first step is the negative one. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works through homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation and naturalisation; it "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)" while doing the same to the Rest (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory written from the Rest must therefore refuse the homogenising operation in both directions. Said's account of Orientalism makes the same point on the Eastern axis: the Orient is "a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88), and the theory from outside must refuse that ontological distinction.

The second step is the constructive one. French's *Born in Blackness* models what inverting the cartography looks like: not adding Africa to a European story but inverting the current view (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4) makes the global texture of early-modern accumulation visible from a non-European starting point. Dussel articulates the philosophical version: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65), and the constructive task is "trans-modernity" understood as "co-realization … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76).

The third step is the methodological one. Mohanty insists that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Theory from the periphery, on this principle, must be granular, not generic. Fanon's analysis of the white-eye scene on the train (Fanon, 1967: 323-5) is methodological in this sense too: theory begins from a specific incident in a specific body and works outward.

The concept of a theory from the periphery faces serious limitations, and the L9 materials make several of them visible. First, "the South" risks the same homogenisation Hall criticises for the West. Hall explicitly warns that "the Rest" "covers enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions, for example, between the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" and "can equally encompass the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory of the South that bundled these together would reproduce, with inverted signs, the very simplification it set out to refuse. Mohanty's parallel critique on the gender axis applies in full: collapsing the experience of women across vastly different Southern societies into a single figure is "an essentially truncated" representation (Mohanty, 1984: 261).

Second, Hall reminds us that "the West had its own internal 'others'": Jews ostracised, Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", and Western women represented as inferior to Western men (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory that treats the West as monolithic misses these internal differentiations and the cross-cutting solidarities they make possible. Said likewise treats Orientalism as a discourse with internal complexity, "an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7), not a uniform conspiracy.

Third, Fanon's account of colonised subjectivity warns against assuming that simply standing in the periphery delivers an undistorted theoretical view. The colonial gaze produces doubling: the colonised subject is dissected under white eyes and finds the body "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324). Theory written from the periphery may still be written under that gaze.

Fourth, Dussel's own caution is that the emancipatory "concept" of modernity is intertwined with its "irrational myth" of sacrificial violence (Dussel, 1993: 75-6). A theory from the periphery that simply inverts the Northern story, asserting Southern virtue against Northern vice, would risk producing its own sacrificial myth.

The defence of the project, on the L9 reading, runs through Dussel's distinction between negation and trans-modernity. The point is not to replace the centre but to make space for "an incorporative solidarity … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Hall's, French's, Said's, Fanon's, Mohanty's and Dussel's resources together do not deliver a finished theory of the South; they deliver the materials for one, and they flag in advance the homogenisations, doublings and inversions to which such a theory is prone.

### 2023 Q11, How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between "the West and the Rest"?

This question is materially identical to 2025 Q11 above; I write a distinct answer here pivoting on the historical-archive dimension. Post-colonial theorists from the 1970s onwards have challenged the West/Rest distinction at three depths: as a discursive system (Hall, Said), as an experiential reality for the colonised (Fanon, Mohanty), and as a historical falsehood about who made the modern world (Dussel, French).

At the discursive depth, Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest demonstrates that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277). Hall identifies four functions of the concept: it classifies (West vs non-West), represents (a composite image), measures (a standard of comparison), and evaluates (developed = good = desirable). "In short, it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). The discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). The binaries, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, are the operating code of the discourse. Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation in the Eastern direction. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). The point is structural: the West cannot be the West without the Rest because its identity is *constituted* by contrast.

At the experiential depth, Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* tells us what the West/Rest binary feels like inside a body. The white child's "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323) is the discourse hailing the colonised; Fanon is fixed, dissected, given back his body "sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The West/Rest distinction is not just a sentence in a textbook; it is a wound on the flesh. Mohanty extends this analysis along the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) precisely by producing a "third world woman" who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The challenge is to refuse the binary in both its halves.

At the historical depth, Dussel and French rewrite the genealogy of modernity. Dussel argues that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates" (Dussel, 1993: 65) and that the Iberian conquest of the New World from 1492 is constitutive, not incidental. French's *Born in Blackness* makes Africa central: we must not ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity, and the point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit, the trade of Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s (slide 4), is the concrete evidence that European capital accumulation depended from the start on extractive global links. Dussel's own seven-point myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) catalogues the mechanisms: modern civilisation understands itself as the most developed; this obliges it to "develop" others; the path is the one Europe followed; opposition justifies "the violence necessary to remove the obstacles to modernization"; the violence becomes "almost ritualistic"; the barbarian is in "a state of guilt"; the "suffering and sacrifices" are "inevitable and necessary".

Each register has a textual form in the L9 materials. Hall's discursive challenge runs through his four functions of the West/Rest concept (Hall, 1992: 277). Said's challenge runs through the slide-12 list of categories the Orientalist discourse still produces: Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia". Fanon and Mohanty's experiential challenge runs through the white-eye scene (Fanon, 1967: 323-5) and the third-world-woman figure (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Dussel and French's historical challenge runs through the 1492-as-birth claim and the inversion of the cartography (slide 4; Dussel, 1993: 65-7).

A balanced conclusion notes the limits. Hall reminds us that the discourse is "destructive" precisely because it "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280); that destructive force is not removed by being named. The Mercator projection on slide 31 is still the default classroom map. Said's slide-12 list of contemporary Orientalist effects names categories that the discourse still organises. Yet the post-colonial intellectual achievement registered in the L9 materials is real: the West/Rest binary can no longer be deployed innocently. Hall taught us its grammar; Said its archive; Fanon its embodiment; Mohanty its feminist version; Dussel its historical falsification; French its missing African heart. The challenge is now to convert this denaturalisation into structural change.

### 2023 Q13, Critically analyse Southern Theory as an alternative to European Modernity. What are its limits?

[VERIFY SOURCE: "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in L9 slides or readings; the answer below treats it as the question's label for the broader project of theorising modernity from outside the European centre, and uses Hall, Said, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel as the L9 resources for that project.]

European modernity, in its classical telling, is a story of self-generated progress that radiates outward from a small north-western corner of Europe to instruct the rest of humanity. A theory written from the position of the Rest, which I take to be what the question calls "Southern theory", refuses this story. It is both a critique (European modernity is not what it says it is) and an alternative (the formerly colonised world has been producing modernity all along, on terms the Eurocentric story conceals). I will analyse the alternative drawn from the L9 materials and then catalogue its limits.

The alternative comes in several voices within the L9 readings. Hall's account is the foundational one. The West is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277); the discourse of the West and the Rest functions through homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation and naturalisation; the four functions of the West-concept are classification, representation, comparison and evaluation, and together "it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). The implication is that an alternative to European modernity must begin by denaturalising the West as concept. Said adds the institutional account: Orientalism is "a corporate institution for dealing with the Orient" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 25, 88-9); an alternative must dismantle that institutional structure. French historicises: Africa is not an addendum to modernity but a co-producer of it, and the task is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4) shows the global texture of early-modern accumulation. Dussel articulates the philosophical version: an alternative to European modernity is "trans-modernity" understood as "the co-realization … between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76).

The strengths of this alternative are several. It punctures the self-congratulation of European modernity by exposing its dependency on the periphery from the start (French, slide 4; Dussel, 1993: 65). It legitimates non-European intellectual labour: Fanon writes from the French Antilles, Said from Palestine/Egypt/New York, Mohanty from Mumbai/Syracuse, Dussel from Argentina/Mexico. It refuses the homogenising operation in both directions, against the West as monolithic and against the Rest as monolithic (Hall, 1992: 279-80). It supplies methodological direction: Mohanty's principle that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) generalises into a refusal of generic theory.

The limits, however, are real and the L9 materials themselves make several of them visible. First, "the South" risks the same homogenisation Hall criticises for the West. Hall is explicit that "the Rest" covers "enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions, for example, between the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280). Mohanty's parallel critique of the figure of the "third world woman" applies to any theory that bundles vastly different Southern lives into a single subject (Mohanty, 1984: 261).

Second, Fanon's account of colonial subjectivity warns that the periphery is not automatically a transparent vantage point. The colonised subject is "dissected under white eyes" and finds the body "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324-5). Theory written from inside that doubling does not automatically escape it.

Third, Hall reminds us that the West has "its own internal 'others'", that Jews were "frequently excluded and ostracized", Eastern Europeans were "regarded as 'barbaric'", and Western women "were represented as inferior to Western men" (Hall, 1992: 280). A theory that treats the West as homogeneous misses these cross-cutting axes of exclusion and the cross-cutting solidarities they make possible.

Fourth, Dussel's own caution about the "two faces" of modernity, the emancipatory rational kernel and the sacrificial myth (Dussel, 1993: 75-6), warns against simply inverting the value signs. Dussel writes explicitly that "we do not propose a critique of reason as such; but we do accept their critique of a violent, coercive, genocidal reason. We do not deny the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment, only its irrational moment as sacrificial myth" (Dussel, 1993: 75). A theory of the South that retains the violent dualism while reversing the polarity would reproduce the structure Dussel calls the "myth of modernity".

Fifth, Said warns that Orientalism is "produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7); a theory from the periphery is itself produced inside global power asymmetries, and the institutional infrastructure for its dissemination is part of those asymmetries. Sixth, the L9 conclusion slide ends on Spivak's question, "Who can speak for whom?" (slide 30). The question is not rhetorical: a theory written in the name of the periphery may, like Spivak's subaltern, struggle to register on available frames.

A balanced conclusion: a theory from the position of the Rest is a necessary corrective to a Eurocentric sociology, and Hall, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel together open the theoretical space the discipline needs. But the alternative is not finished. It needs Hall's warning against homogenising the West and the Rest, Mohanty's insistence on specificity, Fanon's awareness of the doubling produced by the colonial gaze, Dussel's refusal of simple inversion, and Said's awareness of how knowledge is produced inside power. The alternative to European modernity, on the L9 reading, is not Southern modernity as mirror image but Dussel's trans-modernity, a global conversation in which the periphery finally counts as theory.

### 2023 Q14, How are non-western models proposing a different relationship between modernity and technology? Discuss with examples.

[VERIFY SOURCE: The L9 materials do not contain a specific non-Western theorist of technology by name (no Gandhi, no buen vivir, no STS). I therefore answer the question by reading the L9 materials' resources for what they say about modernity, science, knowledge and the West/Rest binary, and treat "non-Western models of technology" as derivable from those resources rather than as separately attested in L9.]

The dominant story about modernity and technology, on the L9 reading, is one in which technology is the property of the West and is part of the same discourse that makes the West the West. Hall lists "science/magic" as one of the binary oppositions through which "the West and Modernity" are "created in terms of what they are not" (Hall, 1992: 277; slide 9). Said sharpens the point: the technical and scientific sciences are part of the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Technology, in this framing, is never a neutral instrument; it is always also a discourse that allocates rationality, agency and modernity asymmetrically. The non-Western model of the technology-modernity relation must therefore begin by refusing the science/magic binary.

The first non-Western move, made available by Hall and Said, is to deny that "Western" technology is rational and "non-Western" practice is magical. Hall is clear that the West's claim to monopolise rationality is part of the discourse, not part of reality; the four functions of the West-concept include "criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked" with "powerful positive and negative feelings" (Hall, 1992: 277). A non-Western theory of technology refuses this evaluative ranking. It treats technologies as embedded practices that organise bodies, communities and ecologies, and it evaluates them on those terms rather than on a single axis of "progress".

The second non-Western move, made available by French (slide 4) and Dussel, is to insist that the apparently autonomous European technological story is in fact a global story. The Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4) shows that European capital accumulation from the 1500s onwards depended on Indian textiles, African gold and inter-continental trade circuits. French's slide-4 argument is that we "must not ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that the point is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view". Dussel pushes this into philosophy: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65). A non-Western theory of technology accordingly refuses the diffusionist story in which technology starts in Europe and spreads outward; it insists that the techniques, materials and trade-circuits of "European" technology were globally constituted from the start.

The third non-Western move, articulated by Dussel, is to read the costs of technological modernity through its sacrificial myth. The seven-point myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) catalogues how modern civilisation justifies "the violence necessary to remove the obstacles to modernization" and renders "the colonized, the slave, the woman, the ecological destruction of the earth" as "participants in a process of redemptive sacrifice". A non-Western theory of technology refuses the redemptive narrative and counts the costs.

The fourth non-Western move, also Dussel's, is to articulate "trans-modernity" as the constructive alternative: a "co-realization" between "center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Trans-modern technology, on this reading, is not anti-modern; it incorporates technical knowledge but subordinates it to ethical, ecological and incorporative norms.

Critically, these moves face their own limits. First, Hall's warning against homogenising the Rest applies: "the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280) do not share a single technological tradition or a single relation to modernity. A non-Western theory of technology that bundled them into a single counter-position would reproduce the homogenising operation it set out to refuse. Second, Fanon's analysis of colonised subjectivity (Fanon, 1967: 324-5) warns that a theory from inside the colonial relation may still be written under the white gaze; the science/magic binary may have become internal to the colonised subject's own self-understanding. Third, Dussel's two-faces argument (Dussel, 1993: 75-6) cuts in both directions: just as Northern modernity must be criticised for its sacrificial myth, a non-Western theory of technology must not simply reject the "rational kernel" of modern technical practice.

The strongest non-Western reading available within the L9 materials is therefore neither a wholesale rejection of modern technology nor a celebration of supposedly authentic non-Western alternatives. It is a refusal of the science/magic binary (Hall), a re-historicisation of the technical circuits of modernity as global from the start (French, slide 4), a counting of the costs (Dussel, 1993: 75), and a constructive articulation of trans-modernity as "incorporative solidarity" across centre and periphery (Dussel, 1993: 76). The critical post-colonial point is that thinking about technology is also thinking about modernity, and that thinking about modernity is also thinking about who counts.

### 2022 Q13, How does the concept of "the West" set itself up in distinction to "the Rest"?

The concept of "the West" sets itself up against "the Rest" through a discursive operation that Hall describes with unusual clarity in *Formations of Modernity*. I will trace this operation in four steps, homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation, and naturalisation, and then assess the political work it does.

Step one is homogenisation. The West is internally diverse, Germanic, Latin, Nordic, Mediterranean, Anglo-Saxon, and the Rest is even more so, China, India, the Islamic world, sub-Saharan Africa, indigenous Americas, Aboriginal Australasia. Yet the discourse of the West and the Rest "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, 1992: 280). The internal differences of the West are collapsed in favour of its shared character; the internal differences of the Rest are collapsed in favour of their shared difference from the West.

Step two is polarisation. The discourse arranges itself through binary oppositions: rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic (lecture slide 9; Hall, 1992: 277). Saussure taught us that meaning works through difference, "night" means what it does because it is opposite to "day." Hall extends this to cultural identity: "national cultures acquire their strong sense of identity by contrasting themselves with other cultures" (Hall, 1992: 279). The West knows itself by *not being* the Rest. The Rest is the negative pole that makes the positive pole legible.

Step three is hierarchisation. The binaries are not symmetrical. The first term of each pair carries positive value; the second carries negative value. The West is rational, cultured, intellectual, progressive, scientific; the Rest is irrational, savage, embodied, traditional, magical. Hall is explicit: the discourse "provides criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked and around which powerful positive and negative feelings cluster… 'the West' = developed = good = desirable; or the 'non-West' = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). The West/Rest binary is not difference; it is hierarchy. It legitimates colonial intervention, civilising missions, and developmental aid as moral obligations to lift the Rest toward the West.

Step four is naturalisation. The discourse hides its own constructed character. Hall insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277), but the discourse presents the West as a geographical fact, an obvious self-evident location on the map. The Mercator projection visually naturalises Eurocentric geography. The university teaching of "Western civilisation" naturalises the West as a continuous tradition from Athens to Washington via Jerusalem, Rome, Florence, London and Paris, a fictional genealogy that omits the Islamic transmission of Greek philosophy and treats the West as autochthonous.

Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation on the Eastern axis. "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88). The Orient is constructed as Europe's "deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Mohanty extends this to the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities" against a "third world woman" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261).

The political work of this discursive operation is extensive. The slide-5 list of former British colonies (US states until 1776, Canada 1867, Australia 1900, Ireland 1922, India and Pakistan 1947, Ghana 1957, Jamaica 1962, Hong Kong 1997, plus Palestine and Iraq as administered territories) makes the geographical reach concrete: for several centuries these populations were "British subjects … under the authority of the government in London and the Queen (or King)" (slide 5). The slide-6 list of French colonies (Vietnam and Cambodia, Algeria and Morocco, Ivory Coast, Cameroon and Gabon, Martinique, Syria and Lebanon) does the parallel work for French empire. The discourse legitimated all of these (slide 18: "All the above legitimate French and British colonialism"). Said's slide-12 list, Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia", names the contemporary effects: the West-Rest discourse "still has effects today in how people in the West think about and act about people in that region" (slide 12). Hall's diagnosis is that the discourse is "so destructive" because "it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280).

To set up the West against the Rest is therefore not merely to draw a line; it is to perform a sustained ideological operation that homogenises, polarises, hierarchises and naturalises difference, and that, on the L9 reading, has organised five centuries of colonialism. To undo the West/Rest distinction is to refuse all four operations, to recognise internal heterogeneity on both sides (Hall, 1992: 279-80), to break the binary lockstep (Hall, 1992: 277), to refuse the implicit hierarchy (Hall, 1992: 277), and to re-historicise what the discourse presents as natural geography (French, slide 4; Dussel, 1993: 65).

### 2022 Q15, Why is it important to examine social theory which emerges from beyond the West?

Examining social theory from beyond the West matters for at least four reasons that the L9 materials make explicit: epistemic, political, methodological and ethical. I will take each in turn, drawing on Hall, Said, French, Mohanty, Fanon and Dussel.

The epistemic reason is that the canonical Western tradition is partial. Hall reminds us that the West is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277) and that the standard Enlightenment account "treated the West as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278). That internalist story is, as Hall and French argue, false: "the rise of the West is also a global story" (Hall, 1992: 278), and "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies (the Rest)" (Hall, 1992: 278). French's *Born in Blackness* makes the historical content of that global story explicit: Africa is "central to the making of the modern world" and the task is "not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (slide 4). Without theory from beyond the West, what we have is not a global sociology but a parochial European sociology presented as universal.

The political reason is that knowledge production is itself power. Said is explicit: Orientalism is "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25). The "corporate institution" of European Orient-knowledge "was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9). If knowledge is power, then producing knowledge from the periphery is itself a political act. Hall's four functions of the West-concept, classification, representation, comparison and evaluation, show how knowledge of "the Rest" was simultaneously ideology (Hall, 1992: 277). Refusing the discourse requires producing theory from outside its central position.

The methodological reason is that theory generated in one setting and applied uncritically to another distorts both. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" is exemplary: Western feminist scholarship, applied to women of the periphery without grounding in their specific social formations, produces "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized etc.)" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The remedy is to theorise from the ground: "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Methodologically, examining non-Western theory is required for empirical accuracy, not optional decoration.

The ethical reason is the deepest. Dussel argues that modernity has two faces: an emancipatory rational kernel and "an irrational myth, a justification for genocidal violence" (Dussel, 1993: 66, 75). The seven-point myth lists the victims, "the colonized, the slave, the woman, the ecological destruction of the earth" (Dussel, 1993: 75). The victims of modernity have a moral claim to be heard, and the L9 conclusion slide explicitly poses the question, "Who can speak for whom? Spivak" (slide 30). Examining theory from beyond the West is part of the ethical recovery Dussel calls trans-modernity, where "the negated and victimized 'other-face' of modernity … must, in the first place, discover itself as innocent" (Dussel, 1993: 76) and where modernity can be judged.

The L9 materials themselves illustrate the case. Said wrote *Orientalism* from a position structured by Palestine, Egypt and New York (slide 10). Mohanty wrote "Under Western Eyes" from a position structured by Mumbai (slide 19). Fanon wrote *Black Skin, White Masks* from a position structured by Martinique and Paris (slide 24). French wrote *Born in Blackness* with a thesis explicitly designed to invert the European-internalist account (slide 4). Dussel wrote his Frankfurt lectures from Argentina and Mexico (Dussel, 1993: 66-7). The theoretical resources of the L9 reading list are themselves theory from beyond the West, and their availability is the precondition of the critique L9 mounts.

Objections must be addressed. The universalist objection says that knowledge is knowledge regardless of where it is produced. The L9 reply is Hall's: the discourse functions as ideology, and ideologies are produced in particular places (Hall, 1992: 277). The relativist objection says that non-Western theory is just one perspective among many. The L9 reply is Dussel's: the choice is not between universalism and relativism but between modernity-as-myth and trans-modernity-as-incorporative-solidarity (Dussel, 1993: 76). The diluted-canon objection says that broadening the syllabus dilutes quality. The L9 reply is empirical: Said, Hall, Fanon, Mohanty, French and Dussel are not diluting the canon; they are correcting it.

In conclusion, examining social theory from beyond the West matters because epistemically it corrects partial knowledge (Hall, French), politically it refuses knowledge-as-domination (Said), methodologically it secures empirical accuracy (Mohanty), and ethically it acknowledges modernity's victims (Dussel, Fanon, Spivak via slide 30). It is not a courtesy; it is a condition of intelligent sociology about the world we actually live in.

### 2021 Q13, Using at least two theorists, outline and assess the postcolonial critique of "the West".

I will use Stuart Hall and Frantz Fanon as my two theorists, and I will assess the postcolonial critique of "the West" along three dimensions: discursive (Hall), experiential (Fanon), and political. I will then weigh the strengths and limits of the critique.

Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest, set out in *Formations of Modernity* pp. 276-280, makes the critique discursive. The West, Hall argues, is "as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, 1992: 276). It is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277), and it functions as ideology. The concept performs four operations: classification (West/non-West), representation (a composite image), comparison (a measuring stick) and evaluation (a hierarchical ranking). The discourse works through binary oppositions, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, and through a simplifying logic that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). Crucially, Hall shows that the West constituted itself partly by contrast: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West cannot be the West alone; its identity is relational, parasitic on the Rest it produced as Other.

Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* makes the critique experiential. Fanon takes the discursive structures Hall describes and shows what they feel like in a human life. The famous scene on the train, where a white child cries "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), turns the discourse into a hail. The Black colonised subject is fixed by the white gaze: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am *fixed*" (Fanon, 1967: 325). His body is "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored…" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The white world bars him "from all participation. A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324). What Hall describes structurally Fanon registers viscerally: the West is not just an idea, it is a wound on the colonised body.

Together, Hall and Fanon constitute a powerful critique. Hall provides the structural anatomy; Fanon provides the lived testimony. The critique can be summarised in three claims. First, the West is a historical construct, not a natural fact (Hall, 1992: 277). Second, the West constitutes itself by negation, by producing and contrasting itself with the Rest (Hall, 1992: 278, 280). Third, this constitution does material and psychic harm to those positioned as the Rest (Fanon, 1967: 323-5; Mohanty, 1984: 261). The critique extends naturally to the L9 conclusions: the Mercator projection (slide 31) as the cartographic form of the discourse; Said's slide-12 list (Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia") as the contemporary categories the discourse still produces; Mohanty's parallel critique on the gender axis (slides 19-23); and Spivak's question of who can speak for whom (slide 30).

Assessing the critique requires acknowledging both strengths and limits. The strengths are considerable. The critique denaturalises a category that had been taken for granted (Hall, 1992: 277); it links discourse to power following Foucault (Said, 1978: 88-9); and it supplies a vocabulary, Orientalism, the binary of West and Rest, the white-eye gaze, the third-world-woman figure, that organises L9's account.

The limits are also real, and the L9 materials themselves flag several. First, the critique can produce its own essentialism: by speaking constantly of "the West" the critique risks reifying the very entity it wants to dissolve. Hall is alert to this, "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279), and reminds us that the West has its "internal 'others'", Jews "frequently excluded and ostracized", Eastern Europeans regarded as "barbaric", Western women represented as inferior to Western men (Hall, 1992: 280). A critique that treats the West as monolithic misses these cross-cutting axes. Second, Hall's warning extends to the Rest: it covers "enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions, for example, between the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia" (Hall, 1992: 280). Third, Fanon's account of colonial subjectivity, while powerful, requires reading the train scene alongside the conclusion that "the white world, the only honourable one, barred me from all participation" and that "since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The doubling is not a static condition but a condition out of which a new subjectivity must be made. Fourth, Dussel's warning about the two faces of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75-6) cuts in both directions: the critique must not become a sacrificial myth in reverse, the rational kernel of modernity must be retained.

A balanced conclusion: Hall and Fanon together produce one of the most important critical achievements registered in the L9 readings. They have given us a structural account of how the West constitutes itself against the Rest, and a phenomenological account of what that constitution does to a body. The critique remains indispensable. But it needs to be held with the cautions the L9 materials themselves flag, internal heterogeneity (Hall, 1992: 279-80), specificity in theorising (Mohanty, 1984: 262), the active making of new subjectivity (Fanon, 1967: 324), and refusal of inverted essentialism (Dussel, 1993: 75-6). The point is not to retire the critique but to deepen it.

### 2021 Q16, In what ways does 'Southern theory' take forward debates in 'Western' social theory?

[VERIFY SOURCE: "Southern theory" as a named programme does not appear in L9 slides or readings; the answer below treats it as the question's label for the broader project of theorising from outside the European centre, and uses Hall, Said, French, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel as the L9 resources.]

The question asks how theory from the periphery extends, rather than replaces, debates in the Western tradition. The L9 materials supply five lines along which this extension occurs: the sociology of knowledge, the theory of modernity, feminist theory, theories of empire and capitalism, and the question of who can speak for whom.

In the sociology of knowledge, Said's *Orientalism* is the L9 exemplar. Said takes Foucault's notion of discourse, of "a corporate institution" through which "European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 25, 88-9), and turns it on the imperial archive. Foucault had analysed discourse internally to European institutions (the asylum, the clinic, the prison). Said extends the analysis to the global discourse through which Europe constituted its non-European objects. This is taking Foucault forward, not abandoning him. Hall does the parallel work for the West/Rest discourse, identifying four functions of the West-concept, classification, representation, comparison and evaluation, and concluding that "it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277), which extends classical theories of ideology rather than discarding them.

In the theory of modernity, Hall and Dussel together rewrite a debate that the L9 materials show was largely settled internally to Europe. Hall criticises the inherited story that "treated the West as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278) and insists that "the rise of the West is also a global story" (Hall, 1992: 278). French's *Born in Blackness* historicises this with the Portuguese cotton-gold-coinage circuit (slide 4). Dussel goes further, dating "the 'birth' of modernity" to 1492 and arguing that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). This is not the rejection of modernity-theory but its global re-grounding, and Dussel's trans-modernity is its constructive extension (Dussel, 1993: 76).

In feminist theory, Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* takes forward debates that Western feminism opened but could not complete. Western feminist scholarship, Mohanty argues, "implies a notion of gender … which can be applied universally and cross-culturally" (Mohanty, 1984: 261); the figure of "Third world woman" is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The corrective is to theorise from particular societies: "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). This is feminist theory extended by the L9-flagged Mohanty critique, not feminism abandoned.

In theories of empire and capitalism, French's slide-4 inversion of the European-internalist account, the Portuguese using Indian cotton to buy African gold for European coinage in the 1500s, takes forward Marx's account of primitive accumulation by making the global texture of early-modern accumulation visible. Hall's reminder that "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278) does the same for any account of the rise of capitalism. Dussel's claim that the standard genealogy of modernity occludes "the role of Spain and Portugal in the formation of the modern world system from the late fifteenth to the mid-seventeenth centuries" (Dussel, 1993: 65) extends the historiography rather than discarding it.

In the question of who can speak for whom, Fanon's white-eye scene (Fanon, 1967: 323-5) takes forward the existentialist and psychoanalytic accounts of self-other relation that he had inherited from Sartre. The slide-25 note explicitly compares Fanon to Du Bois and Foucault. The lecture's closing question, "Who can speak for whom? Spivak" (slide 30), opens an extension of philosophy-of-recognition debates Western theory had begun. Mohanty's principle of theorising "*within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) supplies the methodological form of that extension.

The argument that theory from the periphery takes Western theory forward, rather than replacing it, has strategic and analytical merits. Strategically, it makes the case for non-Western theory more easily heard. Analytically, it captures the genuine relation in the L9 materials: Said draws on Foucault; Hall's vocabulary of "system of representation" extends post-Saussurean semiotics (Hall, 1992: 277); Fanon writes alongside Sartrean existentialism (slide 24); Mohanty engages Western feminist scholarship as her interlocutor; French and Dussel argue with Hegel and Habermas (Dussel, 1993: 66-74). None is an alien import; each is the post-colonial extension of debates the Western tradition started but could not finish from within.

Caveats are needed. First, the extension thesis must not subordinate the periphery to the centre; the L9 materials are clear that theory from the Rest does its own work and must not be folded back as a footnote to the West (Hall, 1992: 277-80; Dussel, 1993: 65). Second, some of what the L9 materials do is rupture, not extension; the seven-point myth of modernity (Dussel, 1993: 75) breaks decisively with the Habermasian account. Third, Hall's warning against homogenising the West (Hall, 1992: 279-80) reminds us that "Western theory" is not a single thing. A balanced view holds that theory from the periphery, on the L9 reading, is sometimes extension and sometimes rupture, and that the discipline benefits from taking both moves seriously.

### Mock 1, "The Orient is an idea that has a history" (Said). Discuss.

I take Said's epigrammatic claim that the Orient is "an idea that has a history and a tradition of thought, imagery, and vocabulary that have given it reality and presence in and for the West" (Said, 1978: 87) as a doorway into three claims: that the Orient is an idea, that ideas have histories, and that the Orient's history is a Western history. I treat each in turn and end with a contemporary application.

The first claim, that the Orient is an idea, refuses the realist commonsense that the East is just a place. The Orient as Said writes about it is not the geography of Beirut, Cairo, Baghdad and Tehran but the discursive object that European philology, fiction, painting, opera and policy produced about that geography. "The Orient was almost a European invention, and had been since antiquity a place of romance, exotic beings, haunting memories and landscapes, remarkable experiences" (Said, 1978: 87). The Orient is therefore real in the constructivist sense: real as a system of representation, real in its political and material effects, but not a self-standing fact prior to representation. This is the Foucauldian move at the heart of *Orientalism*. The "corporate institution for dealing with the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25), universities, museums, ministries, archives, novels, produced its object as it described it.

The second claim, that the idea has a history, distinguishes Said from purely structuralist analysis. Said dates the modern crystallisation to the late eighteenth century, after Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 and the establishment of academic Oriental studies. Earlier European images, medieval crusader polemic, Marco Polo, the Ottoman threat to Vienna in 1683, fed into but did not yet constitute the Orientalist discourse that consolidates around Silvestre de Sacy, Ernest Renan and Edward Lane. The idea has a datable rise, an institutional habitat, a canon of texts.

The third claim, that the Orient's history is a Western history, is the political punch. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Hall's parallel about the West and the Rest generalises Said's claim eastwards: the West needs the Rest to define itself (Hall, 1992: 280). The Orient is therefore part of Europe's autobiography, not of Asia's.

Three qualifications. First, Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies to Said too, since the Orientalist canon was not monolithic. Said himself frames the discourse as "produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7), allowing for internal variation. Second, the Orient writes back: Said writes from Jerusalem, Egypt and New York (slide 10) and his very book is part of the Orient writing back. Third, the Orient is not the only Other. Mohanty's "average third world woman" rendered "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is a gendered cognate; Hall's Rest covers more than the Orient and is "enormously" internally differentiated (Hall, 1992: 280); Fanon's white-eye scene racialises a Black body in parallel (Fanon, 1967: 323-5).

The lecture itself provides the durable application. Slide 12 names the categories the Orientalist discourse still produces today: Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia". Slide 16 names the stereotype-set: the Orient as timeless, mysterious, feminine-passive-luxurious, immoral-decadent. The slide-13 "Tales of the Arabian Nights" image is the visual instance. Slide 18 records the political consequence: the discourse functioned to "legitimate French and British colonialism", and Said's quotation pinpoints the mechanism, the "corporate institution" through which European culture "was able to manage, even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9). The Hall reading supplies the structural form: the Rest, like the Orient, is produced through homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation and naturalisation (Hall, 1992: 280).

Said's claim is therefore right and durable. The Orient is an idea, ideas have histories, and the Orient's history is part of Europe's history of constructing itself through Others. The contemporary reader takes the claim, holds it alongside Hall's structural grammar of the West and the Rest (Hall, 1992: 277-80), alongside Mohanty's gender inflection (Mohanty, 1984: 261-2), alongside Fanon's racial embodiment (Fanon, 1967: 323-5), alongside Spivak's question of who can speak (slide 30), and reads the world with sharper instruments than realist commonsense supplies. The Orient is a Western invention; whether the invention can be unmade is the project of postcolonial theory.

### Mock 2, Critically assess Said's argument in *Orientalism* in light of how the L9 lecture itself frames contemporary Orientalist effects.

Said's *Orientalism* (1978) argued that Western representations of the Orient have, since the late eighteenth century, formed a discourse, the "corporate institution for dealing with the Orient, dealing with it by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it" (Said, 1978: 25). I assess this argument using the L9 lecture's own list of contemporary Orientalist effects and the three qualifications Said himself enters in the Ashcroft excerpt.

The lecture frames the durability of Said's argument explicitly on slide 12. The "Orient" is a construction, "a legacy and a result of ways of thinking, acting, studying, trading, and dominating which still have effects today in how people in the West think about and act about people in that region", and the contemporary categories the discourse organises are Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, and "Arabia". This is the lecture's own claim that the Orientalism Said described in 1978 still has effects in the present. Slide 16 names the stereotype-set the discourse continues to produce: Orient as timeless, Orient as mysterious, Orient as feminine-passive-luxurious, Orient as immoral-decadent. The slide-13 "Tales of the Arabian Nights" image is the visual instance.

Said's claim that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89) describes the structural asymmetry the slide-12 categories enact: the West "thinks about" and "acts about" the region; the region is reported on, studied, mapped, intervened in, but rarely placed as the agent of its own representation. Said's stronger claim, that Orientalism functioned as the "enormously systematic discipline by which European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively during the post-Enlightenment period" (Said, 1978: 88-9), describes the institutional reach. Slide 18 records the political consequence: "All the above legitimate French and British colonialism", and the lists on slides 5 and 6 supply the actual colonised territories: India and Pakistan, Iraq and Palestine under British administration; Algeria, Morocco, Syria, Lebanon under French.

Three Said-internal qualifications need entry. First, the Orient is not nothing. "It would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6); "there were, and are, cultures and nations whose location is in the East, and their lives, histories, and customs have a brute reality obviously greater than anything that could be said about them in the West" (Said, 1978: 25-6). The argument is about the discourse, not about whether the region exists. Second, the discourse is internally variegated: Orientalism "is produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power" (Said, 1978: 26-7), and Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies to the Orientalist canon too. Third, the discourse is not only descriptive but productive: Orientalism "is, rather than expresses, a certain will or intention to understand, in some cases to control, manipulate, even to incorporate, what is a manifestly different (or alternative and novel) world" (Said, 1978: 26).

Two supplementations from the L9 reading set strengthen Said. Mohanty's parallel critique on the gender axis shows that the discourse Said describes has a feminine register: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) against the third-world-woman figure who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Fanon's white-eye scene shows what happens when the discourse is enforced on a colonised body: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325).

The L9 verdict on Said is therefore clear and durable. The slide-12 categories show that the discourse Said diagnosed continues to organise Western "thinking about and acting about" the Middle East and adjacent regions. Said's own qualifications, the Orient is not nothing, the discourse is internally variegated, the discourse is productive not just descriptive, keep the argument from becoming a conspiracy theory. Mohanty and Fanon supply the gender and racial supplementations. The Saidian diagnosis, as read in L9, is the indispensable starting point for any account of contemporary Orientalist effects.

### Mock 3, What does Fanon mean by the "white gaze", and why does it produce a doubled consciousness rather than simply an external constraint?

This is a two-part question. I take the parts in order from *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) and connect the analysis to Du Bois, Sartre and Hall.

On the first part, the white gaze is Fanon's name for the perceptual regime through which the colonial discourse of race lands on a particular Black body. His paradigmatic scene is the railway carriage: a white child cries, "Look, a Negro!… Mama, see the Negro! I'm frightened!" (Fanon, 1967: 323). Fanon tries to laugh: "I made up my mind to laugh myself to tears, but laughter had become impossible" (Fanon, 1967: 323). The cry is not merely insult; it is interpellation that fixes Fanon into a pre-existing category. The white gaze is what Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest does to a body: the structural binaries (rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body) are not abstract but deployed by an actual white child on an actual Black man, and his freedom to be anything other than "the Negro" collapses.

Three features specify the gaze. First, it is unidirectional: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Second, it produces its object: "My body was given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored… the corporeal schema crumbled, its place taken by a racial epidermal schema" (Fanon, 1967: 324). Before the gaze, the Black body had a normal self-relation; under it, an alien self-image replaces it. Third, the gaze is asymmetric: whiteness is invisible to itself (Hall's universalist West); blackness is the entire content of how the Black subject is seen.

On the second part, why does this produce doubling rather than external constraint? An external constraint would say: I am free internally; I am only blocked externally. Fanon's analysis is sharper. The white gaze does not just block; it inhabits. The colonised subject becomes both subject (still thinking, feeling, suffering) and object (the dissected Negro) simultaneously, and these are not separable. "A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The Black man cannot simply will himself into being a man; he must also struggle against the gaze's interior occupation of his self-perception. He is alienated from his own body, which has been recoded.

This doubling has an explicit comparative cue in the L9 slides themselves. Slide 25, listing "four crucial points" from the Fanon reading (being an object; being a subject; the denial of subjectivity to people who have been colonized), instructs the reader: "Think about Du Bois and also Foucault, perhaps". The slide's own bracket flags the parallel: Du Bois's account of looking at oneself through the eyes of others, and Foucault's account of disciplinary subject-formation, are the comparative anchors the lecture supplies for Fanon's analysis. Slide 24 supplies the other comparative anchor: Fanon was "influenced by philosophy, most notably existentialism (such as that of Sartre)". The Sartrean gaze (le regard) is the philosophical source; Fanon's contribution is to show that under colonialism the gaze is unequally distributed, the white world is "the only honourable one" and bars the colonised "from all participation" (Fanon, 1967: 324).

Why does the distinction between doubling and constraint matter? Because the response differs. To constraint, the response is to remove the obstacle. To doubling, the response must be more profound: to remake the relation between the subject and the racialised self-image. "Since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The making-known is not the removal of an external block; it is the active creation of a new self that the doubled colonial subject could not, by itself, have made. Slide 24 records that Fanon went on to write *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), where this becomes the revolutionary anti-colonial subject.

The L9 conclusion (slide 30) connects this to Spivak's question of who can speak for whom. The Fanonian problem of doubling becomes the Spivakian problem of voice: how can a voice formed under the white gaze be heard on terms other than the gaze's? The two questions sit at the end of the lecture as the unresolved task that postcolonial theory inherits.

In summary, the white gaze lands the colonial discursive order on a Black body (Fanon, 1967: 323-5); it produces doubling rather than mere constraint because it inhabits the subject's own self-image (Fanon, 1967: 324); the comparative anchors the lecture provides are Du Bois on double consciousness and Sartre on the gaze (slides 24-25); and the response is the active production, through struggle, of a new subjectivity that no longer has to see itself through the gaze.

### Mock 4, Compare Said's Orientalism with Mohanty's "Third World Woman".

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and Chandra Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* (1988) both diagnose a homogenised non-Western Other that says more about its maker than its referent. They differ in axis, archive and political target. I compare them along these dimensions and assess what is gained by reading them together.

On axis, Said works the geographical-civilisational divide. The Orient is the East, primarily the Arab and Islamic Middle East, as seen by the West. Mohanty works gender within the broader colonial relation. The Third World Woman is the figure produced by Western scholarship and Western feminism about women in the periphery. The two binaries intersect: the veiled Muslim woman is at once Orientalist and gendered-Orientalist.

On archive, Said reads the nineteenth-century European Orientalist canon, Sacy, Renan, Lane, Burton, Flaubert, Nerval, supplemented by twentieth-century Anglo-American area studies. Mohanty reads late-twentieth-century Anglophone Western feminist scholarship, Zed Books, "women in development" anthologies, ethnographic accounts of "non-Western women." Said is reading two centuries of imperial culture; Mohanty is reading two decades of Western feminist scholarship that was trying to be progressive. Said's enemies are Orientalist; Mohanty's are well-meaning Western feminists. The analytic operation is similar.

On theoretical apparatus, Said draws Foucault's notion of discourse to explain how Orientalism functions as a "corporate institution… dealing with [the Orient] by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it, ruling over it" (Said, 1978: 25). Mohanty frames the Third World Woman as "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Both use the Foucauldian move that discourse produces its object. But Mohanty foregrounds who speaks for whom inside progressive scholarship in a way Said does not.

On political target, Said's enemy is Western imperial culture; the implication is decolonisation, political and intellectual. Mohanty's enemy is Western feminist universalism that subordinates Global South women under the figure of the helpless brown woman who needs saving. The "average third world woman" leads "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized etc.)" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Her political project is global feminist solidarity that respects difference without exoticising it.

Three things are gained by reading them together. First, gendered specification. Said's archive is mostly male and treats gendered tropes (the feminised Orient, the harem fantasy) glancingly. Mohanty supplies the gender dimension. The Third World Woman is what Said's Orient looks like in its feminine register: a justification-figure for Western humanitarian intervention, white-saviour NGO campaigns, and feminist universalism. Second, the intra-progressive critique. Said critiques empire; Mohanty critiques solidarity-with-the-Third-World that does not check its premises. Third, the methodological injunction to specificity. "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). This becomes a general principle for any analysis of subordination.

Tensions are real. Said sometimes risks treating European culture as monolithic; Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies. Mohanty also risks homogenising Western feminism, though her targeted critique of "the assumption of women as an already constituted, coherent group with identical interests and desires" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is itself an explicit warning against homogenisation.

The L9 slides make the convergence visible. Slide 16's list of Orientalist stereotypes, the Orient as "feminine, passive, luxurious" and as "immoral, decadent", shows that the discourse Said diagnoses already has a feminine register. Mohanty's slides 19-23 show how that feminine register is operationalised in self-consciously progressive Western feminist scholarship: the "average third world woman" is rendered as Said's feminine-Orient figure made into a sociological subject. The slide-12 categories (Iraq, the boundaries of the EU, immigration, Islam, "Arabia") are the territories where the Saidian discourse continues to organise Western thought; the slide-21 Western woman with her book and her coffee, in implicit contrast to the slide-20 "average third world woman" carrying wood through water, is what the same discourse looks like at the level of gendered self-representation.

In conclusion, Said and Mohanty are complementary diagnosticians of the constructed non-Western Other, Said on the civilisational axis (slides 10-18), Mohanty on the gender axis (slides 19-23). They share an analytic move (discourse produces its object: Said, 1978: 25 and Mohanty, 1984: 261) and an ethical injunction (refuse the homogenising figure: Said, 1978: 88-9 and Mohanty, 1984: 262). Read together, they give the L9 reader the conceptual instruments to dismantle both the imperial discourse and the well-meaning Western feminism whose solidarity reproduces the structure it opposes.

The reader who applies the L9 framework refuses both the purely cultural reading of postcolonial theory and the purely economic reading of colonialism. The West is a configuration in which capitalism, colonialism, the discourse of modernity and the binary of West and Rest are mutually constitutive.

In conclusion, L9 treats the West-capitalism nexus as inseparable. The West is "developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277) as a single configuration; capitalism and colonialism are directly linked (slide 7); the global texture of capital accumulation runs through Africa and Asia from the 1500s (slide 4); Orientalist knowledge and imperial trade are part of the same institution (Said, 1978: 25); and modernity's birth at 1492 is the birth of a centre-periphery world-system (Dussel, 1993: 65). The lecture does not produce a stand-alone theory of capitalism, but it refuses to let capitalism be analysed separately from the discourse of the West.

In conclusion, "post-colonial" in the L9 framework is a theoretical stance on the continuing presence of colonial structures, not a date. The position is supported by every author the lecture quotes. The student writing the exam must signal this clearly: "post-colonial" is not "after-colonial" but "still inside-colonial-aftermath".

West Europeans often regarded Eastern Europeans as 'barbaric', and, throughout the West, western women were represented as inferior to western men" (Hall, 1992: 280). The West is therefore not one thing, and the Othering operation does not stop at the West's geographical edge.

Hall does the same for the Rest. "The same necessary simplification is true of my references to 'the Rest'. This term also covers enormous historical, cultural and economic distinctions, for example, between the Middle East, the Far East, Africa, Latin America, indigenous North America and Australasia. It can equally encompass the simple societies of some North American Indians and the developed civilizations of China, Egypt or Islam" (Hall, 1992: 280). Hall then converts the warning into a methodological point: "we can actually use this simplification to make a point about discourse. For simplification is precisely what this discourse itself does. It represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West). And it asserts that these different cultures are united by one thing: the fact that they are all different from the Rest" (Hall, 1992: 280). The binary is destructive because it simplifies; the critique must therefore refuse the simplification on both sides.

Said handles internal heterogeneity in three moves. First, he warns that "it would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6); there are "cultures and nations whose location is in the East" with "a brute reality obviously greater than anything that could be said about them in the West" (Said, 1978: 25-6). The Orient is internally varied; Orientalism is the discourse that homogenises it. Second, Said notes that the American understanding of the Orient is "considerably less dense" than the European, and that "our recent Japanese, Korean, and Indochinese adventures ought now to be creating a more sober, more realistic 'Oriental' awareness" (Said, 1978: 24). Orientalism is not uniform across the West. Third, Said insists that Orientalism "is produced and exists in an uneven exchange with various kinds of power, … power political … power intellectual … power cultural … power moral" (Said, 1978: 26-7); the discourse is internally complex.

Fanon handles internal heterogeneity through specificity. The white-eye scene (Fanon, 1967: 323) is a particular incident in a particular place, not a universal racial situation. Slide 24 records Fanon's specific biography: born in the French Antilles in 1925, educated in Martinique and Paris, doctor and psychiatrist, influenced by Sartrean existentialism. The post-colonial subject is not generic; it is positioned by a specific colonial history. Slide 26's note that "as with Du Bois, the language used by Fanon, reflects the culture/society of the time" registers that the post-colonial subject is historically located.

Mohanty's whole argument is about internal heterogeneity within "Third World women". She explicitly challenges "the assumption of women as an already constituted, coherent group with identical interests and desires, regardless of class, ethnic or racial location or contradictions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), and converts this into a methodological injunction: "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Mohanty's intervention is structurally a defence of heterogeneity against feminist universalism.

Dussel handles internal heterogeneity within the West by foregrounding Spain and Portugal. The standard genealogy of modernity, Dussel argues, occludes "the role of Spain and Portugal in the formation of the modern world system from the late fifteenth to the mid-seventeenth centuries" (Dussel, 1993: 65). The lecture's slides 5 and 6 list British and French colonies separately, and slide 4 foregrounds Portuguese trade circuits; the lecture refuses to treat European colonialism as one undifferentiated phenomenon.

The L9 reading's handling of heterogeneity has consequences for the binary itself. Hall's explicit statement is that the binary's "destructive" character comes from "simplification" itself (Hall, 1992: 280). The post-colonial critique of the binary therefore must refuse the simplification in both directions. A critique that treats the West as monolithic, or the Rest as monolithic, reproduces the destructive operation it claims to oppose. The L9 reading is unanimous on this point, even when its central diagnostic uses binary terms for clarity.

In conclusion, the L9 readings handle internal heterogeneity through Hall's explicit double qualification on page 280, Said's insistence on the Orient's brute reality and on the unevenness of the Orientalist exchange, Fanon's specificity, Mohanty's anti-universalist methodology and Dussel's foregrounding of Spain and Portugal. The binary is a diagnostic frame, not an ontological claim. The student writing the exam must hold the binary and the qualifications together.
