# L09, Post-Colonial Theory

## 1. Core Argument

Post-colonial theory insists that colonialism is not a regrettable side-event in the rise of European modernity but its constitutive condition. My core argument runs through four moves. First, colonialism has three intertwined faces, settlement (the displacement of indigenous populations by the colonising power), economic exploitation (the appropriation of land, labour and resources tied tightly to the rise of capitalism) and unequal power relations (Lecture 9 slides). Howard French, in *Born in Blackness*, contends that we must not "ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that the point is not to "add Africa in" but to "invert the current view" (French, 2021, [VERIFY p.?]); the Portuguese trade of Indian cotton for African gold to mint European coinage in the 1500s makes the global texture of modernity inescapable. Second, the West is constituted discursively against the Rest. Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works through binary oppositions, "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277), and these binaries are not descriptions but hierarchies which the discourse "simplifies" (Hall, 1992: 280) into a single dichotomy. Third, Said's account of Orientalism shows that "the Orient is not only adjacent to Europe; it is also the place of Europe's greatest and richest and oldest colonies… and one of its deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87) and that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89). Following Foucault, Said treats Orientalism as a discourse that "manage[s], and even produce[s], the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). Fourth, the colonised are denied subjectivity. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* registers the white child's hail, "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), that fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" extends this, the figure is "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), not an empirical group, and Spivak's question of subaltern speech asks whether the oppressed can articulate themselves outside the categories of the coloniser. Enrique Dussel's intervention deepens these arguments: modernity is "a European phenomenon, but one constituted in a dialectical relation with a non-European alterity that is its ultimate content" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Post-colonial theory, then, is a critique of modernity's myth of self-origination, of its binaries, of its epistemic violence, and of its denial of voice to those it makes peripheral.

## 2. Thinkers and Concepts

- **Edward Said (1935-2003)**, Born in Jerusalem under the British Mandate, raised in Egypt, taught in New York. *Orientalism* (1978). Key concepts: Orientalism, the Orient as Europe's contrasting Other, false binary divisions, discourse/power following Foucault, "flexible positional superiority" (Said, 1978: 26), the imperial archive.
- **Stuart Hall (1932-2014)**, Jamaican-British cultural theorist. *Formations of Modernity* (1992). Key concepts: the West and the Rest, the West as historical not geographical construct, discourse as "system of representation" (Hall, 1992: 277), binary oppositions, internal others (Jews, Eastern Europeans, women).
- **Frantz Fanon (1925-1961)**, Born in Martinique, French Antilles; psychiatrist, fought in French resistance and later in Algerian liberation. *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961). Key concepts: object/subject dialectic, the colonial gaze, the denial of subjectivity, the colonial wound, decolonisation as struggle for voice.
- **Chandra Mohanty (1955–)**, Born in Mumbai; teaches at Syracuse. *Under Western Eyes* (1988). Key concepts: the third-world woman, discursive colonisation, women as "the other of the other," refusal of universal sisterhood.
- **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (1942–)**, Indian-American literary theorist. *Can the Subaltern Speak?* (1988). Key concepts: the subaltern, epistemic violence, strategic essentialism, the impossibility of pure recovered voice.
- **Howard French**, *Born in Blackness* (2021). Argues Africa is central to making modern world; inversion of perspective.
- **Enrique Dussel (1934-2023)**, Argentine-Mexican liberation philosopher. *Eurocentrism and Modernity* (1993). Key concepts: the myth of modernity, the colonial wound, the colonial difference, trans-modernity, the fallacy of developmentalism, 1492 as birth of modernity.
- **Raewyn Connell**, Australian sociologist. *Southern Theory* (2007). Key concepts: Southern theory, the metropole/periphery in sociology, the imperial archive of social science, decolonising knowledge.
- **Shmuel Eisenstadt**, Israeli sociologist. *Multiple Modernities* (2000). Key concepts: multiple modernities, civilisational variants, refusal of unilinear convergence.
- **Mohandas Gandhi (1869-1948)**, *Hind Swaraj* (1909). Key concepts: appropriate technology, swadeshi, technology as ethical practice, critique of industrial civilisation, the spinning wheel as counter-symbol.
- **Homi Bhabha**, Indian-British theorist. *The Location of Culture* (1994). Key concepts: hybridity, ambivalence, mimicry, the third space.
- **Paul Gilroy**, Black British theorist. *The Black Atlantic* (1993), *After Empire* (2004). Key concepts: planetary humanism, conviviality, post-colonial melancholia.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

- Treating "post-colonial" as merely "after colonialism" temporally, it is rather a theoretical stance on the continuing presence of colonial structures, including in formally independent states.
- Reducing Said to "the West invented the East", Said is careful that "the Orient is not an inert fact of nature" but also that "it would be wrong to conclude that the Orient was essentially an idea, or a creation with no corresponding reality" (Said, 1978: 25-6). The Orient exists; Orientalism is the discourse about it.
- Treating Hall's binaries as descriptive of two real groups, they are productive fictions that organise power.
- Treating the subaltern as just "the poor", Spivak's subaltern is the structurally voiceless, particularly the subaltern woman.
- Treating Southern theory as anti-Western, Connell wants knowledge from the South taken seriously, not Western theory expelled.
- Treating multiple modernities as cultural relativism, Eisenstadt holds that modernity is real and global, but its institutional forms are plural.
- Treating Gandhi as anti-technology, he was anti-industrial, not anti-tool; the *charkha* is technology, ethically chosen.
- Forgetting that post-colonial theory is itself contested by Marxist critics (Ahmad, Dirlik) who argue it culturalises what is fundamentally about capital accumulation.
- Forgetting Dussel's point that modernity has *two* faces: an emancipatory concept and a sacrificial myth, critique must hold both.

## 7. Past-Year Exam Answers

### 2025 Q11, How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between 'the West and the Rest'?

The distinction between the West and the Rest is, on the surface, a piece of casual geography. Post-colonial theorists from Hall onwards have argued that it is in fact a discursive machine, and they have attacked it on four fronts: showing that the West is a historical not a geographical entity; revealing the binary logic that sustains it; exposing the power relations it conceals; and producing alternative cartographies that displace it.

The first move is Hall's. In *Formations of Modernity*, Stuart Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277). Geographically, Hall notes, Japan is east but counts as West; eastern Europe is in Europe but is unsure if it is in the West; Latin America is in the western hemisphere but is filed under the Third World. "The West is as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, 1992: 276). The West is a historical construct, a name for "a society that is developed, industrialized, urbanized, capitalist, secular, and modern" (Hall, 1992: 277), and therefore something that was made, can be unmade, and could have been otherwise.

The second move is to expose the binaries. Hall lays out the oppositional grammar: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (lecture slide 9; Hall, 1992: 277). Each pair is a hierarchy, not just a difference. The West is the positive term; the Rest is what the West is not. Said's account of Orientalism extends this to the Eastern variant: "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88). The Orient is timeless, mysterious, feminine, decadent. Hall's key sentence is that this discourse "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, 1992: 280). The discourse collapses internal heterogeneity on both sides.

The third move is to expose power. "The relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89). For Said, drawing on Foucault, the West/Rest discourse is not innocent description; it is "Orientalism as a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25). The British Museum's Benin bronzes, the East India Company's tax registers, the colonial census of India, the anthropological monograph on "savage" societies, all are technologies through which the West produced, managed and ruled the Rest. Dussel sharpens the point: the West is not just a name for one civilisation among others but for the self-appointed centre of "World History," with the periphery serving as raw material for its self-image (Dussel, 1993: 65).

The fourth move is to displace the cartography. Howard French inverts the standard map by showing that "the Portuguese use cotton from India to buy gold in Africa to use as coinage in Europe (in the 1500s!)" (lecture slide 4), there is no Europe without Africa and Asia. The Gall-Peters and "World Turned Upside Down" projections shown in the lecture make the Mercator's distortion visible. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" challenges the West/Rest binary inside feminism, showing how Western women's self-image as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) depends on a fabricated third-world woman. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity shows what the binary feels like from inside: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325).

Concrete contemporary instances make the challenge urgent. Western media in 2022 framed Ukraine as "civilised, European" against Russia as "barbaric," and contrasted blue-eyed Ukrainian refugees with "less relatable" Iraqi and Afghan ones, Hall's binaries in real time. Vaccine apartheid in COVID showed the West/Rest distinction governing access to life itself. Rhodes Must Fall and "decolonise the curriculum" target the discursive machinery in the universities. The Bandung Conference of 1955 is the classic counter-cartography from below, and the African Union's resistance to IMF structural adjustment is its contemporary descendant.

A balanced assessment must acknowledge that these challenges are not symmetrical successes. The Mercator projection still hangs on classroom walls. The British Museum still holds the bronzes. "Civilised vs barbaric" still organises international politics. But post-colonial theorists have made the West/Rest distinction visible as a distinction, they have denaturalised it. We can no longer treat it as innocent geography. Hall, Said, Fanon, Mohanty and Dussel together have replaced a description with an indictment.

### 2025 Q13, Outline and assess if Southern theory addresses inequalities in the Global South.

Southern theory, in Raewyn Connell's formulation, is a critical project that aims to displace the metropolitan dominance of Northern sociological theory and to take seriously the intellectual production of the Global South. I will outline the project's main claims, then assess how far it actually addresses the inequalities that scar the South itself.

The outline runs as follows. Connell argues that the canonical European tradition was generated in conditions of imperial dominance and reflects metropolitan preoccupations. Marx's account of capitalism takes the British factory as its paradigm; Durkheim's anomie diagnoses a French nineteenth-century crisis; Weber's rationalisation thesis is European to its core. Yet these are taught as universal sociology. Meanwhile, theories generated in the South, Latin American dependency theory (Prebisch, Cardoso, Frank), African post-colonial state theory (Mahmood Mamdani, Achille Mbembe), Indian subaltern studies (Ranajit Guha, Partha Chatterjee), Aboriginal Australian sovereignty thinkers, Islamic political economy, are filed under "area studies" or "anthropology" rather than read as theory. Southern theory demands intellectual equality. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis is a close ally, arguing that "modernity" is plural and that civilisational matrices shape different modernity paths. Dussel's trans-modernity, Gandhi's appropriate technology, the Bandung Conference of 1955 and the African Union's ongoing critique of IMF conditionality are practical companions.

The assessment of whether Southern theory addresses inequalities *within* the Global South is more ambivalent. There are five reasons to be hopeful and five to be cautious.

On the optimistic side: first, Southern theory enables Southern intellectuals to take their own concerns as theoretical starting points rather than imported ones. The Grameen Bank's microfinance, Brazilian participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, the Indian Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act all emerged from Southern intellectual cultures that took the rural poor as central subjects of theory. Second, it provides language for analysing intra-Southern phenomena, Chinese investment in Africa, Indian capital in Bangladesh, Brazilian agribusiness in Paraguay, that Northern theory could only call "development." Third, dependency theory, a paradigmatic Southern contribution, gave Latin Americans the analytical tools to explain why decades of post-independence growth still left them peripheral. Fourth, multiple modernities pluralises political imagination: Iranian modernity, Japanese modernity, Indian modernity become possible objects rather than failures of Northern modernity. Fifth, Southern theory animates the decolonise-the-curriculum movement that is reshaping universities from Cape Town to Cambridge, opening curricula to Black, indigenous and Southern voices.

On the cautious side: first, "the Global South" is itself a homogenising category. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies: the lived realities of São Paulo's elites and Bolivian indigenous women are not the same. Second, much Southern theory is produced in English in Northern universities, Spivak at Columbia, Bhabha at Harvard, Mbembe at Duke, and travels through Northern publishing infrastructures. The Indian peasant whose voice subaltern studies wanted to recover does not write Routledge books. Third, Southern theory can be co-opted by nationalist authoritarianism. Hindu nationalism under Modi mobilises "decolonisation" to attack Muslims and Dalits, weaponising the language of intellectual decolonisation for sectarian ends. Fourth, the new Southern capitalisms, China, India, Brazil, reproduce the extractive patterns of Northern colonialism inside the South. The Belt and Road Initiative's debt-trap critique echoes 1970s dependency theory but with Beijing as creditor. Southern theory has been slow to develop the conceptual tools to analyse this. Fifth, Marxist critics like Vivek Chibber argue that Southern theory's emphasis on cultural difference obscures capital's universal logic and disarms class politics. The Bangladeshi garment worker, on this reading, shares more with the Manchester mill worker of 1850 than with the Dhaka professor who theorises her.

The assessment, then, is mixed. Southern theory is indispensable for putting Northern theory in its place and for legitimating Southern intellectual production. It has produced concrete political and economic experiments. But it does not, by itself, address inequalities within the South. The Adivasi displaced by a dam in Madhya Pradesh, the rural woman in northern Nigeria denied schooling, the Bolivian indigenous communities whose lithium feeds Northern batteries, their inequalities are produced both by global structures and by Southern elites. Southern theory has a tendency to focus on the North-South axis and to bracket the class, caste, gender and ethnic stratifications that organise Southern societies. A truly decolonial Southern theory needs Mohanty's intersectional warning alongside Connell's geographical one. It needs Dussel's trans-modernity, which explicitly refuses to make the South a mirror image of the North. Whether such a Southern theory exists fully in 2026 is debatable. The seeds are present in subaltern studies, in liberation theology, in indigenous jurisprudence, in feminist work like Mohanty's. But the project remains unfinished, and the global academy's structural inequalities still skew its development.

### 2025 Q15, Critically discuss two aspects of the Gandhian view on technology.

Gandhi's view of technology, set out most fully in *Hind Swaraj* (1909) and lived out across forty years of political practice, is one of the most distinctive non-Western contributions to the philosophy of modernity. I will discuss two aspects in particular: technology as ethical and embodied practice rather than instrument, and technology as a question of scale and dignity, captured in the icon of the *charkha* (spinning wheel). I will then assess each critically.

The first aspect is technology as ethical practice. For Western modernity from Bacon onwards, technology is instrumental: a means to a separately chosen end. The plough, the steam engine, the algorithm, these are neutral tools, and whatever moral content they have lies in how humans use them. Gandhi rejects this view. Technology, for him, embeds an entire ethical world. The factory does not just produce cloth; it produces a particular kind of human being, one disciplined by the clock, alienated from the rhythms of nature, separated from the product of their labour. Industrial technology, even when used "for good," carries with it a metaphysical commitment to speed, mastery, and extraction. The hand-loom, by contrast, embeds a different ethical world: it links body, breath and material, it preserves the dignity of the labourer, it situates the worker in a community of craft. Gandhi's *Hind Swaraj* therefore opposes railways, machinery and mass production not because he believes humans should be unable to weave or travel but because the particular technologies of British industrial modernity carry the colonial relation inside them. The cotton mills of Manchester ran on cotton picked by enslaved labour in the Americas and woven into cloth that destroyed the Bengali handloom industry. Gandhi's *swadeshi* (self-reliance) movement asked Indians to spin their own thread, weave their own cloth, and thereby refuse the embedded ethics of the imperial supply chain.

Critically, this aspect has both strengths and weaknesses. Its strength is that it anticipates by half a century what STS scholarship and Latour have argued more recently: that technologies are never neutral, that they carry social relations in their design. The smartphone is not just a tool; it is a particular form of attention, sociality and surveillance. The drone is not just a flying camera; it is a particular form of warfare. Gandhi's intuition is now mainstream in the philosophy of technology. The weakness is that the ethical-embedment argument can slide into nostalgia. The handloom is not automatically more ethical than the power loom; village India in 1909 was not a paradise of dignified craft but a caste-stratified, patriarchal hierarchy in which Dalit weavers were despised. B. R. Ambedkar's critique of Gandhi runs hard here: the village is not the answer for those whom the village oppressed.

The second aspect is technology as a question of scale and dignity. Gandhi famously contrasted the *charkha* (spinning wheel) with the steel plant. Nehru, the modernising prime minister, called the great dams and steel plants "temples of modern India." Gandhi answered with the spinning wheel: small, local, controllable, distributed, intelligible. Technology, for Gandhi, should be on a human scale, small enough that the person using it understands it, owns it, and is not owned by it. This anticipates E. F. Schumacher's "appropriate technology" (or "intermediate technology") movement of the 1970s, and the contemporary critiques of platform capitalism. The Belt and Road Initiative, large-scale agribusiness in Brazil, hydroelectric mega-projects in India that displace millions, all are instances of the Nehruvian, not Gandhian, road. The Grameen Bank's microfinance, locally rooted credit cooperatives, and small solar systems for off-grid villages are nearer the Gandhian sensibility.

Critically, the scale-and-dignity argument has its own strengths and weaknesses. Its strength is its prophetic accuracy. The ecological costs of mega-scale technology, the Amazon deforestation crisis, climate breakdown, the resource demands of digital infrastructure, vindicate Gandhi's warning that mastery has its price. His critique of consumerism, "the world has enough for everyone's need but not for everyone's greed", echoes through every contemporary degrowth movement. Its weakness is that "appropriate technology" can become an excuse for under-investing in the South. Vaccine apartheid during COVID illustrated this: when the Global South needed mRNA technology fast, "appropriate technology" was no answer. Insulin, antiretrovirals, fertilisers and electricity grids cannot all be made on a spinning wheel. Decolonising technology means having access to the most advanced tools, not retreating from them. The Hindu-nationalist invocation of "Indian wisdom" against "Western science", which has, under Modi, included claims about ancient Indian aviation and plastic surgery, shows how Gandhian critique can be debased.

The two aspects together give us a partial but powerful view. Gandhi's ethical and scalar critique of technology was an early and prescient non-Western philosophy of modernity, and it continues to animate appropriate-technology, degrowth, food sovereignty and digital-commons movements globally. But the critique needs to be held alongside the Ambedkarite warning that not every Gandhian village is a paradise, and the developmentalist warning that some Western technologies, vaccines, antibiotics, contraceptives, semiconductors, are non-negotiable for Southern dignity. The strongest reading is one that takes Gandhi as offering critical *questions* about technology, about what it does to bodies, communities, and ecologies, rather than fixed answers about which technologies to use.

### 2024 Q10, What is postcolonial theory? In what ways is it a critique of modernity?

Postcolonial theory is the body of work that emerged from the late 1970s, Said, Spivak, Bhabha, Hall, Fanon read retrospectively, Mohanty, Connell, Dussel, which insists that European colonialism is not an external chapter in the story of modernity but its inner architecture. I will argue that postcolonial theory critiques modernity on four registers: historical, discursive, subjective and epistemological.

Historically, postcolonial theory rewrites the periodisation of modernity. The conventional sociological story, feudalism breaks down, the Reformation, the Enlightenment, industrial capitalism, runs internally to Europe. Dussel, by contrast, claims that "1492 is the date of the 'birth' of modernity" and that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates; the 'periphery' that surrounds this center is consequently part of its self-definition" (Dussel, 1993: 65). Howard French's *Born in Blackness* makes the same move from a different angle: African gold, African labour and African slaves underwrite European capital accumulation from the 1500s onwards. The East India Company's revenue extraction in Bengal financed British industrialisation; the cotton processed in Manchester was grown by enslaved labour in the Americas. Stripping these connections out, what Hall calls the West's tendency to treat itself "as the result of forces largely internal to Europe's history and formation" (Hall, 1992: 278), is itself an ideological act.

Discursively, postcolonial theory critiques the binaries through which modernity tells its story. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest works by polarisation: "rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic" (Hall, 1992: 277). These are not descriptions but the simplifying grammar of a "system of representation" that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). Said's account of Orientalism develops the same critique on the Eastern axis: the Orient was "one of [Europe's] deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Modernity in this telling is not just a description of European societies; it is a comparative claim that requires a contrasting Other.

Subjectively, postcolonial theory tracks what modernity does to the colonised. Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity captures the moment when the modern liberal claim of universal personhood collides with race: the white child's call, "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), fixes him as object: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Modernity, which advertises itself as the kingdom of the autonomous subject, in fact distributes subjectivity unequally; the colonised is granted the body but not the mind, the spectacle but not the gaze. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" shows the same logic on the gender axis, the average third-world woman is rendered "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) so that Western women can appear as the modern subject.

Epistemologically, postcolonial theory attacks the production of knowledge itself. Said borrows Foucault to argue that "without examining Orientalism as a discourse one cannot possibly understand the enormously systematic discipline by which European culture was able to manage, and even produce, the Orient politically, sociologically, militarily, ideologically, scientifically, and imaginatively" (Said, 1978: 88-9). The imperial archive, the museum, the gazetteer, the colonial census, the anthropological monograph, is part of how modernity knows the world. Spivak's question of subaltern speech extends this critique into the present: even sympathetic Western scholarship risks epistemic violence when it claims to "recover" subaltern voices that can only speak through Western categories.

Examples make the critique concrete. The British Museum's continuing custody of the Benin bronzes, looted in 1897, is modernity's epistemic violence in material form. The Rhodes Must Fall campaign at the University of Cape Town and Oxford targets exactly this: the entanglement of imperial extraction and modern knowledge. Vaccine apartheid in COVID, where Global North states blocked the TRIPS waiver, demonstrates that the colonial difference still organises the global distribution of life chances. Western media commentary in early 2022 that contrasted "civilised" Ukrainian refugees with "barbaric" Middle Eastern ones reproduced Hall's binaries verbatim. Postcolonial theory does not deny that modernity emancipated some, Dussel concedes "the rational kernel of the universalist rationalism of the Enlightenment" (Dussel, 1993: 75), but it insists that this emancipation was paid for in genocides, expropriations and erasures whose ghosts continue to haunt the present. Its critique of modernity is therefore not nostalgic but trans-modern: it asks for a modernity that finally accounts for its full cost.

### 2024 Q11, What is a Southern theory? What are the limitations of the concept?

Southern theory, as Raewyn Connell formulates it in her 2007 book of the same name, is a project of provincialising Northern social theory. The argument has three steps. First, the canonical sociological tradition, Marx, Weber, Durkheim, the Chicago school, Parsons, Bourdieu, was produced in the imperial metropole and read the world through metropolitan eyes. Second, the periphery generated its own knowledge, Indian sociology, African ubuntu philosophy, Latin American dependency theory, Aboriginal jurisprudence, Islamic ethics, that has been systematically ignored or downgraded. Third, the task is not merely to "add" Southern thinkers to a Northern syllabus but to invert the centre-periphery relation so that the South becomes a site of theory production, not just a field of data extraction.

Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis is a close cousin. Modernity, he argues, is real and global but not unilinear: Japanese modernity, Indian modernity, Iranian modernity each combine industrialism, bureaucracy and mass politics with distinctive civilisational substrates. Dussel's trans-modernity pushes further, calling for "the co-realization of that which it is impossible for modernity to accomplish by itself: that is, of an incorporative solidarity… between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Gandhi on appropriate technology, the spinning wheel as alternative to the steel mill, and the Bandung Conference of 1955 are practical anticipations of Southern theory.

The concept does important work. It defamiliarises what Northern theorists treat as universal. Durkheim's account of the division of labour is plainly a description of nineteenth-century France presented as a general law of social evolution. Weber's "Protestant ethic" privileges one particular European trajectory. The teaching of "classical theory" in sociology departments around the world still tends to treat European white men as the source of theory and Africans, Indians and Latin Americans as the source of cases. Southern theory legitimates Indian sociologists like Veena Das, African theorists like Achille Mbembe, and Latin American liberation thinkers as theorists, not informants.

But the concept faces serious limitations. First, "the South" is itself a homogenising category. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies here: collapsing Brazil, Bangladesh, Botswana and Bolivia into a single epistemic location risks the very violence Southern theory protests. The cosmopolitan elites of São Paulo and the indigenous communities of the Amazon are both "Southern" in geography but their relation to global capital, knowledge production and modernity itself is vastly different. Second, the global-South/global-North geography has become unstable. China's Belt and Road Initiative, the rise of Indian capital, Brazilian agribusiness, these are Southern actors that reproduce Northern patterns of extraction. The South-South solidarity imagined at Bandung in 1955 is fractured by new sub-imperialisms. Third, there is a risk of nativism. The Modi government in India invokes the language of decolonisation while persecuting Muslims and Dalits; Hindu nationalism wears Southern theory's clothes for authoritarian ends. Fourth, Southern theory shares with Said's Orientalism the danger of inverted essentialism: if the West is described in monolithic terms, Cartesian, instrumental, dualist, then the South ends up being defined as everything the West is not, reinscribing the very binary Hall taught us to suspect. Fifth, Connell's project, like much Anglophone postcolonial theory, is produced in English-language Northern universities; the very dissemination of Southern theory depends on infrastructures of the North. Sixth, there are Marxist critics, Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber, who argue that focusing on the geography of theory occludes the more fundamental question of class and capital, which run across both hemispheres.

A defender of Southern theory can answer some of these. Connell does not claim that all knowledge from the South is liberatory or that all knowledge from the North is corrupt; she argues for symmetrical seriousness. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities does not collapse difference but proliferates it. Dussel's trans-modernity explicitly tries to avoid both the universalism of Northern theory and the relativism of pure Southern particularism. Concrete examples, the African Union's pushback against IMF structural adjustment, the Latin American "pink tide" governments' attempts to articulate post-neoliberal political economy, the Grameen Bank's microfinance model, show Southern theory generating practical political innovation. Yet the limitations are real. Southern theory works best when it is granular, when it talks about particular Southern thinkers, particular Southern struggles, particular Southern epistemic traditions, and weakest when it deploys "the South" as a homogeneous counter-pole to "the North." In that mode it risks repeating, with the signs reversed, the Eurocentrism it set out to displace.

### 2023 Q11, How have post-colonial theorists challenged the distinction between "the West and the Rest"?

This question is materially identical to 2025 Q11 above; I write a distinct answer here pivoting on the historical-archive dimension. Post-colonial theorists from the 1970s onwards have challenged the West/Rest distinction at three depths: as a discursive system (Hall, Said), as an experiential reality for the colonised (Fanon, Mohanty), and as a historical falsehood about who made the modern world (Dussel, French).

At the discursive depth, Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest demonstrates that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277). Hall identifies four functions of the concept: it classifies (West vs non-West), represents (a composite image), measures (a standard of comparison), and evaluates (developed = good = desirable). "In short, it functions as an ideology" (Hall, 1992: 277). The discourse "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). The binaries, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, are the operating code of the discourse. Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation in the Eastern direction. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). The point is structural: the West cannot be the West without the Rest because its identity is *constituted* by contrast.

At the experiential depth, Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* tells us what the West/Rest binary feels like inside a body. The white child's "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323) is the discourse hailing the colonised; Fanon is fixed, dissected, given back his body "sprawled out, distorted, recolored" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The West/Rest distinction is not just a sentence in a textbook; it is a wound on the flesh. Mohanty extends this analysis along the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities, and the freedom to make their own decisions" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) precisely by producing a "third world woman" who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The challenge is to refuse the binary in both its halves.

At the historical depth, Dussel and French rewrite the genealogy of modernity. Dussel argues that "modernity appears when Europe affirms itself as the 'center' of a World History that it inaugurates" (Dussel, 1993: 65) and that the Iberian conquest of the New World from 1492 is constitutive, not incidental. French's *Born in Blackness* makes Africa central: "He argues that we must not ignore the role of Africa in the development of modernity" and that "the point is not simply to 'add' Africa in but to invert the current view" (lecture slide 4). The standard narrative, Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution, that Habermas inherits from Hegel and that omits Spain, Portugal, the slave trade and the colonised periphery is not just incomplete; it is a Eurocentric distortion. The imperial archive, the museum, the gazetteer, the colonial map, is part of the discourse and part of the falsification.

Each register has its political form. Hall's discursive challenge animates the "decolonise the curriculum" movement, Why Is My Curriculum White?, and Rhodes Must Fall. Said's challenge animates museum repatriation debates: the British Museum's Benin bronzes, the Parthenon marbles, the Quai Branly's African collections. Fanon and Mohanty's experiential challenge animates Black Lives Matter and post-colonial feminism. Dussel and French's historical challenge animates the 1619 Project, the reparations debate and the reconsideration of British imperial history in school curricula. Each of these contemporary movements is, in the strict sense, a post-colonial challenge to the West/Rest binary in action.

A balanced conclusion notes the limits. The Mercator projection still organises classroom geography. The Bretton Woods institutions still organise global finance. Western media in 2022 reverted instantly to "civilised vs barbaric" framing for Ukraine. Vaccine apartheid showed that the discourse retains material force in life-and-death distribution. Yet the post-colonial intellectual achievement is real: the West/Rest binary can no longer be deployed innocently. Hall taught us its grammar, Said its archive, Fanon its embodiment, Mohanty its feminist version, Dussel its historical falsification, and French its missing African heart. The challenge is now to convert this denaturalisation into structural change.

### 2023 Q13, Critically analyse Southern Theory as an alternative to European Modernity. What are its limits?

European modernity, in its classical telling, is a story of self-generated progress, the Renaissance, Reformation, Enlightenment, industrial revolution, parliamentary democracy, that radiates outward from a small north-western corner of Europe to instruct the rest of humanity. Southern theory, as Connell articulates it, refuses this story. It is both a critique (European modernity is not what it says it is) and an alternative (the Global South has been producing modernity all along, on its own terms). I will analyse the alternative and then catalogue its limits.

The alternative comes in several voices. Connell's Southern theory itself argues that "Western theory ignores knowledge from the Global South" and demands that the sociological canon be re-read with this exclusion in mind. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis holds that modernity is real but plural: Japanese modernity, Indian modernity, Iranian modernity each combine institutional rationalisation with civilisational substrates that produce distinctive social forms. Gandhi on appropriate technology offers a normative alternative, the spinning wheel against the steel plant, swadeshi against industrial dependency. Dussel's trans-modernity offers a philosophical alternative, "the co-realization of that which it is impossible for modernity to accomplish by itself: that is, of an incorporative solidarity… between center/periphery, man/woman, different races, different ethnic groups, different classes, civilization/nature, Western culture/Third World cultures" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Howard French historicises the alternative: African gold and labour built European modernity, so the South was never the receiver but always a co-producer.

The strengths of this alternative are several. It punctures the self-congratulation of European modernity. It legitimates Southern intellectual labour. It explains why classical sociology never quite fits Southern societies, Durkheim's anomie, Weber's iron cage, even Marx's English working class are not the keys to understanding rural Bihar or the Bolivian altiplano. It generates political experimentation: the Bandung Conference of 1955, the African Union's resistance to IMF structural adjustment, the Latin American "pink tide," indigenous-led environmental movements in Ecuador and Bolivia. It opens space for non-Western traditions, Confucian, Islamic, Hindu, indigenous, to be considered as theoretical resources rather than ethnographic curiosities.

The limits, however, are real. First, "Southern theory" risks a new homogeneity. The South is enormous and internally fractured. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" applies here: collapsing the world's diversities into a single Southern voice repeats the simplifying violence that Hall's "the West and the Rest" performed in the other direction. Second, multiple modernities can become culturalist apology. Eisenstadt's framework risks treating every authoritarianism that wears local clothes as "another modernity." Hindu nationalism under Modi, Erdogan's Turkey, Putin's Russia, the Saudi monarchy: are these multiple modernities or are they ordinary authoritarianisms with local accents? The framework's relativism can disable critique.

Third, Southern theory can be co-opted. Modi's BJP uses the language of decolonisation to legitimate Hindu majoritarianism. The Chinese Communist Party uses Southern theory to defend its surveillance state as a non-Western modernity. Belt and Road infrastructure investment is framed as "South-South cooperation" even when it reproduces extractive patterns. Fourth, the alternative depends materially on Northern infrastructure. Connell's *Southern Theory* is published by Polity Press in Cambridge. The papers of Southern scholars are indexed in Northern citation databases. Even the critique of the metropole travels via the metropole. Fifth, Marxist critics, Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber, Neil Lazarus, argue that focusing on the geography of theory obscures the universal logic of capital. The Bangladeshi garment worker and the British call-centre worker face the same world market; treating their experiences as incommensurable Southern and Northern modernities risks disarming class solidarity.

Sixth, Southern theory tends to focus on the macro North-South relation and to neglect intra-Southern stratifications, caste, ethnicity, gender, urban-rural. Liberation theology and feminist Southern theorists like Maria Lugones and Veronica Gago do this better than Connell's original frame. Seventh, there is a danger of nostalgia. Gandhi on appropriate technology is brilliant but the village is not automatically good. Ambedkar's critique of Gandhi, that the village is the *site* of caste oppression, still stands. Eighth, the "alternative modernities" register can paper over how thoroughly globalised the South already is. The Indian middle class consumes the same Netflix shows, drives the same SUVs and chases the same MBA credentials as its Northern counterparts. Treating India as an "alternative modernity" can be a way of not noticing how Northern it already is.

A balanced conclusion: Southern theory is a necessary corrective to a Eurocentric sociology, and Eisenstadt, Dussel, Gandhi and Connell together open theoretical space that the discipline desperately needs. But Southern theory is not a finished alternative. It needs Mohanty's intersectional warning, Ambedkar's caste critique, Marxist class analysis and Bhabha's hybridity together to avoid becoming a new orthodoxy. The alternative to European modernity is not Southern modernity but trans-modernity in Dussel's sense, a global conversation in which the South finally counts as theory.

### 2023 Q14, How are non-western models proposing a different relationship between modernity and technology? Discuss with examples.

The dominant Western model of the modernity-technology relation, inherited from Bacon, Descartes and the Industrial Revolution, treats technology as the instrumental means by which humanity masters nature and accelerates history. Non-Western models challenge this in several distinct registers: ethical, scalar, ecological, and democratic. I will discuss three, Gandhi on appropriate technology, Chinese state technology, and indigenous and Latin American buen vivir, with examples, and then assess the broader question.

Gandhi on appropriate technology is the most theoretically developed non-Western alternative. *Hind Swaraj* (1909) argues that industrial technology embeds an entire ethical world: the factory produces alienated labour, the railway produces hurried lives, the steam engine produces colonial extraction. The *charkha* (spinning wheel) is the counter-symbol: small, local, controllable, dignifying. Swadeshi (self-reliance) movements in early twentieth-century India organised around hand-spun *khadi* cloth, both as economic resistance to the Lancashire cotton mills and as ethical practice. The point is not that Indians should be primitive but that the *choice* of technology is a moral choice about what kind of human being and what kind of community one wants to build. E. F. Schumacher's "Small is Beautiful" (1973) and the appropriate technology movement carry this forward; contemporary degrowth, food sovereignty (La Via Campesina) and digital commons movements are descendants.

Chinese state technology offers a very different non-Western alternative: not anti-industrial but state-directed and tied to a different political theory. The Belt and Road Initiative, the rapid build-out of solar and high-speed rail, and the social credit system together represent a model where technology is mobilised by a developmental state for collective goals rather than purely commercial ones. From a Western liberal perspective this is alarming; from a Southern theory perspective it complicates the binary, showing that technological modernity does not require Anglo-American liberal individualism. Example: China's installation of more solar capacity than the rest of the world combined, and the BRI's infrastructure investments across Asia and Africa, demonstrate a different state-technology relation. Critics, including post-colonial scholars, note that this is also a new sub-imperialism and that ethnic minorities (Uyghurs) face surveillance modernity at its sharpest. So this is not a liberatory alternative, but it is a non-Western one.

Indigenous and Latin American buen vivir (Quechua *sumak kawsay*, Aymara *suma qamaña*) offers a third model. Buen vivir is enshrined in the Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutions and proposes a relation to technology mediated by ecological and spiritual obligations to *Pachamama* (Mother Earth). Technologies that extract irreversibly from the earth, open-pit mining, lithium extraction for batteries, mega-dams, are evaluated negatively even when they produce growth. Indigenous-led environmental movements have stopped pipelines (Standing Rock in the US), forced consultation regimes on mining companies, and produced legal innovations like rights of nature. Example: Ecuador's 2008 constitution gave nature legal personhood; this is technological-modernity-rethought from the ground of indigenous cosmology.

Dussel's trans-modernity provides a philosophical frame for these alternatives. It is "the co-realization of that which it is impossible for modernity to accomplish by itself", political, economic, ecological, erotic, pedagogical and religious liberation across centre and periphery (Dussel, 1993: 76). Trans-modern technology is not anti-modern; it incorporates Northern technical knowledge but subordinates it to ethical, ecological and democratic norms developed elsewhere. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis frames the more general claim: there are many technology-modernity relations, and the unilinear Northern story is one option among several.

Critically, these alternatives have limits. Gandhi's appropriate technology cannot make insulin. Chinese state technology produces surveillance and Uyghur internment camps. Buen vivir constitutional provisions in Ecuador have been routinely overridden by extractive governments needing oil revenues. Hindu nationalism's invocation of "Indian science" includes embarrassing claims about ancient flying machines that should warn us about how easily decolonisation rhetoric can degrade into pseudoscience.

The serious question is whether these non-Western models offer ways to combine the genuine benefits of modern technology, vaccines, antibiotics, contraception, electricity, telecommunications, with a relationship to nature and community that is not extractive and not alienated. The answer in 2026 is partial yes. The Grameen Bank's microfinance, mobile banking innovations like M-Pesa in Kenya, the spread of solar in rural Africa via small distributed systems, India's Aadhaar-enabled welfare delivery (with all its problems), the indigenous-led "rights of nature" movement, and the BRICS Bank as alternative to the World Bank all gesture toward a different modernity-technology relation. None is sufficient on its own. Together they suggest that the Western instrumental model is not the only available grammar for the technological future. The critical post-colonial point, Gandhi's, Dussel's, Eisenstadt's, Connell's, is that thinking about technology is also thinking about modernity, and that thinking about modernity is also thinking about who counts.

### 2022 Q13, How does the concept of "the West" set itself up in distinction to "the Rest"?

The concept of "the West" sets itself up against "the Rest" through a discursive operation that Hall describes with unusual clarity in *Formations of Modernity*. I will trace this operation in four steps, homogenisation, polarisation, hierarchisation, and naturalisation, and then assess the political work it does.

Step one is homogenisation. The West is internally diverse, Germanic, Latin, Nordic, Mediterranean, Anglo-Saxon, and the Rest is even more so, China, India, the Islamic world, sub-Saharan Africa, indigenous Americas, Aboriginal Australasia. Yet the discourse of the West and the Rest "represents what are in fact very differentiated (the different European cultures) as homogeneous (the West)… [and] the Rest, though different among themselves, are represented as the same in the sense that they are all different from the West" (Hall, 1992: 280). The internal differences of the West are collapsed in favour of its shared character; the internal differences of the Rest are collapsed in favour of their shared difference from the West.

Step two is polarisation. The discourse arranges itself through binary oppositions: rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic (lecture slide 9; Hall, 1992: 277). Saussure taught us that meaning works through difference, "night" means what it does because it is opposite to "day." Hall extends this to cultural identity: "national cultures acquire their strong sense of identity by contrasting themselves with other cultures" (Hall, 1992: 279). The West knows itself by *not being* the Rest. The Rest is the negative pole that makes the positive pole legible.

Step three is hierarchisation. The binaries are not symmetrical. The first term of each pair carries positive value; the second carries negative value. The West is rational, cultured, intellectual, progressive, scientific; the Rest is irrational, savage, embodied, traditional, magical. Hall is explicit: the discourse "provides criteria of evaluation against which other societies are ranked and around which powerful positive and negative feelings cluster… 'the West' = developed = good = desirable; or the 'non-West' = under-developed = bad = undesirable" (Hall, 1992: 277). The West/Rest binary is not difference; it is hierarchy. It legitimates colonial intervention, civilising missions, and developmental aid as moral obligations to lift the Rest toward the West.

Step four is naturalisation. The discourse hides its own constructed character. Hall insists that "the West is therefore also an idea, a concept" (Hall, 1992: 277), but the discourse presents the West as a geographical fact, an obvious self-evident location on the map. The Mercator projection visually naturalises Eurocentric geography. The university teaching of "Western civilisation" naturalises the West as a continuous tradition from Athens to Washington via Jerusalem, Rome, Florence, London and Paris, a fictional genealogy that omits the Islamic transmission of Greek philosophy and treats the West as autochthonous.

Said's account of Orientalism shows the same operation on the Eastern axis. "Orientalism is a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'" (Said, 1978: 88). The Orient is constructed as Europe's "deepest and most recurring images of the Other" (Said, 1978: 87), and "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Mohanty extends this to the gender axis: Western women self-represent as "educated, modern, as having control over their own bodies and sexualities" against a "third world woman" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) who is "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261).

The political work of this discursive operation is extensive. It legitimated the East India Company's rule in Bengal as the gift of order to the irrational. It legitimated French colonisation in Algeria as the *mission civilisatrice*. It legitimated American intervention in the Middle East as the export of democracy. It legitimates contemporary media framings, "civilised" Ukrainians versus "barbaric" Russians and the absent Afghan refugee; "white saviour" NGO campaigns; the moral asymmetry between Israeli and Palestinian deaths in international coverage. Hall's point is that the discourse is "so destructive, it draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280).

To set up the West against the Rest is therefore not merely to draw a line; it is to perform a sustained ideological operation that homogenises, polarises, hierarchises and naturalises difference, and that has organised five centuries of colonialism, racism and global inequality. To undo the West/Rest distinction is to refuse all four operations, to recognise internal heterogeneity on both sides, to break the binary lockstep, to refuse the implicit hierarchy, and to re-historicise what the discourse presents as natural geography.

### 2022 Q15, Why is it important to examine social theory which emerges from beyond the West?

Examining social theory from beyond the West matters for at least four reasons: epistemic, political, methodological and ethical. I will take each in turn, drawing on Connell, Eisenstadt, Dussel, Gandhi and Mohanty, and then assess the case for and against.

The epistemic reason is that the canonical Western sociological tradition is partial. Marx, Weber and Durkheim wrote about nineteenth-century Europe and generalised their findings into universal laws. Marx's account of capitalism takes the English factory as paradigm; Durkheim's account of solidarity takes industrial France; Weber's rationalisation thesis takes Protestant Northern Europe. When we try to use these theories to understand contemporary India or West Africa or indigenous Mexico, they fit awkwardly at best. Connell's Southern theory argues that Indian sociology (Veena Das, Partha Chatterjee), African theory (Mahmood Mamdani, Achille Mbembe), Latin American dependency theory (Cardoso, Frank) and indigenous jurisprudence have produced concepts that classical sociology lacks, concepts of communal land, of subaltern politics, of post-colonial state, of cosmovision. Without these we do not have a global sociology; we have a parochial European sociology dressed up as universal.

The political reason is that knowledge production is itself power. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest functions as ideology; Said's account of Orientalism shows that European knowledge of the East was "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25). If knowledge is power, then producing knowledge from the periphery is part of political emancipation. Bandung in 1955, the non-aligned movement, dependency theory's challenge to modernisation theory, the African Union's recent push back against IMF structural adjustment, indigenous-led "rights of nature" constitutionalism in Ecuador and Bolivia, these are political projects that depend on theoretical work done outside the metropole. The decolonise-the-curriculum movement in UK universities applies the same logic to higher education itself.

The methodological reason is that theory generated in one setting and applied uncritically to another distorts both. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman" is exemplary: Western feminist theory, applied to South Asian or African women without grounding in their actual lives, produces "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world'" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). The remedy is to theorise from the ground: "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis applies the same methodological principle to modernity itself: there are many institutional combinations, and treating one trajectory as universal misdescribes the others. Methodologically, then, examining non-Western theory is not optional decoration; it is required for empirical accuracy.

The ethical reason is the deepest. Dussel's trans-modernity argues that modernity has two faces: an emancipatory concept and a sacrificial myth. The myth has produced genocides, the Americas after 1492, the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the Belgian Congo, partition violence in 1947, the famines under British rule in India. The victims of modernity have a moral claim to be heard. Spivak's question of subaltern speech captures the ethical stakes: even when we want to listen, the categories through which we listen may silence those we want to hear. Examining non-Western social theory is an attempt to extend the categories, to make theoretical space for voices that the canonical tradition cannot accommodate. Dussel writes that the "negated and victimized 'other-face' of modernity… must, in the first place, discover itself as innocent, as the 'innocent victim' of a ritual sacrifice, who, in the process of discovering itself as innocent may now judge modernity as guilty" (Dussel, 1993: 76). Examining non-Western theory is part of that ethical recovery.

Concrete examples sharpen the case. The Grameen Bank's microfinance was developed by Bangladeshi economist Muhammad Yunus and has been imitated globally, a Southern theoretical innovation that travelled. Latin American dependency theory has reshaped global development economics. Indian subaltern studies has influenced historiography worldwide. Indigenous-led environmental movements have produced concepts (rights of nature, buen vivir, the seventh-generation principle) that are now central to climate ethics. Gandhi on appropriate technology has animated degrowth and sustainable-development thinking. Without these non-Western contributions, contemporary social theory would be massively poorer.

There are objections to address. First, the objection from universalism: knowledge is knowledge regardless of where it is produced; geography is not epistemology. Reply: this is true in principle but false in practice, because the global academy structurally over-represents Northern voices and citations. Second, the objection from quality: not all non-Western theory is good. Reply: of course not; nor is all Western theory good. The principle of equal seriousness does not entail equal validity for each particular claim. Third, the Marxist objection: focusing on theory's geography obscures class. Reply: a properly Southern Marxism (Walter Rodney, Samir Amin, José Carlos Mariátegui) shows that class and geography are not alternatives but co-constituents.

In conclusion, examining non-Western social theory matters because epistemically it corrects partial knowledge, politically it underwrites emancipatory projects, methodologically it secures accuracy, and ethically it acknowledges the victims of modernity. It is not a courtesy extended to the South; it is a condition of sociological intelligence about the world we actually live in.

### 2021 Q13, Using at least two theorists, outline and assess the postcolonial critique of "the West".

I will use Stuart Hall and Frantz Fanon as my two theorists, and I will assess the postcolonial critique of "the West" along three dimensions: discursive (Hall), experiential (Fanon), and political. I will then weigh the strengths and limits of the critique.

Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest, set out in *Formations of Modernity* pp. 276-280, makes the critique discursive. The West, Hall argues, is "as much an idea as a fact of geography" (Hall, 1992: 276). It is "a historical, not a geographical, construct" (Hall, 1992: 277), and it functions as ideology. The concept performs four operations: classification (West/non-West), representation (a composite image), comparison (a measuring stick) and evaluation (a hierarchical ranking). The discourse works through binary oppositions, rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body, progress/tradition, science/magic, and through a simplifying logic that "draws crude and simplistic distinctions and constructs an over-simplified conception of 'difference'" (Hall, 1992: 280). Crucially, Hall shows that the West constituted itself partly by contrast: "the so-called uniqueness of the West was, in part, produced by Europe's contact and self-comparison with other, non-western, societies" (Hall, 1992: 278). The West cannot be the West alone; its identity is relational, parasitic on the Rest it produced as Other.

Fanon's analysis of colonial subjectivity in *Black Skin, White Masks* makes the critique experiential. Fanon takes the discursive structures Hall describes and shows what they feel like in a human life. The famous scene on the train, where a white child cries "Look, a Negro!" (Fanon, 1967: 323), turns the discourse into a hail. The Black colonised subject is fixed by the white gaze: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am *fixed*" (Fanon, 1967: 325). His body is "given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored…" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The white world bars him "from all participation. A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324). What Hall describes structurally Fanon registers viscerally: the West is not just an idea, it is a wound on the colonised body.

Together, Hall and Fanon constitute a powerful critique. Hall provides the structural anatomy; Fanon provides the lived testimony. The critique can be summarised in three claims. First, the West is a historical construct, not a natural fact. Second, the West constitutes itself by negation, by producing and contrasting itself with the Rest. Third, this constitution does material and psychic harm to those positioned as the Rest. The critique extends naturally to contemporary phenomena: museum repatriation debates over the Benin bronzes; Rhodes Must Fall; the Western media's "civilised vs barbaric" framing of Ukraine 2022; vaccine apartheid during COVID; the "white saviour" critique of NGO interventions; and Mohanty's parallel critique of "Third World Woman."

Assessing the critique requires acknowledging both strengths and limits. The strengths are considerable. The critique denaturalises a category that had been taken for granted; it links discourse to power following Said and Foucault; it provides intellectual resources for political movements; it has reshaped curricula, museums and media. It has supplied a vocabulary, Orientalism, the colonial wound, decolonising knowledge, epistemic violence, the imperial archive, that has travelled globally.

The limits are also real. First, the critique can produce its own essentialism: by speaking constantly of "the West" the critique risks reifying the very entity it wants to dissolve, treating it as a coherent agent rather than a contested space. Hall is alert to this, "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279), but his readers often forget. Second, the critique can be culturalist, focusing on representation at the expense of political economy. Marxist post-colonial critics (Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber) argue that the Bangladeshi garment worker and the British call-centre worker face the same global capitalism, and that focusing on cultural difference disarms class solidarity. Third, Fanon's account of colonial subjectivity, while powerful, is sometimes read as static, as if every Black subject in every setting experiences the white gaze identically. Fanon himself, who was also a revolutionary in Algeria, did not believe this; the *Wretched of the Earth* is precisely about agency and refusal. Fourth, the critique is now sometimes co-opted by authoritarian nationalisms, Hindu nationalism, Putin's Russia, Erdoğan's Turkey, that wear decolonisation's clothes for sectarian ends.

A balanced conclusion: Hall and Fanon together produce one of the most important critical achievements of twentieth-century social theory. They have changed how we read the news, how we walk through museums, how we teach the canon, and how we think about ourselves. The critique remains indispensable. But it needs to be held with care, with class analysis, with attention to internal heterogeneity, with awareness of how easily critique can be appropriated. The point is not to retire the critique but to deepen it.

### 2021 Q16, In what ways does 'Southern theory' take forward debates in 'Western' social theory?

Southern theory, in Connell's formulation, is sometimes presented as an alternative to Western social theory. I will argue that it is better understood as taking forward debates that Western social theory itself began but could not complete. Southern theory advances Western theory in five areas: the sociology of knowledge, the theory of modernity, feminist theory, theories of capitalism, and ecological theory.

In the sociology of knowledge, Western theory had already produced Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia*, Bourdieu's account of the academic field, Foucault's archaeology of knowledge, and Latour's actor-network theory. Each tried to show that knowledge is shaped by its conditions of production. But Western theory rarely turned this insight on itself; it generally exempted the canonical sociological tradition from its own sociology of knowledge. Connell's Southern theory completes the gesture. It asks: what does it mean that the sociological canon was produced overwhelmingly by white men in imperial metropoles between 1830 and 1930? It points out that classical theory's empirical universe was Europe; its informants were European; its problems were European. The honest extension of Bourdieu's reflexive sociology is to recognise the imperial conditions of sociology itself. Subaltern studies (Guha, Chatterjee) and Mbembe's African post-colonial theory take forward Foucault's analysis of power-knowledge by extending it to the imperial archive.

In the theory of modernity, Weber's account of rationalisation, Habermas's account of the public sphere, Giddens's account of disembedding, Beck's account of risk society, all tried to characterise the distinctive features of modernity. But they all treated modernity as a European achievement that then radiated outward. Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis takes the debate forward by pluralising it: Japanese modernity, Indian modernity, Iranian modernity are not failed approximations of European modernity but distinctive combinations. Dussel's trans-modernity goes further, asking what a modernity that fully acknowledged its colonial costs would look like. These are not rejections of Habermas; they are corrections that Habermas could not produce from within his own framework.

In feminist theory, second-wave Western feminism produced massive theoretical achievements, Beauvoir's *Second Sex*, Friedan, Millett, Firestone, MacKinnon. But, as Mohanty showed, it tended to treat its own historical particularity as universal. Mohanty's critique of "Third World Woman", the figure as "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), extends second-wave feminism into a genuinely global feminism. The insistence that "male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies" (Mohanty, 1984: 262) takes forward feminist theory's own commitment to intersectionality before that word was widely used. Black feminist theory (Crenshaw, hooks, Hill Collins) does similar work from within the Western academy but with similar Southern-theoretical inflections.

In theories of capitalism, Marx had identified primitive accumulation and the role of colonial plunder but his successors in the European Marxist tradition tended to treat colonialism as an addendum. Dependency theory (Prebisch, Cardoso, Frank, Wallerstein) takes Marx forward by making the world-system rather than the nation the unit of analysis. Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* extends Marx into the African context. Howard French's *Born in Blackness* completes the historiographical task. Together they take forward the Marxist debate by showing that capitalism is constitutively a world-system, not a European phenomenon with global extensions.

In ecological theory, Western environmentalism (Carson, Schumacher, the Brundtland Report) had identified ecological limits to growth but tended to frame ecological politics in technocratic terms. Southern theory contributions, Vandana Shiva's eco-feminism, indigenous buen vivir constitutionalism in Ecuador and Bolivia, Gandhi on appropriate technology, the rights-of-nature movement, take ecological theory forward by linking it to cosmovisions of human-nature relations that Western theory had largely abandoned. The Standing Rock protests in the US, the indigenous-led opposition to Amazon deforestation, the climate-justice movement's centring of Global South vulnerability, these are political enactments of theoretical work done outside the metropole.

The argument that Southern theory takes Western theory forward, rather than replacing it, has both strategic and analytical merits. Strategically, it makes the case for Southern theory more easily heard in Northern institutions: Southern theory does not ask sociology to abandon its tradition but to extend it. Analytically, it captures the genuine relation: most Southern theorists were trained in Western institutions, read Marx and Weber and Foucault, and are engaged in serious dialogue with the canon. Spivak teaches at Columbia; Bhabha at Harvard; Mbembe and Mahmood Mamdani are deeply read in Hegel, Marx and Foucault. Southern theory is not an alien import; it is post-colonial extension of debates the Western tradition started but could not finish from within.

Caveats are needed. First, taking Western theory forward is not the only mode in which Southern theory operates; some Southern theory genuinely breaks with Western frameworks (indigenous epistemologies that refuse the subject-object distinction, for instance). Second, framing Southern theory as extension can risk subordinating it to the Western canon; Connell would reject this. Third, Western theory's own internal critics (Marxists, feminists, Foucauldians) had already opened many of the debates Southern theory deepens. A balanced view holds that Southern theory and Western theory are in genuine dialogue, that Southern theory is sometimes extension and sometimes rupture, and that the global academy benefits from taking both moves seriously. The Bandung Conference's intellectual descendants, from Frantz Fanon in Algeria to Walter Rodney in Tanzania to the contemporary Latin American decolonial school, are not visitors to Western theory; they are co-authors of contemporary social thought.

### Mock 1, "The Orient is an idea that has a history" (Said). Discuss.

I take Said's epigrammatic claim that the Orient is "an idea that has a history and a tradition of thought, imagery, and vocabulary that have given it reality and presence in and for the West" (Said, 1978: 87) as a doorway into three claims: that the Orient is an idea, that ideas have histories, and that the Orient's history is a Western history. I treat each in turn and end with a contemporary application.

The first claim, that the Orient is an idea, refuses the realist commonsense that the East is just a place. The Orient as Said writes about it is not the geography of Beirut, Cairo, Baghdad and Tehran but the discursive object that European philology, fiction, painting, opera and policy produced about that geography. "The Orient was almost a European invention, and had been since antiquity a place of romance, exotic beings, haunting memories and landscapes, remarkable experiences" (Said, 1978: 87). The Orient is therefore real in the constructivist sense: real as a system of representation, real in its political and material effects, but not a self-standing fact prior to representation. This is the Foucauldian move at the heart of *Orientalism*. The "corporate institution for dealing with the Orient" (Said, 1978: 25), universities, museums, ministries, archives, novels, produced its object as it described it.

The second claim, that the idea has a history, distinguishes Said from purely structuralist analysis. Said dates the modern crystallisation to the late eighteenth century, after Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 and the establishment of academic Oriental studies. Earlier European images, medieval crusader polemic, Marco Polo, the Ottoman threat to Vienna in 1683, fed into but did not yet constitute the Orientalist discourse that consolidates around Silvestre de Sacy, Ernest Renan and Edward Lane. The idea has a datable rise, an institutional habitat, a canon of texts.

The third claim, that the Orient's history is a Western history, is the political punch. "European culture gained in strength and identity by setting itself off against the Orient as a sort of surrogate and even underground self" (Said, 1978: 89). Hall's parallel about the West and the Rest generalises Said's claim eastwards: the West needs the Rest to define itself (Hall, 1992: 280). The Orient is therefore part of Europe's autobiography, not of Asia's.

Three qualifications. First, Said sometimes risks a monolithic European culture; critics (Aijaz Ahmad, Sadiq al-Azm) note that European Orientalism was internally contested, philo-Islamic scholars like Louis Massignon, Marxist critics of empire, Jewish Orientalists in ambivalent positions. Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies to Said too. Second, the Orient writes back: Arabic journalism, Iranian cinema, Pakistani Anglophone fiction, Egyptian novels, Al Jazeera. Third, the Orient is not the only Other. Mohanty's "average third world woman" rendered "ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is a gendered cognate; Hall's Rest covers the rest of the globe; Fanon's white gaze racialises Black bodies in parallel.

A contemporary application makes the durability of the idea visible. When Western media in early 2022 contrasted "civilised" Ukrainian refugees with the "barbaric" Middle Eastern wave of 2015-16, they were speaking pure Orientalism: an Orient incapable of producing the conditions of civilised life from within, refugees who therefore bring chaos rather than entitlement. The British Museum's continued custody of the Benin bronzes, looted in 1897, materialises the Orientalist (and Hallian) imperial archive. Modi's Hindutva, by contrast, complicates the picture: it speaks the language of decolonisation while reproducing the structure of othering against Indian Muslims. The "Orient" was always shifting, and the idea has continued to mutate. AI's English-language bias, large language models trained overwhelmingly on Anglophone web text and Wikipedia, then deployed globally, is the latest chapter of the imperial archive.

Said's claim is therefore right and durable. The Orient is an idea, ideas have histories, and the Orient's history is part of Europe's history of constructing itself through Others. The contemporary reader takes the claim, holds it alongside Hall's structural grammar of the West and the Rest, alongside Mohanty's gender inflection, alongside Fanon's racial embodiment, alongside Spivak's question of who can speak, and reads the world with sharper instruments than realist commonsense supplies. The Orient is a Western invention; whether the invention can be unmade is the project of postcolonial theory.

### Mock 2, Critically assess Said's argument in *Orientalism* in light of contemporary representations of the Middle East.

Said's *Orientalism* (1978) argued that Western representations of the Middle East have, since the late eighteenth century, formed a discourse, "the corporate institution for dealing with the Orient, dealing with it by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it, ruling over it" (Said, 1978: 25). I assess this argument by testing it against four kinds of contemporary representation of the Middle East, news coverage, cinema and television, museum and exhibition practice, and counter-terrorism policy, and conclude that the argument substantially holds, with three modifications.

News coverage of the Middle East still tracks the Saidian diagnosis closely. The Western framing of the 2003 invasion of Iraq as bringing democracy to a region presumed incapable of producing it; the recurrent figure of the veiled Muslim woman as cipher for an oppressed, voiceless East; the 2014-19 framing of ISIS as a manifestation of "Islamic civilisation's failure to modernise"; the 2022 contrast between "civilised European" Ukrainian refugees and the "barbaric" Middle Eastern wave of 2015-16, all repeat the Orientalist binary structure. Said's claim that "the relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (Said, 1978: 89) describes the structural asymmetry: the Middle East is a place that is reported on by Western correspondents, intervened in by Western armies, narrated in Western frames, but rarely heard as a self-narrating voice. The 2023-24 Gaza coverage in Western outlets has been a particularly stark case: Israeli sources granted authority and named subjects, Palestinian sources rendered as masses and statistics, the textbook Orientalist asymmetry.

Cinema and television show a more mixed picture. The Hollywood canon (*True Lies*, *Aladdin*, *American Sniper*, *Argo*, *Zero Dark Thirty*) has reproduced Orientalist tropes for decades. But significant counter-currents now exist. Iranian cinema (Asghar Farhadi, Jafar Panahi) circulates globally; Palestinian directors (Elia Suleiman, Annemarie Jacir) and Saudi women filmmakers (Haifaa al-Mansour) speak from within. Middle Eastern women scholars, Lila Abu-Lughod, Saba Mahmood, refuse the victim/native-informant binary that Mohanty's parallel critique of Third World Woman identified.

Museum practice is Orientalist in inheritance and contested in present. The British Museum's Ottoman and Persian holdings, the Louvre's Department of Islamic Art, and the Quai Branly's ethnographic frame materialise the Saidian "Orient on display" (Said, 1978: 26). The Maqdala treasures' partial returns to Ethiopia and the Louvre Abu Dhabi's reframing of "Islamic" objects complicate but do not abolish the imperial archive.

Counter-terrorism is where Said's framework has hardened. Talal Asad and Mahmood Mamdani extended Said into the post-9/11 surveillance state. The "good Muslim/bad Muslim" distinction (Mamdani), the UK Prevent programme, the US no-fly list and the architecture of border security institutionalise what was once mainly literary into bureaucracy. The Orientalist library has become the Orientalist database, a strengthening, not a weakening, of Said's diagnosis.

Three modifications need to be entered. First, the Middle East produces its own globalised forms, Al Jazeera English, Arabic Netflix originals, Saudi cultural projects, that compete with the Western Orientalist canon. Second, the rise of regional sub-imperialisms (Erdoğan's Turkey, MBS's Saudi Arabia, Iran's regional ambitions) means the Middle East produces its own intra-regional Orientalisms (Persian Gulf representation of Yemenis and Syrians; Turkey's representation of Kurds). Said's framework is West-on-East; we need East-on-East too. Third, Mohanty's Third World Woman needs to be held alongside Said: gendered Orientalism (the veiled Muslim woman) does specific work Said's largely male archive underplays.

The critique of Said is not the rejection of Said. The 2026 student who reads *Orientalism* alongside Mamdani's *Good Muslim, Bad Muslim* (2004), Lila Abu-Lughod's *Do Muslim Women Need Saving?* (2013), Talal Asad's *Formations of the Secular* (2003) and contemporary Middle Eastern self-representation has the instruments to read Western coverage of Gaza, of Iran's nuclear programme, of Syrian refugees, of Saudi Vision 2030, with sharp critical eyes. The Saidian diagnosis is durable; the world has changed enough to need supplementation, but the core argument remains the indispensable starting point for understanding contemporary Western representations of the Middle East. Without it, the student sees only what *The Economist* and *The New York Times* want them to see.

### Mock 3, What does Fanon mean by the "white gaze", and why does it produce a doubled consciousness rather than simply an external constraint?

This is a two-part question. I take the parts in order from *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) and connect the analysis to Du Bois, Sartre and Hall.

On the first part, the white gaze is Fanon's name for the perceptual regime through which the colonial discourse of race lands on a particular Black body. His paradigmatic scene is the railway carriage: a white child cries, "Look, a Negro!… Mama, see the Negro! I'm frightened!" (Fanon, 1967: 323). Fanon tries to laugh: "I made up my mind to laugh myself to tears, but laughter had become impossible" (Fanon, 1967: 323). The cry is not merely insult; it is interpellation that fixes Fanon into a pre-existing category. The white gaze is what Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest does to a body: the structural binaries (rational/irrational, cultured/savage, mind/body) are not abstract but deployed by an actual white child on an actual Black man, and his freedom to be anything other than "the Negro" collapses.

Three features specify the gaze. First, it is unidirectional: "I am being dissected under white eyes, the only real eyes. I am fixed" (Fanon, 1967: 325). Second, it produces its object: "My body was given back to me sprawled out, distorted, recolored… the corporeal schema crumbled, its place taken by a racial epidermal schema" (Fanon, 1967: 324). Before the gaze, the Black body had a normal self-relation; under it, an alien self-image replaces it. Third, the gaze is asymmetric: whiteness is invisible to itself (Hall's universalist West); blackness is the entire content of how the Black subject is seen.

On the second part, why does this produce doubling rather than external constraint? An external constraint would say: I am free internally; I am only blocked externally. Fanon's analysis is sharper. The white gaze does not just block; it inhabits. The colonised subject becomes both subject (still thinking, feeling, suffering) and object (the dissected Negro) simultaneously, and these are not separable. "A man was expected to behave like a man. I was expected to behave like a black man" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The Black man cannot simply will himself into being a man; he must also struggle against the gaze's interior occupation of his self-perception. He is alienated from his own body, which has been recoded.

This doubling has an ancestor in Du Bois's "double consciousness", "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5 [VERIFY YEAR?]). Fanon extends Du Bois into psychoanalytic registers: the racial epidermal schema is what double consciousness does at the level of body-image. He also extends Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943), which had described the gaze (le regard) as a general feature of being-with-others. Fanon shows that under colonialism the gaze is unequally distributed: whites look, Blacks are looked at.

Why does the distinction between doubling and constraint matter? Because the response differs. To constraint, the response is to remove the obstacle. To doubling, the response must be more profound: to remake the relation between the subject and the racialised self-image. "Since the other hesitated to recognise me, there remained only one solution: to make myself known" (Fanon, 1967: 324). The making-known is not the removal of an external block; it is the active creation of a new self that the doubled colonial subject could not, by itself, have made. In *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), this becomes revolutionary struggle.

The analysis remains live. Black Lives Matter, after Trayvon Martin and George Floyd, is a contest over the white gaze. Rhodes Must Fall targeted the statue as crystallised gaze. Decolonising the curriculum addresses the doubling produced by an education in which the colonised reads themselves through the coloniser's categories. Spivak's question of subaltern speech inherits the Fanonian problem: how can a voice formed under the gaze be heard on terms other than the gaze's?

In summary, the white gaze lands the colonial discursive order on a Black body; it produces doubling rather than mere constraint because it inhabits the subject's own self-image; the response is the active production, through struggle, of a new subjectivity that no longer has to see itself through the gaze.

### Mock 4, Compare Said's Orientalism with Mohanty's "Third World Woman".

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and Chandra Mohanty's *Under Western Eyes* (1988) both diagnose a homogenised non-Western Other that says more about its maker than its referent. They differ in axis, archive and political target. I compare them along these dimensions and assess what is gained by reading them together.

On axis, Said works the geographical-civilisational divide. The Orient is the East, primarily the Arab and Islamic Middle East, as seen by the West. Mohanty works gender within the broader colonial relation. The Third World Woman is the figure produced by Western scholarship and Western feminism about women in the periphery. The two binaries intersect: the veiled Muslim woman is at once Orientalist and gendered-Orientalist.

On archive, Said reads the nineteenth-century European Orientalist canon, Sacy, Renan, Lane, Burton, Flaubert, Nerval, supplemented by twentieth-century Anglo-American area studies. Mohanty reads late-twentieth-century Anglophone Western feminist scholarship, Zed Books, "women in development" anthologies, ethnographic accounts of "non-Western women." Said is reading two centuries of imperial culture; Mohanty is reading two decades of Western feminist scholarship that was trying to be progressive. Said's enemies are Orientalist; Mohanty's are well-meaning Western feminists. The analytic operation is similar.

On theoretical apparatus, Said draws Foucault's notion of discourse to explain how Orientalism functions as a "corporate institution… dealing with [the Orient] by making statements about it, authorizing views of it, describing it, by teaching it, settling it, ruling over it" (Said, 1978: 25). Mohanty frames the Third World Woman as "an effect of discourse" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Both use the Foucauldian move that discourse produces its object. But Mohanty foregrounds who speaks for whom inside progressive scholarship in a way Said does not.

On political target, Said's enemy is Western imperial culture; the implication is decolonisation, political and intellectual. Mohanty's enemy is Western feminist universalism that subordinates Global South women under the figure of the helpless brown woman who needs saving. The "average third world woman" leads "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender… and being 'third world' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized etc.)" (Mohanty, 1984: 261). Her political project is global feminist solidarity that respects difference without exoticising it.

Three things are gained by reading them together. First, gendered specification. Said's archive is mostly male and treats gendered tropes (the feminised Orient, the harem fantasy) glancingly. Mohanty supplies the gender dimension. The Third World Woman is what Said's Orient looks like in its feminine register: a justification-figure for Western humanitarian intervention, white-saviour NGO campaigns, and feminist universalism. Second, the intra-progressive critique. Said critiques empire; Mohanty critiques solidarity-with-the-Third-World that does not check its premises. Third, the methodological injunction to specificity. "Male violence must be theorized and interpreted *within* specific societies, both in order to understand it better, as well as in order to effectively organize to change it" (Mohanty, 1984: 262). This becomes a general principle for any analysis of subordination.

Tensions are real. Said sometimes treats Western culture as monolithic; Mohanty sometimes treats Western feminism as more uniform than it was (Black feminism, working-class feminism, lesbian feminism, Chicana feminism all complicated the binary). Both have been accused of constructing their own homogenising figure of "the Western X" in order to critique it. Hall's reminder that "the West has always contained many internal differences" (Hall, 1992: 279) applies to both.

Concrete examples make the convergence live. The Western media's deployment of the figure of the oppressed Muslim woman to justify the 2001 invasion of Afghanistan is simultaneously Saidian (Orientalist Islam) and Mohantian (helpless Third World Woman). The #BringBackOurGirls campaign of 2014 mobilised the helpless African girl trope. White-saviour NGO campaigns like Kony 2012 fuse the two operations. The British Museum's holdings include both Orientalist objects and ethnographic representations of non-Western women. Vaccine apartheid during COVID showed both Saidian (the periphery as object) and Mohantian (gendered impact unequal) structures. Modi's Hindutva takes the form of internal Orientalism within the post-colonial state, with Indian Muslim women as a target, showing that both Said and Mohanty illuminate intra-Third-World dynamics, not only North-South.

In conclusion, Said and Mohanty are complementary diagnosticians of the constructed non-Western Other, Said on the civilisational axis, Mohanty on the gender axis. They share an analytic move (discourse produces its object) and an ethical injunction (refuse the homogenising figure). Read together, they give the contemporary reader the conceptual instruments to dismantle both the obvious imperial enemy and the well-meaning Western friend whose solidarity reproduces the structure they oppose.

### Mock 5, How does Spivak's "can the subaltern speak?" help us think about climate-migrant testimonies at COP?

Gayatri Spivak's 1988 essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" asked whether the most structurally voiceless members of a colonised society, particularly women at the intersection of colonial, patriarchal and class hierarchies, could speak in a way that registered on available discursive frames. I apply her question to climate-displaced testimony at the UN Conference of the Parties (COP), particularly around the Loss and Damage debate that culminated in COP27 (Sharm el-Sheikh, 2022) and COP28 (Dubai, 2023). I argue Spivak's question is indispensable here, with three modifications.

The subaltern in Spivak's precise sense is not the marginal but the structurally unrepresentable. The subaltern is not the proletarian, who has unions and parties; nor the colonised intellectual, who has the academy. The subaltern is the figure for whom available frames cannot register what they say. Spivak's paradigm case is Bhubaneswari Bhaduri, the seventeen-year-old Bengali who committed suicide in 1926 while menstruating, in part to prevent her death being read as romantic-illicit. The signal she embedded, the menstruation that ruled out pregnancy, was missed by family and historians. "The subaltern cannot speak" does not mean she has nothing to say; it means the frames in which her speech is heard cannot register it without distortion.

Apply this to COP. The Tuvaluan minister speaking from a podium with trousers rolled up because the sea is encroaching; the Bangladeshi farmer flown to Dubai to describe displacement from the Bay of Bengal; the Vanuatu PM appealing for an ICJ advisory opinion on climate obligations, these subjects are visibly speaking. They are translated, given microphone time. The question Spivak raises is what is heard. COP is structured around state delegations, framework texts, finance commitments in dollars. The frames are economic, security, and narrowly scientific. A Marshallese mother's testimony about ancestral graves submerging, about the impossibility of relocation when the land is the people, about a planetary injustice that cannot be priced, this falls into the gap between frames. It is heard as moving anecdote, not analysis.

Representation in Spivak's vocabulary has two senses: vertretung (political proxy) and darstellung (aesthetic portrait). Climate testimonies at COP are heavily darstellung. The vertretung, political representation in binding decisions, finance flows, COP outcomes, lags. The Loss and Damage Fund established at COP27 had a few hundred million by COP28 against estimated needs in the hundreds of billions; its governance ended up at the World Bank, which Southern delegations had resisted as a colonial-financial structure. The portrait is mobilised; the political representation is foreclosed.

Spivak's epistemic-violence concept applies in real time. Even sympathetic Northern recovery risks ventriloquising. Western journalists fly to the Pacific to "document" the climate-displaced; NGO campaigns deploy the climate refugee in ways resembling Mohanty's Third World Woman. The structural reality, that the Global North's historical emissions caused Southern climate displacement, gets converted into a charity story.

Three modifications. First, the climate-displaced are not the most subaltern within their own societies. The minister at COP is a national elite; the rural Bangladeshi woman whose land is salting may not have travelled. The subaltern within the subaltern, indigenous peoples, women within communities, lower-caste groups, is what is still unrepresented. Second, Spivak resisted the despair reading; her later work emphasises strategic essentialism. The Loss and Damage campaign moved the dial, slowly. The Vanuatu-led ICJ advisory opinion has produced a normative shift that may reshape international climate law. Third, the vocabulary now includes voices Spivak's 1988 essay could not anticipate: indigenous defenders (Standing Rock; the Wet'suwet'en against Coastal GasLink; the Kogi of Colombia); Pacific climate justice networks; the youth climate movement from Greta Thunberg to Vanessa Nakate (cropped from a 2020 photograph in a way that materialised the racial structure Spivak's frame predicts).

Concrete examples sharpen the application. COP28's invitation to fossil fuel CEO Sultan Al Jaber as summit president made vivid the asymmetry. Vaccine apartheid during COVID is a parallel, Southern voices for the TRIPS waiver, led by India and South Africa, were heard but overridden. The 2022 Western media contrast between civilised Ukrainian and barbaric Middle Eastern refugees is the frame in which climate-displaced Brown people will be received at Northern borders.

In conclusion, Spivak's question is indispensable for climate-migrant testimony at COP. It alerts us to the gap between speaking and being heard, between aesthetic and political representation, and to epistemic violence in well-meaning Northern reception. But three decades of political organisation now overlay Spivak's 1988 pessimism. The subaltern at COP is speaking; the question is whether the world is yet capable of listening.

### Mock 6, The British Museum, the Benin bronzes and the postcolonial moment.

The Benin bronzes are the brass and ivory sculptures looted by British soldiers from Benin City in the punitive expedition of February 1897. They are perhaps the most iconic single set of objects in contemporary repatriation debate, and the British Museum is their most contested custodian. I treat them as a node where Said, Hall, Fanon, Spivak and Dussel converge with contemporary movement politics.

The historical event. The Benin Kingdom was a centuries-old state in what is now southern Nigeria with elaborate brass casting and ivory carving. The British punitive expedition of 1897 followed the killing of a British party who advanced on Benin City during a religious festival in defiance of local warning. The British looted 3,000-4,000 objects; the Foreign Office and British Museum took the lion's share, partly to fund the expedition's costs. The Oba was exiled to Calabar, the kingdom incorporated into the protectorate of Southern Nigeria. The artefacts are loot in the precise legal-historical sense.

The museum. The British Museum, founded in 1753 on Sir Hans Sloane's bequest (Caribbean slave plantation profits), was already the imperial museum par excellence by 1897, Elgin Marbles since 1816, Rosetta Stone since 1802, Assyrian reliefs since the 1840s-50s, Maqdala treasures from Ethiopia since 1868. The bronzes joined a collection assembled almost wholly through imperial conquest, missionary activity or unequal commerce. The Orient (and the larger Rest) was "in a sense, on display in the museum" (Said, 1978: 26). The British Museum is the imperial archive made physical.

The discourse. Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest classifies, represents, compares and evaluates (Hall, 1992: 280). The British Museum's organisation embeds all four. The bronzes sit in the "Africa" gallery; European art of the same period sits in the National Gallery. The classification "ethnographic" versus "fine art" reproduces the very evolutionist hierarchy Hall traces. Captioning has, until recently, framed the bronzes as "sophisticated" African craft, a backhanded compliment that preserves the binary.

The postcolonial moment. Nigerian governments have requested return since the 1960s, but the present moment is distinctive. Three convergent pressures make the British Museum's position increasingly untenable. First, the Black Lives Matter and decolonisation cycle since 2020 reframed colonial loot, Edward Colston's statue in Bristol, the Rhodes Must Fall campaign, the decolonise-the-curriculum movement. Second, the German government's 2022 agreement to return Benin bronzes held in German museums broke the Western consensus. Third, the construction of the Edo Museum of West African Art (EMOWAA) in Benin City, designed by David Adjaye, answered the museum's traditional objection that returned objects might not be properly housed.

The British Museum's defences are threadbare. The "universal museum" defence, that gathering cultures in one place benefits humanity, is the West presenting itself as universal (Hall). The "British Museum Act 1963" defence, that the museum is legally barred from deaccessioning, is procedural cover for underlying refusal. The "at risk in country of origin" defence is the paternalist white gaze applied to Beninese curation (Fanon). The "long custody creates legitimacy" defence is the legitimation of original violence by duration (Dussel).

Spivak's question of subaltern speech arises within the debate. Who speaks for the bronzes? The Oba? The Nigerian federal state? The Edo state government? The Beninese diaspora? Local Edo communities? The bronzes were sacred court objects, not freely circulating art; their proper context was ritual use within the Oba's court, not display on a wall. Returning them as "art objects" to a "museum" reproduces Western framing even in the act of return. The most thoughtful return projects, the Smithsonian's 2022 transfer, the Horniman Museum's transfer, EMOWAA's plans, negotiate this complexity with Edo authorities.

Consequences are unfolding. The Smithsonian transferred its bronzes in 2022. Aberdeen returned a bronze in 2021. Jesus College Cambridge returned a cockerel in 2019. The Horniman returned objects in 2022. The British Museum has returned nothing significant. The disjunction between its position and the moving consensus of smaller institutions is now politically conspicuous.

In conclusion, the Benin bronzes at the British Museum exemplify the postcolonial moment in its most material and visible form. They are simultaneously a Saidian object (imperial archive), Hallian object (West/Rest discourse), Fanonian object (colonial wound), Spivakian object (subaltern speech and its proxies), and Dusselian object (the demand for trans-modern repair). The trajectory points toward eventual return, but British Museum resistance, legal architecture, and the genuine complexity of who speaks for the bronzes mean the postcolonial moment for these objects is not yet over.

### Mock 7, Does the rise of China and India vindicate or complicate postcolonial theory's critique of "the West"?

China and India in the 2020s are economic, geopolitical and cultural powers whose rise unsettles the simple North-South map. I argue their rise both vindicates and complicates postcolonial critique.

The vindication runs along three lines. First, China and India falsify the developmentalist assumption that modernity is unilinear and Western-defined. By 2026 China is the world's largest manufacturer and second-largest economy; India is the most populous nation and fifth-largest economy. Both have modernised, but not on the Western pattern. China's modernity is authoritarian-capitalist, post-Maoist, with state-directed investment and surveillance infrastructure. India's is parliamentary-democratic but increasingly Hindu-majoritarian. This vindicates Eisenstadt's multiple modernities thesis and Connell's Southern theory: modernity does not have a single Western face.

Second, the colonial difference has reorganised. China's per capita GDP overtook several Eastern European EU members in the 2010s; India's middle class is one of the world's largest. The Indian Space Research Organisation's Mars Orbiter Mission cost less than *Gravity* and reached Mars on first attempt. Dussel's claim that modernity is centre-periphery is right about the founding moment (1492); the contemporary geometry is no longer Europe-centred. This is exactly the kind of trans-modern development Dussel hopes for.

Third, Chinese and Indian voices increasingly refuse the Western monopoly on theoretical universality. Tu Weiming on Confucian humanism, Wang Hui on post-89 politics, Zhao Tingyang on tianxia as alternative to Westphalian sovereignty; Amartya Sen on multiple identities, Partha Chatterjee on political society, Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe* (2000), Rajeev Bhargava on Indian secularism, these claim universal status that contests the European universal. Connell's Southern theory project is realised in practice.

But complications are substantial. First, China and India themselves engage in new sub-imperial patterns. The Belt and Road Initiative (2013) has made China a major creditor across Africa, Central Asia and parts of Europe; Chinese state-owned enterprises operate copper mines in Zambia, port leases in Sri Lanka (Hambantota), Greece (Piraeus) and Pakistan (Gwadar). Indian capital flows into Bangladesh and Nepal in patterns local critics describe as sub-imperialism. The colonial difference is no longer a single North-South line but a multiply-nested set of centre-periphery relations.

Second, both reproduce internal Orientalisms. Modi's Hindutva casts Indian Muslims, Christians, Dalits and Adivasis as internal Rest against a Hindu West: the 2019 Citizenship Amendment Act, the abrogation of Article 370 in Kashmir, the 2024 Ram Mandir consecration on the Babri Masjid site, periodic communal violence. China's treatment of Uyghurs in Xinjiang, Tibetans, Hong Kongers and Mongolians displays a parallel Han-centred internal Orientalism. Postcolonial critique must apply its frame to formerly colonised states.

Third, new South-North and South-South knowledge flows complicate the decolonising-knowledge project. Indian generic medicines (HIV/AIDS treatment), Chinese solar panel manufacturing, but also Chinese surveillance technology exported to authoritarian regimes and Indian English-language hegemony within South Asia. The South is not unitary.

Concrete examples sharpen the analysis. Vaccine apartheid during COVID was both vindication and complication: Western states blocked the TRIPS waiver, but India and South Africa, the waiver's proponents, were themselves significant pharmaceutical producers (the Serum Institute in Pune, Aspen). The 2022 Western media framing of Ukraine, civilised Europeans against barbaric Russians, was textbook Hallian discourse, yet Chinese and Indian neutrality on the war showed Western moral universalism is no longer global. Bandung 1955 imagined a unified non-aligned periphery; BRICS in 2026, after the 2024 expansion to Iran, Egypt, Ethiopia, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, is something more complex.

In conclusion, the rise of China and India both vindicates and complicates postcolonial critique. It vindicates modernity's plurality, the re-geometrising of the colonial difference, and Southern theory in practice. It complicates the framework by showing that formerly colonised states reproduce sub-imperial and internal-Orientalist structures, and that the South is not unitary. The colonial structure of modernity is not undone by new centres; it is reorganised. The critique remains live, but must recognise that the West is no longer the only centre and the Rest is no longer the only periphery.

### Mock 8, Can sociology be decolonised, or is decolonisation always already co-opted?

The demand to decolonise sociology has been one of the most visible intellectual projects of the late 2010s and 2020s, Rhodes Must Fall at Cape Town (2015) and Oxford (2016), UCL's "Why is My Curriculum White?", reading-list reviews across UK and US sociology departments. The question is whether this project can succeed or whether universities will always co-opt it. I argue decolonisation is possible but partial, that co-optation is a real risk but not inevitable, and that the answer depends on how decolonisation is structured.

The literature distinguishes three programmes. First, curricular decolonisation: changing the canon to include Du Bois, Fanon, Cabral, Quijano, Connell, Spivak, Mohanty, Mbembe. Second, methodological decolonisation: collaborative rather than extractive research, indigenous methodologies, refusal of the comparative gaze. Third, institutional decolonisation: staff hiring, doctoral recruitment, the geography of academic prestige, the language politics of publishing.

The case for possibility is strong. Curricular shifts have been substantial. Du Bois is widely taught alongside Weber; Connell's *Southern Theory* (2007) is on many syllabi; Quijano's coloniality of power has entered the sociology vocabulary; Mbembe's *On the Postcolony* (2001) and *Critique of Black Reason* (2017) are major reference points. Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999) produced procedural changes in how Northern researchers work with indigenous communities. New chairs in Black studies, journals like *Postcolonial Studies* and *Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education and Society*, and increased visibility of African, Latin American and Asian sociologists at international conferences are not nothing.

But the co-optation thesis is also serious. The global academic prestige economy still concentrates value in Anglo-American institutions, English-language publication, and citation networks centred on the Global North. A Brazilian sociologist publishing in Portuguese in São Paulo journals is read less than one publishing in English in *American Sociological Review*. University funding depends on "impact" legible to Northern policy and to ranking systems (REF, Times Higher Education) that are themselves Northern. The figure of the "decolonising scholar" can become a hireable brand for Northern universities signalling progress without changing structures. Spivak warned about exactly this: the metropolitan academy's ability to absorb critique into its CV culture.

Tuck and Yang argued famously that decolonisation "is not a metaphor": real decolonisation involves land return to indigenous peoples, not just syllabus reform. From this perspective, decolonising the curriculum is the co-optation that lets settlers feel they have done something while leaving the underlying structure intact. Rhodes Must Fall organisers in Cape Town pressed exactly this: take down the statue, yes, but also redistribute land and confront the university's continuing whiteness in staffing.

Yet the strong co-optation thesis has its own problems. If all reforms are co-optation, no reform is worth fighting for, which paralyses politics. It underestimates the agency of decolonising scholars and students: UCL's reading lists were rewritten; Rhodes came down; Germany agreed to return Benin bronzes. It assumes "the institution" is more unified than it is. Universities are riven by internal debates, precarious academics with critical politics, and student movements.

A middle position. Decolonisation is possible at the curricular and methodological levels, harder but possible at the institutional level. It is co-opted where it remains within the existing prestige economy, uncoopted where it transforms how knowledge is produced, who counts as a knower, and what counts as theory. 2026 sociology is more decolonised than 2010 sociology and less decolonised than 2036 sociology may be.

Three examples. First, the Du Bois case: Aldon Morris's *The Scholar Denied* (2015) substantially shifted American sociology curricula. Second, indigenous methodologies have transformed Australian, New Zealand and Canadian social research; indigenous scholars set the terms. Third, African theory: Mbembe at WiSER, Sylvia Tamale on African feminism, Ndlovu-Gatsheni on coloniality from Zimbabwe, theorists read globally on their own terms, not as data for Northern theorists.

Failures must be acknowledged. Staffing and PhD demographics have changed less than reading lists; academic precarity disproportionately affects scholars of colour and from the Global South; English-language hegemony has intensified. AI's English-language bias, large language models trained on Anglophone web text, is the latest chapter of the imperial archive.

In conclusion: can sociology be decolonised? Yes, partially. Is decolonisation always already co-opted? Sometimes, but not always; co-optation is itself a political question, not a fixed metaphysical fact. The decolonising scholar does the work knowing the institution will try to absorb it but refusing to grant it the last word. Connell's Southern theory, Quijano's coloniality of power, Mbembe's necropolitics, Spivak's epistemic violence, Smith's decolonising methodology are now resources of the discipline. The question for 2026 is not whether to start but how to deepen and protect what has begun.

### Mock 9, How convincing do you find Bhabha's account of hybridity and mimicry?

Homi Bhabha's account of hybridity and mimicry, developed in *The Location of Culture* (1994), is among the most influential and most contested contributions to postcolonial theory. I assess it on three criteria, analytical sharpness, empirical reach, political adequacy, and conclude that it is moderately convincing: a real contribution that supplements Said and Hall but vulnerable to the charges that it depoliticises power and overstates colonised agency.

Hybridity names the mixed forms that emerge in colonial encounter rather than the supposed purity of either coloniser or colonised culture. The "third space" is the zone where new identities are negotiated. Mimicry is more specific: the colonised subject who imitates the coloniser does not simply submit; the imitation is "almost the same but not quite" (Bhabha, 1994: 122 [VERIFY p.?]), and the difference destabilises the original. The colonial subject who learns English, wears a suit and quotes Shakespeare exposes the contingency of "Englishness" as a racial essence. Mimicry, for Bhabha, is agency from within the colonial relation.

Three things make the account analytically sharp. First, it gives postcolonial theory a vocabulary for productive ambiguity neither Said nor Hall systematically theorised: the ICS clerk who knew British procedure better than the Briton he served, the African nationalist deploying Enlightenment categories against European empire. Second, hybridity captures contemporary diasporic experience: the British-Pakistani novelist, the Nigerian-American afrobeats artist, the Indian software engineer in Silicon Valley. Third, mimicry is precise: "almost the same but not quite" gives a measurable diagnostic of where colonial imitation falls short and what the shortfall reveals about the original's claim to universality.

The empirical reach is real, Rushdie's *Midnight's Children* (1981), Kureishi's *Buddha of Suburbia* (1990), Smith's *White Teeth* (2000), Shamsie's *Burnt Shadows* (2009), Adichie's *Americanah* (2013); afrobeats, dancehall, K-pop, grime; Bollywood and Nollywood travelling globally.

But three criticisms have force. First, hybridity can depoliticise. If everyone is hybrid, the asymmetries Said and Hall diagnose can fade from view. Aijaz Ahmad in *In Theory* (1992) argued that Bhabha's hybridity is a culturalist celebration of cosmopolitan elites that ignores structured global inequalities. The Bangladeshi garment worker is hybrid (jeans, Android, Bollywood) but her hybridity is structured by a global capitalist hierarchy that hybridity-talk obscures. Said himself, in *Culture and Imperialism* (1993), pushed back against what he saw as Bhabha making the colonial encounter sound more two-sided than it was.

Second, hybridity flattens power. Bhabha's mimicry has a utopian edge, but empirically mimicry has often been co-optation. The ICS clerk was admitted to the lower ranks of imperial administration; many of the most successful mimics became enthusiastic agents of empire. Fanon's *peau noire, masques blancs* diagnoses mimicry as injury, not agency: the Black West Indian who internalises Frenchness is alienated, not subversive. Bhabha's reading of Fanon as theorist of hybridity is contested; Fanon's text resists it on most pages.

Third, hybridity is not equally available. The post-colonial elite is more hybrid (in Bhabha's celebratory sense) than the poor. Spivak's question of subaltern speech reminds us hybridity-talk can attend to those most like the metropole and ignore those who are not. The Adivasi displaced by an Indian dam is not usefully hybrid; her position is enforced marginality.

One qualification. Bhabha is most convincing where applied to elites; for subaltern subjects, Fanon's and Spivak's frameworks are more apt. Reading Bhabha alongside Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) and Hall's *Cultural Identity and Diaspora* (1990) gives a fuller, less culturalist account.

Concrete examples sharpen the verdict. Kureishi is a Bhabhaian figure par excellence. Modi's Hindutva is a darker case: the RSS-BJP politics deploys hybrid forms (English, parliamentary procedure, modern media) while pursuing a militantly anti-hybrid politics of Hindu purity. Bhabha must acknowledge that hybridity can produce its own opposites. Vaccine apartheid during COVID and the 2022 Ukraine framing were moments when Hallian asymmetry asserted itself against hybridity-talk; the framework's analytic purchase was limited.

In conclusion, Bhabha's account is moderately convincing. It supplements Said and Hall by capturing productive ambiguity and contemporary diasporic experience; it has empirical reach in literary, musical and filmic production. But it depoliticises asymmetries Said and Hall correctly diagnose, over-attributes agency to elite mimics, and is unequally available across the post-colonial class structure. Hybridity is a useful supplement, not a replacement, for Said and Hall; the contemporary reader holds it alongside Fanon, Spivak, Mohanty and Dussel, deploying each where it illuminates best.

### Mock 10, Using the work of at least two authors, discuss postcolonial nationalisms (Modi's Hindutva, Bolsonaro, Erdoğan) and what they reveal about the limits of postcolonial theory.

Postcolonial nationalisms in the 2010s and 2020s, Hindu nationalism under Modi, Bolsonaro's right-wing nationalism in Brazil, Erdoğan's Islamist-nationalist consolidation in Turkey, with parallels in Orbán's Hungary and Duterte's Philippines, challenge a postcolonial theory written largely against Western empire. These nationalisms speak the language of resistance to Western hegemony while operating as exclusionary, majoritarian, often violent projects. Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) is the starting point because Fanon anticipated the problem. "The Pitfalls of National Consciousness" warned that nationalist movements led by the indigenous bourgeoisie risk substituting a national bourgeoisie for the colonial administration without changing the underlying structure. The Indian National Congress inherited the colonial state apparatus largely intact; Brazil's post-1985 transition did not transform rural and racial inequalities; Kemalist secularism excluded Kurdish and religious-traditional populations. The conditions for the Modi-Bolsonaro-Erdoğan turn were laid in the failures Fanon predicted.

But Fanon expected the disappointed masses to move leftwards. The actual movement has been rightwards. The BJP, the AKP and Bolsonaro's coalition mobilise the same disappointment but turn it against minorities, migrants, intellectuals and the secular liberal middle class. Fanon's framework underweights the cultural and religious vectors along which disappointment now travels.

Hall's discourse of the West and the Rest is sharper for the discursive dimension. Hall identified four functions: classification, representation, comparison, evaluation (Hall, 1992: 280). Postcolonial nationalisms apply this discourse internally. Modi's Hindutva classifies Indians into Hindu majority and non-Hindu minorities; represents Hindu civilisation as Indian essence and Muslim presence as foreign accretion; compares "us" against "them"; evaluates Hindu culture as superior. Erdoğan's project classifies Turks into Sunni majority and secular-Alevi-Kurdish minorities; Bolsonaro's deployed an anti-PT, anti-indigenous discourse that pitted "real Brazilians" against indigenous peoples, Afro-Brazilians, queer people and the left intelligentsia. The Hallian framework applies almost without modification to internal Othering.

The postcolonial critique of Western Othering is therefore right and structurally generalisable, but the postcolonial state itself becomes an Othering agent. Said's Orientalism applies internally too: Hindutva is internal Orientalism, casting Indian Muslims in the role British colonialism cast Indians. Mbembe, Pankaj Mishra and Partha Chatterjee theorise the post-colonial state as ambivalent inheritor of colonial discursive logic.

Mohanty's Third World Woman critique is also relevant. Modi's Hindutva mobilises the Hindu woman as needing protection from Muslim "love jihad," reproducing the gendered-Othering Mohanty critiqued, but with Hindu women as protected and Muslim men as threat. The structure (woman as symbolic stake, gendered threat from Other) is preserved; the identities are inverted.

Three limits this reveals. First, postcolonial theory under-theorises the post-colonial state. It grew out of literary and cultural studies and reads the state as discursive site rather than coercive apparatus. Mbembe's *On the Postcolony* (2001) and *Necropolitics* (2003) have begun this work; the field needs more. Second, anti-Western critique is no longer a reliable marker of emancipation. Modi speaks multipolar order; Erdoğan deploys anti-EU and anti-US rhetoric; Bolsonaro courted Trump while opposing the international left. Postcolonial theorists need to distinguish anti-imperialist critique from right-populist anti-Western nationalism that operates with imperial discursive logic internally. Third, internal Orientalisms are under-analysed. Said's framework was West-on-East; we now need East-on-East, majority-on-minority within post-colonial states.

Concrete examples sharpen the analysis. The 2019 Indian Citizenship Amendment Act codified religious discrimination; the 2019 abrogation of Article 370 reorganised the federation against the Muslim-majority Kashmir Valley; the 2024 Ram Mandir consecration on the Babri Masjid site marked a symbolic culmination. Turkey's 2016 coup attempt was followed by purges of academics and journalists; the 2017 constitutional amendments concentrated power in the presidency. Bolsonaro's environmental policy facilitated Amazon deforestation with disproportionate effects on indigenous communities. Each deploys techniques the field would readily diagnose if a Western state were the agent.

In conclusion, postcolonial nationalisms reveal real limits of postcolonial theory but require its extension, not its rejection. Fanon predicted the structural disappointment; Hall's framework applies internally as well as externally; Said's Orientalism turns into internal Orientalism; Mohanty's Third World Woman becomes the symbolically protected majority woman against the demonised minority man. The contemporary theorist must apply the framework to the post-colonial state itself, not only to Western empires.
