# L6 — Standpoints, Perspectives and Epistemology

**Module:** SC301 Rethinking Modernity
**Lecture:** 6
**Theme:** Knowledge, situatedness, feminist and postcolonial epistemologies; the critique of objectivism.

---

## 1. Key concepts (≤200 words)

**Epistemology** asks how and what we know, and what justifies a knowledge-claim. **Traditional epistemology** (which Harding labels **objectivism**) treats knowledge as independent of the knower; philosophical objectivity assumes a real external world and methodological objectivity demands value-free, dispassionate research. Feminist critique exposes this neutrality as itself a position: the universal knower performs **the god trick** (Haraway), speaking from nowhere while actually speaking from a dominant location. **Situated knowledge** insists all thought bears the fingerprints of its producers. **Standpoint epistemology** (Hartsock, Harding, Smith) argues that marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate less partial accounts. **Strong objectivity** treats the knower as part of the object of inquiry, demanding **strong reflexivity**. **The reference man** (Criado-Perez) names the Caucasian-male default in crash-test dummies, PPE, and pharmacology. **Bifurcation** — mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, men/women — sustains masculinist science. **Perspectival epistemology** brings multiple positions into dialogue. Gilroy reconstructs modernity from **the slaves' standpoint**; hooks' **the oppositional gaze** treats looking as resistance; Collins' **the matrix of domination** maps intersecting axes of oppression.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded quotes

The Harding reading is the spine of the lecture and is built around a single counter-intuitive claim: that situated, politically engaged knowledge produces **more**, not less, objectivity. Harding opens by arguing that "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women", but that the disciplines "permitted no conceptual frameworks in which women as a group… became the subjects — the authors — of knowledge" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 4). The implied speaker of scientific sentences "was supposed to be humanity in general", performing what Haraway names "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (p. 4). This is the core diagnosis: objectivism universalises a particular location.

Harding insists the problem is not "bad science" but the conceptual frameworks themselves, which "promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns, which ensured that the knowledge produced through them was always socially situated" (p. 5). "Commitment to an objectivity defined as maximizing social neutrality was not itself socially neutral in its effects" (p. 5). She lists what androcentric frameworks rendered invisible: violence against women treated as women "asking for it"; the unpaid double day naturalised; menstruation and menopause medicalised; rationality narrowed to the instrumental rationality of institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (pp. 5-6). The "remedy", Harding writes, "was to start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities (or labor)" (p. 6) — but never as ethnography of women alone; standpoint projects "must 'study up'" (p. 6).

In chapter 8 Harding consolidates this into **strong objectivity**. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*; it is too weak" (p. 128). Conventional method eliminates only those values "that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (pp. 136-137) — leaving culture-wide assumptions untouched. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge. Thus, strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (p. 136). Crucially, "a maximally critical study of scientists and their communities can be done only from the perspective of those whose lives have been marginalized by such communities" (p. 136).

Harding pre-empts three charges. Against the ethnocentrism charge she notes that "standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (p. 129) — Hegel was not a slave, Marx not a wage-labourer. Against the relativism charge: "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (pp. 131-132). Against the essentialism charge: the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (p. 134), starting from "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (p. 134).

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* sits alongside Harding as a parallel critique of the gendered structure of vision. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (p. 47). Berger shows that the European nude is structured by an unseen male spectator — "the principal protagonist is never painted. He is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man" (p. 54). This anticipates hooks: power resides in who is licensed to look. hooks' oppositional gaze is the inverted, resistant move: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 116).

The lecture chains these arguments into one claim: epistemology is political. Whose body counts as the reference body — in crash tests, drug trials, PPE — is decided by who is in the room when the standards are set. Criado-Perez's evidence (women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in crashes; medical research excluding women through the 1970s-80s; PPE failing female health workers in the COVID pandemic) is not a series of accidents but the empirical face of objectivism.

### 2024 (Q5): In what ways does the feminist epistemology overturn the canons and practices of traditional epistemology?

Feminist epistemology does not abolish epistemology; it rebuilds it. I will argue that it overturns the traditional canon in three connected ways: by exposing the universal knower as a partial knower, by reconstructing objectivity as situated rather than detached, and by reorganising the practical institutions through which knowledge is produced. Together these moves do not weaken the search for truth — they make it more rigorous.

The traditional canon, as the lecture set out, rests on two definitions of objectivity. Philosophically, it posits a real world independent of the knower, which science discovers; methodologically, it demands that the researcher be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding's notion of objectivism collapses these into a single claim: that knowledge can be produced from no particular location. Donna Haraway's account of situated knowledge names this fantasy "the god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 4). The first overturning of the canon is this exposure: the universal knower of modern Western philosophy was always a specific knower — male, white, bourgeois, European — whose social location was concealed by being treated as default. Spender's phrase, cited in the lecture, captures the inversion sharply: "objectivity is male subjectivity" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980).

The second overturning is the reconstruction of objectivity itself. Critics often suppose that feminist epistemology must choose between objectivity and relativism. Harding refuses this dichotomy. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 128). Conventional method, she argues, can only eliminate the values that "differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (p. 137). Culture-wide assumptions — what an entire research community already takes for granted — slip through. Harding's notion of strong objectivity demands that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical plane as the object: "strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (p. 136). The canon's commitment to detachment is replaced by a commitment to disclosed, examined, accountable location.

The third overturning is institutional and practical. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* documents what happens when the reference man becomes the default. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg) leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. PPE designed for European and American male bodies failed female nurses during the COVID pandemic. Standard office temperatures were calibrated to the male metabolic rate. Pharmaceutical doses were calculated against a "Caucasian Man" whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). Clinical trials excluded women through the 1970s and 1980s, contributing to under-recognised symptoms in heart disease and to pain being systematically under-treated in women. The Henrietta Lacks case and the long history of medical experimentation on Black women add a racialised layer to the same structural failure. These are not failures of methodology in the narrow sense; they are failures of who is in the room when the categories are set.

Feminist epistemology overturns the canon by changing the personnel and procedures of knowledge production. Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, "in and out of the home", gives them access to dimensions of social reality the dominant group cannot see, but only when that experience is articulated through political struggle. As Harding insists in the slide quoted from her 1991 book, "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). A standpoint is not an opinion poll of women; it is a worked-up critical perspective.

This third overturning extends beyond gender. Collins' matrix of domination, hooks' oppositional gaze, and Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint generalise the move. Each insists that historically marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate critical insights unavailable from the centre. Police data on missing women, Black maternal mortality, the gender data gap in AI training sets — these become visible only when the categories of analysis are themselves contested.

A critic might object that this remains too tied to identity, risking essentialism or fragmentation. Harding's reply, which I find persuasive, is that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (p. 134); it is the thinker "whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (p. 134). The alternative — pretending no one is in the room — is what produced the reference man in the first place.

Feminist epistemology therefore overturns the canon at three levels: at the level of the knower (exposing the god trick), at the level of objectivity (replacing detachment with strong reflexivity), and at the level of institutions (reorganising who counts and who is counted). It is, as the lecture put it, not about undermining knowledge but about producing "better, more robust forms of knowledge".

### 2025 (Q8): Why is the concept of perspectives important for understanding the status of epistemology within modernity?

The concept of perspectives matters for modernity because modernity inherited an epistemology that disavowed perspective altogether. The modern view from nowhere — the Enlightenment ideal of a universal knower — is the conceit Harding calls objectivism and Haraway names the god trick. I will argue that recovering the concept of perspectives is decisive for understanding the status of epistemology in modernity for three reasons: it diagnoses modernity's epistemological fiction, it explains the political effects of that fiction, and it offers a route to a more accurate, plural science.

First, modernity defined itself epistemically against superstition by claiming a method that transcended location. The bifurcations rehearsed in the lecture — mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, science/superstition, objectivity/subjectivity, men/women — encode this. Science is on the rational, cultural, mind-side; superstition, nature, body and women on the other. Harding's notion of strong objectivity exposes the trick: "All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 128). The pretence that scientific thought escapes this is "a delusion — and a historical identifiable one" (p. 128). The concept of perspectives names what modernity tried to hide: that every claim is made from somewhere.

The slide-headline of the lecture put the consequence sharply: "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity." This is not a paradox once we see that the alternative — pretending we have no perspective — does not eliminate perspective, it only hides it. Hidden perspectives become culture-wide assumptions transported into research findings.

Second, perspectives matter because their absence has political effects. The lecture opens this through Criado-Perez's documentation of "a world designed for men". When the reference man is the only body imagined, women die at higher rates in car crashes, are misdiagnosed in cardiac emergencies, and receive doses calculated for bodies they do not have. Medical research excluded women through the 1970s and 1980s. Pain is under-treated in women. PPE failed female health workers in COVID. Police miss recurring patterns in missing-women cases. AI training data, drawn from histories where dominant perspectives dominated, encodes those perspectives at scale. Black maternal mortality in the United States is over three times the white rate. None of these is a methodological accident; each is what happens when a single perspective is universalised.

Modernity's pretence of no-perspective is itself a perspective — and a powerful one. As Harding writes, "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge and the claims resulting from adherence to such standards should be found preferable by all rational creatures, past, present, and future" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 131). The concept of perspectives therefore matters because it lets us name the political work that no-perspective claims do.

Third, the concept of perspectives matters because it offers a constructive epistemology rather than a sceptical one. Standpoint epistemology — Hartsock's feminist standpoint, Smith's everyday-life sociology, Collins' matrix of domination, Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint, hooks' oppositional gaze — does not say all perspectives are equal. It says different positions afford different insights and that some positions, when politically articulated, see what dominant positions cannot. Hartsock's two claims, reproduced on the slide, are that men's and women's understandings differ because of their material labour and access to public space, and that the dominant group's views attempt to "structure the world for everybody". Standpoint, the lecture stresses, "is not automatic — it is an achievement won from political struggle".

Bringing multiple perspectives together does not produce a single objective view but a "multiple objectivity": different vantages corroborating, contesting and supplementing one another. This is what the lecture calls a perspectival epistemology. It refuses bifurcation — refuses to choose between universal objectivity and relativism — and produces what Harding calls strong objectivity: a knowledge that is more accountable because its location is disclosed.

Modernity's self-image as the epoch of universal reason is therefore both a description and a problem. As a description it captures the ambition that gave us science; as a problem it captures the suppression of perspective that gave us the reference man. The concept of perspectives lets us hold both: it allows us to keep the ambition (truth, evidence, testability) while abandoning the fiction (the view from nowhere). Gilroy's claim that "the time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" is not anti-modern — it is a demand that modernity finally take seriously its own commitment to evidence by including the evidence it has excluded. hooks' insistence that "there is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115) generalises the point: who looks, and from where, is constitutive of what is known.

The status of epistemology in modernity, then, is unstable without the concept of perspectives. With it, modernity becomes intelligible — and correctable.

### 2023 (Q8): What do sociologists mean by the term "standpoint epistemology"? How useful is this approach?

Standpoint epistemology is the claim that what we know is shaped by where we stand in social structure, and that some social locations, when their inhabitants reflect critically on them, afford less partial accounts of how a society works than other locations do. I will define the term, distinguish it from neighbouring positions, and then assess its usefulness, concluding that it is highly useful, especially when paired with Harding's notion of strong objectivity.

The standpoint thesis was systematised by Nancy Hartsock's 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism", developed by Dorothy Smith's everyday-world sociology, Harding's three books in the 1980s and 1990s, and Patricia Hill Collins' *Black Feminist Thought*. The lecture summarised it in three propositions. First, men's and women's understandings of the world differ because of the different labour they do and their different access to public space. Second, the dominant group's view attempts to structure the world for everyone. Third, because the feminist standpoint reveals the partiality of the dominant view, it opens up a path to liberation. Crucially, the lecture insists that "a standpoint is not automatic — it is an achievement won from political struggle".

This last clause distinguishes standpoint epistemology from three positions with which it is often confused. It is not identity politics: being a woman does not guarantee a feminist standpoint, and Harding emphasises that men can produce feminist knowledge by "starting from women's lives" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 135). It is not relativism: standpoint theory denies that all perspectives are equally adequate to a given reality. As Harding writes, "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (p. 131). And it is not ethnocentrism: the claim that women's lives are a good place to start thinking does not assert that women's culture is superior; it asserts that marginal locations offer epistemic resources that central ones suppress.

Three concrete examples show what the approach offers. First, the lecture's reference-man material. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male; women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. Standpoint epistemology predicts this kind of distortion: when only one social position is in the room when categories are set, the categories will reflect that position. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* (2019) documents this across pharmacology, PPE, urban planning and AI. Clinical trials excluded women through the 1970s-80s; medical research treated the male body as default; pain in women is under-diagnosed.

Second, Collins' analysis of African-American domestic workers as "outsiders within". Their location in white households gave them empirical access to how dominant groups thought when they thought no Black worker was listening. The standpoint here is not an opinion but a vantage that becomes critical when articulated through Black feminist theory. Collins' matrix of domination shows how race, class, gender and sexuality intersect — extending standpoint thinking beyond a single axis.

Third, Gilroy's *Black Atlantic*. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view." Modernity, told from Liverpool and Glasgow, is industrial revolution and Enlightenment; told from the Middle Passage and the plantation, it is also slavery, racialised capitalism and the long denial of personhood. hooks' oppositional gaze is the same move at the level of vision: "there is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115).

The usefulness of the approach is therefore considerable. It explains structural patterns (the gender data gap, racialised medical neglect) that frameworks ignoring social position cannot easily account for. It generates research programmes (feminist science studies, Black sociology, postcolonial historiography). It improves science by widening the evidence base — which is exactly Harding's argument for strong objectivity: more locations, more reflexivity, more rigour.

There are objections. Critics worry about essentialism — assuming a unitary "women's standpoint". Harding answers that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (p. 134), including the liberal, socialist, Sandinista, Islamic and Third World feminisms which start from different women's lives. Critics worry about regress: who decides which standpoint is more illuminating? Harding's answer is empirical — judge by which produces more accurate, less partial accounts of nature and social relations. Critics worry about politics replacing evidence, but the lecture and the reading insist that "good politics" produces "good science" by widening evidence and exposing assumptions, not by replacing them.

A more serious worry is that standpoint epistemology is most useful for the social sciences and less obviously applicable to physical science. Harding addresses this by pointing out that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts — "wild animals" are categorised differently by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (p. 140, n.17). The point generalises: the agenda, funding, and ethical limits of physical science are social.

Standpoint epistemology is thus useful both as diagnosis and as method. It does not replace evidence; it widens it. Its central insight — that the disclosed location of knowledge is a resource rather than a contaminant — is one of the more durable contributions of late twentieth-century sociology.

### 2022 (Q9): Does feminist epistemology reject the concept of objectivity?

The short answer is no. Feminist epistemology, as articulated by Harding, Haraway, Hartsock, Smith and Collins, does not reject objectivity; it rejects objectivism — the conflation of objectivity with detached neutrality. I will argue this in three steps: first, by distinguishing objectivity from objectivism; second, by showing that Harding's notion of strong objectivity is a strengthening, not a weakening, of objectivity; third, by considering and rebutting the charge that feminist epistemology slides into relativism.

The lecture set out two traditional definitions of objectivity. The philosophical sense holds that there is a real world independent of human thought, which science discovers. The methodological sense holds that researchers must be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding labels the conflation of these two definitions, and the assumption that science as actually practised achieves them, as **objectivism**. "Harding refers to the traditional notion that science (natural and social) should be objective in the sense of value-free and without bias… as objectivism" (lecture slide quoting Harding, 1991: 143). She continues: "the concept of value-free, impartial, dispassionate research… has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (p. 143). Objectivism, in other words, claims neutrality but operationalises it as agreement among a homogeneous community. Anything that community already takes for granted slips through.

Feminist epistemology rejects this. It does not reject the underlying ambition — that knowledge should track reality and not merely opinion. Harding is explicit: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 131). The lecture restated the headline: "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge."

What feminist epistemology offers in place of objectivism is Harding's notion of strong objectivity. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (p. 128). Strong objectivity requires that "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (p. 136). The knower is part of the system being known; the social position of the researcher is itself open to scientific scrutiny. This is what Harding calls "strong reflexivity" (p. 136). It is more rigorous than weak objectivity because it widens the field of what must be examined to include the researcher's own assumptions.

The empirical case for this is in the lecture's reference-man material. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male; PPE designed for European male bodies; pharmacology calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male; clinical trials excluding women through the 1970s-80s. Each of these is the product of research that believed itself objective. None of them were objective in any meaningful sense: they encoded a single social position as universal. Strong objectivity demands that the location of the researcher and the research community be disclosed and examined; weak objectivity calls anything not visible to the dominant community "neutral".

The Berger reading supports this from the side of culture. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at. This determines not only most relations between men and women but also the relation of women to themselves" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The European nude exemplifies a pseudo-objective image — the woman painted as if free of context — which is in fact organised around an unpainted male spectator. The "objectivity" of the painting is a covert positioning. Feminist epistemology applied to science finds the same pattern: covert positioning passed off as no positioning.

A common objection is that feminist epistemology, once it admits that all knowledge is situated, has no resources to distinguish better from worse claims and so collapses into relativism. Harding pre-empts this. "Standpoint theory provides arguments for the claim that some social situations are scientifically better than others as places from which to start off knowledge projects" (p. 131). Some locations afford insights others suppress. The standard is empirical: which research programme generates less partial and distorted accounts? Standpoint feminism's record — producing accounts of unpaid domestic labour, of gendered violence, of the medical neglect of women, of the gender data gap — is itself the evidence.

A subtler objection is that strong objectivity is just objectivity with reflexivity bolted on. I would reply that the bolt-on changes the structure. ### 2021 (Q9): Outline and assess the feminist epistemologist critique of "masculinist" approaches to knowledge.

The feminist critique of masculinist knowledge has three components: a critique of who has produced knowledge, a critique of what counts as knowledge, and a critique of how knowledge has been validated. I will outline each, illustrate with examples, and then assess the critique, concluding that it is largely successful and remains the most productive sociological account of the politics of knowledge.

The first component is a critique of personnel. As the lecture put it, "most philosophers and scientists and sociologists (in the West) have been men. As Sandra Harding puts it, this means that the concerns, the topics and procedures of science, philosophy and much social theory are 'man-made'". Dale Spender extended the point to language itself. This is not a complaint about individual men. It is a structural observation: when a research community is homogeneous along a major axis of social stratification, the assumptions it shares will not show up to it as assumptions. Harding makes this explicit: conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 137). Whatever the entire community takes for granted is invisible to its method.

The second component is a critique of content. The lecture catalogued the empirical consequences via Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women*. Crash-test dummies based on the 50th-percentile male leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash. PPE based on male European bodies failed women health workers during COVID. Office temperatures were calibrated to male metabolic rates. Pharmaceutical doses were designed around a 70kg Caucasian man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not." Medical research excluded women through the 1970s and 1980s. Pain in women is under-diagnosed. Police data on missing women have repeatedly missed pattern offending — the failures around Stephen Port and around Sarah Everard's killing involved categories of risk that did not see what was in front of them. Black maternal mortality in the US runs at three to four times the white rate. AI training sets, drawn from histories shaped by these exclusions, propagate them.

The third component is a critique of validation — the standards by which a claim is accepted as knowledge. The masculinist tradition operationalises objectivity as detachment and value-freedom. Feminist epistemology argues this is itself a value, a position, and one that systematically disadvantages knowledge claims emerging from marginalised lives. Harding's strong objectivity replaces detachment with "strong reflexivity" (p. 136): the researcher's location is itself an object of inquiry. Haraway's situated knowledge replaces the god trick with "feminist objectivity… quite simply situated knowledges". Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, articulated through political struggle, generates accounts that "reveal the partiality of the dominant (male) vision of social relations".

These three critiques converge on a single claim: masculinist knowledge is partial knowledge passing as universal. Berger demonstrates this from the side of vision. "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The supposed neutrality of the European nude is the suppression of a male spectator who organises the entire image. hooks generalises: "there is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). Who sees, and from where, is constitutive of what is known.

Assessing the critique, I find it largely persuasive on three grounds. Empirically, the reference-man examples are not anecdotes; they are documented, systemic, and lethal. Methodologically, the move from objectivism to strong objectivity strengthens rather than weakens science by widening reflexivity. Politically, the critique has been generative: it has produced research programmes — feminist science studies, Black feminist sociology, postcolonial historiography, disability studies — that have changed what counts as a legitimate question.

Three reservations are worth registering. First, the critique risks essentialism if "masculinist" is treated as a property of all men's thought rather than a property of a structure. Harding addresses this: men can produce feminist knowledge by starting from women's lives (p. 135), and women can reproduce masculinist assumptions. Second, the critique can be misread as anti-science. Harding's defence of strong objectivity rebuts this; the lecture's slogan — "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge" — should be taken at face value. Third, there is a question of scope: does the critique apply to physical sciences as fully as to social? Harding argues yes, on the grounds that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts, but the case is harder to make for, say, the standard model of particle physics than for medical research. The strongest version of the critique applies where the object of knowledge is itself socially constituted — which covers most of what social science studies and a large portion of biomedical and applied science.

The critique succeeds. It diagnoses a real failure of self-understanding in the masculinist tradition; it offers concrete tools (strong objectivity, strong reflexivity, situated knowledge, the matrix of domination) to repair that failure; and it has demonstrated empirically that doing so produces better knowledge.

### Mock 1: "The god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all." Discuss with reference to Haraway.

Haraway's phrase the god trick is the single sharpest diagnosis of what is wrong with the inherited scientific ideal of objectivity, and I want to defend it on three grounds: as an accurate description of how mainstream science represents itself, as an account of why that self-representation has had damaging empirical consequences, and as the foundation for Haraway's positive proposal that knowledge is constitutively situated. The quotation, reproduced in Harding's *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, captures the conceit precisely: a knower who speaks from no body, no time, no class position, no gender, and who therefore claims authority over every body, every time, every position. I will argue that this is not just a rhetorical jab but a structural claim.

The inherited ideal of objectivity has two components: the metaphysical claim that there is a world independent of our beliefs about it, and the methodological claim that good researchers should minimise the influence of their own biases. Haraway does not deny either component. What she denies is a third claim, often smuggled in with the first two: that good method as currently practised actually achieves the second. The god trick is the device by which the smuggling happens. It rebrands the perspective of a specific knower (historically: white, male, bourgeois, European) as no perspective at all.

The empirical case for Haraway's diagnosis is the gender data gap. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male — 1.77m, 76kg — through most of the post-war period; the consequence, documented by Criado-Perez in *Invisible Women*, is that women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. PPE during the COVID pandemic was sized to European and American male bodies, with the result that female nurses (a substantial majority of the nursing workforce) wore badly-fitting masks and gowns. Pharmacological doses were calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, *Invisible Women*, 2019). Zolpidem (Ambien) was prescribed at male doses for two decades before the FDA halved the recommended dose for women. Each of these is a god-trick failure: the engineering and the pharmacology presented themselves as knowledge of human bodies in general but were actually knowledge of one kind of body, generalised by fiat.

Harding's gloss on Haraway makes the structural point explicit: "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge and the claims resulting from adherence to such standards should be found preferable by all rational creatures, past, present, and future" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 131). The god trick is sociologically possible only from positions of social power; it is the epistemology of dominance reflected back as universal reason.

Haraway's positive proposal — situated knowledge — flows directly from the diagnosis. "Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 127). Knowledge is always made from a body, a place, a history, and a set of material practices. To declare this is not to give up on objectivity; it is to deepen it. Disclosed location is more scientifically informative than concealed location, because the conditions of knowing become part of what can be evaluated. This is what Harding develops as strong objectivity: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (p. 136). Haraway and Harding converge here. The god trick is the foil against which both build a positive epistemology.

A common objection is that abandoning the god trick collapses into relativism — that if every claim is situated, no claim is better than another. Haraway and Harding both reject this inference. Harding is sharp: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (p. 131). Situated knowledge is not the claim that all situations are equally informative; it is the claim that the situation must be disclosed, and that some situations — particularly those of historically marginalised groups — afford insights that dominant situations suppress. The crash-dummy case is the proof: the claim that women are 47% more likely to be injured is not a relativist alternative to the claim that crash dummies are objective. It is the more accurate claim, generated from a research programme that started from women's bodies.

A more sophisticated objection is that the god trick is a caricature of how scientists actually understand their work. Real scientists, the objection runs, acknowledge uncertainty, peer-review their results, and revise in light of evidence. Haraway's answer, which I find convincing, is that the question is not whether individual scientists are humble but whether the institutional self-representation of science presents its findings as situated or as universal. Pharmacopoeias, engineering standards, and AI training pipelines treat outputs as universal. That is where the god trick lives.

A third site that confirms the diagnosis is contemporary AI. Buolamwini and Gebru's *Gender Shades* study found facial-recognition error rates around 0.8% for white men and up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women — the same reference-body problem at digital scale. The Obermeyer 2019 *Science* study found a US healthcare-allocation algorithm systematically under-prioritised Black patients because it used historical spending as a proxy for need. ChatGPT and similar models reproduce underrepresented dialects badly because the training distribution is skewed. The systems are god-trick machines: they generalise from particular populations and claim universal applicability.

The god trick is therefore not a slogan but a diagnostic concept. It names the operation by which partial knowledge is laundered into universal knowledge, and it points to the structural reforms — disclosed location, diversified research communities, demographically disaggregated data — that produce more, not less, objectivity.

### Mock 3: What does Haraway mean by the god trick, and why does she treat partiality as a methodological virtue?

The question has two parts; I will answer each in turn and then connect them. By the god trick Donna Haraway means the move in which a knower presents their location-bound, body-bound, history-bound perspective as if it were no perspective at all — what the lecture-quoted formulation calls "speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 4). By partiality as a methodological virtue she means that the disclosure of one's location, and the explicit acknowledgement that one sees only some of the picture, is a stronger basis for objectivity than the pretence of seeing all of it. The two halves are connected: the god trick is the vice; situated, partial knowledge is the virtue.

I take the first half first. The god trick is not the claim that scientists are arrogant individuals. It is a description of an institutional epistemology. Haraway's diagnosis is that these outputs are in fact produced from very specific locations. The clearest illustrations are the reference-man cases catalogued by Criado-Perez in *Invisible Women*. Pharmacological dosing was calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not". In each case the institutional output denies its location of production. That denial is the god trick.

Why is the denial damaging? Because, as Harding adds, conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 137). Assumptions shared by the entire research community do not show up to that community as assumptions. The god trick is institutionally productive because it makes these shared assumptions invisible. The clinical-trials exclusion of women through the 1970s and 1980s, the Henrietta Lacks extraction of HeLa cells without consent, the police data on missing women that repeatedly missed serial offenders — these are produced by communities that did not see their own location as a location.

First, partiality is empirically more accurate than universalism. The crash-dummy data is the proof: the more honest claim is "this dummy represents 50th-percentile male bodies". That partial claim opens to correction; the universal claim "this dummy represents passengers" closes to correction. The Buolamwini and Gebru *Gender Shades* study generalises the point to AI: the partial claim "this facial-recognition system was trained on a population skewed toward white men" predicts the system's failure rate of up to 34.7% on darker-skinned women; the universal claim "this is facial recognition" obscures the prediction.

Second, partiality enables joining. Haraway's situated-knowledge essay argues that partial perspectives, joined together, produce richer accounts than any single perspective. The pandemic experience of care work — disproportionately performed by women, often migrant women — became visible only when multiple partial perspectives (nurses, social workers, family carers, migrant-rights organisations) were articulated together. No god-trick "view from nowhere" would have produced this composite.

Third, partiality is more honest about the conditions of knowledge production. Harding makes this explicit: strong objectivity "requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 136). Reflexivity means examining the conditions under which one's knowledge is made: the funding structure, the research-team composition, the conceptual framework. Partiality is the precondition of reflexivity, because you cannot reflect on a location you deny.

The standard objection is that partiality slides into relativism — if everyone is partial, no claim is better than another. Haraway and Harding both reject the inference. "Judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (p. 131). Partial knowledge is testable, comparable, and ranked: by empirical adequacy, by reflexive transparency, and by the breadth of joined perspectives. The reference-man examples show this in action: the partial standpoint of women's lives produced testably better predictions than the universal standpoint of the reference man.

The god trick and partiality-as-virtue are therefore two sides of one argument. The trick claims a universal vantage and produces parochial knowledge; partiality discloses location and produces, through reflexivity and combination, more reliable knowledge. The lecture's slogan applies precisely: a perspectival approach to knowledge produces more, not less, objectivity.

### Mock 4: Compare Hartsock's feminist standpoint with Haraway's situated knowledge.

The matrix of domination is what happens when you take Hartsock's materialism seriously and notice that there is more than one material axis.

The artificial choice would be to pick one. The honest answer is that contemporary sociology uses both. Standpoint thinking provides the political grounding and the lineage to social struggle; situated knowledge provides the methodological flexibility to handle technology and intersection. Harding's strong objectivity is the bridge, and Collins's matrix is the extension. The two essays of 1983 and 1988 are best read as a single argument articulated twice.

First, they are trained on corpora whose composition is opaque and skewed. The empirical record confirms this. Buolamwini and Gebru's *Gender Shades* logic — error rates of 0.8% for white men and up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women in facial recognition — has analogues in language: LLMs perform worse on African American Vernacular English, on Indigenous languages, on minority dialects. A researcher who uses an LLM to summarise the experiences of a marginalised community without examining whose voices were in the training data, and without checking the output against community testimony, is reproducing the structural failure Harding diagnosed.

Third, they are usable in ways that conform to strong objectivity. The same technology can be deployed reflexively: with disclosure of the model version and training distribution, with auditing across demographic axes, with integration of community voices in evaluation, and with treatment of model outputs as data to be tested rather than findings to be accepted. The pre-registration norms emerging in social science can be extended to LLM use: declare the model, declare the prompt, declare the evaluation protocol, disaggregate the results.

So: do LLMs vindicate or undermine Harding?

The undermining reading runs: LLMs make god-trick research cheaper and faster. A single researcher can now produce plausible-sounding analysis from any vantage on any topic, with no community engagement, no reflexivity, no demographic disaggregation. The structural inequality of who builds and who controls these models — concentrated in a small number of US and Chinese firms — means that the locations from which "general purpose" social knowledge will be produced are narrower than they were a decade ago. On this reading LLMs intensify the very pattern Harding's framework was built to diagnose.

The vindicating reading runs: LLMs make Harding's framework more urgently necessary, and they vindicate her diagnosis by exhibiting at scale the failure modes she predicted in 1991. The framework is empirically confirmed: the prediction was that universalising partial perspectives produces systematic failure, and LLMs are producing exactly that failure in ways that pre-LLM social science could not have generated at the same scale.

My answer is that both readings are correct, and that the disjunction in the question is misleading. LLMs are a stress test for Harding. They vindicate her diagnosis and they intensify the conditions that require her remedy. Whether the remedy will be applied is an open empirical question, and the answer depends on the structural reform of who builds, audits, and uses these systems. The lecture's slogan — that a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity — is the standard against which LLM-based social research should be evaluated. Some research will meet it; much will not.

### Mock 9: How convincing do you find Hartsock's argument that women's labour generates a privileged standpoint?

Nancy Hartsock's argument, set out in her 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism", runs that women's labour — reproductive, domestic, caring — gives women systematic access to dimensions of social reality that men's labour does not, and that this access, when politically articulated, yields a less partial account of social life. Hartsock's three claims, as summarised in the lecture, are these. First, that men's and women's understandings of the world differ because the labour they do and their access to public space differ. Second, that the dominant group's view attempts to structure social reality for everyone, while subordinate groups must in addition understand the dominant view in order to survive within it. Third, that the feminist standpoint, by articulating women's situated experience through political struggle, reveals the partiality of the dominant view and opens the possibility of liberation. The framework follows Marx's structure: just as the proletariat's labour gives it a vantage on capitalist social relations that capital cannot see, women's labour gives them a vantage on patriarchal social relations.

Consider the empirical case for. Care work and domestic labour are not merely under-recognised; they are the precondition of all other social activity, and they are mostly performed by women. The pandemic made this visible at scale: when schools closed and care infrastructures collapsed, the gendered burden of unpaid domestic and caring work spiked, and the productivity of formal economies depended visibly on what feminist economists had been documenting for decades. These are exactly the kinds of distortion Hartsock's framework predicts: when one group's labour and bodies are paradigmatic, the standards constructed around them perform poorly when applied universally.

Sandra Harding's gloss on the standpoint tradition formalises it: "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 133). If knowledge is produced by communities, and the communities of research and design have been demographically homogeneous, then the unrepresented locations in those communities — historically, women, especially working-class and racialised women — will be epistemically resourceful in proportion to their exclusion. Hartsock's argument is the original version of this insight.

Consider, third, the qualifications. The privilege is not automatic. As the lecture insists, "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle"; it is not a property of women as such. Harding makes this explicit: men can produce feminist knowledge "from their particular social situation" (p. 135), and women can reproduce dominant assumptions. The standpoint is conditional on articulation through political and intellectual community. Hartsock's 1983 framing sometimes sounds more automatic than this — as if labour produced consciousness mechanically — and that framing is unconvincing. The conditional reading is the convincing one.

The second qualification is intersectional. Hartsock's 1983 essay implicitly took the labour of a particular kind of woman (white, often middle-class, often Western) as paradigmatic. Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination, articulated in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990), corrects this: women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, partly conflicting. The standpoint of an African-American domestic worker in 1950s Chicago is not the same as the standpoint of a white middle-class housewife. Collins's "outsider within" concept — Black domestic workers' access to white family life from a position of structural exclusion — is closer to what Hartsock should have said about labour. Harding agrees: the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (p. 134).

Harding's reply, which I endorse, is empirical: which standpoint generates testably less partial accounts? The reference-man data is the test, and standpoint research passes it. The other is the essentialism worry: doesn't grounding standpoint in labour fix women into a category? The reply is the conditional reading: labour generates standpoint only when articulated through political community; the category is opened, not closed, by articulation.

How convincing? Substantially, with qualifications. The 1983 framework needs the matrix of domination and the achievement reading. With those, Hartsock's argument remains foundational. Without them, it is essentialist. The qualifications are themselves available within the broader standpoint tradition, so the convincing version of Hartsock is the one read alongside Collins and Harding.

### Mock 10: "There is power in looking." Critically discuss with reference to hooks and the oppositional gaze.

"The politics of slavery, of racialized power relations, were such that slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). This is not a metaphor. It is a documented disciplinary practice. The right to look — to fix one's attention, to study, to evaluate, to refuse to avert one's eyes — was a right that the racial-political order systematically withheld from Black people, and especially from Black women. The corresponding right to be looked at, in the structured way that recognises personhood, was also withheld: Black women's bodies were viewed in slavery, but they were viewed as property, as labour, as sexual access, not as subjects whose looking-back mattered.

Berger's binary (looker / looked-at) becomes hooks's triangle (looker / looked-at / forbidden looker). The forbidden looker is the structural position from which the oppositional gaze emerges.

Begin then with the second claim: vision is a site of resistance. "All attempts to repress our/black peoples' right to gaze had produced in us an overwhelming longing to look, a rebellious desire, an oppositional gaze" (hooks, p. 116). The oppositional gaze is the active reclamation of the right to look — not as a return to the structure Berger describes (women looked at by men) but as a third position that refuses both the looked-at position and the false universal of the looker. hooks is theorising Black female spectatorship: the way Black women have watched Hollywood cinema critically, refusing the cinema's invitation to identify with white female protagonists and white male spectators, instead developing a critical practice of looking-against. "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality.' Even in the worse circumstances of domination, the ability to manipulate one's gaze in the face of structures of domination that would contain it, opens up the possibility of agency" (p. 116).

Now the third claim: the conditions of looking are political. The right to look is unevenly distributed. The right to be a credible looker — to have one's looking taken seriously as evidence — is even more unevenly distributed. Women's testimony about their bodies has been routinely discounted: pain reports interpreted as anxiety, gynaecological symptoms coded as psychological, cardiac symptoms in women missed because the diagnostic template was male. Black women's testimony has been more systematically discounted still, with US Black maternal mortality at three to four times the white rate, a substantial portion of the differential attributable to dismissed reports of symptoms. The Henrietta Lacks case in 1951 is the limit case: HeLa cells extracted from a Black woman without consent, her looking and her testimony irrelevant to the scientific community that profited from her tissue. "There is power in looking" names this distribution, and the politics of dismantling it.

Read alongside the standpoint tradition, hooks's framework is recognisably an account of achieved standpoint. The oppositional gaze is not given by Black female identity; it is produced through political and intellectual struggle — through the Black feminist tradition from Sojourner Truth and Anna Julia Cooper to Audre Lorde and hooks herself. This matches Harding's insistence that "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991, p. 124). The oppositional gaze is the articulated observation; it is not a property of biology but a practice of community.

One critical worry. The metaphor of looking is generative but bounded. Not all standpoints are visual; care knowledge, embodied knowledge, kinaesthetic knowledge of work resist visual formulation. hooks does not claim universality for the visual register; the framework is most powerful where vision is the medium of power (cinema, surveillance, AI image classification). Buolamwini and Gebru's *Gender Shades* finding — facial-recognition error rates of 0.8% for white men and up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women — extends hooks directly: the AI looks but mis-sees, and the corrective is the same as hooks's. Power in looking; the look that changes reality.

A sociology that requires positionality statements, that disaggregates findings demographically, that pre-registers research protocols including the demographic composition of the studied population, that integrates community testimony into evaluation — this sociology is more rigorous than the alternative. Pre-registration of trial protocols, demographic disaggregation in published data, and ethics-board scrutiny of consent procedures are now standard in good biomedical research; they are direct legacies of feminist epistemology's pressure on conventional method. They make research more, not less, objective.

Third, the community argument. Harding's claim that "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (p. 133) implies that the composition of the research community is an epistemically relevant variable. A demographically homogeneous community produces shared invisible assumptions; a more diverse community catches more of them. The objectivity of sociology depends on the structural reform of its personnel — not as a political concession but as a methodological requirement.

The reference-man examples were not products of overt political bias; they were products of communities whose political assumptions were invisible to them. Visibility is the precondition of evaluation.

Sociology can therefore pursue objectivity only by taking standpoint seriously. The disjunction in the question is the disjunction objectivism wants us to accept; standpoint epistemology dissolves it. The lecture's slogan is the right summary: a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity. Take standpoint seriously, and objectivity follows.

### Mock 13: Critically assess Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* as a development of standpoint epistemology.

Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment* (1990) is the most generative single development of standpoint epistemology after its 1983 founding by Nancy Hartsock. The book does three things at once: it intersectionalises standpoint, replacing the implicitly unitary "women" of 1983 with the multiple, intersecting locations of the matrix of domination; it makes standpoint epistemology empirically richer by directing attention to specific intersections (Black women's experience of healthcare, of policing, of domestic labour); and it integrates standpoint theory with social-movement politics by treating the production of standpoint knowledge as continuous with the political organisation of marginalised communities. I will argue that the book is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition for contemporary sociology, and that the few criticisms it has attracted are best handled within its own framework.

Collins's starting point is the insufficiency of single-axis standpoint claims. Hartsock's 1983 essay grounded standpoint in women's labour, but the labour of which women? Collins's analytical move is to take the fit problem as productive. The standpoint of African-American women is generative not because it is more authentic than other standpoints, but because the intersection of race, class and gender that African-American women occupy is structurally informative about the operation of multiple systems of domination simultaneously. The matrix of domination is Collins's vocabulary for this. Race, class, gender and (in later work) sexuality are interlocking, not additive, axes; their integration shapes lived experience and the knowledge that flows from it.

Three features of the book deserve emphasis.

First, the rescue from essentialism. Hartsock's framework risks treating "women" as a unitary subject. Collins shows that women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, and partly conflicting. Her concept of "outsider within" knowledge — African-American domestic workers' access to white family life from a position of structural exclusion — is one of the most generative ideas in twentieth-century sociology. The outsider within sees, because of structural exclusion, what insiders cannot, and is seen by insiders, when seen at all, as a non-witness. Sandra Harding echoes the formulation when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 134).

Second, the empirical productivity. The matrix of domination directs researchers to specific intersections where multiple systems intersect to produce distinctive forms of knowledge. The US Black maternal mortality data — over three times the white rate, with a substantial share of the differential attributable to disbelief of Black women's reports of symptoms — is a matrix effect: neither race nor gender alone explains it. Buolamwini and Gebru's *Gender Shades* finding (facial-recognition error rates of 0.8% for white men, up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women) is a matrix effect. Obermeyer's 2019 *Science* paper on US healthcare-allocation algorithms under-prioritising Black patients is structurally related. In each case single-axis fairness measures would miss what the matrix sees.

Third, the integration of standpoint and politics. Collins's standpoint is articulated through Black feminist intellectual tradition — Sojourner Truth, Ida B. Wells, Anna Julia Cooper, Audre Lorde, June Jordan, Angela Davis — which is also a political tradition. The production of standpoint knowledge is inseparable from the political organisation of marginalised communities. This recovers Hartsock's original insistence that "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle".

Three critical points are worth registering.

First, the matrix of domination is more powerful as a heuristic than as a precise theoretical apparatus. It directs attention productively but does not specify the mechanisms by which axes integrate. The follow-up work by Kimberlé Crenshaw on intersectionality, by Leslie McCall on intersectional methodology, and by Collins herself in *Intersectionality as Critical Social Theory* (2019), provides more precision.

Second, the framework is sometimes accused of producing analyses so multi-axis that they become unfalsifiable. The reply is that intersectional claims, like single-axis claims, are subject to empirical test — Black maternal mortality data is exactly such a test, and the *Gender Shades* finding is exactly such a test. Both confirm intersectional predictions that single-axis frameworks did not generate.

Third, the framework is sometimes accused of being too tied to the US racial context. Collins's revised editions address this; the matrix is portable to other racialised societies (Brazil, France, the UK) by attention to local histories. The British literature on the policing of Black women, on Asian women's labour, on Traveller communities' encounters with the state, has applied the framework productively.

For sociology today, Collins's framework is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition. It retains the political grounding of Hartsock, the methodological reflexivity of Harding, the situated-knowledge insistence of Haraway, and adds the structural integration of multiple axes of oppression. It is, in short, standpoint epistemology brought up to date, and it has been the framework against which much of the last two decades of empirical sociology of inequality has been built.

The lecture's connection is direct. hooks's oppositional gaze and Gilroy's Black Atlantic are intersectional standpoints in Collins's sense. The lecture's slogan that perspectives produce more objectivity is best read through Collins: multiple intersected perspectives produce more objectivity than either no perspective or single-axis perspectives. Collins's contribution to standpoint epistemology is therefore both critical and constructive. She criticised the single-axis tendency of the earlier tradition and built a richer framework in its place — a framework that remains the most useful tool we have for empirical sociology of inequality.
