# L6: Standpoints, Perspectives and Epistemology

**Module:** SC301 Rethinking Modernity
**Lecture:** 6
**Theme:** Knowledge, situatedness, feminist and postcolonial epistemologies; the critique of objectivism.

---

## 1. Key concepts (≤200 words)

**Epistemology** asks how and what we know, and what justifies a knowledge-claim. **Traditional epistemology** (which Harding labels **objectivism**) treats knowledge as independent of the knower; philosophical objectivity assumes a real external world and methodological objectivity demands value-free, dispassionate research. Feminist critique exposes this neutrality as itself a position: the universal knower performs **the god trick** (Haraway), speaking from nowhere while actually speaking from a dominant location. **Situated knowledge** insists all thought bears the fingerprints of its producers. **Standpoint epistemology** (Hartsock, Harding, Smith) argues that marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate less partial accounts. **Strong objectivity** treats the knower as part of the object of inquiry, demanding **strong reflexivity**. **The reference man** (Criado-Perez) names the Caucasian-male default in crash-test dummies, PPE, office temperature and pharmacology. **Bifurcation**, mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, men/women, sustains masculinist science. **Perspectival epistemology** brings multiple positions into dialogue. Gilroy reconstructs modernity from **the slaves' standpoint**; hooks' **the oppositional gaze** treats looking as resistance; Collins' **the matrix of domination** maps intersecting axes of oppression; her **outsider within** names the bifurcated consciousness Harding endorses.

The problem of objectivity.** Two traditional definitions: (1) **philosophical**, a real world independent of humans, science discovers truth about it; (2) **methodological**, researchers must be neutral, detached, value-free. Harding calls both **objectivism**. ## 4. Critical analysis with embedded quotes

The Harding reading is the spine of the lecture and is built around a single counter-intuitive claim: that situated, politically engaged knowledge produces **more**, not less, objectivity. Harding opens by arguing that "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women", but that the disciplines "permitted no conceptual frameworks in which women as a group… became the subjects, the authors, of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 4). The implied speaker of scientific sentences "was supposed to be humanity in general", performing what Haraway names "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). This is the core diagnosis: objectivism universalises a particular location.

Harding insists the problem is not "bad science" but the conceptual frameworks themselves, which "promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns, which ensured that the knowledge produced through them was always socially situated" (Harding, 2004: 5). "Commitment to an objectivity defined as maximizing social neutrality was not itself socially neutral in its effects" (Harding, 2004: 5). She lists what androcentric frameworks rendered invisible: violence against women treated as women "asking for it"; the unpaid double day naturalised; menstruation and menopause medicalised; rationality narrowed to the instrumental rationality of institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (Harding, 2004: 5-6). The "remedy", Harding writes, "was to start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities (or labor)" (Harding, 2004: 6), but never as ethnography of women alone; standpoint projects "must 'study up'" (Harding, 2004: 6).

In chapter 8 Harding consolidates this into **strong objectivity**. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*; it is too weak" (Harding, 2004: 128). Conventional method eliminates only those values "that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 136-137), leaving culture-wide assumptions untouched. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge. Thus, strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). Crucially, "a maximally critical study of scientists and their communities can be done only from the perspective of those whose lives have been marginalized by such communities" (Harding, 2004: 136).

Harding pre-empts three charges. Against the ethnocentrism charge she notes that "standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129), Hegel was not a slave, Marx not a wage-labourer. Against the relativism charge: "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (Harding, 2004: 131-132). Against the essentialism charge: the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (Harding, 2004: 134), starting from "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134).

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* sits alongside Harding as a parallel critique of the gendered structure of vision. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, 1972: 47). Berger shows that the European nude is structured by an unseen male spectator, "the principal protagonist is never painted. He is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man" (Berger, 1972: 54). This anticipates hooks: power resides in who is licensed to look. hooks' oppositional gaze is the inverted, resistant move: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, 1992: 116).

The lecture chains these arguments into one claim: epistemology is political. Whose body counts as the reference body, in crash tests, drug trials, PPE, office thermostats, is decided by who is in the room when the standards are set. Criado-Perez's evidence (women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in crashes; PPE based on European and US male bodies; office temperature calibrated to a male metabolic rate; pharmacology designed around a 70kg Caucasian man) is not a series of accidents but the empirical face of objectivism.

### 2025 (Q8): Why is the concept of perspectives important for understanding the status of epistemology within modernity?

The concept of perspectives matters for modernity because modernity inherited an epistemology that disavowed perspective altogether. The modern view from nowhere, the Enlightenment ideal of a universal knower, is the conceit Harding calls objectivism and Haraway names the god trick. I will argue that recovering perspectives is decisive for three reasons: it diagnoses modernity's epistemological fiction, it explains the political effects of that fiction, and it offers a route to a more accurate, plural science.

First, modernity defined itself epistemically against superstition by claiming a method that transcended location. The bifurcations rehearsed in the lecture, mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, objectivity/subjectivity, men/women, encode this. Science is on the rational, mind-side; superstition, nature, body and women on the other. Halewood treats fact/value as the cornerstone bifurcation of modernity (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7), the gulf that Weberian value-freedom institutionalised; standpoint epistemology is the corrective response that crosses this gulf by treating the researcher's location as evidence rather than contaminant. Harding exposes the trick: "All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, 2004: 128). The pretence that scientific thought escapes this is "a delusion, and a historical identifiable one" (Harding, 2004: 128). The concept of perspectives names what modernity tried to hide: that every claim is made from somewhere. The lecture's headline put it sharply: "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity." Pretending we have no perspective does not eliminate perspective, it only hides it. Hidden perspectives become culture-wide assumptions transported into research findings.

Second, perspectives matter because their absence has political effects. Criado-Perez's documentation of "a world designed for men" opens this. When the reference man is the only body imagined, women die at higher rates in car crashes; PPE designed around male bodies fits female workers badly. Office temperature, calibrated in the 1960s to the male metabolic rate, leaves female office workers in rooms designed against them. Pharmaceutical dosing is calculated against the 70kg reference man, despite different immune systems. Bags of cement and bricks are sized to male bodies. The same pattern shows up in contemporary AI: facial-recognition trained on skewed datasets misclassifies women and people of colour at higher rates, and healthcare algorithms under-recommend treatment for groups whose data is sparse. None of these is a methodological accident; each is what happens when a single perspective is universalised. As Harding writes, "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge…should be found preferable by all rational creatures" (Harding, 2004: 131). Perspectives lets us name the political work that no-perspective claims do.

Third, the concept of perspectives offers a constructive epistemology rather than a sceptical one. Standpoint epistemology, Hartsock's feminist standpoint, Smith's everyday-life sociology, Collins's matrix of domination, Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint, hooks's oppositional gaze, does not say all perspectives are equal. It says different positions afford different insights and some positions, when politically articulated, see what dominant positions cannot. Hartsock's two claims are that men's and women's understandings differ because of material labour and access to public space, and that the dominant group's view attempts to "structure the world for everybody". A standpoint "is not automatic, it is an achievement won from political struggle".

Bringing multiple perspectives together does not produce a single objective view but a multiple objectivity: different vantages corroborating, contesting and supplementing. This perspectival epistemology refuses bifurcation, refuses the choice between universal objectivity and relativism, and produces what Harding calls strong objectivity: knowledge accountable because its location is disclosed.

Modernity's self-image as the epoch of universal reason is therefore both a description and a problem. As a description it captures the ambition that gave us science; as a problem it captures the suppression of perspective that gave us the reference man. Perspectives lets us hold both: keep the ambition (truth, evidence, testability) while abandoning the fiction (the view from nowhere). Gilroy's claim that "the time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" is not anti-modern; it demands that modernity finally take seriously its own commitment to evidence by including the evidence it has excluded. hooks's insistence that "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) generalises the point: who looks, and from where, is constitutive of what is known. The status of epistemology in modernity is unstable without perspectives. With them, modernity becomes intelligible and correctable.

### 2024 (Q5): In what ways does the feminist epistemology overturn the canons and practices of traditional epistemology?

Feminist epistemology does not abolish epistemology; it rebuilds it. I will argue that it overturns the traditional canon in three connected ways: by exposing the universal knower as a partial knower, by reconstructing objectivity as situated rather than detached, and by reorganising the practical institutions of knowledge production. These moves do not weaken the search for truth; they make it more rigorous.

The traditional canon rests on two definitions of objectivity. Philosophically, it posits a real world independent of the knower, which science discovers; methodologically, it demands the researcher be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding's objectivism collapses these into a single claim: knowledge produced from no particular location. Haraway names this fantasy "the god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). The first overturning is the exposure: the universal knower of modern Western philosophy was always a specific knower, male, white, bourgeois, European, whose social location was concealed by being treated as default. Spender captures the inversion: "objectivity is male subjectivity".

The second overturning is the reconstruction of objectivity itself. Critics often suppose feminist epistemology must choose between objectivity and relativism. Harding refuses this dichotomy. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Conventional method can only eliminate values that "differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). Culture-wide assumptions slip through. Strong objectivity demands the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical plane as the object: it "requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). Detachment is replaced by disclosed, examined, accountable location.

The third overturning is institutional. Criado-Perez documents what happens when the reference man becomes the default. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male leave women significantly more likely to be seriously injured. PPE based on male populations fails female workers; buying smaller sizes does not suffice. Office temperature, set in the 1960s around male metabolic rate, leaves women cold. Doses are calculated against a 70kg reference man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). Bags of cement and bricks complete the picture, and AI bias extends the pattern. These are failures not of methodology in the narrow sense but of who is in the room when categories are set.

Feminist epistemology overturns the canon by changing personnel and procedures. Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, in and out of the home, gives access to dimensions of social reality the dominant group cannot see, but only when articulated through political struggle. Beauvoir had already prepared this ground by treating woman not as a natural kind but as a becoming, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 13); standpoint epistemology extends Beauvoir's anti-essentialism into method by treating the conditions of becoming as evidence. As Harding insists, "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). A standpoint is not an opinion poll of women; it is a worked-up critical perspective.

This extends beyond gender. Collins's matrix, hooks's oppositional gaze, and Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint generalise the move: marginalised positions, politically articulated, generate critical insights unavailable from the centre. Aptheker's catalogue of standpoint sites (Harding, 2004: 129) becomes visible only when categories of analysis are themselves contested.

A critic might object that this remains too tied to identity, risking essentialism. Harding's reply is that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (Harding, 2004: 134); it is the thinker "whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within". The alternative, pretending no one is in the room, is what produced the reference man.

Feminist epistemology therefore overturns the canon at three levels: at the level of the knower (exposing the god trick), at the level of objectivity (replacing detachment with strong reflexivity), and at the level of institutions (reorganising who counts and who is counted). It is not about undermining knowledge but about producing "better, more robust forms of knowledge".

### 2023 (Q8): What do sociologists mean by the term "standpoint epistemology"? How useful is this approach?

Standpoint epistemology is the claim that what we know is shaped by where we stand in social structure, and that some social locations, when their inhabitants reflect critically on them, afford less partial accounts than other locations do. I will define the term, distinguish it from neighbouring positions, and assess its usefulness, concluding that it is highly useful, especially paired with Harding's strong objectivity.

The standpoint thesis was systematised by Hartsock's 1983 essay, developed by Dorothy Smith's everyday-world sociology, Harding's books, and Collins's *Black Feminist Thought*. The lecture summarised it in three propositions. First, men's and women's understandings differ because of the labour they do and their access to public space. Second, the dominant group's view attempts to structure the world for everyone. Third, the feminist standpoint reveals the partiality of the dominant view, opening a path to liberation. Crucially, "a standpoint is not automatic, it is an achievement won from political struggle".

This distinguishes standpoint epistemology from three positions with which it is often confused. It is not identity politics: being a woman does not guarantee a feminist standpoint, and men can produce feminist knowledge by "starting from women's lives" (Harding, 2004: 135). It is not relativism: standpoint theory denies all perspectives are equally adequate, "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (Harding, 2004: 131). And it is not ethnocentrism: the claim that women's lives are a good place to start thinking asserts that marginal locations offer epistemic resources that central ones suppress.

Three examples show what the approach offers. First, the reference-man material. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male; women die at higher rates. Standpoint epistemology predicts this: when only one social position is in the room, the categories reflect it. Criado-Perez (2019) documents the pattern across PPE (sized to male populations), thermostats (set in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate) and pharmaceutical doses (calculated around the 70kg reference man). AI bias recurs it: facial-recognition and healthcare algorithms underperform on groups absent from training data.

Second, Collins's analysis of "outsiders within". The location in dominant households gave African-American domestic workers empirical access to how dominant groups thought when they assumed no one was listening. The standpoint is not opinion but a vantage that becomes critical when articulated through Black feminist theory. Du Bois's earlier formulation of double consciousness, "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), is the founding articulation of this bifurcated knowing; Collins extends it into method. The matrix of domination shows how race, class, gender and sexuality intersect, extending standpoint beyond a single axis. Harding endorses this when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134).

Third, Gilroy's *Black Atlantic*. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view." Modernity told from Liverpool is industrial revolution and Enlightenment; told from the Middle Passage and the plantation, it is also slavery, racialised capitalism and the long denial of personhood. hooks's oppositional gaze is the same move at the level of vision: "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Berger's analysis of paintings shows the unmarked male spectator structuring European visual culture.

The approach is therefore useful. It explains structural patterns (reference-man cases, the racial canon, gendered visual culture) that position-blind frameworks cannot. It generates research programmes and improves science by widening the evidence base: more locations, more reflexivity, more rigour.

Objections. Critics worry about essentialism. Harding answers that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134). Critics worry about regress: who decides which standpoint illuminates? The answer is empirical: judge by which produces more accurate accounts. Critics worry about politics replacing evidence, but "good politics" produces "good science" by widening evidence.

A more serious worry is that standpoint epistemology is most useful for social sciences. Harding addresses this by noting that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts, "wild animals" categorised differently by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17).

Standpoint epistemology is useful as diagnosis and as method. It does not replace evidence; it widens it. Its central insight, that disclosed location is a resource rather than a contaminant, is among the more durable contributions of late twentieth-century sociology.

### 2022 (Q9): Does feminist epistemology reject the concept of objectivity?

The short answer is no. Feminist epistemology, as articulated by Harding, Haraway, Hartsock, Smith and Collins, does not reject objectivity; it rejects objectivism, the conflation of objectivity with detached neutrality. I will argue this in three steps: by distinguishing objectivity from objectivism; by showing that strong objectivity strengthens rather than weakens objectivity; and by rebutting the relativism charge.

The lecture set out two traditional definitions. The philosophical sense holds there is a real world independent of human thought, which science discovers. The methodological sense holds researchers must be neutral, detached and value-free. Halewood treats this value-freedom as one of the fact/value bifurcations on which modernity is built (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7), so the feminist critique inherits a wider quarrel with modernity's habit of splitting the knower from the known. Harding labels the conflation of these definitions, and the assumption that science as actually practised achieves them, as **objectivism**. "The concept of value-free, impartial, dispassionate research… has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 143). Objectivism claims neutrality but operationalises it as agreement among a homogeneous community. Anything that community takes for granted slips through.

Feminist epistemology rejects this but does not reject the underlying ambition that knowledge should track reality. Harding is explicit: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). The lecture's headline: "this is not about undermining knowledge…It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge."

What feminist epistemology offers in place of objectivism is strong objectivity. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Strong objectivity requires "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). The knower is part of the system known; the researcher's social position is open to scientific scrutiny. This is "strong reflexivity". It is more rigorous than weak objectivity because it widens the field of what must be examined to include the researcher's own assumptions.

The empirical case is the reference-man material. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male; PPE designed for male bodies; office temperature calibrated to a male metabolic rate; pharmacology calibrated to the 70kg reference man; bags of cement and bricks sized to male bodies. Each is research that believed itself objective; none were. The same pattern appears in AI bias (facial-recognition, healthcare algorithms trained on homogeneous data) and in clinical trials that under-included women. Strong objectivity demands the location of the research community be disclosed and examined; weak objectivity calls anything not visible to the dominant community "neutral".

Berger supports this from the side of culture. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). The European nude is organised around an unpainted male spectator (Berger, 1972: 54). The "objectivity" of the painting is covert positioning. Feminist epistemology applied to science finds the same pattern: covert positioning passed off as no positioning.

A common objection is that admitting all knowledge is situated collapses into relativism. Harding pre-empts this. "Standpoint theory provides arguments for the claim that some social situations are scientifically better than others as places from which to start off knowledge projects" (Harding, 2004: 131). Some locations afford insights others suppress. The standard is empirical: which research programme generates less partial accounts? Standpoint feminism's record, producing accounts of unpaid domestic labour, gendered violence, and medical neglect of women through the reference-man framework, is itself the evidence. The cost-of-living crisis has further exposed how care work, performed disproportionately by women, has been undervalued in mainstream economic analysis.

A subtler objection is that strong objectivity is just objectivity with reflexivity bolted on. The bolt-on changes the structure. Conventional objectivity treats the subject as a hazard to control; strong objectivity treats the subject as a resource, evidence that must be made visible. This is not cosmetic. It changes the criteria for good science: it requires the integration of marginalised voices into scientific communities as a condition of rigour, not a concession to politics.

Feminist epistemology rejects the objectivism that impersonated objectivity. It defends objectivity by making it stronger. The lecture's slogan, "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity", is precisely the answer. Objectivity is preserved; objectivism is abandoned.

### 2021 (Q9): Outline and assess the feminist epistemologist critique of "masculinist" approaches to knowledge.

The feminist critique of masculinist knowledge has three components: a critique of who has produced knowledge, a critique of what counts as knowledge, and a critique of how knowledge has been validated. I will outline each, illustrate, then assess, concluding that the critique is largely successful and remains the most productive sociological account of the politics of knowledge.

The first component is a critique of personnel. "Most philosophers and scientists and sociologists (in the West) have been men. As Sandra Harding puts it, this means that the concerns, the topics and procedures of science, philosophy and much social theory are 'man-made'". Spender extended the point to language. This is not a complaint about individual men. It is a structural observation: when a research community is homogeneous along a major axis, the assumptions it shares will not show up as assumptions. Conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). What the community takes for granted is invisible to its method.

The second component is a critique of content. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* catalogues the consequences. Crash-test dummies based on the 50th-percentile male leave women significantly more likely to be seriously injured. PPE is built around male populations; smaller sizes do not suffice. Office thermostats were set in the 1960s to the male metabolic rate. Doses are designed around the 70kg reference man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not". Bags of cement and bricks repeat the pattern, as do AI bias in facial-recognition and healthcare algorithms and clinical trials that under-included women. Harding's wider catalogue fills in the social side: violence against women coded as "asking for it" (Harding, 2004: 5), menstruation medicalised, the unpaid double day naturalised, rationality restricted to institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (Harding, 2004: 5-6).

The third component is a critique of validation. The masculinist tradition operationalises objectivity as detachment and value-freedom. Feminist epistemology argues this is itself a value, a position that systematically disadvantages knowledge claims emerging from marginalised lives. Harding's strong objectivity replaces detachment with "strong reflexivity" (Harding, 2004: 136): the researcher's location is itself an object of inquiry. Haraway's situated knowledge replaces the god trick with "feminist objectivity…quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, 2004: 127). Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, articulated through political struggle, generates accounts that reveal the partiality of the dominant vision.

These three critiques converge on a single claim: masculinist knowledge is partial knowledge passing as universal. Berger demonstrates this from the side of vision. "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, 1972: 47). The supposed neutrality of the European nude is the suppression of a male spectator who organises the entire image. hooks generalises: "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Who sees, and from where, is constitutive of what is known. Butler radicalises the same diagnostic at the level of the subject: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2), which means the masculinist tradition shaped not only what counted as knowledge but who counted as a knower.

Assessing, I find it persuasive on three grounds. Empirically, the reference-man examples are documented and systemic. Methodologically, the move from objectivism to strong objectivity strengthens science by widening reflexivity. Politically, it has been generative: feminist science studies, Black feminist sociology, postcolonial historiography have changed what counts as a legitimate question.

Three reservations. First, the critique risks essentialism if "masculinist" is treated as a property of all men's thought rather than of a structure. Harding addresses this: men can produce feminist knowledge by starting from women's lives (Harding, 2004: 135). Second, the critique can be misread as anti-science; the slogan "this is not about undermining knowledge…It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge" should be taken at face value. Third, scope: Harding argues even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17), though abstract physics is a harder case.

The critique succeeds. It diagnoses a real failure of self-understanding in the masculinist tradition; it offers concrete tools (strong objectivity, strong reflexivity, situated knowledge, the matrix of domination) to repair that failure; and it has demonstrated empirically that doing so produces better knowledge.

### Mock 1: "How do we know what we know?" Using the Big Ben example and Harding's claim that the topics and procedures of science are "man-made", explain why traditional epistemology mistakes one kind of knower for the universal knower.

The lecture opens its discussion of epistemology with two questions and a picture: "Do you know that Big Ben is in London? How do you know this?" The pedagogical point is that even mundane knowledge claims rest on socially produced sources, testimony, photographs, schooling, maps, the institutional architecture that decides what counts as evidence. From this elementary point follows a less elementary one: if knowledge is always mediated, then the question of who mediates it, whose testimony, whose photographs, whose schooling, becomes part of the question of what is known. I will argue that the Big Ben example, paired with Harding's claim that the procedures of science are "man-made", exposes how traditional epistemology mistakes one socially located knower for the universal knower, and that this mistake is structural rather than accidental.

Begin with the example. Almost no reader has measured Big Ben's location personally. We know its location because the British state, the BBC, Ordnance Survey, geography textbooks and travel media have agreed on it for generations. The agreement is reliable, but it is an agreement among institutions, not a direct encounter with an unmediated object. Harding generalises the diagnosis: "All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, 2004: 128). Knowledge production is not a solitary encounter between a knower and a fact; it is the institutional aggregation of testimonies, instruments and shared assumptions. The Big Ben case makes this benignly visible. The same structure operates, less benignly, throughout the production of scientific knowledge.

Harding's second move is to ask who has been doing the aggregating. "Most philosophers and scientists and sociologists (in the West) have been men. As Sandra Harding puts it, this means that the concerns, the topics and procedures of science, philosophy and much social theory are 'man-made'." Spender extends the same point to language. The methods themselves carry the assumptions of the communities that built them. This is not a complaint about individual men. It is a structural observation: when the testimony pool, the photograph-takers, the textbook-writers, the standards-setters, are demographically narrow on a major axis, the assumptions they share will not show up as assumptions inside the framework. They will look like the world.

The reference-man material in Criado-Perez catalogues what happens when this structure operates in domains less benign than London geography. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg); women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die. PPE was designed around European and US male populations, with employers wrongly assuming smaller sizes would suffice for female workers. Office temperature was calibrated in the 1960s to the male metabolic rate. Doses were modelled on a 70kg "reference man" whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). Bags of cement and bricks repeat the pattern. Each is a Big-Ben-structure case (institutional aggregation of testimony) where the testimony pool was narrow. The aggregation is then deployed as if it were a view from nowhere.

This is what Haraway names "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). The trick is not that institutions lie. The trick is that they universalise a particular location, the location of the dominant testimony pool, and then forget that they have done so. Halewood's account of modernity as a structure of bifurcations (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7) provides the wider frame: the fact/value gulf permits a researcher to treat his own location as a "fact" about objectivity while treating others' locations as "values" to be eliminated. Big Ben's location does not turn on this asymmetry; the reference man does.

Traditional epistemology mistakes one kind of knower for the universal knower not by reasoning badly but by structuring its self-image around a forgetfulness. Harding's remedy, that we "construct new feminist modes of knowledge" producing "better, more robust forms of knowledge", does not abandon the Big Ben structure of institutionally mediated knowledge. It widens the testimony pool, demands disclosure of who is in it, and treats the conceptual framework of any discipline as itself an object of inquiry. The lecture's slogan, "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity", follows directly: more locations, more checks, more accurate aggregation.

### Mock 2: "Objectivity is male subjectivity" (Spender, 1980). Using the bifurcations of mind/body, rational/irrational, objectivity/subjectivity, men/women, critically discuss why feminist epistemology treats knowledge as partial, situated and constructed.

Spender's epigram, "objectivity is male subjectivity", compresses the central feminist diagnosis of modern epistemology into four words. I will argue that the sentence makes sense, and is largely correct, when read against the bifurcations the lecture rehearses (mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, science/superstition, objectivity/subjectivity, men/women) and that this reading explains why feminist epistemology treats knowledge as partial, situated and constructed rather than universal, view-from-nowhere, and merely discovered.

The bifurcations operate as a single conceptual structure. The first term of each pair is privileged and coded as masculine; the second is denigrated and coded as feminine. Mind, rational, culture, science, objectivity, men is one column; body, irrational, nature, superstition, subjectivity, women is the other. The encoding is not symmetrical: it does not merely describe a difference, it ranks one column above the other, and it presents the ranking as natural. Halewood reads this as the structuring move of modernity (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7), the fact/value bifurcation that licenses science to claim a vantage outside human valuing while every other discourse is consigned to value. Feminist epistemology refuses both halves of this move.

Spender's sentence operates inside this structure. If objectivity is one term and subjectivity the other; if objectivity is coded as masculine and subjectivity as feminine; if the supposedly universal practice of objectivity is in fact the practice of a community that is overwhelmingly male; then "objectivity is male subjectivity" is the right description. The supposedly neutral, dispassionate, view-from-nowhere position is a specific, located, gendered position presenting itself as no position at all. Haraway names the same trick: "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). Harding's term for the conflation is objectivism, the conflation of the metaphysical claim that there is a knowable world with the methodological claim that detached research achieves universal access to it.

The empirical face of Spender's epigram is the reference-man material. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured. PPE designed around European and US male bodies fails female workers. Office temperatures, set in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate, leave women too cold. Pharmacological doses are calibrated to a 70kg "reference man" whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). Bags of cement and bricks repeat the pattern. Each is engineering done objectively in the conventional sense, tested, measured, standardised, that was also engineering done from a male subjectivity treated as universal. Spender's sentence is descriptive of the empirical pattern, not just rhetorical.

If this is the diagnosis, why does feminist epistemology treat knowledge as partial, situated and constructed? Three reasons follow from the bifurcations themselves.

First, partial. The bifurcations divide knowable reality into two halves and then treat one half as illegitimate. Mind without body, rational without irrational, culture without nature, science without superstition, objectivity without subjectivity, men without women. Each privileged term is intelligible only against the term it excludes. Knowledge built on this exclusion is partial in the literal sense: it has discarded half the data. Beauvoir's prior observation that "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 13) identifies the second column as a social product, not a natural kind, which means the excluded half is doubly partialised, denigrated and then naturalised.

Second, situated. If objectivity has actually been operating as male subjectivity, then it has been operating from somewhere, a body, a class, a labour position, a national location. Hartsock's standpoint theory takes this seriously: men's and women's understandings differ because of the labour they do and their access to public space, and the dominant group's view attempts to structure social reality for everyone. Knowledge is always made from somewhere. Disclosing the somewhere is part of the work of knowing.

Third, constructed. The bifurcations are not natural categories. They are historical achievements of particular communities, transported into research findings as if they were findings. Harding's strong objectivity is the remedy: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Hill Collins's matrix of domination (Hill Collins, 1990) extends this by treating race, class, gender and sexuality as interlocking axes of construction, none separable from the others.

The lecture's slogan applies. A perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity, because it abandons the bifurcations Spender named and replaces them with disclosed, examined, situated knowing.

### Mock 3: "This applies not just to 'feminist' epistemology but also to other marginalised or excluded groups." Critically discuss the extension of standpoint epistemology beyond gender, drawing on at least two of Gilroy, hooks, Collins or Du Bois.

The lecture is clear that standpoint epistemology is not the property of feminism. Why epistemology matters, the slide insists, "applies not just to 'feminist' epistemology but also to other marginalised or excluded groups". The same slide gestures at the generalisation: the concept of a standpoint or perspective "could help us develop knowledge from other 'oppressed' or neglected positions". I will argue that this extension is not an optional addition but a methodological requirement, and that Gilroy, hooks, Collins and Du Bois each operationalise it in ways that strengthen rather than dilute the original framework.

Begin with the structural reason for the extension. Hartsock's framework grounds standpoint in two facts: men's and women's understandings differ because of the labour they do and access to public space, and the dominant group's view attempts to structure social reality for everyone. Neither premise is gender-specific. Both apply wherever a structural axis of dominance produces a homogeneous standard imposed on differently situated others. Race, class, colonial position, sexuality each generate analogous structures. To restrict standpoint to gender would be to repeat the universalisation feminist epistemology was built to refuse.

Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) is the founding articulation of a racial standpoint. "It is a peculiar sensation, this double-consciousness, this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5). Double consciousness is a precise epistemic claim: the subordinate group must understand both its own life and the dominant world that frames it, while the dominant group needs only the second. This is structurally identical to Hartsock's prediction (the dominant view attempts to structure social reality for everyone) and supplies the racial template Collins later systematises.

Collins's matrix of domination is the most systematic generalisation. Race, class, gender and sexuality are not additive but interlocking; each axis shapes lived experience and the knowledge that flows from it. The concept of "outsider within" knowledge, African-American domestic workers' access to white family life from a position of structural exclusion, names the bifurcated consciousness Du Bois first identified, and Harding endorses Collins explicitly when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134). The matrix is what happens when you take Hartsock's premises seriously and notice that there is more than one structural axis of dominance.

Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* extends the framework to historiography. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view." The methodological promise is that political economy reconstructed from the plantation, philosophy reconstructed from the slave narrative, cultural history reconstructed from the Middle Passage, will be more accurate, not less, than histories that take Manchester or Paris as the centre and the slave port as the periphery. Gilroy's "ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood…constructed from the slaves' standpoint" is the historiographical operationalisation of the lecture's general claim that "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity".

hooks's oppositional gaze brings the extension to vision. "The gaze has always been political in my life" (hooks, 1992: 115); "the politics of slavery, of racialized power relations, were such that slaves were denied their right to gaze". Punishment for looking, documented historically, generalises to a structural denial of credibility-as-spectator. The oppositional gaze is the active reclamation: "Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality" (hooks, 1992: 116). Berger's parallel analysis of European art, "men act and women appear" (Berger, 1972: 47), is the gendered version; hooks's contribution is the racialised one. The two together show that the right to look, and to have one's looking credited as knowledge, is unevenly distributed along multiple axes.

What does the extension add? Three things. First, it confirms Hartsock's structural claim by showing it works on axes other than gender. Second, it complicates the singular "women" of 1983 by directing attention to intersections. Third, it integrates standpoint theory with the broader project of decolonising knowledge: indigenous-knowledge perspectives on climate science, for instance, are intelligible within this framework as another instance of the same general argument. The framework, extended, is the more powerful version of the original. Harding's catalogue of standpoint sites (Harding, 2004: 129), holocaust survivors, cannery workers, older lesbians, African-American women in slavery, makes the same point: standpoint is plural by design.

### Mock 4: What is a "perspective" in the sense the lecture intends? Distinguish it from a viewpoint, a subjective opinion and an imaginary projection, and explain why a perspective must be articulated.

The lecture is unusually careful about its central term. A perspective, in the sense the lecture intends, "is not just a viewpoint on the world (but it will involve a view), it is not subjective, it is not imaginary, it is experienced (and felt), perhaps by a group of people, but it needs to be 'discovered' or 'articulated'". I will argue that each negation matters and each positive determination is load-bearing, and that the resulting concept is what allows standpoint epistemology to escape both the relativism objection and the essentialism objection.

Begin with the negations. A perspective is not a viewpoint. A viewpoint is the spatial metaphor of looking from one position rather than another; perspectives in this sense are interchangeable, and one could in principle adopt any one of them by changing position. The lecture's perspective is heavier than this. Hartsock's standpoint is grounded in labour and access to public space; one cannot adopt the standpoint of women's reproductive and caring labour by deciding to. The standpoint is shaped by what the body does in the world over time.

A perspective is not subjective. Subjective opinion is the realm classical objectivism wants to expel from science, and if perspectives were merely subjective, the objectivist would be right to expel them. The lecture insists the opposite: a perspective is not opinion. It is not a feeling one happens to have, not a preference, not a taste. It is structural; it is the cognitive consequence of a structural position.

A perspective is not imaginary. One does not invent or fabricate a perspective. It is not what an outsider imagines an insider must feel. It is also not a literary construct. Mohanty's parallel critique sharpens this: when Western feminist scholarship produced "the third world woman" as "an effect of discourse… not a real empirical group" (Mohanty, 1984: 261), the failure was precisely treating a perspective as imaginary projection rather than as the articulated knowledge of an actual community.

Now the positive determinations. A perspective is experienced and felt. It has a phenomenological dimension: the wearer of a perspective lives it, in the body, in the household, in the workplace. Beauvoir's account of woman as a becoming (de Beauvoir, 1949: 13) gives the temporal grammar: the perspective accumulates as the body is shaped by social structure. The reference-man examples in Criado-Perez are the empirical face of this: women experience cold offices, ill-fitting PPE, miscalibrated doses, not as opinions but as bodily facts.

A perspective is held, often, by a group. Standpoints are not individual; they are collective epistemic resources. This is what distinguishes them from idiosyncratic opinion. It is also what gives them political traction: a group of differently-situated people each holding the same articulated perspective is the precondition of standpoint as social-movement practice.

A perspective must be articulated. This is the most important determination, and the one that distinguishes standpoint from mere experience. The lecture is direct: "dominated groups don't automatically have the most objective views of the world; a 'standpoint' is an achievement, won from political struggle; production of knowledge inseparable from political struggle". Harding's gloss in the Reader is the canonical statement: "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124).

Why articulation matters. Without articulation, experience is private and politically inert; articulation is the move from a felt position to a critical perspective that can be tested, communicated, joined to other perspectives and used as a basis for further inquiry. The Black feminist tradition Collins develops in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) is the paradigm: African-American women's experience becomes a standpoint when it is theorised through Black feminist intellectual and political community, not before. Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint is also an articulation, not a recovery of unmediated experience; the slave narratives, the resistance traditions, the Black Atlantic intellectual tradition are what makes the standpoint usable.

The lecture's careful definition does substantial work. It defends standpoint against the charge of relativism (perspectives are structural, not preferential), against the charge of essentialism (they require articulation and are not biological), and against the charge of mere subjectivity (they are experienced and tested in collective practice). The slogan that perspectives produce more, not less, objectivity depends on each of these distinctions holding.

### Mock 5: "A perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity." Critically discuss the lecture's headline claim, explaining why feminist epistemology is "not about undermining knowledge…it is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge".

The lecture's headline is also its central paradox: perspectives, properly understood, produce more rather than less objectivity. Read superficially, the claim sounds contradictory, since perspectives are particular and objectivity is supposed to be universal. Read carefully, the claim is the most important sociological proposition the module defends. I will argue that the paradox dissolves once Harding's distinction between objectivity and objectivism is in place, and that the empirical record vindicates the headline rather than the inherited objectivist alternative.

Begin with what the headline does not mean. It does not mean that feminist epistemology is anti-science. Harding is explicit: "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge." Nor does it mean that all perspectives are equally good; "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). The headline rejects both the objectivist position (perspectives are noise, eliminate them) and the relativist position (perspectives are all there is, abandon objectivity).

The pivot is Harding's distinction. Objectivity is the goal: knowledge that tracks reality, is testable, comparable, accountable. Objectivism is the failed methodological model that tried to instantiate it by demanding view-from-nowhere detachment. The model fails empirically. "The concept of value-free, impartial, dispassionate research…has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 1991: 143). Assumptions shared by the whole community slip through. The view from nowhere turns out to be the view from somewhere very specific, presented as no view at all, what Haraway calls "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4).

The empirical case for the headline is the reference-man material. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured. PPE designed around European and US male bodies fits female workers badly; smaller sizes do not suffice. Office temperatures, calibrated in the 1960s to the male metabolic rate, leave women too cold. Pharmacological doses are calibrated to a 70kg "reference man" whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). Bags of cement and bricks repeat the pattern; AI bias repeats it at digital scale. Each was objective by conventional standards (tested, measured, peer-reviewed) but produced systematically less accurate accounts than research starting from differently-situated bodies. The honest claim "this dummy represents 50th-percentile male bodies" is more objective than the dishonest claim "this dummy represents passengers".

Strong objectivity is the constructive proposal. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). The remedy demands that "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). This is "strong reflexivity": the conditions of knowledge production (who funded it, who designed it, whose body it tested) are themselves data. Halewood reads this as the corrective to modernity's fact/value bifurcation (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7): the gulf the bifurcation imposed between knower and known is closed by treating the knower as part of the system known.

A perspectival approach produces more objectivity because it brings more locations into the analysis. Haraway's situated knowledge supplies the methodological corollary: "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196). Larger vision is built from disclosed locations, joined and tested against each other, rather than from a single position pretending to be all positions. Collins's matrix of domination directs attention to intersections where multiple axes combine; her "outsider within" supplies the bifurcated consciousness Harding endorses (Harding, 2004: 134).

Two objections. The relativism objection holds that more perspectives means no shared truth; reply, perspectives are tested against each other and the empirical record. The reference-man data is the proof. The politicisation objection holds that integrating standpoint blurs research and advocacy; reply, research ignoring standpoint hides its politics rather than removing them.

The headline is therefore a corrective, not a paradox. Perspectives, disclosed and articulated, produce more objectivity because they widen the field of what can be examined. Feminist epistemology rebuilds the project of reliable knowledge by abandoning the model that confused universality with detachment. The slogan stands.

### Mock 6: "Dominated groups don't automatically have the most objective views of the world; a 'standpoint' is an achievement, won from political struggle." Using Hartsock's three propositions on materialist feminism, explain why standpoint is an achievement rather than a given, and what follows for the relation between knowledge production and political struggle.

The slide on Hartsock ends with a sentence the lecturer underlines: "Dominated groups don't automatically have the most objective views of the world; a 'standpoint' is an achievement, won from political struggle; production of knowledge inseparable from political struggle." It refuses epistemic privilege as a property of identity, reroutes it through articulation, and ties knowledge to politics. I will defend each move using Hartsock's three propositions.

The three are: men's and women's understandings differ because of "the work they do ('in' and 'out' of the 'home'), because of their different level of access to the public space"; "the views of the dominating group attempt to structure the world for everybody"; feminist standpoint "provides a way of moving beyond existing gendered social relations, and this is liberating". First, occupying a structural position is not the same as making sense of it. Hartsock's first proposition says women's labour differs from men's; it does not say doing it delivers a theory of it. Beauvoir's diagnosis, "one is not born but rather one becomes a woman" (de Beauvoir, 1949: 13), supplies the temporal grammar; cross-link to L5. Butler radicalises the point: "the category of 'women,' the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (Butler, 1990: 2). The subject who would articulate is herself an object of what she would describe.

Second, the dominant view "attempts to structure the world for everybody", which means it structures the consciousness of the dominated too. Du Bois's double consciousness, "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), states the racial standpoint version; cross-link to L8. Achievement is the work of disentangling.

Harding's gloss is canonical: "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). Articulation, not experience, produces standpoint, and the articulation is collective.

What follows for knowledge and politics? Three things.

First, knowledge production becomes inseparable from political struggle in a strict sense. Without the organisation that builds shared analysis, experience stays private. Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* is the paradigm: African-American women's experiences became a standpoint through the Black feminist intellectual community that theorised them. Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity "from the slaves' point of view" relied on the tradition of slave narratives and Black Atlantic scholarship.

Halewood's reading of modernity as built on bifurcations (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7) is the wider frame; cross-link to L1.

Third, the politics is integral to objectivity. Harding's strong objectivity demands "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Haraway's situated knowledge supplies the parallel; cross-link to L10.

Two objections. The essentialism worry: doesn't grounding standpoint in women's labour fix women into a category? Achievement is the answer. ### Mock 8: "The god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all." Discuss with reference to Haraway.

Haraway's phrase the god trick is the sharpest diagnosis of what is wrong with the inherited scientific ideal of objectivity. The quotation captures the conceit: a knower who speaks from no body, no time, no class, no gender, claiming authority over every body. This is a structural claim, not a rhetorical jab.

The inherited ideal of objectivity has two components: the metaphysical claim that there is a world independent of our beliefs, and the methodological claim that researchers should minimise their biases. Haraway denies neither. What she denies is a third claim smuggled in with these: that good method as currently practised achieves the second. The god trick is the smuggling device. It rebrands the perspective of a specific knower (white, male, bourgeois, European) as no perspective at all.

The empirical case is the reference-man material catalogued by Criado-Perez. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male leave women significantly more likely to be seriously injured. PPE sized to male bodies fails female workers. Office thermostats were calibrated in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate. Pharmacological doses are calibrated to the 70kg reference man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019), despite different immune systems and hormones. Bags of cement and bricks are sized to male bodies. Each is a god-trick failure: engineering and pharmacology presented themselves as knowledge of human bodies in general but were knowledge of one kind of body, generalised by fiat. AI bias is the same logic at digital scale.

Harding's gloss makes the structural point: "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge…should be found preferable by all rational creatures" (Harding, 2004: 131). The god trick is sociologically possible only from positions of social power; it is the epistemology of dominance reflected back as universal reason.

Haraway's positive proposal flows from the diagnosis. "Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, 2004: 127); in her own essay she puts the corollary as a methodological demand, "we might become answerable for what we learn how to see" (Haraway, 1991: 190). Knowledge is always made from a body, a place, a history. To declare this is not to give up on objectivity but to deepen it. Disclosed location is more scientifically informative than concealed location, because the conditions of knowing become part of what can be evaluated. Harding develops this as strong objectivity: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136).

A common objection is that abandoning the god trick collapses into relativism. Both Haraway and Harding reject this: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). Situated knowledge does not claim all situations are equally informative; it claims situation must be disclosed, and that marginalised positions afford insights dominant situations suppress. The crash-dummy data is proof: the claim that women are at higher risk is the more accurate claim, generated from research that started from women's bodies.

A more sophisticated objection is that the god trick caricatures how scientists actually work. Real scientists, the objection runs, acknowledge uncertainty and revise. Haraway's answer is that the question is not whether individuals are humble but whether the institutional self-representation of science presents findings as situated or universal. Pharmacopoeias and standardised testing protocols treat outputs as universal. That is where the god trick lives.

The point generalises beyond the engineering of bodies. The same logic explains why "wild animals" are categorised differently by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17): even framing an object reflects the framing community's location. Smith's parallel claim, that women's perspective "discredits sociology's claim to constitute an objective knowledge independent of the sociologist's situation" (Harding, 2004: 128), confirms the diagnosis from sociological method.

The god trick is therefore not a slogan but a diagnostic concept. It names the operation by which partial knowledge is laundered into universal knowledge, and it points to the structural reforms, disclosed location, diversified research communities, integration of marginalised voices, that produce more, not less, objectivity.

### Mock 10: What does Haraway mean by the god trick, and why does she treat partiality as a methodological virtue?

The question has two parts. By the god trick Haraway means the move in which a knower presents their location-bound, body-bound, history-bound perspective as if it were no perspective at all, "speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). By partiality as a methodological virtue she means that disclosure of one's location, and explicit acknowledgement that one sees only some of the picture, is a stronger basis for objectivity than the pretence of seeing all of it. The two halves are connected: the god trick is the vice; situated, partial knowledge is the virtue.

The god trick is not the claim that scientists are arrogant individuals. It is a description of institutional epistemology. Pharmacopoeias, engineering standards, peer-reviewed journals and standardised testing protocols present their outputs as universal, applicable to "the human body", "the average user", "the population". The clearest illustrations are the reference-man cases in Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women*. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male generate safety standards presented as protecting "passengers", with women significantly more likely to be seriously injured or killed. PPE designed for European and US male bodies fails female workers. Office thermostats were set in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate (women's is significantly lower). Pharmacological dosing was calibrated to the 70kg reference man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not". Bags of cement and bricks belong here too. AI bias today follows the same logic: facial-recognition systems trained on homogeneous datasets are deployed as if neutral. In each case the institutional output denies its location of production. That denial is the god trick.

Why is the denial damaging? Conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). Assumptions shared by the entire community do not show up as assumptions. The god trick is institutionally productive because it makes these shared assumptions invisible.

Why does Haraway recommend partiality as a methodological virtue? Three reasons.

First, partiality is empirically more accurate than universalism. The crash-dummy data is the proof: the honest claim is "this dummy represents 50th-percentile male bodies". That partial claim opens to correction; the universal claim "this dummy represents passengers" closes to correction. The pharmacological claim "this dose is calibrated to a 70kg male" is testable; "this is the correct dose for an adult" hides the calibration.

Second, partiality enables joining. Haraway's situated-knowledge essay argues that partial perspectives, joined, produce richer accounts than any single perspective: "the only way to find a larger vision is to be somewhere in particular" (Haraway, 1991: 196). Larger vision is built from disclosed locations, not from their disavowal. Aptheker's catalogue of standpoint sites, holocaust survivors, cannery workers, older lesbians, African-American women in slavery (Harding, 2004: 129), each contribute a partial perspective whose joining produces accounts no single location could generate.

Third, partiality is more honest about the conditions of knowledge production. Strong objectivity "requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). Reflexivity means examining the conditions under which one's knowledge is made: funding structure, research-team composition, conceptual framework. Partiality is the precondition of reflexivity, because you cannot reflect on a location you deny.

The standard objection is that partiality slides into relativism. Haraway and Harding both reject the inference. "Judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). Partial knowledge is testable, comparable and ranked: by empirical adequacy, reflexive transparency, and breadth of joined perspectives. The reference-man examples show this: the partial standpoint of women's lives produced testably better predictions than the universal standpoint of the reference man. Hill Collins's matrix of domination (Hill Collins, 1990) sharpens the comparative test by directing the researcher to intersected locations whose joint vantage no single-axis framework could occupy.

The god trick and partiality-as-virtue are therefore two sides of one argument. The trick claims a universal vantage and produces parochial knowledge; partiality discloses location and produces, through reflexivity and combination, more reliable knowledge. The lecture's slogan applies: a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity.

Second, the strength of the privilege claim. Hartsock approaches the claim that the feminist standpoint generates a less partial account of social reality as such. Haraway is more pluralist: partial perspectives must be joined together for richer knowledge, and no single perspective is the master.

The honest answer is that contemporary sociology uses both. Standpoint thinking provides the political grounding and the lineage to social struggle; situated knowledge provides the methodological flexibility to handle plural locations and overlapping framings. Harding's strong objectivity is the bridge, and Collins's matrix is the extension. The two essays of 1983 and 1988 are best read as a single argument articulated twice.

PPE built around male bodies, office thermostats calibrated to a male metabolic rate, pharmacological dosing around the reference man, bags of cement and bricks all repeat the pattern. AI bias is the same logic at digital scale. Each is an epistemic failure produced by a political condition (homogeneity of the design community) and corrected by a political-epistemic intervention.

Second, the methodology connects them. Strong reflexivity requires the researcher examine her own location, including her political commitments, as part of the data. This is not advocacy; it is rigour. Conventional method treats the researcher's political location as a contaminant to hide; strong objectivity treats it as evidence to examine. The methodological commitment is itself political (insists on disclosure) and epistemological (improves accuracy).

Collins's matrix of domination, race, class, gender, sexuality as interlocking axes, is articulated through Black feminist political tradition. Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity "from the slaves' point of view" is both historiography and political reconstruction.

Is standpoint theory an epistemology or a politics? It is both, and the integration is the point. To force the disjunction would be to accept the very bifurcation standpoint theory was built to refuse. The lecture's slogan, a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity, depends on the integration; pull the two apart, and the slogan collapses.

Each is a case in which a homogeneous design community produced a technical artefact calibrated to one kind of body, deployed it as if it served all bodies, and discovered (or refused to discover) that it served some bodies far worse than others. The three cases form the empirical core of the case for strong objectivity: not because the engineering is unscientific, but because conventional engineering objectivity does not catch a particular kind of failure, the failure that lives in the conceptual default, not in the execution.

Begin with the crash-test dummy. The standard crash-test dummy was developed in the 1950s and standardised on the 50th-percentile male body. Criado-Perez (2019) documents the consequences: women are significantly more likely to be seriously injured or killed in a comparable crash. This is not a failure of engineering rigour in the narrow sense; the tests were run, data collected, statistics published. It is a failure of conceptual framework: the reference body was set, and the framework was invisible to the framework-setters.

Move to the office thermostat. Standard office temperature was developed in the 1960s around the metabolic resting rate of the average man. Women's office-work metabolic rate is significantly lower. Offices are too cold for the women who work in them, year after year, because the comfort standard was set against a body that women do not have. The engineering did its job (stable temperature); the conceptual framework did not (it defined "comfortable" against one kind of body).

The reference man is the abstract figure behind both. He weighs 70kg, and his "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). He appears in pharmacology, where men and women have different immune systems and hormones but doses are designed around the reference body; in PPE, sized to male populations with employers wrongly assuming smaller sizes suffice; and in bags of cement and bricks, sized to shoulders, hands and centres of gravity that are not those of female construction workers.

What unites the three? Harding names it: conventional objectivity "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). Assumptions shared by the entire design community are invisible to that community. The crash dummy, the thermostat and the reference man each codify such an assumption. Haraway's god trick is the institutional cousin: the system claims a view from nowhere when it has a view from one place. The same logic appears today in AI bias: facial-recognition, voice-recognition and healthcare algorithms trained on homogeneous data are deployed as universal protocols and fail under-represented users.

Strong objectivity is the corrective. "The subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Applied to engineering: choices about reference body are part of the engineering evidence, not methodological background. Applied to medicine: dose-response curves must come from demographically diverse trials, not be generalised from male subjects (women have historically been under-included in clinical trials). Applied to ergonomics: the standard environment must be calibrated against the bodies that will live in it.

Hartsock's framework adds the structural prediction. Men's and women's understandings differ because of the labour they do and their access to public space. The design community of mid-twentieth-century automotive engineering, HVAC standards-setting, and pharmacological trial design was overwhelmingly male, and the artefacts reflect the labour and body of that community. Hill Collins's matrix of domination (Hill Collins, 1990) sharpens the prediction: the "default" body coded into the reference man is also racially and class-marked, and disaggregating outcomes by race as well as gender (darker-skinned women misclassified by facial-recognition at higher rates than either men of colour or white women) is the intersectional confirmation single-axis fairness measures miss. The corrective is not better statistics within the existing community but structural diversification of the community itself.

The remedy is structural rather than procedural. Better statistics will not fix a reference body; tighter regulation alone will not fix a homogeneous design culture. What is required is diversification of the design community and elevation of the conceptual framework to a first-order object of scientific scrutiny. Strong objectivity demands no less, and produces, as the slogan insists, more not less objectivity.

### Mock 15: Does the dominance of a homogeneous research community vindicate or undermine Harding's account of strong objectivity?

The question asks whether Harding's framework predicts what happens when a research community is demographically narrow. The empirical record of the reference-man cases vindicates the diagnostic half decisively; whether the remedial half is vindicated depends on whether the community undertakes the structural reforms her framework requires. Harding's account is therefore descriptively confirmed and prescriptively pending.

Strong objectivity, as Harding sets it out, has three components. First, "the problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Second, it "requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Third, "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133). The framework requires reflexivity, structural diversification, and disclosure of the conditions of knowledge production.

Homogeneous research communities are a sustained failure case. The reference-man material shows the pattern at scale. Crash-test design, developed in the 1950s by an overwhelmingly male engineering community, produced a 50th-percentile male standard dummy and safety standards that leave women significantly more likely to be seriously injured. PPE design, dominated by manufacturers oriented to male workforces, produced equipment sized to male bodies. Office-thermostat standards set by 1960s HVAC engineering encoded a male metabolic rate. Pharmacological dosing was calibrated to a 70kg reference man despite different immune systems and hormones; clinical trials under-included women. AI bias is the same logic in software. Mohanty's analysis of how Western feminist scholarship produced "the third world woman" as "an effect of discourse… not a real empirical group but… produced in Western (and some western feminist) approaches" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is the parallel diagnosis: homogeneous communities universalise their own location, whether the location is male or Western.

Each is a god-trick failure of exactly the kind Harding's framework predicts. Within each community, assumptions about whose body is the reference body were not visible as assumptions; they were the unmarked default. Conventional method "operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137) could not catch the failure because the failure lived in what the whole community shared. This is the diagnostic vindication.

Three features of homogeneous communities interact with Harding's framework.

First, they make the conceptual framework invisible. The reference body is not a hypothesis tested against alternatives; it is a background assumption transported into research findings unexamined. "Culturewide assumptions that have not been criticized within the scientific research process are transported into the results of research" (Harding, 2004: 129) is exactly what produces the reference-man pattern.

Second, they generate plausible-looking outputs from skewed inputs. The crash-test data, dose-response curves and temperature standards all look authoritative. The absence of disclosed location is the hallmark of the god trick. A scientist working in a homogeneous community without examining the demographic composition of the testing population, and without checking the output against marginalised testimony, is reproducing the structural failure Harding diagnosed.

Third, they can be deployed in ways that conform to strong objectivity if the community undertakes structural reform. The same engineering and pharmacological apparatus can be deployed reflexively: disclosure of testing population, auditing across demographic axes, integration of marginalised voices in evaluation, treatment of standards as data to be tested rather than findings to be accepted.

So: does the dominance of a homogeneous research community vindicate or undermine Harding?

The undermining reading: if homogeneous research communities are common and persistent, Harding's framework may underestimate how difficult structural reform is. The reference-man cases were documented decades after they began, and many underlying standards have not been overhauled. On this reading the framework names a real problem but its remedy is slower in practice than in theory.

The vindicating reading: every documented reference-man case is an instance of Harding's general claim that homogeneous communities encode their assumptions invisibly. The framework is empirically confirmed across crash safety, PPE, ergonomics, pharmacology and now AI design.

Both readings are correct, and the disjunction in the question is misleading. Homogeneous research communities are a stress test for Harding. They vindicate her diagnosis and intensify the conditions that require her remedy. Whether the remedy is applied is an open empirical question, depending on structural reform of who builds and audits these systems. The slogan, a perspectival approach produces more not less objectivity, is the standard against which such communities should be evaluated.

### Mock 16: How convincing do you find Hartsock's argument that women's labour generates a privileged standpoint?

Hartsock's 1983 essay argues that women's labour, reproductive, domestic, caring, gives systematic access to dimensions of social reality men's labour does not, and that this access, when politically articulated, yields a less partial account of social life. I find the argument substantially convincing, with two qualifications: the privilege is conditional rather than automatic, and the unitary "women" of 1983 must be replaced by the multiple women of the matrix of domination. With those qualifications the argument is one of the most empirically successful claims in late-twentieth-century sociology; without them, it is vulnerable to essentialism.

Hartsock's three claims, as summarised in the lecture, are: men's and women's understandings differ because the labour they do and their access to public space differ; the dominant group's view attempts to structure social reality for everyone, while subordinate groups must additionally understand the dominant view to survive; the feminist standpoint, articulating women's experience through political struggle, reveals the partiality of the dominant view. The framework follows Marx's structure: just as the proletariat's labour gives it a vantage on capitalist relations that capital cannot see, women's labour gives them a vantage on patriarchal relations.

Consider the empirical case. Care work and domestic labour are not merely under-recognised; they are the precondition of all other social activity, and are mostly performed by women. The cost-of-living crisis has further exposed how unwaged care work absorbs shocks invisible to mainstream economics. Harding's introduction lists what androcentric frameworks rendered invisible: violence against women coded as "asking for it" (Harding, 2004: 5), the unpaid double day naturalised, menstruation medicalised, rationality narrowed to instrumental rationality of institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred. Criado-Perez catalogues cognate distortions in technical knowledge: crash-test dummies, PPE, office thermostats, pharmacology and AI bias all under-serve women. These are exactly the distortions Hartsock predicted: when one group's labour is paradigmatic, standards perform poorly when applied universally. Du Bois's prior formulation of double consciousness, "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others" (Du Bois, 1903: 5), supplies the racial precedent: the subordinate group must understand both its own life and the dominant world that frames it, while the dominant group needs only one of these.

Consider the structural reason the argument has force. "Communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133). If knowledge is produced by communities, and the communities of research and design have been homogeneous, then the unrepresented locations in those communities will be epistemically resourceful in proportion to their exclusion. Hartsock's argument is the original version of this insight.

Consider the qualifications. The privilege is not automatic. "A standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle". Men can produce feminist knowledge "from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135), and women can reproduce dominant assumptions. The standpoint is conditional on articulation through political and intellectual community. Hartsock's 1983 framing sometimes sounds more automatic than this, as if labour produced consciousness mechanically; that framing is unconvincing. The conditional reading is the convincing one.

The second qualification is intersectional. Hartsock's 1983 essay implicitly took the labour of a particular kind of woman (white, often middle-class, Western) as paradigmatic. Collins's matrix of domination in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) corrects this: women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, partly conflicting. The standpoint of an African-American domestic worker, Collins's "outsider within", is not that of a white middle-class housewife. Harding endorses this when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134), and points to a wider catalogue of standpoint sites (Harding, 2004: 129), each generating a different version of the standpoint Hartsock first theorised in the singular.

Two further objections. The relativism worry: if women's labour yields privileged access, why not anyone's labour? The reply is empirical: which standpoint generates testably less partial accounts? The reference-man data is the test, and standpoint research passes it. The essentialism worry: doesn't grounding standpoint in labour fix women into a category? The reply is the conditional reading: labour generates standpoint only when articulated through political community; the category is opened, not closed, by articulation.

How convincing? Substantially, with qualifications. The 1983 framework needs the matrix of domination and the achievement reading. With those, Hartsock's argument remains foundational. The qualifications are themselves available within the broader standpoint tradition, so the convincing version of Hartsock is the one read alongside Collins and Harding.

### Mock 17: "There is power in looking." Critically discuss with reference to hooks and the oppositional gaze.

hooks's sentence, "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), compresses three claims into five words: vision is a site of social power; vision is a site of resistance; and the conditions under which one looks, and is permitted to look, are themselves political. I want to defend each, draw out the oppositional gaze, and argue that the framework is most powerful when read as a contribution to standpoint epistemology.

Begin with the first claim. hooks's argument starts from a historical fact: enslaved Black people were punished for looking at white people. "The politics of slavery, of racialized power relations, were such that slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). The autobiographical layer: "I remember being punished as a child for staring, for those hard intense direct looks children would give grown-ups, looks that were seen as confrontational" (hooks, 1992: 115). This is not metaphor; it is a documented disciplinary practice. The right to look, to fix attention, to evaluate, was systematically withheld from Black people, especially Black women.

Berger's analysis in *Ways of Seeing* (1972), "men act and women appear. Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47), captures a related gendered structure but not the racialised denial of the gaze. hooks's contribution is the racial extension. Berger's binary (looker / looked-at) becomes hooks's triangle (looker / looked-at / forbidden looker). The forbidden looker is the structural position from which the oppositional gaze emerges. Contemporary surveillance capitalism, which extracts data extensively from groups already over-surveilled, replays the same uneven distribution of who watches and who is watched.

Take the second claim: vision is a site of resistance. "All attempts to repress our/black peoples' right to gaze had produced in us an overwhelming longing to look, a rebellious desire, an oppositional gaze" (hooks, 1992: 116). The oppositional gaze is the active reclamation of the right to look, a third position that refuses both the looked-at position and the false universal of the looker. hooks is theorising Black female spectatorship: how Black women have watched Hollywood critically, refusing the invitation to identify with white female protagonists and white male spectators, developing a critical practice of looking-against. "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality.' Even in the worse circumstances of domination, the ability to manipulate one's gaze…opens up the possibility of agency" (hooks, 1992: 116).

Now the third claim: the conditions of looking are political. The right to look is unevenly distributed. The right to be a credible looker, to have one's looking taken seriously as evidence, is even more unevenly distributed. Berger's analysis of the European nude makes the point for the gendered version. The nude is structured for an unpainted male spectator who "is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man" (Berger, 1972: 54). The supposed objectivity of the image is the suppression of one specific looker's location, what Haraway calls "the god-trick of seeing everything from nowhere" (Haraway, 1991: 189). hooks generalises to race.

Read alongside the standpoint tradition, hooks's framework is recognisably an account of achieved standpoint. The oppositional gaze is not given by Black female identity; it is produced through political and intellectual struggle, through Black feminist tradition, which the lecture connects to Collins's matrix of domination. This matches Harding: "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). The oppositional gaze is the articulated observation; it is not a property of biology but a practice of community.

One critical worry. The metaphor of looking is generative but bounded. Not all standpoints are visual; care knowledge, embodied knowledge, kinaesthetic knowledge resist visual formulation. hooks does not claim universality for the visual register; the framework is most powerful where vision is the medium of power (cinema, surveillance, the European nude). Where the medium is the body's design or the dose's calibration (the reference-man cases), the gaze metaphor needs Hartsock's labour-based framework and Harding's strong objectivity. The three are best read as a single argument articulated through different empirical sites.

"There is power in looking." hooks's sentence is diagnostic, resistance, and programme for standpoint epistemology applied to vision. It belongs at the centre of any account of how perspectives produce, rather than diminish, objectivity.

### Mock 18: Using the work of at least two authors, assess Gilroy's claim that the primal history of modernity should be reconstructed from the slaves' standpoint.

Gilroy's claim that "the time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" is the strongest single application of standpoint epistemology to historiography. The argument maps cleanly onto Harding's strong objectivity and Haraway's situated knowledge. I will use both authors to defend the claim, draw out concrete sites, and consider objections.

Harding first. The central argument of strong objectivity is that conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). The historiography of modernity has been dominated by historians writing within European national traditions, reading European-language archives, reasoning within disciplinary specialisations that took European modernity as default object. Within that community, assumptions about whose voices count, whose archives matter, are largely shared. The diagnostic implication is that assumptions invisible to the community distort the history.

Haraway second. "Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, 2004: 127), refusing the god trick. Knowledge is always made from somewhere, and the somewhere shapes what can be seen. Modernity is global, but its standard historiography is local: written from Liverpool, Manchester, Paris, Amsterdam, with plantation, slave ship and colony as peripheries to a metropolitan core. Gilroy refuses the periphery framing. The Atlantic, the Middle Passage and the plantation are constitutive of modernity; the slaves' standpoint is primary, not supplementary.

Three sites make the case. First, the political economy of capitalism. Standard histories trace industrial capitalism through Manchester cotton mills. The slaves' standpoint reveals the same history through the Caribbean sugar plantation, the cotton field, the slave ship's hold; the cotton in the Manchester mill is the labour of enslaved people, and a history that treats the mill as primary and the plantation as derivative gets the causal structure backwards. Gilroy promises an "ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood…constructed from the slaves' standpoint", the methodological promise that political economy reconstructed from the slaves' standpoint will be more accurate.

Second, the philosophy of modernity. Standard histories of Enlightenment thought treat reason, autonomy and rights as European achievements gradually extended outward. The slaves' standpoint reveals that the universal claims of these concepts were tested first by the people they excluded; the Haitian Revolution, the slave narratives, the resistance traditions were where modernity's universal claims met their critical reckoning. The conceptual frameworks of the disciplines "promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns" (Harding, 2004: 5); the corrective is to begin thought from the lives of those the frameworks excluded.

Third, the cultural history of modernity. Berger's analysis, "the surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, 1972: 47), shows the unmarked spectator of European art is gendered. Gilroy's parallel argument is that the unmarked spectator of European modernity is racialised. The corrective is the same: disclose the location of the spectator and read culture from the standpoints excluded. Indigenous-knowledge perspectives on climate science are a parallel case.

What do Harding and Haraway add? Harding adds the structural argument: the homogeneity of mainstream historiography is itself an epistemic problem, requiring structural correction (diversification of who writes history) rather than merely procedural (more attention to slavery within a still-European narrative). Haraway adds the methodological argument: situated knowledge is not a supplement to "real" history but a constitutive component of accurate historical description. Together they generate strong objectivity applied to historiography: more rigorous because more locations, archives and languages are open to scrutiny.

Two objections. One is that the slaves' standpoint is hard to reconstruct because archives are thin. The reply is that the thinness is itself diagnostic: it is a product of the same epistemic structure that produces the bias. Reconstructing the standpoint requires assembling fragmentary evidence into a critical account, "the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations" (Harding, 1991: 124). The other is the appropriation worry: reconstruction risks speaking for those it claims to amplify. Mohanty sharpens this when she shows how Western feminist scholarship produced "the third world woman" as "an effect of discourse… not a real empirical group" (Mohanty, 1984: 261); reconstruction from the slaves' standpoint must avoid the equivalent move and remain community-grounded.

Gilroy's claim is therefore not a political concession but a strong-objectivity requirement. Both Harding and Haraway predict this conclusion; the slaves' standpoint is constitutive of an honest history of modernity.

### Mock 19: Can sociology pursue objectivity while taking standpoint seriously?

The question presents the choice as a constraint to be satisfied. My answer is that sociology cannot pursue objectivity except by taking standpoint seriously: the conjunction is a requirement, not a balancing act. The argument turns on Harding's distinction between objectivity (the goal) and objectivism (the failed methodological model that has tried to instantiate it). Standpoint-aware sociology is the more rigorous route to objectivity, and the apparent tension is an artefact of the objectivism Harding's framework was built to displace.

The traditional worry is that taking standpoint seriously treats knowledge as a function of social position, which gives up on objectivity, since objectivity is supposed to be position-independent. The worry is reasonable on the assumption that objectivity equals view-from-nowhere. But the assumption is exactly what standpoint epistemology contests. Haraway's god trick names the view-from-nowhere as an epistemological fiction; Harding's strong objectivity names the alternative. Halewood reads this as the fact/value bifurcation that has structured modern sociology since Weber (Halewood, 2014: Ch. 7), and his diagnosis fits: the disjunction in the question is an artefact of a modern habit of splitting evaluation from knowing.

Harding's argument is sharp: "the problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Conventional objectivity, operationalised as detached value-freedom, "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). Assumptions shared by the whole community slip through. Strong objectivity demands more: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). The researcher's standpoint becomes part of what must be scrutinised. This is more objective, not less, because it widens the scope of critical scrutiny.

Three arguments support this.

First, the empirical argument. Sociology that ignores standpoint produces systematic distortions. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* catalogues these: crash-test dummies leave women more likely to be seriously injured; PPE built on male bodies fits female workers badly; office thermostats set to a male metabolic rate leave women too cold; pharmacology calibrated to the 70kg reference man misdoses women; bags of cement repeat the pattern. The same logic operates in AI bias and in surveillance-capitalism data extraction. Each is what objectivism produces when standpoint is ignored: a partial perspective universalised, with empirical failure as consequence.

Second, the methodological argument. Reflexivity is a strong-objectivity move. A sociology that requires positionality statements, disaggregates findings demographically, and integrates community testimony into evaluation is more rigorous, not less. It makes research more objective by placing the researcher's location on the same critical plane as the object.

Third, the community argument. "Communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133) implies the composition of the research community is epistemically relevant. A homogeneous community produces shared invisible assumptions; a diverse community catches more of them. Harding: "If the community of 'qualified' researchers and critics systematically excludes…all African-Americans and women of all races…it is not plausible to imagine that racist and sexist interests and values would be identified within a community of scientists composed entirely of people who benefit…from institutionalized racism and sexism" (Harding, 2004: 137). Objectivity depends on structural reform of personnel as methodological requirement.

Two objections.

The first is the relativism objection: that taking standpoint seriously slides into the view that all perspectives are equally valid. Harding rejects this: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). Standpoint theory ranks claims by empirical adequacy and reflexive transparency. The reference-man data is the proof: the claim that women are at higher risk is not a relativist alternative; it is a testably better claim, generated from research that started from women's bodies.

The second is the politicisation objection: integrating standpoint blurs the line between research and advocacy. Harding's reply is the converse: research that ignores standpoint is research whose politics is hidden. The reference-man examples were not products of overt political bias; they were products of communities whose political assumptions were invisible to them. Visibility is the precondition of evaluation.

Sociology can pursue objectivity only by taking standpoint seriously. The disjunction in the question is the one objectivism wants us to accept; standpoint epistemology dissolves it. The slogan is the right summary: a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity. Take standpoint seriously, and objectivity follows.

### Mock 20: Critically assess Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* as a development of standpoint epistemology.

Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) is the most generative single development of standpoint epistemology after Hartsock's 1983 founding. The book does three things at once: it intersectionalises standpoint, replacing the implicitly unitary "women" of 1983 with the multiple, intersecting locations of the matrix of domination; it makes standpoint epistemology empirically richer by directing attention to specific intersections; and it integrates standpoint theory with social-movement politics by treating the production of standpoint knowledge as continuous with the political organisation of marginalised communities. The book is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition for contemporary sociology.

Collins's starting point is the insufficiency of single-axis standpoint claims. Hartsock grounded standpoint in women's labour, but the labour of which women? The standpoint of African-American women is generative not because it is more authentic but because the intersection of race, class and gender African-American women occupy is structurally informative about multiple systems of domination simultaneously. The matrix of domination is Collins's vocabulary for this. Race, class, gender and sexuality are interlocking, not additive, axes; their integration shapes lived experience and the knowledge that flows from it.

Three features deserve emphasis.

First, the rescue from essentialism. Hartsock's framework risks treating "women" as unitary. Collins shows that women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, and partly conflicting. Her concept of "outsider within" knowledge, African-American domestic workers' access to white family life from structural exclusion, is one of the most generative ideas in twentieth-century sociology. The outsider within sees what insiders cannot, and is treated, when seen at all, as a non-witness. Harding endorses the formulation when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134), citing Collins's argument that starting thought from the lives of poor African-American women "reveals important truths about the lives of intellectuals" as well as about those women (Harding, 2004: 130).

Second, the empirical productivity. The matrix directs researchers to intersections where multiple systems combine to produce distinctive forms of knowledge. Aptheker's catalogue (Harding, 2004: 129) gives the texture: each is an intersected location whose knowledge would be missed by a single-axis framework. The reference-man cases (crash-test dummies, PPE, thermostats, pharmacology) have a matrix dimension: the "average woman" implied as deviation is usually white, Western, middle-class. AI bias compounds this: facial-recognition fails darker-skinned women at higher rates than either men of colour or white women, the intersectional pattern Collins predicts and single-axis measures miss.

Third, the integration of standpoint and politics. Collins's standpoint is articulated through Black feminist intellectual and political tradition. The production of standpoint knowledge is inseparable from the political organisation of marginalised communities. This recovers Hartsock's insistence that "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle" and matches Harding's claim that "political struggle itself produced insight" (Harding, 2004: 6).

Three critical points.

First, the matrix of domination is more powerful as a heuristic than as a precise theoretical apparatus. It directs attention productively but does not specify the mechanisms by which axes integrate. Subsequent work has supplied more precision.

Second, the framework is sometimes accused of producing analyses so multi-axis that they become unfalsifiable. The reply is that intersectional claims, like single-axis claims, are subject to empirical test. The reference-man cases, disaggregated by race as well as gender, generate testable predictions single-axis fairness measures would miss.

Third, the framework is sometimes accused of being too tied to one national context. Collins's revised editions address this; the matrix is portable to other racialised societies by attention to local histories. Mohanty's parallel critique of how Western feminist scholarship produced "the third world woman" as "an effect of discourse… not a real empirical group" (Mohanty, 1984: 261) is what the matrix avoids: intersectional analysis is community-grounded rather than centrally synthesised. The portability is part of what makes the framework a development of standpoint epistemology rather than a parochial instance.

For sociology today, Collins's framework is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition. It retains the political grounding of Hartsock, the methodological reflexivity of Harding, the situated-knowledge insistence of Haraway, and adds the structural integration of multiple axes of oppression.

hooks's oppositional gaze and Gilroy's Black Atlantic are intersectional standpoints in Collins's sense. The slogan that perspectives produce more objectivity is best read through Collins: multiple intersected perspectives produce more objectivity than either no perspective or single-axis perspectives. Collins built a richer framework in place of single-axis standpoint, one that remains the most useful tool we have for empirical sociology of inequality.
