# L6 — Standpoints, Perspectives and Epistemology

**Module:** SC301 Rethinking Modernity
**Lecture:** 6
**Theme:** Knowledge, situatedness, feminist and postcolonial epistemologies; the critique of objectivism.

---

## 1. Key concepts (≤200 words)

**Epistemology** asks how and what we know, and what justifies a knowledge-claim. **Traditional epistemology** (which Harding labels **objectivism**) treats knowledge as independent of the knower; philosophical objectivity assumes a real external world and methodological objectivity demands value-free, dispassionate research. Feminist critique exposes this neutrality as itself a position: the universal knower performs **the god trick** (Haraway), speaking from nowhere while actually speaking from a dominant location. **Situated knowledge** insists all thought bears the fingerprints of its producers. **Standpoint epistemology** (Hartsock, Harding, Smith) argues that marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate less partial accounts. **Strong objectivity** treats the knower as part of the object of inquiry, demanding **strong reflexivity**. **The reference man** (Criado-Perez) names the Caucasian-male default in crash-test dummies, PPE, and pharmacology. **Bifurcation** — mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, men/women — sustains masculinist science. **Perspectival epistemology** brings multiple positions into dialogue. Gilroy reconstructs modernity from **the slaves' standpoint**; hooks' **the oppositional gaze** treats looking as resistance; Collins' **the matrix of domination** maps intersecting axes of oppression.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded quotes

The Harding reading is the spine of the lecture and is built around a single counter-intuitive claim: that situated, politically engaged knowledge produces **more**, not less, objectivity. Harding opens by arguing that "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women", but that the disciplines "permitted no conceptual frameworks in which women as a group… became the subjects — the authors — of knowledge" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 4). The implied speaker of scientific sentences "was supposed to be humanity in general", performing what Haraway names "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (p. 4). This is the core diagnosis: objectivism universalises a particular location.

Harding insists the problem is not "bad science" but the conceptual frameworks themselves, which "promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns, which ensured that the knowledge produced through them was always socially situated" (p. 5). "Commitment to an objectivity defined as maximizing social neutrality was not itself socially neutral in its effects" (p. 5). She lists what androcentric frameworks rendered invisible: violence against women treated as women "asking for it"; the unpaid double day naturalised; menstruation and menopause medicalised; rationality narrowed to the instrumental rationality of institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (pp. 5-6). The "remedy", Harding writes, "was to start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities (or labor)" (p. 6) — but never as ethnography of women alone; standpoint projects "must 'study up'" (p. 6).

In chapter 8 Harding consolidates this into **strong objectivity**. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*; it is too weak" (p. 128). Conventional method eliminates only those values "that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (pp. 136-137) — leaving culture-wide assumptions untouched. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge. Thus, strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (p. 136). Crucially, "a maximally critical study of scientists and their communities can be done only from the perspective of those whose lives have been marginalized by such communities" (p. 136).

Harding pre-empts three charges. Against the ethnocentrism charge she notes that "standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (p. 129) — Hegel was not a slave, Marx not a wage-labourer. Against the relativism charge: "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (pp. 131-132). Against the essentialism charge: the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (p. 134), starting from "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (p. 134).

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* sits alongside Harding as a parallel critique of the gendered structure of vision. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (p. 47). Berger shows that the European nude is structured by an unseen male spectator — "the principal protagonist is never painted. He is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man" (p. 54). This anticipates hooks: power resides in who is licensed to look. hooks' oppositional gaze is the inverted, resistant move: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 116).

The lecture chains these arguments into one claim: epistemology is political. Whose body counts as the reference body — in crash tests, drug trials, PPE — is decided by who is in the room when the standards are set. Criado-Perez's evidence (women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in crashes; medical research excluding women through the 1970s-80s; PPE failing female health workers in the COVID pandemic) is not a series of accidents but the empirical face of objectivism.

### 2024 (Q5): In what ways does the feminist epistemology overturn the canons and practices of traditional epistemology?

Feminist epistemology does not abolish epistemology; it rebuilds it. I will argue that it overturns the traditional canon in three connected ways: by exposing the universal knower as a partial knower, by reconstructing objectivity as situated rather than detached, and by reorganising the practical institutions through which knowledge is produced. Together these moves do not weaken the search for truth — they make it more rigorous.

The traditional canon, as the lecture set out, rests on two definitions of objectivity. Philosophically, it posits a real world independent of the knower, which science discovers; methodologically, it demands that the researcher be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding's notion of objectivism collapses these into a single claim: that knowledge can be produced from no particular location. Donna Haraway's account of situated knowledge names this fantasy "the god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 4). The first overturning of the canon is this exposure: the universal knower of modern Western philosophy was always a specific knower — male, white, bourgeois, European — whose social location was concealed by being treated as default. Spender's phrase, cited in the lecture, captures the inversion sharply: "objectivity is male subjectivity" (Spender, *Man Made Language*, 1980).

The second overturning is the reconstruction of objectivity itself. Critics often suppose that feminist epistemology must choose between objectivity and relativism. Harding refuses this dichotomy. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 128). Conventional method, she argues, can only eliminate the values that "differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (p. 137). Culture-wide assumptions — what an entire research community already takes for granted — slip through. Harding's notion of strong objectivity demands that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical plane as the object: "strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (p. 136). The canon's commitment to detachment is replaced by a commitment to disclosed, examined, accountable location.

The third overturning is institutional and practical. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* documents what happens when the reference man becomes the default. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg) leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. PPE designed for European and American male bodies failed female nurses during the COVID pandemic. Standard office temperatures were calibrated to the male metabolic rate. Pharmaceutical doses were calculated against a "Caucasian Man" whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). Clinical trials excluded women through the 1970s and 1980s, contributing to under-recognised symptoms in heart disease and to pain being systematically under-treated in women. The Henrietta Lacks case and the long history of medical experimentation on Black women add a racialised layer to the same structural failure. These are not failures of methodology in the narrow sense; they are failures of who is in the room when the categories are set.

Feminist epistemology overturns the canon by changing the personnel and procedures of knowledge production. Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, "in and out of the home", gives them access to dimensions of social reality the dominant group cannot see, but only when that experience is articulated through political struggle. As Harding insists in the slide quoted from her 1991 book, "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). A standpoint is not an opinion poll of women; it is a worked-up critical perspective.

This third overturning extends beyond gender. Collins' matrix of domination, hooks' oppositional gaze, and Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint generalise the move. Each insists that historically marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate critical insights unavailable from the centre. Police data on missing women, Black maternal mortality, the gender data gap in AI training sets — these become visible only when the categories of analysis are themselves contested.

A critic might object that this remains too tied to identity, risking essentialism or fragmentation. Harding's reply, which I find persuasive, is that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (p. 134); it is the thinker "whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (p. 134). The alternative — pretending no one is in the room — is what produced the reference man in the first place.

Feminist epistemology therefore overturns the canon at three levels: at the level of the knower (exposing the god trick), at the level of objectivity (replacing detachment with strong reflexivity), and at the level of institutions (reorganising who counts and who is counted). It is, as the lecture put it, not about undermining knowledge but about producing "better, more robust forms of knowledge".

### 2025 (Q8): Why is the concept of perspectives important for understanding the status of epistemology within modernity?

The concept of perspectives matters for modernity because modernity inherited an epistemology that disavowed perspective altogether. The modern view from nowhere — the Enlightenment ideal of a universal knower — is the conceit Harding calls objectivism and Haraway names the god trick. I will argue that recovering the concept of perspectives is decisive for understanding the status of epistemology in modernity for three reasons: it diagnoses modernity's epistemological fiction, it explains the political effects of that fiction, and it offers a route to a more accurate, plural science.

First, modernity defined itself epistemically against superstition by claiming a method that transcended location. The bifurcations rehearsed in the lecture — mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, science/superstition, objectivity/subjectivity, men/women — encode this. Science is on the rational, cultural, mind-side; superstition, nature, body and women on the other. Harding's notion of strong objectivity exposes the trick: "All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 128). The pretence that scientific thought escapes this is "a delusion — and a historical identifiable one" (p. 128). The concept of perspectives names what modernity tried to hide: that every claim is made from somewhere.

The slide-headline of the lecture put the consequence sharply: "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity." This is not a paradox once we see that the alternative — pretending we have no perspective — does not eliminate perspective, it only hides it. Hidden perspectives become culture-wide assumptions transported into research findings.

Second, perspectives matter because their absence has political effects. The lecture opens this through Criado-Perez's documentation of "a world designed for men". When the reference man is the only body imagined, women die at higher rates in car crashes, are misdiagnosed in cardiac emergencies, and receive doses calculated for bodies they do not have. Medical research excluded women through the 1970s and 1980s. Pain is under-treated in women. PPE failed female health workers in COVID. Police miss recurring patterns in missing-women cases. AI training data, drawn from histories where dominant perspectives dominated, encodes those perspectives at scale. Black maternal mortality in the United States is over three times the white rate. None of these is a methodological accident; each is what happens when a single perspective is universalised.

Modernity's pretence of no-perspective is itself a perspective — and a powerful one. As Harding writes, "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge and the claims resulting from adherence to such standards should be found preferable by all rational creatures, past, present, and future" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 131). The concept of perspectives therefore matters because it lets us name the political work that no-perspective claims do.

Third, the concept of perspectives matters because it offers a constructive epistemology rather than a sceptical one. Standpoint epistemology — Hartsock's feminist standpoint, Smith's everyday-life sociology, Collins' matrix of domination, Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint, hooks' oppositional gaze — does not say all perspectives are equal. It says different positions afford different insights and that some positions, when politically articulated, see what dominant positions cannot. Hartsock's two claims, reproduced on the slide, are that men's and women's understandings differ because of their material labour and access to public space, and that the dominant group's views attempt to "structure the world for everybody". Standpoint, the lecture stresses, "is not automatic — it is an achievement won from political struggle".

Bringing multiple perspectives together does not produce a single objective view but a "multiple objectivity": different vantages corroborating, contesting and supplementing one another. This is what the lecture calls a perspectival epistemology. It refuses bifurcation — refuses to choose between universal objectivity and relativism — and produces what Harding calls strong objectivity: a knowledge that is more accountable because its location is disclosed.

Modernity's self-image as the epoch of universal reason is therefore both a description and a problem. As a description it captures the ambition that gave us science; as a problem it captures the suppression of perspective that gave us the reference man. The concept of perspectives lets us hold both: it allows us to keep the ambition (truth, evidence, testability) while abandoning the fiction (the view from nowhere). Gilroy's claim that "the time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" is not anti-modern — it is a demand that modernity finally take seriously its own commitment to evidence by including the evidence it has excluded. hooks' insistence that "there is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115) generalises the point: who looks, and from where, is constitutive of what is known.

The status of epistemology in modernity, then, is unstable without the concept of perspectives. With it, modernity becomes intelligible — and correctable.

### 2023 (Q8): What do sociologists mean by the term "standpoint epistemology"? How useful is this approach?

Standpoint epistemology is the claim that what we know is shaped by where we stand in social structure, and that some social locations, when their inhabitants reflect critically on them, afford less partial accounts of how a society works than other locations do. I will define the term, distinguish it from neighbouring positions, and then assess its usefulness, concluding that it is highly useful, especially when paired with Harding's notion of strong objectivity.

The standpoint thesis was systematised by Nancy Hartsock's 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism", developed by Dorothy Smith's everyday-world sociology, Harding's three books in the 1980s and 1990s, and Patricia Hill Collins' *Black Feminist Thought*. The lecture summarised it in three propositions. First, men's and women's understandings of the world differ because of the different labour they do and their different access to public space. Second, the dominant group's view attempts to structure the world for everyone. Third, because the feminist standpoint reveals the partiality of the dominant view, it opens up a path to liberation. Crucially, the lecture insists that "a standpoint is not automatic — it is an achievement won from political struggle".

This last clause distinguishes standpoint epistemology from three positions with which it is often confused. It is not identity politics: being a woman does not guarantee a feminist standpoint, and Harding emphasises that men can produce feminist knowledge by "starting from women's lives" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 135). It is not relativism: standpoint theory denies that all perspectives are equally adequate to a given reality. As Harding writes, "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (p. 131). And it is not ethnocentrism: the claim that women's lives are a good place to start thinking does not assert that women's culture is superior; it asserts that marginal locations offer epistemic resources that central ones suppress.

Three concrete examples show what the approach offers. First, the lecture's reference-man material. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male; women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. Standpoint epistemology predicts this kind of distortion: when only one social position is in the room when categories are set, the categories will reflect that position. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* (2019) documents this across pharmacology, PPE, urban planning and AI. Clinical trials excluded women through the 1970s-80s; medical research treated the male body as default; pain in women is under-diagnosed.

Second, Collins' analysis of African-American domestic workers as "outsiders within". Their location in white households gave them empirical access to how dominant groups thought when they thought no Black worker was listening. The standpoint here is not an opinion but a vantage that becomes critical when articulated through Black feminist theory. Collins' matrix of domination shows how race, class, gender and sexuality intersect — extending standpoint thinking beyond a single axis.

Third, Gilroy's *Black Atlantic*. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view." Modernity, told from Liverpool and Glasgow, is industrial revolution and Enlightenment; told from the Middle Passage and the plantation, it is also slavery, racialised capitalism and the long denial of personhood. hooks' oppositional gaze is the same move at the level of vision: "there is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115).

The usefulness of the approach is therefore considerable. It explains structural patterns (the gender data gap, racialised medical neglect) that frameworks ignoring social position cannot easily account for. It generates research programmes (feminist science studies, Black sociology, postcolonial historiography). It improves science by widening the evidence base — which is exactly Harding's argument for strong objectivity: more locations, more reflexivity, more rigour.

There are objections. Critics worry about essentialism — assuming a unitary "women's standpoint". Harding answers that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (p. 134), including the liberal, socialist, Sandinista, Islamic and Third World feminisms which start from different women's lives. Critics worry about regress: who decides which standpoint is more illuminating? Harding's answer is empirical — judge by which produces more accurate, less partial accounts of nature and social relations. Critics worry about politics replacing evidence, but the lecture and the reading insist that "good politics" produces "good science" by widening evidence and exposing assumptions, not by replacing them.

A more serious worry is that standpoint epistemology is most useful for the social sciences and less obviously applicable to physical science. Harding addresses this by pointing out that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts — "wild animals" are categorised differently by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (p. 140, n.17). The point generalises: the agenda, funding, and ethical limits of physical science are social.

Standpoint epistemology is thus useful both as diagnosis and as method. It does not replace evidence; it widens it. Its central insight — that the disclosed location of knowledge is a resource rather than a contaminant — is one of the more durable contributions of late twentieth-century sociology.

### 2022 (Q9): Does feminist epistemology reject the concept of objectivity?

The short answer is no. Feminist epistemology, as articulated by Harding, Haraway, Hartsock, Smith and Collins, does not reject objectivity; it rejects objectivism — the conflation of objectivity with detached neutrality. I will argue this in three steps: first, by distinguishing objectivity from objectivism; second, by showing that Harding's notion of strong objectivity is a strengthening, not a weakening, of objectivity; third, by considering and rebutting the charge that feminist epistemology slides into relativism.

The lecture set out two traditional definitions of objectivity. The philosophical sense holds that there is a real world independent of human thought, which science discovers. The methodological sense holds that researchers must be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding labels the conflation of these two definitions, and the assumption that science as actually practised achieves them, as **objectivism**. "Harding refers to the traditional notion that science (natural and social) should be objective in the sense of value-free and without bias… as objectivism" (lecture slide quoting Harding, 1991: 143). She continues: "the concept of value-free, impartial, dispassionate research… has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (p. 143). Objectivism, in other words, claims neutrality but operationalises it as agreement among a homogeneous community. Anything that community already takes for granted slips through.

Feminist epistemology rejects this. It does not reject the underlying ambition — that knowledge should track reality and not merely opinion. Harding is explicit: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 131). The lecture restated the headline: "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge."

What feminist epistemology offers in place of objectivism is Harding's notion of strong objectivity. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (p. 128). Strong objectivity requires that "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (p. 136). The knower is part of the system being known; the social position of the researcher is itself open to scientific scrutiny. This is what Harding calls "strong reflexivity" (p. 136). It is more rigorous than weak objectivity because it widens the field of what must be examined to include the researcher's own assumptions.

The empirical case for this is in the lecture's reference-man material. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male; PPE designed for European male bodies; pharmacology calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male; clinical trials excluding women through the 1970s-80s. Each of these is the product of research that believed itself objective. None of them were objective in any meaningful sense: they encoded a single social position as universal. Strong objectivity demands that the location of the researcher and the research community be disclosed and examined; weak objectivity calls anything not visible to the dominant community "neutral".

The Berger reading supports this from the side of culture. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at. This determines not only most relations between men and women but also the relation of women to themselves" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The European nude exemplifies a pseudo-objective image — the woman painted as if free of context — which is in fact organised around an unpainted male spectator. The "objectivity" of the painting is a covert positioning. Feminist epistemology applied to science finds the same pattern: covert positioning passed off as no positioning.

A common objection is that feminist epistemology, once it admits that all knowledge is situated, has no resources to distinguish better from worse claims and so collapses into relativism. Harding pre-empts this. "Standpoint theory provides arguments for the claim that some social situations are scientifically better than others as places from which to start off knowledge projects" (p. 131). Some locations afford insights others suppress. The standard is empirical: which research programme generates less partial and distorted accounts? Standpoint feminism's record — producing accounts of unpaid domestic labour, of gendered violence, of the medical neglect of women, of the gender data gap — is itself the evidence.

A subtler objection is that strong objectivity is just objectivity with reflexivity bolted on. I would reply that the bolt-on changes the structure. ### 2021 (Q9): Outline and assess the feminist epistemologist critique of "masculinist" approaches to knowledge.

The feminist critique of masculinist knowledge has three components: a critique of who has produced knowledge, a critique of what counts as knowledge, and a critique of how knowledge has been validated. I will outline each, illustrate with examples, and then assess the critique, concluding that it is largely successful and remains the most productive sociological account of the politics of knowledge.

The first component is a critique of personnel. As the lecture put it, "most philosophers and scientists and sociologists (in the West) have been men. As Sandra Harding puts it, this means that the concerns, the topics and procedures of science, philosophy and much social theory are 'man-made'". Dale Spender extended the point to language itself. This is not a complaint about individual men. It is a structural observation: when a research community is homogeneous along a major axis of social stratification, the assumptions it shares will not show up to it as assumptions. Harding makes this explicit: conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 137). Whatever the entire community takes for granted is invisible to its method.

The second component is a critique of content. The lecture catalogued the empirical consequences via Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women*. Crash-test dummies based on the 50th-percentile male leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash. PPE based on male European bodies failed women health workers during COVID. Office temperatures were calibrated to male metabolic rates. Pharmaceutical doses were designed around a 70kg Caucasian man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not." Medical research excluded women through the 1970s and 1980s. Pain in women is under-diagnosed. Police data on missing women have repeatedly missed pattern offending — the failures around Stephen Port and around Sarah Everard's killing involved categories of risk that did not see what was in front of them. Black maternal mortality in the US runs at three to four times the white rate. AI training sets, drawn from histories shaped by these exclusions, propagate them.

The third component is a critique of validation — the standards by which a claim is accepted as knowledge. The masculinist tradition operationalises objectivity as detachment and value-freedom. Feminist epistemology argues this is itself a value, a position, and one that systematically disadvantages knowledge claims emerging from marginalised lives. Harding's strong objectivity replaces detachment with "strong reflexivity" (p. 136): the researcher's location is itself an object of inquiry. Haraway's situated knowledge replaces the god trick with "feminist objectivity… quite simply situated knowledges". Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, articulated through political struggle, generates accounts that "reveal the partiality of the dominant (male) vision of social relations".

These three critiques converge on a single claim: masculinist knowledge is partial knowledge passing as universal. Berger demonstrates this from the side of vision. "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The supposed neutrality of the European nude is the suppression of a male spectator who organises the entire image. hooks generalises: "there is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). Who sees, and from where, is constitutive of what is known.

Assessing the critique, I find it largely persuasive on three grounds. Empirically, the reference-man examples are not anecdotes; they are documented, systemic, and lethal. Methodologically, the move from objectivism to strong objectivity strengthens rather than weakens science by widening reflexivity. Politically, the critique has been generative: it has produced research programmes — feminist science studies, Black feminist sociology, postcolonial historiography, disability studies — that have changed what counts as a legitimate question.

Three reservations are worth registering. First, the critique risks essentialism if "masculinist" is treated as a property of all men's thought rather than a property of a structure. Harding addresses this: men can produce feminist knowledge by starting from women's lives (p. 135), and women can reproduce masculinist assumptions. Second, the critique can be misread as anti-science. Harding's defence of strong objectivity rebuts this; the lecture's slogan — "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge" — should be taken at face value. Third, there is a question of scope: does the critique apply to physical sciences as fully as to social? Harding argues yes, on the grounds that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts, but the case is harder to make for, say, the standard model of particle physics than for medical research. The strongest version of the critique applies where the object of knowledge is itself socially constituted — which covers most of what social science studies and a large portion of biomedical and applied science.

The critique succeeds. It diagnoses a real failure of self-understanding in the masculinist tradition; it offers concrete tools (strong objectivity, strong reflexivity, situated knowledge, the matrix of domination) to repair that failure; and it has demonstrated empirically that doing so produces better knowledge.

### Mock 1: To what extent does Harding's notion of strong objectivity rescue rather than abandon the ideal of scientific objectivity?

Harding's strong objectivity is, I will argue, a rescue rather than an abandonment of scientific objectivity — but a rescue that requires real revisions to the inherited ideal. The clearest evidence for this reading is Harding's own framing: "the problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 128). She is upgrading the standard, not retiring it.

The inherited ideal of objectivity has two components: the metaphysical claim that there is a world independent of our beliefs about it, and the methodological claim that good researchers should minimise the influence of their own biases. Harding does not deny either component. What she denies is a third claim, often smuggled in with the first two: that good method as currently practised actually achieves the second. She calls this conflation "objectivism".

The empirical case for the rescue is straightforward. The lecture's reference-man examples — crash-test dummies, PPE, pharmacology, clinical trials, office temperatures — are not failures of metaphysical realism. The world is real; bodies do collide with steering columns at measurable speeds. They are failures of methodological objectivity: the research community failed to eliminate assumptions about whose body counts as the body. Strong objectivity addresses precisely this kind of failure by demanding "strong reflexivity" — the requirement that "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (p. 136). The metaphysical realism is preserved; the methodology is strengthened.

A second piece of evidence for the rescue reading is Harding's explicit rejection of relativism. "Judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (p. 131). She insists feminist and prefeminist claims are "usually not complementary but conflicting, just as the claim that the earth is flat conflicts with the claim that it is round" (p. 132). The standpoint framework is committed to comparison, evaluation and rejection of bad claims; this is incompatible with abandonment of objectivity.

Where the rescue does involve revision is in three areas. First, the subject of knowledge is no longer treated as transparent. Empiricist epistemology treats the knower as a neutral conduit; Harding treats the knower as part of the system to be examined. Second, communities replace individuals as the primary unit of knowing. "Communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (p. 133). Third, value-neutrality is reconceived. Harding draws the crucial distinction: "not all social values and interests have the same bad effects upon the results of research. Democracy-advancing values have systematically generated less partial and distorted beliefs than others" (p. 137). The rescued objectivity is not value-free; it is reflexively value-aware.

Three objections to the rescue reading deserve answers. First, the conservative objection: the revisions are so substantial that what survives no longer deserves the name "objectivity". My reply is that the metaphysical realism survives intact, the criterion of empirical adequacy survives intact, and the demand for inter-subjective verification survives intact — only now expanded to include marginalised inter-subjects. Second, the radical objection (from some poststructuralists): the rescue is too conservative; objectivity is too compromised to be worth keeping. Harding's reply, which I endorse, is that we need a vocabulary to distinguish less partial from more partial accounts, and objectivity is the best such vocabulary we have. Third, the operationalisation objection: how do we actually do strong objectivity in practice? Harding's answer is procedural: integrate marginalised voices into scientific communities, treat the conceptual frameworks of the disciplines themselves as objects of critical inquiry, and adopt strong reflexivity in research design.

The clearest illustration of the rescue at work is the gender data gap. Criado-Perez's research is rigorous empirical science; it uses standard methods of quantitative analysis to demonstrate that women are systematically under-represented in datasets. This work was made possible by adopting the standpoint of women's lives as a starting point for research. It produced findings that are not relativistic, not anti-science, and that have changed regulatory policy in several jurisdictions. It is strong objectivity in action.

To what extent, then, is this a rescue? Almost entirely. Strong objectivity preserves what was worth preserving in the traditional ideal — the commitment to a knowable world, to empirical adequacy, to inter-subjective verification — while abandoning what was indefensible: the pretence that current scientific communities had achieved neutrality. The slogan of the lecture captures the result: "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity". The ideal is rescued by being made more demanding.

### Mock 2: Discuss, with examples, how the concept of the reference man exposes the limits of supposedly universal knowledge.

The lecture noted the consequences: women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a car crash than men, and 17% more likely to die. The seatbelt geometry and crumple-zone calibration assume male torsos and male shoulder positions; women's bodies fit the assumptions less well. PPE is the second example. Pharmacology is the third example: doses are calibrated to bodies with male hormone profiles, male hepatic metabolism, and male body composition. The result was a body of pharmacological evidence drawn almost entirely from male subjects. Pain in women is under-treated; women presenting with the same complaints as men are more likely to be referred to psychiatry rather than gastroenterology or cardiology. The Henrietta Lacks case adds a racialised dimension: HeLa cells, drawn without consent from a Black woman in 1951, underwrote billions of dollars of biomedical research without acknowledgement or compensation. Black maternal mortality in the United States is over three times the white rate. These are not glitches; they are systemic features of knowledge calibrated to a particular body.

Methodologically, the reference man exposes the limits of objectivism in the precise sense Harding describes. The clinical trials that excluded women in the 1970s-80s were objective by every conventional standard: randomised, controlled, peer-reviewed, replicated. They were not objective in the sense that mattered: they failed to ask whose body they were studying. Harding's strong objectivity demands that "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 136). The reference man is what happens when this is not done. The conceptual framework — "an adult human is a 70kg Caucasian male" — was never tested because it was never visible as a framework.

Conceptually, the reference man exposes the structure of pseudo-universal knowledge. The point is not that universal knowledge is impossible. It is that knowledge claims that aspire to universality must be tested across the range they claim to cover. When the testing community itself is homogeneous, this testing does not happen. Criado-Perez's data make this an empirical generalisation: in domains where the testing community is more diverse (paediatrics has long had to take seriously that children are not small adults), the reference-body problem is smaller; in domains where it is more homogeneous, the reference-body problem is larger.

The concept also extends. AI training sets exhibit a reference-man problem at scale. Facial recognition systems trained on predominantly white male faces misidentify Black women at the highest rates (Buolamwini and Gebru, *Gender Shades*). Voice recognition trained on male voices misrecognises female voices. The pattern is the same as the crash-test dummy: a homogeneous training population is treated as universal, and the system fails on bodies and voices outside that population.

The reference man therefore exposes three limits of supposedly universal knowledge. First, that "supposedly universal" is rarely actually universal — it is usually a default whose specificity has been forgotten. Second, that the methodological standards of conventional objectivity do not catch this kind of failure, because the failure lies in the conceptual framework, not in the execution. Third, that the remedy is structural: the personnel of knowledge-producing communities must change for the problem to be visible. Strong objectivity is the conceptual tool; the integration of marginalised voices into research communities is the practical mechanism.

### Mock 3: Compare Hartsock's materialist standpoint theory with Haraway's account of situated knowledge: which is the more useful for sociology today?

Hartsock's feminist standpoint and Haraway's situated knowledge are sister theories with different emphases. Both reject the god trick of view-from-nowhere knowledge. Both argue that all knowledge is socially located. But they differ in where they ground their epistemic privilege, in how strongly they assert that privilege, and in their political register. I will outline each, compare them, and argue that for sociology today the most useful position combines them — but if forced to choose, Haraway's framework is marginally more useful because it is less tied to a specific labour-process story.

Hartsock's feminist standpoint, set out in her 1983 essay and summarised on the lecture slide, has three propositions. First, men's and women's understandings of the world differ because of the different labour they do — Hartsock follows Marx in grounding consciousness in material activity. Second, the dominant group's worldview attempts to structure social reality for everyone. Third, because the feminist standpoint reveals the partiality of the dominant view, it opens the possibility of liberation. The framework is materialist: standpoint follows labour and bodily activity. Hartsock argues that women's reproductive labour, caring labour and unpaid domestic work gives them an embodied vantage on social reality that men's labour does not.

Haraway's situated knowledge, articulated in her 1988 essay "Situated Knowledges", begins from the same anti-objectivism but takes a different route. "Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges" (cited in Harding, *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 127). The famous "god trick" passage is Haraway's: knowledge is always partial, embodied and located, never the view from nowhere. Haraway's framework is informed by science studies, biology and cybernetics rather than by Marx. She is wary of any move that grants automatic epistemic privilege to a category — including women — because such moves risk reinstating a new universal subject. Her cyborg figure (from "A Cyborg Manifesto") explicitly resists the unified standpoint in favour of partial, contradictory, technologically-mediated identities.

The two frameworks share three commitments: rejection of the god trick, insistence that knowledge is located, and the claim that disclosed location strengthens rather than weakens objectivity. They differ in three respects.

First, the source of epistemic privilege. For Hartsock, privilege follows from a specific material location — women's labour. For Haraway, privilege is more diffuse: any disclosed location is epistemically better than a concealed one, and marginal locations afford specific insights but no automatic priority. Haraway is more cautious about identifying a single privileged standpoint.

Second, the strength of the privilege claim. Hartsock comes close to claiming that the feminist standpoint generates a less partial account of social reality tout court. Haraway is more pluralist: partial perspectives must be joined together for fuller knowledge, and no single perspective is the master perspective.

Third, the political register. Hartsock's framework is closer to classical Marxist politics — it locates revolutionary potential in a specific material class (women as a sex-class). Haraway's framework is closer to coalition politics — it locates knowledge in shifting, partial alliances of differently-situated actors.

For contemporary sociology, both frameworks remain productive. Hartsock's materialism is indispensable for thinking about gendered labour, care economies, and the unpaid work that sustains capitalism. The pandemic made this visible: care work, disproportionately performed by women, kept societies running, and the political reckoning with that fact owes much to Hartsock's lineage. Haraway's framework is indispensable for thinking about technology, big data, and the ways in which knowledge is now mediated through socio-technical assemblages. Algorithmic discrimination, surveillance, and AI training-set bias are best addressed through situated-knowledge frameworks.

If forced to choose, I would marginally prefer Haraway for sociology today, for three reasons. First, the labour-process story Hartsock relies on has become more complicated as women's labour-market positions have diversified — there is no longer a single dominant women's labour activity. Second, contemporary sociology must engage technology in ways the 1983 standpoint framework did not anticipate. Third, intersectional analysis — Crenshaw, Collins — sits more naturally inside Haraway's partial-perspectives framework than inside Hartsock's single-standpoint framework. Collins' matrix of domination, which is essentially intersectional standpoint theory, is closer to Haraway in form even though it shares Hartsock's political ambition.

But the choice is artificial. Sociology today uses both. Standpoint thinking provides the political grounding; situated knowledge provides the methodological flexibility. The lecture's framing — that a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity — is best read as a synthesis of the two, with Harding's strong objectivity as the bridge.

### Mock 4: Is standpoint epistemology committed to relativism? Discuss.

Standpoint epistemology is not committed to relativism, and I will argue that this is one of the most carefully defended positions in the feminist epistemological tradition. The accusation of relativism is the single most common misreading of standpoint theory, and it is wrong in three different ways.

The first sense of relativism is what Harding calls "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism" — the view that all knowledge claims are equally valid because all are situated. Harding rejects this explicitly: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 131). She illustrates the rejection sharply: "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses, that only Man the Hunter made important contributions to distinctively human history, that women are biologically programmed to succeed at mothering and fail at equal participation in governing society, that women's preferred modes of moral reasoning are inferior to men's, that targets of rape and battering must bear the responsibility for what happens to them" (pp. 131-132). Feminist standpoint theory asserts these claims as false, not as alternative narratives.

The second sense of relativism is sociological — the descriptive claim that different communities hold different beliefs. Harding accepts this descriptive claim and distinguishes it from the normative claim that all such beliefs are equally adequate. "Sociological relativism permits us to acknowledge that different people hold different beliefs, but what is at issue in rethinking objectivity is the different matter of judgmental or epistemological relativism. Standpoint theories neither hold nor are doomed to it" (p. 132). The distinction matters: standpoint theory is a sociology of knowledge as well as an epistemology, and it can describe variation without endorsing it.

The third sense of relativism is the one critics most often impute: that once you accept that knowledge is socially located, you have no principled basis for ranking competing claims. Standpoint theory's positive thesis directly counters this. "Standpoint theory provides arguments for the claim that some social situations are scientifically better than others as places from which to start off knowledge projects" (p. 131). The criterion is empirical: which research programmes produce less partial and distorted accounts? The criterion is also reflexive: which programmes are willing to examine their own assumptions? Standpoint theory ranks claims by empirical adequacy and reflexive transparency, not by social acceptability.

The positive case against relativism rests on three pillars. First, the metaphysical realism of standpoint theory: it assumes a real social world that research is trying to describe. Hartsock's standpoint theory is materialist; it grounds standpoint in actual labour activity, not in arbitrary linguistic communities. Second, the comparative methodology: standpoint theory compares accounts against one another, asking which sees more of what is going on. Third, the political constraint: standpoint claims are accountable to the lives of the people whose standpoint is being articulated. A feminist standpoint that the women it claims to speak for would reject is, on standpoint theory's own grounds, an inadequate standpoint.

The reference-man examples show this in operation. The claim that crash-test dummies based on the 50th-percentile male produce data that systematically underestimates risk to women is not a relativist claim — it is a factual claim, testable against injury statistics. Women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash. This is not an alternative narrative; it is data. Standpoint theory's intervention is to ask why this data was not collected earlier, and the answer involves the homogeneity of the research community. The intervention is empirical and political, not relativist.

Three objections to my reading deserve answers. First, that standpoint theory's privileging of marginal lives is itself a non-objective move. Harding's reply is that marginal lives provide better starting points for knowledge claims about a stratified society, because they have access to dimensions of social reality the dominant group cannot see. This is an empirical claim, defensible by example: Collins' analysis of African-American domestic workers, Smith's analysis of bifurcated consciousness, Aptheker on holocaust survivors and Chicana cannery workers, Gilroy on the slaves' standpoint. Second, that standpoint theory cannot adjudicate between competing standpoints. Harding's reply: the criterion is the same as for any knowledge claim — empirical adequacy and reflexive transparency. Third, that "less partial" is itself contestable. Yes, but contestable in the same way that all scientific claims are contestable — by evidence and argument, not by appeal to authority.

Standpoint epistemology is therefore not committed to relativism. It is committed to the view that good knowledge is situated, comparable, and accountable. The slogan that organises the lecture — "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity" — is incompatible with relativism. The whole point is more objectivity, not the dissolution of objectivity into a plurality of valid views.

Black feminist health research predicted that bias in pain assessment would produce under-treatment of Black patients; large studies have confirmed this. Predictive utility is the gold standard of scientific quality, and feminist research has it.

Two objections deserve answers.

Conventional research that pretends to no politics is research whose politics is invisible; making politics visible is what allows it to be evaluated. The reference-man examples are not products of political bias — they are products of a research community whose own political assumptions were invisible to it. Feminist research, by making the political dimension explicit, allows it to be examined. The lecture's slogan applies: this produces more objectivity, not less.

The second objection: that feminist epistemology is most useful for the social sciences and biomedical sciences, and less obviously useful for the physical sciences. There is some force in this. The standpoint framework applies most directly where the object of knowledge is socially constituted. But even in physics, the choice of research questions, the allocation of funding, and the composition of research teams shape what is investigated and what is found. Karen Barad's *Meeting the Universe Halfway* makes a strong case that even quantum measurement involves agential cuts that are not neutral. The scope of the framework may be narrower in physics than in sociology, but it is not zero.

A final consideration is sociological rather than methodological. Feminist epistemology has produced not only better individual findings but also better scientific communities — more diverse, more reflexive, more publicly accountable. The 21st-century push for open science, for data transparency, for inclusive review and for the integration of community knowledge owes much to the feminist epistemological tradition. Better science emerges from better scientific communities. Feminist epistemology has contributed to both.

The slogan stands. Feminist epistemology produces more science, more rigorous science, and more useful science. The lecture's framing — "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge" — is, on the evidence, correct.

### Mock 6: Single-author — critically assess Patricia Hill Collins's contribution to standpoint epistemology through her concept of the matrix of domination.

Patricia Hill Collins's contribution to standpoint epistemology, articulated in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) and developed across subsequent work, transforms the field by making intersectionality constitutive of standpoint rather than a complication added on. Her concept of the matrix of domination — race, class, gender and sexuality as interlocking, mutually constituting axes of oppression — solves three problems that earlier standpoint theory struggled with. I will outline the contribution, assess it against the standpoint tradition, and argue that it remains the most generative framework for empirical sociology today.

Collins's starting point is the insufficiency of single-axis standpoint claims. Hartsock's feminist standpoint, grounded in women's labour, implicitly took the labour of a particular kind of woman — usually white, often middle-class, often Western — as paradigmatic. The lived experience of African-American women, especially poor and working-class African-American women, did not fit. Collins's analytical move is to treat this fit problem as analytically productive. The standpoint of African-American women is generative not because it is more authentic than other standpoints, but because the intersection of race, class and gender it occupies is structurally informative about the operation of multiple systems of domination simultaneously.

The matrix of domination is Collins's vocabulary for this. Domination operates not as a sum of separate vectors but as an integrated structure. Gender oppression in the United States cannot be analysed apart from racial oppression; the categories of "woman" and "Black" are mutually constituted by histories of slavery, domestic labour, and reproductive control. Race in turn cannot be analysed apart from class. Collins's matrix puts these together not by addition but by integration.

Three contributions follow.

First, Collins rescues standpoint theory from essentialism. Hartsock's framework risks treating "women" as a unitary subject; Collins shows that women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, and partly conflicting. The standpoint of an African-American domestic worker in 1950s Chicago is not the same as the standpoint of a white middle-class housewife in 1950s Chicago, and the differences are not noise — they are signal. Collins's analysis of "outsider within" knowledge — African-American domestic workers' access to white family life from a position of structural exclusion — is one of the most generative ideas in 20th-century sociology.

Second, Collins makes standpoint epistemology empirically richer. The matrix of domination directs researchers to specific intersections — Black women's experience of healthcare, working-class queer experience of policing, Indigenous women's experience of resource extraction — where multiple systems intersect to produce distinctive forms of knowledge. The Black maternal mortality data is a case in point: a four-times-higher mortality rate for Black women than for white women in the United States is not explained by either race or gender alone; it is a matrix effect.

Third, Collins integrates standpoint epistemology with social movement politics. The standpoint, on Collins's account, is articulated through Black feminist intellectual tradition — Ida B. Wells, Anna Julia Cooper, Sojourner Truth, Audre Lorde — which is also a political tradition. Collins's framework treats the production of standpoint knowledge as inseparable from the political organisation of marginalised communities. This recovers Hartsock's original insistence — quoted in the lecture — that "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle".

Three critical points are worth registering.

First, the matrix of domination is more powerful as a heuristic than as a precise theoretical apparatus. It directs attention productively but does not in itself specify the mechanisms by which axes of oppression integrate. The follow-up work by Crenshaw on intersectionality, by McCall on the methodological complexity of intersectional research, and by Collins herself in *Intersectionality as Critical Social Theory* (2019), has tried to provide more precision.

Second, the framework is sometimes accused of producing analyses so multi-axis that they become unfalsifiable. The reply is that intersectional claims, like single-axis claims, are subject to empirical test — the Black maternal mortality data is precisely such a test, and it confirms an intersectional prediction.

Third, the framework is sometimes accused of being too tied to the US racial context. Collins's later work, especially the comparative material in *Black Feminist Thought*'s revised editions, addresses this; the matrix can be translated to other racialised societies (Brazil, France, the UK) by attention to local histories.

For sociology today, the matrix of domination is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition. Collins's contribution is therefore both critical and constructive. She criticised standpoint theory's tendency to essentialism and built a richer framework in its place. The framework remains the most useful tool we have for empirical sociology of inequality.

### Mock 7: Compare Berger's analysis of the male gaze with hooks' theory of the oppositional gaze.

Berger and hooks share a central insight: vision is political. They differ in what they make of that insight. Berger, writing in 1972 from a position of left-wing cultural criticism, analyses how the male gaze has structured European visual culture. hooks, writing in 1992 from a Black feminist position, analyses how the gaze has been racialised, denied, and ultimately recovered as resistance. I will compare them on three axes — the subject of the gaze, the structure of the gaze, and the political implications of the gaze — and argue that hooks's framework extends and corrects Berger's in a way that makes the two together more powerful than either alone.

Berger's analysis, in chapter 3 of *Ways of Seeing*, is built around a single distinction: "men act and women appear. Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, *Ways of Seeing*, p. 47). The European nude exemplifies the structure. "The principal protagonist is never painted. He is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man. Everything is addressed to him. Everything must appear to be the result of his being there. It is for him that the figures have assumed their nudity. But he, by definition, is a stranger — with his clothes still on" (p. 54). The asymmetry is not aesthetic but social: "the surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female. Thus she turns herself into an object — and most particularly an object of vision: a sight" (p. 47). Berger's account is structural and class-inflected: he reads the nude as connected to property relations, to the sexual economy of patronage, to the visual ideology of bourgeois culture.

hooks's analysis, in "The Oppositional Gaze: Black Female Spectators" in *Black Looks* (1992), takes Berger's structure and adds two layers. The first layer is racial. "When thinking about black female spectators, I remember being punished as a child for staring, for those hard intense direct looks children would give grown-ups, looks that were seen as confrontational, as gestures of resistance, challenges to authority. The 'gaze' has always been political in my life… There is power in looking" (hooks, *Black Looks*, p. 115). hooks recovers a historical fact Berger does not address: enslaved Black people were punished for looking at white people. "The first time I read in history classes that white slave-owners (men, women, and children) punished enslaved black people for looking, I wondered how this traumatic relationship to the gaze had informed… black spectatorship" (p. 115). "The politics of slavery, of racialized power relations, were such that slaves were denied their right to gaze" (p. 115).

The second layer is resistance. "All attempts to repress our/black peoples' right to gaze had produced in us an overwhelming longing to look, a rebellious desire, an oppositional gaze. By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality.' Even in the worse circumstances of domination, the ability to manipulate one's gaze in the face of structures of domination that would contain it, opens up the possibility of agency" (p. 116). The oppositional gaze is not just the female position in Berger's structure — being looked at — but the active reclamation of the right to look back.

Comparing the two:

On the subject of the gaze, Berger identifies a male spectator implicit in the European visual tradition. hooks identifies a white male spectator and shows that the gaze's politics differ when the subject is racialised. Berger's "men act and women appear" is true, but incomplete: white men act, white men look, white women appear, Black women are denied even the position of the surveyed-but-recognised. hooks adds the historical fact that for enslaved Black women the gaze was disciplined out of existence.

On the structure of the gaze, Berger treats the male gaze as a binary structure (subject/object, looker/looked-at). hooks treats it as a triangular or multi-positional structure: looker, looked-at, and forbidden-looker. The forbidden-looker is the position from which the oppositional gaze emerges. This is a structural revision of Berger.

On the political implications, Berger's analysis is critical but largely diagnostic — it shows the gendered structure of vision but offers fewer resources for resistance. hooks's analysis is both critical and constructive — it identifies the gaze as a site of resistance, theorising Black female spectatorship as a productive disruption of dominant cinematic and visual conventions.

Two points to add. First, hooks's framework converges with standpoint epistemology: the oppositional gaze is exactly the achieved standpoint Hartsock and Harding describe — articulated, political, produced through struggle. Second, the convergence matters because it shows visual culture and epistemology to be linked. Who has the right to look is who has the right to know. The lecture's pairing of Berger and hooks is therefore not incidental; it is theoretically tight.

hooks's framework extends and corrects Berger's. Together they offer a richer account of the politics of vision than either alone.

### Mock 8: To what extent can Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* be read as a standpoint epistemology?

Gilroy's *The Black Atlantic* (1993) can be read as a standpoint epistemology in a substantial sense, and I will argue that the reading is not only legitimate but illuminating. Gilroy himself uses the term standpoint in the passages quoted in the lecture: "the value of this project lies in its promise to uncover both an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood…that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint". This is not loose usage. Gilroy is making a recognisable standpoint-epistemological move, applied to the historiography of modernity rather than to the sociology of gender.

The argument proceeds in three steps.

First, Gilroy diagnoses a partial knowledge. The standard story of modernity — Enlightenment, industrial revolution, the rise of the nation-state, the development of science — is told from one set of locations: principally Britain, France, Germany, and their bourgeois intellectual classes. This story has the structure Harding identifies in masculinist science: it presents a partial view as a universal one. As Gilroy puts it on the lecture slide: "the time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view." Modernity, on Gilroy's argument, cannot be told accurately without the Middle Passage, the plantation, the abolition movements, and the Black diaspora's intellectual and cultural production. The standard story is empirically incomplete in the way the reference-man story is empirically incomplete.

Second, Gilroy proposes a corrective vantage. The slaves' standpoint is not merely an additional perspective to be slotted alongside others. It is a vantage from which features of modernity invisible from the centre become visible. Slavery was not external to modernity but constitutive of it: the capitalist economies of Britain, the Netherlands and France were built on enslaved labour and the plantation system; the modern racial categories were forged in the cargo holds and on the plantations; the modern liberal subject was defined partly by who was not allowed to be one. These facts are invisible from a standpoint that takes the European bourgeois intellectual as the paradigmatic subject of modernity; they become visible from the slaves' standpoint.

Third, Gilroy connects standpoint to articulation. Like Hartsock and Harding, Gilroy insists that the standpoint is not just an experiential vantage but a worked-up intellectual tradition. *The Black Atlantic* maps that tradition — Frederick Douglass, W. E. B. Du Bois, Richard Wright, James Baldwin, Toni Morrison — as a counter-modernity that was always in dialogue with European modernity but produced different concepts of selfhood, freedom and history. Du Bois's notion of double consciousness — the experience of seeing oneself simultaneously through one's own eyes and through the eyes of a society that despises one — is, on this reading, a standpoint-epistemological concept. It names the bifurcated consciousness Harding describes as "the outsider within" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 134).

How fully does the standpoint reading capture Gilroy? Three points to consider.

First, Gilroy's framework is more historicist than Hartsock's. Hartsock grounds standpoint in present-day material labour; Gilroy grounds standpoint in a specific historical experience (slavery, diaspora) that has long-running cultural and intellectual consequences. The framework is therefore closer to Du Bois than to Marx, though the family resemblance to Hartsock is real.

Second, Gilroy is wary of nationalist or essentialist readings of Blackness. The Black Atlantic is, for Gilroy, a transatlantic, mobile, hybrid formation — Britain, the Caribbean, the United States and West Africa in dialogue — not a single closed identity. This is a feature his framework shares with Haraway's situated knowledge: partial, located, plural, in dialogue.

Third, Gilroy's framework is constructive as well as critical. The slaves' standpoint, on his account, generates "new conceptions of selfhood" — including a notion of freedom that is not reducible to the liberal-legal framework but emerges from the experience of unfreedom and the long struggle against it. This is parallel to Harding's claim that feminist standpoint produces "better, more robust forms of knowledge" rather than merely critiquing existing knowledge.

To what extent, then, can *The Black Atlantic* be read as standpoint epistemology? Substantially, but with two qualifications. The qualifications are that Gilroy is more historicist and more wary of essentialism than the early Hartsock; both qualifications make his framework closer to Haraway and to Collins than to the original 1983 statement of feminist standpoint. But the central claim — that a marginalised historical position, articulated through political and intellectual tradition, yields a less partial account of modernity than the dominant story — is recognisably a standpoint claim. The lecture's positioning of Gilroy alongside Hartsock, Harding and hooks is theoretically warranted.

The implication is that standpoint epistemology is not narrowly a feminist tool. It is a general framework for thinking about how marginalised locations, when politically articulated, contribute to knowledge. Gilroy's work shows the framework's power in historical sociology. Other applications — postcolonial science studies, disability studies, queer history — extend the framework further.

### Mock 9: Discuss, with examples, the claim that bifurcations such as mind/body and rational/irrational are constitutive of masculinist knowledge.

The bifurcations slide in the lecture is one of the most condensed pieces of theory in the whole module. Mind/Body, Rational/Irrational, Culture/Nature, Science/Superstition, Objectivity/Subjectivity, Men/Women — the pairings line up so that the left column is coded masculine, the right column feminine, and the left column is valued over the right. I will argue that these bifurcations are constitutive of masculinist knowledge in three senses: they structure the boundary between what counts as knowledge and what does not; they distribute epistemic authority along gender lines; and they produce systematic blind spots whose empirical consequences are documented by feminist research.

First, the boundary effect. The bifurcations operate as a sorting mechanism. Anything coded with the left column is admitted to the domain of knowledge; anything coded with the right column is excluded. Knowledge proper is mental, rational, cultural, scientific, objective, masculine. Embodied experience, emotional response, natural processes, traditional or folk wisdom, subjective testimony, women's accounts — these are downgraded to the status of mere belief, opinion, or feeling. The lecture's framing of the European Enlightenment showed how the bifurcations did the work of constituting modern science by excluding what it called superstition.

The empirical consequences are significant. Women's testimony in medical contexts has been routinely downgraded: pain reports are interpreted as anxiety, gynaecological symptoms as psychological, the lived experience of pregnancy and childbirth as not requiring medical attention. The Henrietta Lacks case shows the same pattern racialised: Black women's testimony about their own bodies has been even more systematically discounted. Black maternal mortality in the United States is over three times the white rate, and a substantial portion of the differential is attributable to women's reports of symptoms being disbelieved. The bifurcation between objective medical knowledge and subjective patient experience is doing real harm.

Second, the distribution of authority. The bifurcations not only sort claims but sort claimants. Those associated with the left column — men, the educated, the Western, the credentialled — are positioned as legitimate producers of knowledge. Those associated with the right column — women, the uneducated, the non-Western, the experiential — are positioned as objects of knowledge or as sources of raw material to be processed by legitimate knowers. Harding's account of the implied speaker of scientific sentences — "humanity in general", which turns out to be men — is exactly this distribution: "the implied 'speaker' of scientific (sociological, economic, philosophic, etc.) sentences was never women" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 4).

The history of psychology offers a stark example. For most of the 20th century, theories of moral development, of cognitive development, and of personality were built on studies of male subjects, with results generalised to "humans". Where women's responses differed, the differences were coded as deficits — Kohlberg's stages of moral reasoning, for instance, ranked women as on average less developed than men. Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982) showed that the difference was a feature of the framework, not of women: when the framework was widened, women's reasoning showed as different but not inferior. This is a textbook case of the bifurcation effect: the masculine pole of the pairing was treated as the universal standard, the feminine pole as the deviation.

Third, the production of blind spots. The bifurcations make certain phenomena hard to see. If knowledge is coded as mental and rational, then bodily knowledge — knowledge held in the practice of skilled labour, in the experience of pregnancy and childbirth, in the rhythm of caregiving — is hard to count as knowledge at all. If knowledge is coded as cultural and scientific, then ecological knowledge, traditional ecological knowledge, indigenous knowledge of plants and seasons, is hard to recognise. The colonial discounting of indigenous medical knowledge — only for some of it to be later "rediscovered" by Western pharmacology, often without attribution — is one face of this blind spot.

The reference-man examples are another. Crash-test dummies based on the 50th-percentile male reflect a bifurcation: bodies that look like the dummy's body count, bodies that do not look like that body do not. The 47% higher serious-injury rate for women is what the bifurcation costs. Pharmacology calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male reflects the same bifurcation. Police data on missing women — the long-running pattern in serial offender cases of pattern reports being ignored — reflects a bifurcation between objective evidence and subjective testimony.

A critical question: are these bifurcations really constitutive, or just contingent biases of particular research communities? My argument for constitutive is that the bifurcations are not just empirical patterns of who gets believed; they are part of the conceptual architecture of how "knowledge" is defined. Harding's argument is that what counts as a knowledge claim, what counts as a method, what counts as an admissible piece of evidence — these are themselves structured by the bifurcations. Changing them requires changing the architecture, not just the personnel.

The good news is that the architecture is changing. Pre-registration of trials, demographic disaggregation of data, integration of patient testimony into clinical guidelines, recognition of indigenous knowledge in environmental science — these are pieces of architectural change. The bifurcations are constitutive of masculinist knowledge, but they are not eternal. They can be dismantled, and dismantling them produces, as the lecture's slogan insists, more objectivity, not less.

### Mock 10: How far is a standpoint an achievement rather than a given identity?

A standpoint, on the strongest reading of standpoint epistemology, is an achievement rather than a given identity, and I will argue this is the correct reading. The point is made unambiguously in the lecture: "Dominated groups don't automatically have the most objective views of the world; a 'standpoint' is an achievement, won from political struggle; production of knowledge inseparable from political struggle". I will defend this in three steps: distinguishing standpoint from identity, showing how the achievement is structured, and considering the limits of the achievement framing.

First, the distinction. An identity is a social location: woman, Black, working-class, queer, disabled. A standpoint is a worked-up critical perspective on social reality that may be associated with an identity but is not identical to it. The lecture quoted Harding's formulation: "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). The key word is "articulated". Experience is the raw material; standpoint is what political and intellectual work makes of it.

Three reasons support the distinction. First, identity does not determine consciousness. There are women who reproduce masculinist epistemologies and men who produce feminist knowledge — Harding is explicit that "men, too, must contribute distinctive forms of specifically feminist knowledge from their particular social situation" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 135). Second, identity is itself plural and contested. As Collins's matrix of domination shows, women are not a uniform category; the standpoint of a Black working-class woman differs from the standpoint of a white middle-class woman. There is no single "women's identity" from which a single feminist standpoint flows. Third, identity is shaped by dominant ideology; one's understanding of one's own identity is itself a site of struggle. The achievement of a standpoint includes the achievement of a critical understanding of one's own location.

Second, how the achievement is structured. The lecture and the readings suggest three elements. First, lived experience — the material reality of one's location. This is necessary but not sufficient. Second, collective articulation — the political and intellectual work of communities that share location and that engage in critical reflection. The feminist movement, Black feminist intellectual tradition, postcolonial historiography, disability studies — these are the collective articulators. Third, theoretical formalisation — the work of theorists like Hartsock, Smith, Collins, Harding and others who give the articulated experience analytical form. All three are necessary; missing any one produces something less than a standpoint.

hooks's oppositional gaze illustrates the achievement structure. The lived experience — being a Black woman in the United States, with the racialised history of the denied gaze — is the raw material. The collective articulation — Black feminist film criticism, the broader Black feminist intellectual tradition — is the second layer. The theoretical formalisation — hooks's own conceptualisation of the oppositional gaze as politically constitutive — is the third. The oppositional gaze is not given to Black women; it is achieved through political and intellectual work.

Gilroy's *Black Atlantic* illustrates the same structure at the level of historiography. The lived experience of the African diaspora is the raw material; the intellectual tradition from Du Bois to Morrison is the collective articulation; Gilroy's theorisation of the Black Atlantic as a transcultural formation is the formalisation. The slaves' standpoint is not given by the historical fact of slavery; it is built by long intellectual and political work.

Third, limits of the achievement framing.

One limit is that it can be misread as voluntarist — as if anyone could achieve any standpoint by sufficient effort. The standpoint tradition rejects this. Achievement requires location: an oppositional gaze grounded in the historical experience of slavery and segregation cannot be straightforwardly achieved by someone with no such location. The achievement is real, but it is conditioned by structural position.

A second limit is that the achievement language can suggest that standpoint is a state rather than a process. Standpoint is better understood as a practice that must be sustained — the conditions of its achievement are conditions of its maintenance. A feminist standpoint maintained in isolation, without ongoing political community, tends to attrit. Harding's emphasis on the political conditions of standpoint production reflects this.

A third limit is that the achievement framing can occlude the contribution of those who do not achieve standpoint but whose lives provide its evidence. The standpoint articulated by Black feminist intellectuals draws on the experience of millions of Black women who are not themselves theorists. Recognising standpoint as achievement should not erase this debt; the achievement is collective, not individual.

To what extent, then, is a standpoint an achievement rather than a given identity? Largely an achievement, with the qualifications that the achievement is structurally conditioned, collectively sustained, and indebted to those whose lives furnish its evidence. The lecture's framing — that a standpoint is "an achievement, won from political struggle" — captures the position correctly. The implication for sociology is that standpoint analysis must always attend not just to who occupies what location but to what political and intellectual work has been done at that location.

### Mock 11: Discuss the relevance of feminist epistemology to debates about AI bias and data-driven decision-making.

Feminist epistemology is highly relevant to debates about AI bias and data-driven decision-making, and I will argue that the relevance is structural rather than analogical. The mechanisms feminist epistemology identifies in conventional science — universalised partial perspectives, invisible assumptions, the reference man, the god trick — are exactly the mechanisms now operating at scale in machine learning systems. Three sites make this clear: training data, model design, and deployment context.

Training data first. Machine learning systems learn from historical data, and historical data is shaped by historical inequalities. Facial recognition systems trained on predominantly white male faces misidentify Black women at the highest rates. Buolamwini and Gebru's *Gender Shades* study (2018) found error rates for white men around 0.8% and for darker-skinned women up to 34.7% — a difference of more than an order of magnitude. The pattern is exactly the reference-man pattern: a homogeneous training population is treated as universal, and the system fails on bodies outside that population. The crash-test dummy, the PPE, the pharmacological dose, the facial recognition system: structurally, they are the same failure mode.

Healthcare AI shows the pattern as well. An algorithm widely used in US hospitals to identify high-need patients was found in 2019 to systematically under-prioritise Black patients, because it used historical healthcare spending as a proxy for need; historical spending on Black patients is lower (because of access barriers), so the algorithm read need as lower (Obermeyer et al., *Science*, 2019). This is a god-trick failure: the algorithm presents itself as a neutral measure of need but is in fact a measure of historical spending, which reflects systemic inequality.

Hiring algorithms show the pattern. Amazon's internal recruiting tool, trained on a decade of past hires, learned to downgrade resumes that included the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club captain"). It learned this because the past hires it was trained on were overwhelmingly male; the algorithm read masculinity as a marker of qualification. Amazon abandoned the tool. But the pattern is structural: any model trained on biased history will reproduce the bias.

Model design second. The choice of features, the choice of loss function, the choice of evaluation metric — all of these are conceptual decisions made by research teams. The teams are themselves not demographically representative. The lecture's diagnosis applies: when a research community is homogeneous along major axes of social stratification, the assumptions it shares will not show up to it as assumptions. The AI ethics literature documents this: research teams designing predictive policing systems often lack the lived experience that would make obvious the dangers of training on biased arrest data.

Deployment context third. An AI system is not a free-standing artefact; it is embedded in institutional contexts that determine what its outputs are used for. A facial recognition system deployed by a private museum for security has different stakes from one deployed by a police force for arrest decisions. The feminist epistemological insight here is that knowledge claims must be evaluated in context — what Harding calls strong reflexivity, applied to deployment. Asking who is producing the system, who is being predicted about, who benefits from the predictions, and who is harmed by the errors, is what strong objectivity demands.

Three specific feminist-epistemological tools are being adapted to AI debates.

First, the reference-man framework directly transfers. Criado-Perez's analysis of data gaps in physical infrastructure is being applied to data gaps in training sets. The recommended response — sex-disaggregated and demographically-disaggregated data — is now part of mainstream AI ethics literature.

Second, situated knowledge and the god trick are being explicitly invoked in AI fairness research. Timnit Gebru, Joy Buolamwini, Safiya Noble (*Algorithms of Oppression*), Ruha Benjamin (*Race After Technology*), and others draw directly on Haraway and Harding. The framework provides the vocabulary for naming what is wrong with claims that AI systems are "neutral" or "objective".

Third, the matrix of domination is essential for AI fairness because single-axis fairness measures are inadequate. A system that is accurate for white women on average and Black men on average can still fail badly for Black women at the intersection. Intersectional fairness measures — which the Buolamwini and Gebru study pioneered — are direct applications of Collins's framework.

One is that AI debates sometimes treat fairness as a purely technical problem — a question of better training data or better algorithms. Feminist epistemology insists the problem is also institutional and political: who is in the room when systems are designed, who is consulted, who is accountable. Technical fixes alone do not address this.

Two is that AI systems operate at scales and speeds for which the standpoint tradition was not developed. A misdiagnosis affects one patient; an algorithmic misclassification affects millions. The framework needs scaling up, and the AI fairness literature is doing this work, but the standpoint tradition's emphasis on slow, articulated, politically grounded standpoint development must be adapted to systems that are deployed in months rather than developed over decades.

Feminist epistemology is therefore highly relevant — indispensable, I would say — to AI debates. It provides the diagnostic vocabulary, the structural analysis, and the normative framework that AI fairness research needs. The slogan from the lecture applies directly: a perspectival approach to data-driven decision-making would produce more accurate and more just systems, not fewer or weaker ones.

### Mock 12: To what extent does the god trick still operate in contemporary social science?

Haraway's god trick — the pretence of speaking authoritatively about everything from no particular location — was diagnosed in the 1980s and 1990s as the dominant epistemology of mainstream science. To what extent does it still operate in contemporary social science? Substantially, I will argue, though with important variations across subfields and methodologies. The god trick has been pushed back in some quarters and survives or even intensifies in others.

The retreat first. Several developments have made the god trick harder to sustain in social science.

First, the broad acceptance of reflexivity as methodological norm. Qualitative methods textbooks now routinely require researcher positionality statements; ethnographic monographs typically include a methodological appendix or chapter that discusses the researcher's location relative to the subjects. This is a direct legacy of feminist epistemology and Bourdieu's reflexive sociology. The norm is not universal — quantitative work in economics, for instance, often resists it — but it is dominant in qualitative work.

Second, the broad acceptance that demographic composition of research communities matters. Diversity of researchers is no longer treated as a political concession but as an epistemic resource. Funding bodies increasingly require demographic disclosure of research teams. Journals increasingly require demographic disaggregation of data. The reference-man programme of feminist epistemology has had measurable effects.

Third, the rise of mixed methods and the integration of community-based research. Participatory research designs, community-engaged scholarship, indigenous methodologies, and decolonial approaches all insist on integrating subjects into the production of knowledge. These approaches refuse the god trick by design.

The persistence next. Despite these developments, the god trick persists in significant ways.

First, in policy-oriented quantitative work. Large-N studies in economics, demography, and public policy often present results as if they were observations from nowhere. The model assumptions, the data sources, the variable definitions are typically presented as neutral methodological choices rather than as substantive theoretical commitments with political consequences. The Reinhart-Rogoff debt-to-GDP paper, whose Excel error and methodological choices shaped austerity policy across Europe, is a paradigm case: the paper was treated as an objective empirical finding rather than as a contestable analytic construction.

Second, in big-data social science. The volume and velocity of digital data has produced a class of studies that present themselves as merely descriptive — "we observed the patterns in the data" — without acknowledging that the data is itself produced by particular platforms, particular populations, and particular institutional arrangements. Twitter sentiment analysis treats Twitter as a representative sample of opinion when it is no such thing. Mobile phone records treat mobile phone users as the population when there are significant demographic gradients in mobile ownership.

Third, in algorithmic social science. The deployment of machine learning to social-scientific questions — predicting recidivism, predicting school dropout, predicting health outcomes — often inherits the god-trick framing of its computer-science origins. The model is presented as a neutral predictor; the training data, the choice of target variable, and the deployment context are presented as technical rather than substantive.

Fourth, in disciplinary specialisations that have resisted reflexive turns. Quantitative criminology, mainstream economics, certain branches of psychometrics retain a strongly positivist self-image and treat reflexivity as a contamination of method rather than as a strengthening of it.

Three diagnostic questions for spotting the god trick in contemporary work.

First, does the work present its conceptual framework as a methodological default or as a substantive choice? If terms like "household", "income", "education", "ethnicity" are used without theoretical reflection, the god trick is likely operating. Each of these terms carries assumptions about who counts as a unit of analysis and what counts as relevant about them.

Second, does the work disclose the composition of the research team and the population studied? Demographic disaggregation is a strong-objectivity move; its absence is a god-trick warning sign.

Third, does the work integrate subjects' own understandings or treat subjects as objects of analysis only? The latter is the classic god-trick stance.

Where does this leave the social-scientific landscape? The god trick has lost ground but not been displaced. The dominant epistemology of qualitative social science is now broadly reflexive; the dominant epistemology of large-scale quantitative social science remains broadly objectivist. The integration of the two is uneven. The most exciting recent work — feminist economics, intersectional quantitative methods, computational social science attentive to bias, decolonial historiography — pulls toward strong objectivity. But mainstream practice in many subfields remains stuck in objectivism, often defended on grounds of methodological rigor that, on Harding's analysis, are actually insufficient rigor.

The slogan from the lecture — that a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity — remains a programme to be completed rather than a state already achieved. Contemporary social science is partway through the transition, with substantial god-trick residue still in the system. The continuing relevance of feminist epistemology is exactly its capacity to diagnose this residue and to provide the conceptual tools to dismantle it.

### Mock 13: Compare and contrast objectivism and strong objectivity as ideals of social-scientific inquiry.

Objectivism and strong objectivity are competing ideals for what good social-scientific inquiry should look like. Both claim the mantle of objectivity. Both reject relativism. Both aspire to produce reliable, generalisable, evidence-based knowledge of social reality. They differ in what they require of researchers, what they treat as evidence, what they identify as threats to objectivity, and what reforms they recommend. I will compare and contrast on four dimensions and argue that strong objectivity better serves the goals both ideals share.

First, what they require of researchers. Objectivism requires neutrality and detachment. The researcher should not bring personal values, political commitments, or social positions into the research. The model researcher is, as the lecture put it, "neutral and detached… value-free". Strong objectivity requires reflexivity. The researcher should disclose and critically examine personal values, political commitments and social positions, and treat them as part of the data that the research must engage. As Harding writes, "strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (*Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, p. 136).

Second, what they treat as evidence. Objectivism privileges measurable, replicable, formal data. The standard evidence base is what can be quantified, standardised, and cross-validated by independent researchers using the same methods. Strong objectivity broadens the evidence base to include qualitative testimony, lived experience, and the perspectives of those marginalised by dominant frameworks. The widening is not a relaxation of standards but an extension: the evidence base is larger, and so the conclusions can be tested against more.

Third, what they identify as threats to objectivity. Objectivism identifies individual bias as the primary threat. The protection is methodological: standardised procedures, blind assessment, peer review. Strong objectivity identifies community-wide assumption as the primary threat. The protection is structural: diversification of research communities, inclusion of marginalised voices, examination of the conceptual frameworks of the disciplines themselves. Harding's diagnosis is sharp: "the concept of value-free, impartial, dispassionate research… has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (p. 137). Objectivism cannot see assumptions shared by an entire community; strong objectivity is designed precisely to see them.

Fourth, what reforms they recommend. Objectivism recommends procedural reforms — better methods, better statistical analysis, better reporting standards, pre-registration of hypotheses. Strong objectivity recommends procedural reforms too, but adds structural reforms — diversification of research personnel, integration of community knowledge, demographic disaggregation of data, examination of disciplinary frameworks.

The reference-man programme illustrates the difference. Under objectivism, crash-test dummy research could improve by adopting better instrumentation, larger sample sizes, and more sophisticated statistical analysis — but if the testing community continues to treat the 50th-percentile male as the unit of analysis, no procedural improvement will solve the problem. Under strong objectivity, the reform is structural: include women's bodies as standard test subjects, include women on the engineering and policy teams, treat the choice of reference body as a substantive theoretical decision rather than as a methodological default. The procedural improvements may help; the structural reform is what actually solves the problem.

The clinical trials case shows the same contrast. Through the 1970s and 1980s, clinical trials excluded women of reproductive age on the procedural ground of teratogenic risk. The procedural ground was sound; the structural consequence — that the entire pharmacopoeia was calibrated to male bodies — was disastrous. Objectivism cannot see the structural consequence because objectivism cannot see the framework. Strong objectivity asks who is in the trial, why, with what consequences.

Three points where the two ideals agree are worth noting. Both reject epistemological relativism. Both believe in a knowable social world. Both believe in evidence-based testing of claims. Both believe that some accounts are better than others. These shared commitments mean that strong objectivity is correctly read as a development of objectivity rather than its abandonment.

The most important difference is in self-understanding. Objectivism understands itself as the achievement of neutrality; strong objectivity understands itself as the recognition that neutrality is unachievable and that the next best thing is disclosed location. The first is a fiction that conceals its location; the second is a discipline that examines its location.

For social-scientific inquiry today, strong objectivity is the better ideal. It is more honest about the conditions of knowledge production; it is more rigorous, in Harding's precise sense, because it widens the field of critical scrutiny; it is more empirically successful, on the evidence of feminist research's track record in producing findings that mainstream research missed; and it is more useful for the institutional reform of science, because it identifies the structural conditions of better knowledge.

The slogan of the lecture — "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity" — captures the result. Strong objectivity is more objective than objectivism, because it is more rigorous about more variables. Objectivism is a weaker ideal masquerading as a stronger one. Strong objectivity is the stronger ideal, properly named.
