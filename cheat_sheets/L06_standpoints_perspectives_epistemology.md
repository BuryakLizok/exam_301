# L6: Standpoints, Perspectives and Epistemology

**Module:** SC301 Rethinking Modernity
**Lecture:** 6
**Theme:** Knowledge, situatedness, feminist and postcolonial epistemologies; the critique of objectivism.

---

## 1. Key concepts (≤200 words)

**Epistemology** asks how and what we know, and what justifies a knowledge-claim. **Traditional epistemology** (which Harding labels **objectivism**) treats knowledge as independent of the knower; philosophical objectivity assumes a real external world and methodological objectivity demands value-free, dispassionate research. Feminist critique exposes this neutrality as itself a position: the universal knower performs **the god trick** (Haraway), speaking from nowhere while actually speaking from a dominant location. **Situated knowledge** insists all thought bears the fingerprints of its producers. **Standpoint epistemology** (Hartsock, Harding, Smith) argues that marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate less partial accounts. **Strong objectivity** treats the knower as part of the object of inquiry, demanding **strong reflexivity**. **The reference man** (Criado-Perez) names the Caucasian-male default in crash-test dummies, PPE, office temperature and pharmacology. **Bifurcation**, mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, men/women, sustains masculinist science. **Perspectival epistemology** brings multiple positions into dialogue. Gilroy reconstructs modernity from **the slaves' standpoint**; hooks' **the oppositional gaze** treats looking as resistance; Collins' **the matrix of domination** maps intersecting axes of oppression; her **outsider within** names the bifurcated consciousness Harding endorses.

## 4. Critical analysis with embedded quotes

The Harding reading is the spine of the lecture and is built around a single counter-intuitive claim: that situated, politically engaged knowledge produces **more**, not less, objectivity. Harding opens by arguing that "women's movements needed knowledge that was *for* women", but that the disciplines "permitted no conceptual frameworks in which women as a group… became the subjects, the authors, of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 4). The implied speaker of scientific sentences "was supposed to be humanity in general", performing what Haraway names "the 'God trick' of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). This is the core diagnosis: objectivism universalises a particular location.

Harding insists the problem is not "bad science" but the conceptual frameworks themselves, which "promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns, which ensured that the knowledge produced through them was always socially situated" (Harding, 2004: 5). "Commitment to an objectivity defined as maximizing social neutrality was not itself socially neutral in its effects" (Harding, 2004: 5). She lists what androcentric frameworks rendered invisible: violence against women treated as women "asking for it"; the unpaid double day naturalised; menstruation and menopause medicalised; rationality narrowed to the instrumental rationality of institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (Harding, 2004: 5-6). The "remedy", Harding writes, "was to start off thought and research from women's experiences, lives, and activities (or labor)" (Harding, 2004: 6), but never as ethnography of women alone; standpoint projects "must 'study up'" (Harding, 2004: 6).

In chapter 8 Harding consolidates this into **strong objectivity**. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*; it is too weak" (Harding, 2004: 128). Conventional method eliminates only those values "that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 136-137), leaving culture-wide assumptions untouched. "Strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge. Thus, strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). Crucially, "a maximally critical study of scientists and their communities can be done only from the perspective of those whose lives have been marginalized by such communities" (Harding, 2004: 136).

Harding pre-empts three charges. Against the ethnocentrism charge she notes that "standpoint theorists themselves all explicitly argue that marginal lives that are not their own provide better grounds for certain kinds of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 129), Hegel was not a slave, Marx not a wage-labourer. Against the relativism charge: "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (Harding, 2004: 131-132). Against the essentialism charge: the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (Harding, 2004: 134), starting from "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134).

Berger's *Ways of Seeing* sits alongside Harding as a parallel critique of the gendered structure of vision. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47). "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, 1972: 47). Berger shows that the European nude is structured by an unseen male spectator, "the principal protagonist is never painted. He is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man" (Berger, 1972: 54). This anticipates hooks: power resides in who is licensed to look. hooks' oppositional gaze is the inverted, resistant move: "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality'" (hooks, 1992: 116).

The lecture chains these arguments into one claim: epistemology is political. Whose body counts as the reference body, in crash tests, drug trials, PPE, office thermostats, is decided by who is in the room when the standards are set. Criado-Perez's evidence (women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in crashes; PPE based on European and US male bodies; office temperature calibrated to a male metabolic rate; pharmacology designed around a 70kg Caucasian man) is not a series of accidents but the empirical face of objectivism.

### 2025 (Q8): Why is the concept of perspectives important for understanding the status of epistemology within modernity?

The concept of perspectives matters for modernity because modernity inherited an epistemology that disavowed perspective altogether. The modern view from nowhere, the Enlightenment ideal of a universal knower, is the conceit Harding calls objectivism and Haraway names the god trick. I will argue that recovering the concept of perspectives is decisive for understanding the status of epistemology in modernity for three reasons: it diagnoses modernity's epistemological fiction, it explains the political effects of that fiction, and it offers a route to a more accurate, plural science.

First, modernity defined itself epistemically against superstition by claiming a method that transcended location. The bifurcations rehearsed in the lecture, mind/body, rational/irrational, culture/nature, science/superstition, objectivity/subjectivity, men/women, encode this. Science is on the rational, cultural, mind-side; superstition, nature, body and women on the other. Harding's notion of strong objectivity exposes the trick: "All thought by humans starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, 2004: 128). The pretence that scientific thought escapes this is "a delusion, and a historical identifiable one" (Harding, 2004: 128). The concept of perspectives names what modernity tried to hide: that every claim is made from somewhere.

The slide-headline of the lecture put the consequence sharply: "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity." This is not a paradox once we see that the alternative, pretending we have no perspective, does not eliminate perspective, it only hides it. Hidden perspectives become culture-wide assumptions transported into research findings.

Second, perspectives matter because their absence has political effects. The lecture opens this through Criado-Perez's documentation of "a world designed for men". When the reference man is the only body imagined, women die at higher rates in car crashes (47% more likely to be seriously injured, 17% more likely to die). PPE designed around European and US male bodies fits female workers badly; employers assume smaller sizes will be enough, and Criado-Perez documents that this is not the case. Standard office temperature, calibrated in the 1960s to the male metabolic rate, leaves female office workers, whose metabolic rate a recent Dutch study found to be significantly lower, sitting in rooms designed against them. Pharmaceutical dosing is calculated against a 70kg Caucasian man, despite the fact that men and women have different immune systems and hormones, which affect how chemicals are absorbed. Even bags of cement and bricks are sized to male bodies. None of these is a methodological accident; each is what happens when a single perspective is universalised.

Modernity's pretence of no-perspective is itself a perspective, and a powerful one. As Harding writes, "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge and the claims resulting from adherence to such standards should be found preferable by all rational creatures, past, present, and future" (Harding, 2004: 131). The concept of perspectives therefore matters because it lets us name the political work that no-perspective claims do.

Third, the concept of perspectives matters because it offers a constructive epistemology rather than a sceptical one. Standpoint epistemology, Hartsock's feminist standpoint, Smith's everyday-life sociology, Collins' matrix of domination, Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint, hooks' oppositional gaze, does not say all perspectives are equal. It says different positions afford different insights and that some positions, when politically articulated, see what dominant positions cannot. Hartsock's two claims, reproduced on the slide, are that men's and women's understandings differ because of their material labour and access to public space, and that the dominant group's views attempt to "structure the world for everybody". Standpoint, the lecture stresses, "is not automatic, it is an achievement won from political struggle".

Bringing multiple perspectives together does not produce a single objective view but a "multiple objectivity": different vantages corroborating, contesting and supplementing one another. This is what the lecture calls a perspectival epistemology. It refuses bifurcation, refuses to choose between universal objectivity and relativism, and produces what Harding calls strong objectivity: a knowledge that is more accountable because its location is disclosed.

Modernity's self-image as the epoch of universal reason is therefore both a description and a problem. As a description it captures the ambition that gave us science; as a problem it captures the suppression of perspective that gave us the reference man. The concept of perspectives lets us hold both: it allows us to keep the ambition (truth, evidence, testability) while abandoning the fiction (the view from nowhere). Gilroy's claim that "the time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view" is not anti-modern, it is a demand that modernity finally take seriously its own commitment to evidence by including the evidence it has excluded. hooks' insistence that "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115) generalises the point: who looks, and from where, is constitutive of what is known.

The status of epistemology in modernity, then, is unstable without the concept of perspectives. With it, modernity becomes intelligible, and correctable.

### 2024 (Q5): In what ways does the feminist epistemology overturn the canons and practices of traditional epistemology?

Feminist epistemology does not abolish epistemology; it rebuilds it. I will argue that it overturns the traditional canon in three connected ways: by exposing the universal knower as a partial knower, by reconstructing objectivity as situated rather than detached, and by reorganising the practical institutions through which knowledge is produced. Together these moves do not weaken the search for truth, they make it more rigorous.

The traditional canon, as the lecture set out, rests on two definitions of objectivity. Philosophically, it posits a real world independent of the knower, which science discovers; methodologically, it demands that the researcher be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding's notion of objectivism collapses these into a single claim: that knowledge can be produced from no particular location. Donna Haraway's account of situated knowledge names this fantasy "the god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). The first overturning of the canon is this exposure: the universal knower of modern Western philosophy was always a specific knower, male, white, bourgeois, European, whose social location was concealed by being treated as default. Spender's phrase, cited in the lecture, captures the inversion sharply: "objectivity is male subjectivity" (Spender, 1980).

The second overturning is the reconstruction of objectivity itself. Critics often suppose that feminist epistemology must choose between objectivity and relativism. Harding refuses this dichotomy. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Conventional method, she argues, can only eliminate the values that "differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137). Culture-wide assumptions, what an entire research community already takes for granted, slip through. Harding's notion of strong objectivity demands that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical plane as the object: "strong objectivity requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). The canon's commitment to detachment is replaced by a commitment to disclosed, examined, accountable location.

The third overturning is institutional and practical. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* documents what happens when the reference man becomes the default. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg) leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. PPE based on the sizes and characteristics of male populations from Europe and the US fails female workers, and employers, the slide notes, often assume that buying smaller sizes is enough; it is not. Standard office temperature was developed in the 1960s around the metabolic resting rate of the average man, with a Dutch study showing the metabolic rate of young adult female office workers is significantly lower. Pharmaceutical doses are calculated against a "Caucasian Man" whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019), even though men and women have different immune systems and hormones that affect how chemicals are absorbed. Bags of cement and bricks complete the picture. These are not failures of methodology in the narrow sense; they are failures of who is in the room when the categories are set.

Feminist epistemology overturns the canon by changing the personnel and procedures of knowledge production. Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, "in and out of the home", gives them access to dimensions of social reality the dominant group cannot see, but only when that experience is articulated through political struggle. As Harding insists in the slide quoted from her 1991 book, "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). A standpoint is not an opinion poll of women; it is a worked-up critical perspective.

This third overturning extends beyond gender. Collins's matrix of domination, hooks's oppositional gaze, and Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity from the slaves' standpoint generalise the move. Each insists that historically marginalised positions, when politically articulated, generate critical insights unavailable from the centre. Harding herself points to the wider canon of marginal lives that have grounded standpoint research: holocaust survivors, Chicana cannery workers, older lesbians, African-American women in slavery, Japanese-American concentration-camp survivors (Harding, 2004: 129). These become visible as epistemic resources only when the categories of analysis are themselves contested.

A critic might object that this remains too tied to identity, risking essentialism or fragmentation. Harding's reply, which I find persuasive, is that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (Harding, 2004: 134); it is the thinker "whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134). The alternative, pretending no one is in the room, is what produced the reference man in the first place.

Feminist epistemology therefore overturns the canon at three levels: at the level of the knower (exposing the god trick), at the level of objectivity (replacing detachment with strong reflexivity), and at the level of institutions (reorganising who counts and who is counted). It is, as the lecture put it, not about undermining knowledge but about producing "better, more robust forms of knowledge".

### 2023 (Q8): What do sociologists mean by the term "standpoint epistemology"? How useful is this approach?

Standpoint epistemology is the claim that what we know is shaped by where we stand in social structure, and that some social locations, when their inhabitants reflect critically on them, afford less partial accounts of how a society works than other locations do. I will define the term, distinguish it from neighbouring positions, and then assess its usefulness, concluding that it is highly useful, especially when paired with Harding's notion of strong objectivity.

The standpoint thesis was systematised by Nancy Hartsock's 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism", developed by Dorothy Smith's everyday-world sociology, Harding's three books in the 1980s and 1990s, and Patricia Hill Collins' *Black Feminist Thought*. The lecture summarised it in three propositions. First, men's and women's understandings of the world differ because of the different labour they do and their different access to public space. Second, the dominant group's view attempts to structure the world for everyone. Third, because the feminist standpoint reveals the partiality of the dominant view, it opens up a path to liberation. Crucially, the lecture insists that "a standpoint is not automatic, it is an achievement won from political struggle".

This last clause distinguishes standpoint epistemology from three positions with which it is often confused. It is not identity politics: being a woman does not guarantee a feminist standpoint, and Harding emphasises that men can produce feminist knowledge by "starting from women's lives" (Harding, 2004: 135). It is not relativism: standpoint theory denies that all perspectives are equally adequate to a given reality. As Harding writes, "it is not equally true as its denial that women's uteruses wander around in their bodies when they take math courses" (Harding, 2004: 131). And it is not ethnocentrism: the claim that women's lives are a good place to start thinking does not assert that women's culture is superior; it asserts that marginal locations offer epistemic resources that central ones suppress.

Three concrete examples show what the approach offers. First, the lecture's reference-man material. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male; women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. Standpoint epistemology predicts this kind of distortion: when only one social position is in the room when categories are set, the categories will reflect that position. Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* (2019) documents this across pharmacology, PPE, office temperature and workplace equipment. PPE is sized to European and US male populations; office thermostats were set in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate; pharmaceutical doses are calculated around a 70kg Caucasian male despite hormonal and immune-system differences.

Second, Collins's analysis of African-American domestic workers as "outsiders within". Their location in white households gave them empirical access to how dominant groups thought when they thought no Black worker was listening. The standpoint here is not an opinion but a vantage that becomes critical when articulated through Black feminist theory. Collins' matrix of domination shows how race, class, gender and sexuality intersect, extending standpoint thinking beyond a single axis. Harding endorses this directly when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134).

Third, Gilroy's *Black Atlantic*. "The time has come for the primal history of modernity to be reconstructed from the slaves' point of view." Modernity, told from Liverpool and Glasgow, is industrial revolution and Enlightenment; told from the Middle Passage and the plantation, it is also slavery, racialised capitalism and the long denial of personhood. The lecture connects Gilroy to Dussel and Howard French as part of the same reconstructive project. hooks' oppositional gaze is the same move at the level of vision: "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115).

The usefulness of the approach is therefore considerable. It explains structural patterns (the reference-man cases, the racial structure of the canon, the gendered organisation of visual culture in Berger) that frameworks ignoring social position cannot easily account for. It generates research programmes (feminist science studies, Black sociology, postcolonial historiography). It improves science by widening the evidence base, which is exactly Harding's argument for strong objectivity: more locations, more reflexivity, more rigour.

There are objections. Critics worry about essentialism, assuming a unitary "women's standpoint". Harding answers that the subject of feminist knowledge is "multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory" (Harding, 2004: 134), including the liberal, socialist, Sandinista, Islamic and Third World feminisms which start from different women's lives. Critics worry about regress: who decides which standpoint is more illuminating? Harding's answer is empirical, judge by which produces more accurate, less partial accounts of nature and social relations. Critics worry about politics replacing evidence, but the lecture and the reading insist that "good politics" produces "good science" by widening evidence and exposing assumptions, not by replacing them.

A more serious worry is that standpoint epistemology is most useful for the social sciences and less obviously applicable to physical science. Harding addresses this by pointing out that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts, "wild animals" are categorised differently by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17). The point generalises: the agenda, funding, and ethical limits of physical science are social.

Standpoint epistemology is thus useful both as diagnosis and as method. It does not replace evidence; it widens it. Its central insight, that the disclosed location of knowledge is a resource rather than a contaminant, is one of the more durable contributions of late twentieth-century sociology.

### 2022 (Q9): Does feminist epistemology reject the concept of objectivity?

The short answer is no. Feminist epistemology, as articulated by Harding, Haraway, Hartsock, Smith and Collins, does not reject objectivity; it rejects objectivism, the conflation of objectivity with detached neutrality. I will argue this in three steps: first, by distinguishing objectivity from objectivism; second, by showing that Harding's notion of strong objectivity is a strengthening, not a weakening, of objectivity; third, by considering and rebutting the charge that feminist epistemology slides into relativism.

The lecture set out two traditional definitions of objectivity. The philosophical sense holds that there is a real world independent of human thought, which science discovers. The methodological sense holds that researchers must be neutral, detached and value-free. Harding labels the conflation of these two definitions, and the assumption that science as actually practised achieves them, as **objectivism**. "Harding refers to the traditional notion that science (natural and social) should be objective in the sense of value-free and without bias… as objectivism" (lecture slide quoting Harding, 1991: 143). She continues: "the concept of value-free, impartial, dispassionate research… has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 143). Objectivism, in other words, claims neutrality but operationalises it as agreement among a homogeneous community. Anything that community already takes for granted slips through.

Feminist epistemology rejects this. It does not reject the underlying ambition, that knowledge should track reality and not merely opinion. Harding is explicit: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). The lecture restated the headline: "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge."

What feminist epistemology offers in place of objectivism is Harding's notion of strong objectivity. "The problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Strong objectivity requires that "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). The knower is part of the system being known; the social position of the researcher is itself open to scientific scrutiny. This is what Harding calls "strong reflexivity" (Harding, 2004: 136). It is more rigorous than weak objectivity because it widens the field of what must be examined to include the researcher's own assumptions.

The empirical case for this is in the lecture's reference-man material. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male; PPE designed for European and US male bodies; office temperature calibrated to a male metabolic rate; pharmacology calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male despite different hormones and immune systems. Each of these is the product of research that believed itself objective. None of them were objective in any meaningful sense: they encoded a single social position as universal. Strong objectivity demands that the location of the researcher and the research community be disclosed and examined; weak objectivity calls anything not visible to the dominant community "neutral".

The Berger reading supports this from the side of culture. "Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at. This determines not only most relations between men and women but also the relation of women to themselves" (Berger, 1972: 47). The European nude exemplifies a pseudo-objective image, the woman painted as if free of context, which is in fact organised around an unpainted male spectator who, by definition, is "a stranger with his clothes still on" (Berger, 1972: 54). The "objectivity" of the painting is a covert positioning. Feminist epistemology applied to science finds the same pattern: covert positioning passed off as no positioning.

A common objection is that feminist epistemology, once it admits that all knowledge is situated, has no resources to distinguish better from worse claims and so collapses into relativism. Harding pre-empts this. "Standpoint theory provides arguments for the claim that some social situations are scientifically better than others as places from which to start off knowledge projects" (Harding, 2004: 131). Some locations afford insights others suppress. The standard is empirical: which research programme generates less partial and distorted accounts? Standpoint feminism's record, producing accounts of unpaid domestic labour, of gendered violence, of the medical neglect of women through the reference-man framework, is itself the evidence.

A subtler objection is that strong objectivity is just objectivity with reflexivity bolted on. I would reply that the bolt-on changes the structure. Conventional objectivity treats the subject of knowledge as a methodological hazard to be controlled; strong objectivity treats the subject as a resource, a piece of evidence that must be made visible. This is not a cosmetic adjustment. It changes the criteria for good science: not least, it requires the integration of marginalised voices into scientific communities as a condition of rigour, not as a concession to politics.

So: feminist epistemology rejects the objectivism that has impersonated objectivity. It defends objectivity by making it stronger. The slogan of the lecture, "a perspectival approach to knowledge can produce more not less objectivity", is precisely the answer to the question. Objectivity is preserved; objectivism is abandoned.

### 2021 (Q9): Outline and assess the feminist epistemologist critique of "masculinist" approaches to knowledge.

The feminist critique of masculinist knowledge has three components: a critique of who has produced knowledge, a critique of what counts as knowledge, and a critique of how knowledge has been validated. I will outline each, illustrate with examples, and then assess the critique, concluding that it is largely successful and remains the most productive sociological account of the politics of knowledge.

The first component is a critique of personnel. As the lecture put it, "most philosophers and scientists and sociologists (in the West) have been men. As Sandra Harding puts it, this means that the concerns, the topics and procedures of science, philosophy and much social theory are 'man-made'". Dale Spender extended the point to language itself. This is not a complaint about individual men. It is a structural observation: when a research community is homogeneous along a major axis of social stratification, the assumptions it shares will not show up to it as assumptions. Harding makes this explicit: conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent" (Harding, 2004: 137). Whatever the entire community takes for granted is invisible to its method.

The second component is a critique of content. The lecture catalogued the empirical consequences via Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women*. Crash-test dummies based on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg) leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. PPE is built around the sizes and characteristics of male populations from Europe and the US, with employers assuming smaller sizes will be enough; they are not. Office thermostats were set in the 1960s to the male metabolic rate, with a Dutch study confirming that women's office-work metabolic rate is significantly lower. Pharmaceutical doses are designed around a 70kg Caucasian man whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not"; men and women have different immune systems and hormones that affect how chemicals are absorbed. Even bags of cement and bricks are calibrated to male bodies. Harding's wider catalogue of androcentric distortions, raised in her introduction, fills in the social side: violence against women coded as women "asking for it" (Harding, 2004: 5), menstruation and menopause medicalised, the unpaid double day naturalised, rationality restricted to the institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (Harding, 2004: 5-6).

The third component is a critique of validation, the standards by which a claim is accepted as knowledge. The masculinist tradition operationalises objectivity as detachment and value-freedom. Feminist epistemology argues this is itself a value, a position, and one that systematically disadvantages knowledge claims emerging from marginalised lives. Harding's strong objectivity replaces detachment with "strong reflexivity" (Harding, 2004: 136): the researcher's location is itself an object of inquiry. Haraway's situated knowledge replaces the god trick with "feminist objectivity… quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, 2004: 127). Hartsock's feminist standpoint argues that women's labour, articulated through political struggle, generates accounts that "reveal the partiality of the dominant (male) vision of social relations".

These three critiques converge on a single claim: masculinist knowledge is partial knowledge passing as universal. Berger demonstrates this from the side of vision. "The surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, 1972: 47). The supposed neutrality of the European nude is the suppression of a male spectator who organises the entire image. hooks generalises: "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115). Who sees, and from where, is constitutive of what is known.

Assessing the critique, I find it largely persuasive on three grounds. Empirically, the reference-man examples are not anecdotes; they are documented, systemic, and in the case of crashes, lethal. Methodologically, the move from objectivism to strong objectivity strengthens rather than weakens science by widening reflexivity. Politically, the critique has been generative: it has produced research programmes, feminist science studies, Black feminist sociology, postcolonial historiography, that have changed what counts as a legitimate question.

Three reservations are worth registering. First, the critique risks essentialism if "masculinist" is treated as a property of all men's thought rather than a property of a structure. Harding addresses this: men can produce feminist knowledge by starting from women's lives (Harding, 2004: 135), and women can reproduce masculinist assumptions. Second, the critique can be misread as anti-science. Harding's defence of strong objectivity rebuts this; the lecture's slogan, "this is not about undermining knowledge, epistemology or objectivity… It is about providing better, more robust forms of knowledge", should be taken at face value. Third, there is a question of scope: does the critique apply to physical sciences as fully as to social? Harding argues yes, on the grounds that even physical objects are studied through socially constituted concepts (her example is the different ways Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists categorise "wild animals", Harding, 2004: 140, n.17), but the case is harder to make for, say, the most abstract reaches of mathematical physics than for medical research. The strongest version of the critique applies where the object of knowledge is itself socially constituted, which covers most of what social science studies and a large portion of biomedical and applied science.

The critique succeeds. It diagnoses a real failure of self-understanding in the masculinist tradition; it offers concrete tools (strong objectivity, strong reflexivity, situated knowledge, the matrix of domination) to repair that failure; and it has demonstrated empirically that doing so produces better knowledge.

### Mock 1: "The god trick of speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all." Discuss with reference to Haraway.

Haraway's phrase the god trick is the single sharpest diagnosis of what is wrong with the inherited scientific ideal of objectivity, and I want to defend it on three grounds: as an accurate description of how mainstream science represents itself, as an account of why that self-representation has had damaging empirical consequences, and as the foundation for Haraway's positive proposal that knowledge is constitutively situated. The quotation, reproduced in Harding's *Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader*, captures the conceit precisely: a knower who speaks from no body, no time, no class position, no gender, and who therefore claims authority over every body, every time, every position. I will argue that this is not just a rhetorical jab but a structural claim.

The inherited ideal of objectivity has two components: the metaphysical claim that there is a world independent of our beliefs about it, and the methodological claim that good researchers should minimise the influence of their own biases. Haraway does not deny either component. What she denies is a third claim, often smuggled in with the first two: that good method as currently practised actually achieves the second. The god trick is the device by which the smuggling happens. It rebrands the perspective of a specific knower (historically: white, male, bourgeois, European) as no perspective at all.

The empirical case for Haraway's diagnosis is the reference-man material catalogued by Criado-Perez in *Invisible Women*. Crash-test dummies were modelled on the 50th-percentile male, 1.77m, 76kg; the consequence is that women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a crash and 17% more likely to die. PPE is sized to European and US male bodies, and employers, the slide notes, often assume that buying smaller sizes will fit female workers; it does not. Office thermostats were calibrated in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate, with a Dutch study showing female office-work metabolic rate is significantly lower. Pharmacological doses are calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019), despite the fact that men and women have different immune systems and hormones that change how chemicals are absorbed. Even bags of cement and bricks are sized to male bodies. Each of these is a god-trick failure: the engineering and the pharmacology presented themselves as knowledge of human bodies in general but were actually knowledge of one kind of body, generalised by fiat.

Harding's gloss on Haraway makes the structural point explicit: "only members of the powerful groups in societies stratified by race, ethnicity, class, gender, and sexuality could imagine that their standards for knowledge and the claims resulting from adherence to such standards should be found preferable by all rational creatures, past, present, and future" (Harding, 2004: 131). The god trick is sociologically possible only from positions of social power; it is the epistemology of dominance reflected back as universal reason.

Haraway's positive proposal, situated knowledge, flows directly from the diagnosis. "Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, 2004: 127). Knowledge is always made from a body, a place, a history, and a set of material practices. To declare this is not to give up on objectivity; it is to deepen it. Disclosed location is more scientifically informative than concealed location, because the conditions of knowing become part of what can be evaluated. This is what Harding develops as strong objectivity: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Haraway and Harding converge here. The god trick is the foil against which both build a positive epistemology.

A common objection is that abandoning the god trick collapses into relativism, that if every claim is situated, no claim is better than another. Haraway and Harding both reject this inference. Harding is sharp: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). Situated knowledge is not the claim that all situations are equally informative; it is the claim that the situation must be disclosed, and that some situations, particularly those of historically marginalised groups, afford insights that dominant situations suppress. The crash-dummy case is the proof: the claim that women are 47% more likely to be injured is not a relativist alternative to the claim that crash dummies are objective. It is the more accurate claim, generated from a research programme that started from women's bodies.

A more sophisticated objection is that the god trick is a caricature of how scientists actually understand their work. Real scientists, the objection runs, acknowledge uncertainty, peer-review their results, and revise in light of evidence. Haraway's answer, which I find convincing, is that the question is not whether individual scientists are humble but whether the institutional self-representation of science presents its findings as situated or as universal. Pharmacopoeias, engineering standards and standardised testing protocols treat outputs as universal. That is where the god trick lives.

The point generalises beyond the engineering of bodies. The same logic explains why "wild animals" are categorised differently by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17): even the framing of an object of study reflects the location of the community that frames it. Dorothy Smith's parallel claim, that women's perspective "discredits sociology's claim to constitute an objective knowledge independent of the sociologist's situation" (Harding, 2004: 128), confirms the diagnosis from the side of sociological method.

The god trick is therefore not a slogan but a diagnostic concept. It names the operation by which partial knowledge is laundered into universal knowledge, and it points to the structural reforms, disclosed location, diversified research communities, integration of marginalised voices, that produce more, not less, objectivity.

### Mock 2: Critically assess Harding's argument in *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* in light of the reference-man cases catalogued by Criado-Perez.

Each of these is a domain in which research was objective by conventional standards (tested, measured, standardised) but failed the test of strong objectivity: nobody asked whose body the science was about. Harding's first argument is empirically vindicated.

The second argument is that "the subject of feminist knowledge is itself multiple, heterogeneous, and contradictory or incoherent" (Harding, 2004: 134). She makes this argument to forestall the essentialism critique: feminist epistemology is not built on a single transcendent woman-knower but on the bifurcated consciousness of "the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134). The reference-man record vindicates this too. The crash-dummy and PPE problems centre on bodies coded as "average women", usually white, often Western, but a closer look shows variation within "women": Criado-Perez documents that pregnancy is unrepresented in standard crash-testing populations, that female nurses (a substantial majority of the nursing workforce) and female construction workers experience equipment problems differently, and that office workers and manual workers face different versions of the same reference-man pattern. Collins's matrix of domination is the natural extension of Harding's "multiple, heterogeneous" subject. Harding's argument anticipates this extension and would be weakened only if a unitary woman-knower had turned out to be sufficient, which the record shows she is not.

The third argument is that "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133). The reference-man record vindicates this directly. The crash-dummy problem persisted not because individual engineers were biased but because engineering teams were demographically homogeneous; the PPE problem persisted not because individual designers were prejudiced but because design teams shared the same assumed body; the office-thermostat problem encoded a shared 1960s assumption about an "average" worker. Harding's diagnosis, that conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137), is exactly what produced the reference man. The methodological prescription that follows is structural: diversify research communities, treat the conceptual framework of the discipline as itself an object of inquiry, integrate the voices of those whose lives generate the missing data.

Three critical points are worth registering. First, Harding's framework applies most cleanly where the object of knowledge is socially constituted (medicine, engineering, ergonomics); its application to physics is more controversial, though Harding's own discussion of how even "wild animals" are categorised differently in different national scientific traditions (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17) suggests the framework reaches further than critics allow. Second, the framework risks a circularity: "marginal lives" are identified as epistemically privileged, but the criteria for marginality are themselves contested. Third, the political prescriptions of the book sometimes outrun the epistemological argument, blurring the line between strong objectivity as a methodological standard and feminism as a political programme.

These reservations notwithstanding, *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* offers the conceptual vocabulary the reference-man cases needed. The book diagnosed an epistemic structure; Criado-Perez supplied the case file. Reading them together, Harding's argument is not just defensible but indispensable.

### Mock 3: What does Haraway mean by the god trick, and why does she treat partiality as a methodological virtue?

The question has two parts; I will answer each in turn and then connect them. By the god trick Donna Haraway means the move in which a knower presents their location-bound, body-bound, history-bound perspective as if it were no perspective at all, what the lecture-quoted formulation calls "speaking authoritatively about everything in the world from no particular location or human perspective at all" (Harding, 2004: 4). By partiality as a methodological virtue she means that the disclosure of one's location, and the explicit acknowledgement that one sees only some of the picture, is a stronger basis for objectivity than the pretence of seeing all of it. The two halves are connected: the god trick is the vice; situated, partial knowledge is the virtue.

I take the first half first. The god trick is not the claim that scientists are arrogant individuals. It is a description of an institutional epistemology. Pharmacopoeias, engineering standards, peer-reviewed journals and standardised testing protocols present their outputs as universal, applicable to "the human body", "the average user", "the population". Haraway's diagnosis is that these outputs are in fact produced from very specific locations. The clearest illustrations are the reference-man cases catalogued by Criado-Perez in *Invisible Women*. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg) generate crash-safety standards presented as protecting "passengers", with the consequence that women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die in a crash. PPE designed for European and US male bodies fails female workers, with employers assuming smaller sizes will suffice. Office thermostats were set in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate. Pharmacological dosing was calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not". In each case the institutional output denies its location of production. That denial is the god trick.

Why is the denial damaging? Because, as Harding adds, conventional method "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137). Assumptions shared by the entire research community do not show up to that community as assumptions. The god trick is institutionally productive because it makes these shared assumptions invisible.

To the second half. Why does Haraway recommend partiality as a methodological virtue? Three reasons.

First, partiality is empirically more accurate than universalism. The crash-dummy data is the proof: the more honest claim is "this dummy represents 50th-percentile male bodies". That partial claim opens to correction; the universal claim "this dummy represents passengers" closes to correction. The point generalises across the reference-man cases. The pharmacological claim "this dose is calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male" is testable and falsifiable; the universal claim "this is the correct dose for an adult" hides the calibration and obstructs correction.

Second, partiality enables joining. Haraway's situated-knowledge essay argues that partial perspectives, joined together, produce richer accounts than any single perspective. Harding's catalogue of standpoint-research sites makes the same point at the level of social knowledge: women who are not prostitutes and have not been raped, women who are Native Canadian, women who are African-American domestic workers, women who are holocaust survivors, Chicana cannery workers, older lesbians, African-American women in slavery, Japanese-American concentration-camp survivors (Harding, 2004: 129) each contribute a partial perspective whose joining produces accounts no single location could generate.

Third, partiality is more honest about the conditions of knowledge production. Harding makes this explicit: strong objectivity "requires what we can think of as 'strong reflexivity'" (Harding, 2004: 136). Reflexivity means examining the conditions under which one's knowledge is made: the funding structure, the research-team composition, the conceptual framework. Partiality is the precondition of reflexivity, because you cannot reflect on a location you deny.

The standard objection is that partiality slides into relativism, if everyone is partial, no claim is better than another. Haraway and Harding both reject the inference. "Judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). Partial knowledge is testable, comparable, and ranked: by empirical adequacy, by reflexive transparency, and by the breadth of joined perspectives. The reference-man examples show this in action: the partial standpoint of women's lives produced testably better predictions than the universal standpoint of the reference man.

The god trick and partiality-as-virtue are therefore two sides of one argument. The trick claims a universal vantage and produces parochial knowledge; partiality discloses location and produces, through reflexivity and combination, more reliable knowledge. The lecture's slogan applies precisely: a perspectival approach to knowledge produces more, not less, objectivity.

### Mock 4: Compare Hartsock's feminist standpoint with Haraway's situated knowledge.

Nancy Hartsock's feminist standpoint and Donna Haraway's situated knowledge are sister theories whose family resemblance is closer than their genealogies imply. Both reject the god trick of view-from-nowhere knowledge. Both insist that location is constitutive of knowing. Both treat disclosed partiality as more scientific than concealed universality. They differ, and this is where the interesting work happens, on where the privilege of perspective comes from, on how plural the resulting epistemology is, and on the political register in which the framework operates. I will argue that the two are best read together, with Hartsock supplying the materialist grounding and Haraway the methodological flexibility.

Hartsock's framework, set out in her 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint", is materialist in the Marxist sense. It runs on three propositions, summarised in the lecture: first, men's and women's understandings of the world differ because the labour they do and the access they have to public space differ. Second, the dominant group's worldview attempts to structure social reality for everyone. Third, because the feminist standpoint reveals the partiality of the dominant view, it opens a path to liberation. The framework grounds epistemic privilege in a specific material location, women's labour, especially the reproductive, caring and domestic labour that sustains capitalism but which capitalism does not pay for.

Haraway's situated knowledge, articulated in her 1988 essay, takes a different route to a neighbouring destination. "Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges" (Harding, 2004: 127). The framework is informed by science studies, biology, and feminist technoscience rather than by Marx. Haraway is wary of any move that grants automatic epistemic privilege to a category, including the category "women"; her cyborg figure (and her later writing on companion species) explicitly resists the unified standpoint in favour of partial, contradictory, technologically- and biologically-mediated identities.

The two converge on three points. They share the rejection of the god trick. They share the insistence that knowledge is located. They share the claim, developed by Harding into strong objectivity, that disclosed location strengthens rather than weakens objectivity: "strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136).

They diverge on three points.

First, the source of epistemic privilege. Hartsock locates privilege in a specific material activity, women's labour. Haraway locates it more diffusely: any disclosed location is epistemically better than a concealed one, and marginal locations afford specific insights but no automatic priority. Hartsock's framework predicts that women, as labourers, have systematically different access to social reality; Haraway's framework predicts that any rigorously situated knower can produce stronger knowledge than any god-trick knower.

Second, the strength of the privilege claim. Hartsock approaches the claim that the feminist standpoint generates a less partial account of social reality as such. Haraway is more pluralist: partial perspectives must be joined together for richer knowledge, and no single perspective is the master.

Third, the political register. Hartsock's framework is closer to classical Marxist politics, it locates revolutionary potential in a structurally positioned class. Haraway's framework is closer to coalition politics, it locates knowledge in shifting, partial alliances of differently-situated actors.

The empirical record favours different aspects of each. Hartsock's materialism is freshly visible in the reference-man cases. PPE is designed for European and US male bodies, an artefact of who is in the design room and what bodies do the work being protected. Crash-test dummies encode the labour assumption that the standard driver is a male commuter, with women, children and the elderly as deviations. Office temperature is calibrated to male office workers, with the implicit assumption that women's office presence is secondary. These are exactly the labour-and-bodies dimensions Hartsock predicted. Haraway's framework is indispensable for the framing problem: even physical objects are categorised differently in different scientific traditions, "wild animals" by Japanese, Indian and Anglo-American primatologists (Harding, 2004: 140, n.17), and the proper response is disclosed location plus joined perspectives rather than a single corrected universal.

Collins's matrix of domination sits between the two. It shares Hartsock's political ambition but distributes standpoint across multiple intersecting axes, which is closer to Haraway's plural partiality. The matrix of domination is what happens when you take Hartsock's materialism seriously and notice that there is more than one material axis.

The artificial choice would be to pick one. The honest answer is that contemporary sociology uses both. Standpoint thinking provides the political grounding and the lineage to social struggle; situated knowledge provides the methodological flexibility to handle plural locations and overlapping framings. Harding's strong objectivity is the bridge, and Collins's matrix is the extension. The two essays of 1983 and 1988 are best read as a single argument articulated twice.

Standpoint theory's recommendations for design follow. First, that the design community itself be diversified, because what the design community shares it will not see. Hartsock's claim that "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle" applies directly: women in design, women in safety regulation, women in pharmacology must be present not as tokens but as articulators of a critical standpoint. Adapting the framework to technical-design tempo is ongoing work. ### Mock 6: Is standpoint theory an epistemology or a politics? Discuss.

The question presents a disjunction; my argument is that the disjunction is false. Standpoint theory is constitutively both an epistemology and a politics, and one of its central insights, articulated by Hartsock, Harding, Collins and hooks, is that the apparent separation between the two is itself a feature of the dominant epistemology it criticises. I will defend this in three steps. First, I will show why standpoint theory cannot be reduced to either pole. Second, I will examine each pole in turn. Third, I will argue that the integration is itself a methodological commitment, not a category confusion.

The disjunction trades on a familiar bifurcation: epistemology concerns the conditions of valid knowledge; politics concerns the distribution of power. Mainstream Western philosophy of science has policed this distinction strictly, treating it as the line that separates rigorous inquiry from advocacy. Standpoint theory's first move is to contest the line. As Harding puts it, the conventional notion that science should be value-free has "been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137). Politics is already in epistemology; it is the politics of who counts as a competent judge. Pretending otherwise, what Haraway calls the god trick, is itself a political move.

Consider the epistemological side first. Standpoint theory makes substantive claims about how knowledge is produced. It claims that all thought "starts off from socially determinate lives" (Harding, 2004: 128). It claims that "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133). It claims that disclosed location is more scientifically informative than concealed location, and that this is what Harding calls strong objectivity: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). It claims that some social locations afford insights that other locations suppress, an empirical claim, testable against the record. These are paradigm epistemological claims. They concern what knowledge is, how it is produced, and how its quality can be evaluated.

Consider the political side second. Standpoint theory is also a politics. It identifies struggle as the precondition of standpoint: as the lecture insists, "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle". It connects knowledge production to social movements, feminist, Black feminist, postcolonial. It treats the disclosure of marginalised voices as a structural reform of science rather than as an optional courtesy. It calls for changes in who is funded, who is published, who is in the room when conceptual frameworks are set. Harding, in her introduction, is direct: "political engagement, rather than dispassionate neutrality, was necessary to gain access to the means to do such research, the research training, jobs in research institutions, research funding, and publication" (Harding, 2004: 6).

The question, then, is whether the conjunction of these two sides is incoherent. I argue it is not. Three reasons.

First, the empirical record connects them. The reference-man material, documented by Criado-Perez in *Invisible Women*, is the case study. Crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male produce women's serious-injury rate 47% higher than men's; the testing community's homogeneity is the political condition that produced the epistemic failure. PPE based on European and US male bodies misfits female workers; office thermostats calibrated to a male metabolic rate misjudge female office workers; pharmacological dosing built around a 70kg Caucasian male misdoses women. Each is an epistemic failure produced by a political condition (the homogeneity of the research and design community) and corrected by a political-epistemic intervention (diversification, integration of marginalised standpoints).

Second, the methodology connects them. Strong reflexivity, in Harding's sense, is the requirement that the researcher examine her own location, including her political commitments, as part of the data. This is not advocacy; it is rigour. Conventional method treats the researcher's political location as a contaminant to be hidden; strong objectivity treats it as evidence to be examined. The methodological commitment is itself political (it insists on disclosure) and epistemological (it improves accuracy).

Third, the canon connects them. Hartsock grounds standpoint in women's labour and political struggle. Collins's matrix of domination, race, class, gender, sexuality as interlocking axes, is articulated through Black feminist political tradition. hooks's oppositional gaze, "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), is simultaneously an analysis of vision and a politics of resistance. Gilroy's reconstruction of modernity "from the slaves' point of view" is both historiography and political reconstruction, and the lecture explicitly connects Gilroy's project to Dussel and Howard French as part of one larger reconstructive enterprise.

Is standpoint theory an epistemology or a politics? It is both, and the integration is the point. To force the disjunction would be to accept the very bifurcation standpoint theory was built to refuse. The lecture's slogan, a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity, depends on the integration; pull the two apart, and the slogan collapses.

### Mock 7: Crash-test dummies, office thermostats and the reference man.

Caroline Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* (2019) documents the consequences as the slide records them: women are 47% more likely to be seriously injured in a comparable crash than men, and 17% more likely to die. None of this is a failure of engineering rigour in the narrow sense; the tests were run, the data collected, the statistics published. It is a failure of conceptual framework: the reference body was set, and the framework was invisible to the framework-setters.

Move to the office thermostat. Standard office temperature was developed in the 1960s around the metabolic resting rate of the average man. A recent Dutch study, the slide adds, found that the metabolic rate of young adult females doing office work is significantly lower. Offices are too cold for the women who work in them, year after year, because the comfort standard was set against a body that women do not have. This is the same failure at the scale of a building's HVAC system. The engineering did its job (it produced a stable temperature); the conceptual framework did not (it defined "comfortable" against one kind of body).

The reference man, in Criado-Perez's vocabulary, is the abstract figure that sits behind both. The reference man is a "Caucasian Man" who weighs 70kg, and whose "superpower is being able to represent humanity as a whole. Of course, he does not" (Criado-Perez, 2019). The reference man appears in PPE, where the slide records that medical equipment designed to protect doctors and nurses is based on the sizes and characteristics of male populations from Europe and the US, and that employers who buy smaller sizes for female workers find that this does not solve the problem. The reference man even appears in bags of cement and bricks, sized to shoulders, hands and centres of gravity that are not those of female construction workers.

Sandra Harding names it: conventional objectivity "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137). Assumptions shared by the entire design community are invisible to that community. The crash dummy, the office thermostat and the reference man each codify such an assumption. Donna Haraway's god trick is the institutional cousin: the system claims a view from nowhere when it actually has a view from one place.

Applied to engineering: the design community's choices about reference body are themselves part of the engineering evidence, not part of the methodological background. Applied to medicine: dose-response curves must be derived from demographically diverse trials, not generalised from male subjects. Applied to ergonomics: the standard environment, like the office at a fixed temperature, must be calibrated against the bodies that will live in it.

Hartsock's framework adds the structural prediction. Men's and women's understandings of the world differ, the slide records, because of the labour they do and their access to public space. The design community of mid-twentieth-century automotive engineering, of HVAC standards-setting, of pharmacological trial design, was overwhelmingly male, and the artefacts it produced reflect the labour and body of that community. The corrective is not better statistics within the existing community but the structural diversification of the community itself.

The remedy is, finally, structural rather than procedural. Better statistics will not fix a reference body; tighter regulation alone will not fix a homogeneous design culture. What is required is the diversification of the design community itself, and the elevation of the conceptual framework to a first-order object of scientific scrutiny. Strong objectivity demands no less, and produces, as the lecture's slogan insists, more not less objectivity.

### Mock 8: Does the dominance of a homogeneous research community vindicate or undermine Harding's account of strong objectivity?

The question is essentially asking whether Harding's framework predicts what happens when a research community is demographically narrow. I will argue that the empirical record of the reference-man cases vindicates the diagnostic half of Harding's account decisively, and that whether her remedial half is vindicated depends on whether the research community in question undertakes the structural reforms her framework requires. Harding's account is therefore at once descriptively confirmed and prescriptively pending.

Strong objectivity, as Harding sets it out in *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* (1991), has three components. First, "the problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Second, "strong objectivity requires that the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). Third, "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133). The framework requires reflexivity, structural diversification, and disclosure of the conditions of knowledge production.

Homogeneous research communities, considered against these three requirements, are a sustained failure case. The reference-man material shows the pattern at scale. Crash-test design, developed in the 1950s by an automotive engineering community overwhelmingly male, produced a standard dummy at 50th-percentile male dimensions (1.77m, 76kg) and standards that left women 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die in a crash. PPE design, dominated by manufacturers oriented to European and US male workforces, produced equipment sized to male bodies and a market in which, the slide notes, employers responded to female workers by buying smaller sizes (which does not solve the underlying problem). Office-thermostat standards, set by 1960s HVAC engineering, encoded a male metabolic rate; the Dutch study cited on the slide later found female office-work metabolic rates significantly lower. Pharmacological dosing was calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male body, despite the slide's observation that men and women have different immune systems and hormones that affect how chemicals are absorbed.

Each of these is a god-trick failure of exactly the kind Harding's framework predicts. Within each design community, the assumptions about whose body is the reference body were not visible as assumptions; they were the unmarked default. Conventional method, "operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137), could not catch the failure because the failure lived in what the whole community shared. This is the diagnostic vindication.

Three features of homogeneous communities interact with Harding's framework in instructive ways.

First, they make the conceptual framework invisible. The reference body is not a hypothesis tested against alternatives; it is a background assumption transported into research findings unexamined. Harding's prediction that "culturewide assumptions that have not been criticized within the scientific research process are transported into the results of research" (Harding, 2004: 129) is exactly what produces the reference-man pattern.

Second, they generate plausible-looking outputs from skewed inputs. The crash-test data, the dose-response curves, the temperature standards all look authoritative. The absence of disclosed location is the hallmark of the god trick. A scientist or engineer working within a homogeneous community without examining the demographic composition of the testing population, and without checking the output against marginalised testimony, is reproducing the structural failure Harding diagnosed.

Third, they can be used in ways that conform to strong objectivity if the community undertakes structural reform. The same engineering and pharmacological apparatus can be deployed reflexively: with disclosure of the testing population, with auditing across demographic axes, with integration of marginalised voices in evaluation, and with treatment of standards as data to be tested rather than findings to be accepted. Diversifying the design community, and treating the reference body as a hypothesis rather than a default, are the structural moves Harding's framework requires.

So: does the dominance of a homogeneous research community vindicate or undermine Harding?

The undermining reading runs: if homogeneous research communities are common and persistent, perhaps Harding's framework underestimates how difficult structural reform is. The reference-man cases were documented decades after they began, and many of the underlying standards have not been overhauled. On this reading the framework names a real problem but its remedy is slower in practice than in theory.

The vindicating reading runs: every documented reference-man case is an instance of Harding's general claim that homogeneous research communities encode their assumptions invisibly. The framework is empirically confirmed: the prediction was that universalising partial perspectives produces systematic failure, and the reference-man cases produce exactly that failure across crash safety, PPE, ergonomics and pharmacology.

My answer is that both readings are correct, and that the disjunction in the question is misleading. Homogeneous research communities are a stress test for Harding. They vindicate her diagnosis and they intensify the conditions that require her remedy. Whether the remedy will be applied is an open empirical question, and the answer depends on the structural reform of who builds, audits, and uses these systems. The lecture's slogan, that a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity, is the standard against which homogeneous research communities should be evaluated. Some communities will meet it; many will not.

### Mock 9: How convincing do you find Hartsock's argument that women's labour generates a privileged standpoint?

Nancy Hartsock's argument, set out in her 1983 essay "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism", runs that women's labour, reproductive, domestic, caring, gives women systematic access to dimensions of social reality that men's labour does not, and that this access, when politically articulated, yields a less partial account of social life. I find the argument substantially convincing, but only after two important qualifications: the privilege is conditional rather than automatic, and the unitary "women" of 1983 has to be replaced by the multiple women of the matrix of domination. With those qualifications the argument is one of the most empirically successful claims in late-twentieth-century sociology; without them, it is vulnerable to charges of essentialism that Hartsock herself did not fully forestall.

Hartsock's three claims, as summarised in the lecture, are these. First, that men's and women's understandings of the world differ because the labour they do and their access to public space differ. Second, that the dominant group's view attempts to structure social reality for everyone, while subordinate groups must in addition understand the dominant view in order to survive within it. Third, that the feminist standpoint, by articulating women's situated experience through political struggle, reveals the partiality of the dominant view and opens the possibility of liberation. The framework follows Marx's structure: just as the proletariat's labour gives it a vantage on capitalist social relations that capital cannot see, women's labour gives them a vantage on patriarchal social relations.

Consider the empirical case for. Care work and domestic labour are not merely under-recognised; they are the precondition of all other social activity, and they are mostly performed by women. Harding's introduction lists what an androcentric framework rendered invisible: violence against women coded as women "asking for it" (Harding, 2004: 5), the unpaid double day naturalised, menstruation and menopause medicalised, rationality narrowed to the instrumental rationality of institutions from which women, the poor and non-Western peoples were barred (Harding, 2004: 5-6). Caroline Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women* (2019), as the lecture sets out, catalogues the cognate distortions in technical knowledge: crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male (1.77m, 76kg) leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die in a crash; PPE designed for European and US male bodies fits female workers badly; office thermostats calibrated to a male metabolic rate leave female office workers too cold; pharmacology calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male misdoses women despite different immune systems and hormones. These are exactly the kinds of distortion Hartsock's framework predicts: when one group's labour and bodies are paradigmatic, the standards constructed around them perform poorly when applied universally.

Consider, second, the structural reason the argument has force. Sandra Harding's gloss on the standpoint tradition formalises it: "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133). If knowledge is produced by communities, and the communities of research and design have been demographically homogeneous, then the unrepresented locations in those communities, historically, women, especially working-class and racialised women, will be epistemically resourceful in proportion to their exclusion. Hartsock's argument is the original version of this insight.

Consider, third, the qualifications. The privilege is not automatic. As the lecture insists, "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle"; it is not a property of women as such. Harding makes this explicit: men can produce feminist knowledge "from their particular social situation" (Harding, 2004: 135), and women can reproduce dominant assumptions. The standpoint is conditional on articulation through political and intellectual community. Hartsock's 1983 framing sometimes sounds more automatic than this, as if labour produced consciousness mechanically, and that framing is unconvincing. The conditional reading is the convincing one.

The second qualification is intersectional. Hartsock's 1983 essay implicitly took the labour of a particular kind of woman (white, often middle-class, often Western) as paradigmatic. Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination, articulated in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990), corrects this: women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, partly conflicting. The standpoint of an African-American domestic worker, Collins's "outsider within", is not the same as the standpoint of a white middle-class housewife. Harding endorses the move when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134). She also points to the wider catalogue of standpoint sites: women who are holocaust survivors, Chicana cannery workers, older lesbians, African-American women in slavery, Japanese-American concentration-camp survivors (Harding, 2004: 129), each generating a different version of the standpoint that Hartsock first theorised in the singular.

Two further objections deserve answers. One is the relativism worry: if women's labour yields privileged access, why not anyone's labour? Harding's reply, which I endorse, is empirical: which standpoint generates testably less partial accounts? The reference-man data is the test, and standpoint research passes it. The other is the essentialism worry: doesn't grounding standpoint in labour fix women into a category? The reply is the conditional reading: labour generates standpoint only when articulated through political community; the category is opened, not closed, by articulation.

How convincing? Substantially, with qualifications. The 1983 framework needs the matrix of domination and the achievement reading. With those, Hartsock's argument remains foundational. Without them, it is essentialist. The qualifications are themselves available within the broader standpoint tradition, so the convincing version of Hartsock is the one read alongside Collins and Harding.

### Mock 10: "There is power in looking." Critically discuss with reference to hooks and the oppositional gaze.

bell hooks's sentence, "there is power in looking" (hooks, 1992: 115), compresses three claims into five words. It claims that vision is a site of social power; that vision is a site of resistance; and that the conditions under which one looks, and is permitted to look, are themselves political. I want to defend each of the three claims, draw out hooks's concept of the oppositional gaze, and argue that the framework is most powerful when read as a contribution to standpoint epistemology rather than as a separate theory of vision.

Begin with the first claim: vision is a site of social power. hooks's argument, set out in "The Oppositional Gaze: Black Female Spectators" in *Black Looks* (1992), starts from a historical fact: under slavery in the United States, enslaved Black people were punished for looking at white people. "The politics of slavery, of racialized power relations, were such that slaves were denied their right to gaze" (hooks, 1992: 115). The slide adds the autobiographical layer: "I remember being punished as a child for staring, for those hard intense direct looks children would give grown-ups, looks that were seen as confrontational, as gestures of resistance, challenges to authority" (hooks, 1992: 115). This is not a metaphor; it is a documented disciplinary practice. The right to look, to fix one's attention, to study, to evaluate, to refuse to avert one's eyes, was a right that the racial-political order systematically withheld from Black people, and especially from Black women.

Berger's analysis in *Ways of Seeing* (1972), "men act and women appear. Men look at women. Women watch themselves being looked at" (Berger, 1972: 47), captures a related structure of the gendered gaze in European visual culture, but it does not capture the racialised denial of the gaze. hooks's contribution is precisely the racial extension. Berger's binary (looker / looked-at) becomes hooks's triangle (looker / looked-at / forbidden looker). The forbidden looker is the structural position from which the oppositional gaze emerges.

Take the second claim: vision is a site of resistance. "All attempts to repress our/black peoples' right to gaze had produced in us an overwhelming longing to look, a rebellious desire, an oppositional gaze" (hooks, 1992: 116). The oppositional gaze is the active reclamation of the right to look, not as a return to the structure Berger describes (women looked at by men) but as a third position that refuses both the looked-at position and the false universal of the looker. hooks is theorising Black female spectatorship: the way Black women have watched Hollywood cinema critically, refusing the cinema's invitation to identify with white female protagonists and white male spectators, instead developing a critical practice of looking-against. "By courageously looking, we defiantly declared: 'Not only will I stare. I want my look to change reality.' Even in the worse circumstances of domination, the ability to manipulate one's gaze in the face of structures of domination that would contain it, opens up the possibility of agency" (hooks, 1992: 116).

Now the third claim: the conditions of looking are political. The right to look is unevenly distributed. The right to be a credible looker, to have one's looking taken seriously as evidence, is even more unevenly distributed. Berger's analysis of the European nude makes the point for the gendered version. The nude is structured for an unpainted male spectator who, by Berger's account, "is the spectator in front of the picture and he is presumed to be a man" (Berger, 1972: 54). The supposed objectivity of the image is the suppression of one specific looker's location. hooks generalises the analysis to race: the looker who is presumed and the looker who is denied are politically positioned.

Read alongside the standpoint tradition, hooks's framework is recognisably an account of achieved standpoint. The oppositional gaze is not given by Black female identity; it is produced through political and intellectual struggle, through the Black feminist tradition that the lecture connects to Patricia Hill Collins's matrix of domination. This matches Harding's insistence that "it is not the experiences or the speech that provides the grounds of feminist claims; it is rather the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations…that look at the world from the perspective of women's lives" (Harding, 1991: 124). The oppositional gaze is the articulated observation; it is not a property of biology but a practice of community.

One critical worry. The metaphor of looking is generative but bounded. Not all standpoints are visual; care knowledge, embodied knowledge, kinaesthetic knowledge of work resist visual formulation. hooks does not claim universality for the visual register; the framework is most powerful where vision is the medium of power (cinema, surveillance, the structure of the European nude that Berger analyses). Where the medium of power is the body's design or the dose's calibration, as in the reference-man cases, the gaze metaphor needs to be supplemented by Hartsock's labour-based framework and Harding's strong objectivity. The three are best read as a single argument articulated through different empirical sites.

"There is power in looking." hooks's sentence is a diagnostic, a resistance, and a programme for standpoint epistemology applied to vision. It belongs at the centre of any account of how perspectives produce, rather than diminish, objectivity.

Gilroy's promise on the slide, to "uncover both an ethics of freedom to set alongside modernity's ethics of law and the new conceptions of selfhood…that are waiting to be constructed from the slaves' standpoint", is the methodological promise that political economy reconstructed from the slaves' standpoint will be more, not less, accurate.

The slaves' standpoint reveals that the universal claims of these concepts were tested first by the people they excluded; the Haitian Revolution, the slave narratives, the resistance traditions in the Caribbean and the Americas were where the universal claims of modernity met their critical reckoning. Harding's introduction names this kind of work: the conceptual frameworks of the disciplines "promoted historically distinctive institutional and cultural interests and concerns" (Harding, 2004: 5), and the corrective is to begin thought from the lives of those the frameworks excluded.

Third, the cultural history of modernity. Berger's analysis of European visual culture, "the surveyor of woman in herself is male: the surveyed female" (Berger, 1972: 47), shows that the unmarked spectator of European art is gendered. Gilroy's parallel argument is that the unmarked spectator of European modernity is racialised. The corrective in each case is the same: disclose the location of the spectator and read culture from the standpoints excluded.

What do Harding and Haraway add to Gilroy's empirical case? Harding adds the structural argument: the homogeneity of mainstream historiography is itself an epistemic problem, and the corrective is structural (diversification of who writes the history) rather than merely procedural (more attention to slavery within a still-European narrative). Haraway adds the methodological argument: situated knowledge from particular standpoints is not a supplement to "real" history but a constitutive component of accurate historical description. The two together generate what Harding calls strong objectivity applied to historiography: more rigorous because more locations, more archives, more languages, more communities of memory are open to scrutiny.

Two objections. One is that the slaves' standpoint is hard to reconstruct because the archives are thin and mediated. The reply, Gilroy's and Harding's together, is that the difficulty is itself diagnostic: the thinness of the archive is the product of the same epistemic structure that produces the bias. Reconstructing the standpoint is intellectually demanding precisely because it requires assembling fragmentary evidence into a critical account, "the subsequently articulated observations of, and theory about, the rest of nature and social relations" (Harding, 1991: 124), as Harding puts the standpoint claim. The other is the appropriation worry: that reconstruction risks speaking for those it claims to amplify. The reply is that such reconstruction must be community-grounded, with the relevant historical and contemporary communities as co-authors of interpretation rather than as data sources.

Gilroy's claim is therefore not a political concession but a strong-objectivity requirement. Both Harding and Haraway predict this conclusion; the lecture connects it to Dussel and Howard French as part of a wider reconstructive project; the slaves' standpoint is constitutive of an honest history of modernity.

### Mock 12: Can sociology pursue objectivity while taking standpoint seriously?

The question presents the choice as a constraint to be satisfied. My answer is that sociology cannot pursue objectivity except by taking standpoint seriously, the conjunction is not a balancing act but a requirement. The argument turns on Sandra Harding's distinction between objectivity (the goal) and objectivism (the failed methodological model that has tried to instantiate it). Donna Haraway's god trick names the view-from-nowhere as an epistemological fiction; Harding's strong objectivity names the alternative.

Harding's argument is sharp: "the problem with the conventional conception of objectivity is not that it is too rigorous or too 'objectifying'… but that it is *not rigorous or objectifying enough*" (Harding, 2004: 128). Conventional objectivity, operationalised as detached value-freedom, "has been operationalized to identify and eliminate only those social values and interests that differ among the researchers and critics who are regarded by the scientific community as competent to make such judgements" (Harding, 2004: 137). Assumptions shared by the whole community slip through. Strong objectivity demands more: "the subject of knowledge be placed on the same critical, causal plane as the objects of knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 136). The researcher's standpoint becomes part of what must be scrutinised. This is more objective, not less, because it widens the scope of critical scrutiny.

So: can sociology pursue objectivity while taking standpoint seriously? Yes, and the pursuit of objectivity requires taking standpoint seriously. Three arguments support this.

First, the empirical argument. Sociology that ignores standpoint produces systematic distortions. Caroline Criado-Perez's *Invisible Women*, drawn on through the lecture slides, catalogues these in adjacent fields: crash-test dummies modelled on the 50th-percentile male leave women 47% more likely to be seriously injured and 17% more likely to die in a crash; PPE based on European and US male bodies fits female workers badly; office thermostats set in the 1960s to a male metabolic rate leave women too cold; pharmacology calibrated to a 70kg Caucasian male produces incorrect dosing despite different immune systems and hormones. Each of these is what objectivism produces when standpoint is not taken seriously: a partial perspective universalised, with empirical failure as the consequence. Standpoint-aware research catches these failures; objectivism cannot.

Second, the methodological argument. Reflexivity is a strong-objectivity move. A sociology that requires positionality statements, that disaggregates findings demographically, that integrates community testimony into evaluation, this sociology is more rigorous than the alternative. It makes research more, not less, objective by placing the researcher's location on the same critical plane as the object of research.

Third, the community argument. Harding's claim that "communities and not primarily individuals produce knowledge" (Harding, 2004: 133) implies that the composition of the research community is an epistemically relevant variable. A demographically homogeneous community produces shared invisible assumptions; a more diverse community catches more of them. Harding makes the point graphically: "If the community of 'qualified' researchers and critics systematically excludes, for example, all African-Americans and women of all races and if the larger culture is stratified by race and gender and lacks powerful critiques of this stratification, it is not plausible to imagine that racist and sexist interests and values would be identified within a community of scientists composed entirely of people who benefit, intentionally or not, from institutionalized racism and sexism" (Harding, 2004: 137). The objectivity of sociology depends on the structural reform of its personnel, not as a political concession but as a methodological requirement.

Two objections deserve answers.

The first is the relativism objection: that taking standpoint seriously slides into the view that all perspectives are equally valid. Harding rejects this directly: "judgmental (or epistemological) relativism is anathema to any scientific project, and feminist ones are no exception" (Harding, 2004: 131). Standpoint theory ranks claims by empirical adequacy and reflexive transparency. The reference-man data is the proof: the claim that women are 47% more likely to be injured is not a relativist alternative; it is a testably better claim, generated from research that started from women's bodies.

The second is the politicisation objection: that integrating standpoint blurs the line between research and advocacy. Harding's reply is the converse: research that ignores standpoint is research whose politics is hidden. Making politics visible is what allows it to be evaluated. The reference-man examples were not products of overt political bias; they were products of communities whose political assumptions were invisible to them. Visibility is the precondition of evaluation.

Sociology can therefore pursue objectivity only by taking standpoint seriously. The disjunction in the question is the disjunction objectivism wants us to accept; standpoint epistemology dissolves it. The lecture's slogan is the right summary: a perspectival approach produces more, not less, objectivity. Take standpoint seriously, and objectivity follows.

### Mock 13: Critically assess Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* as a development of standpoint epistemology.

Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment* (1990) is the most generative single development of standpoint epistemology after its 1983 founding by Nancy Hartsock. The book does three things at once: it intersectionalises standpoint, replacing the implicitly unitary "women" of 1983 with the multiple, intersecting locations of the matrix of domination; it makes standpoint epistemology empirically richer by directing attention to specific intersections; and it integrates standpoint theory with social-movement politics by treating the production of standpoint knowledge as continuous with the political organisation of marginalised communities. I will argue that the book is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition for contemporary sociology, and that the few criticisms it has attracted are best handled within its own framework.

Collins's starting point is the insufficiency of single-axis standpoint claims. Hartsock's 1983 essay grounded standpoint in women's labour, but the labour of which women? Collins's analytical move is to take that fit problem as productive. The standpoint of African-American women is generative not because it is more authentic than other standpoints, but because the intersection of race, class and gender that African-American women occupy is structurally informative about the operation of multiple systems of domination simultaneously. The matrix of domination is Collins's vocabulary for this. Race, class, gender and (in later work) sexuality are interlocking, not additive, axes; their integration shapes lived experience and the knowledge that flows from it.

Three features of the book deserve emphasis.

First, the rescue from essentialism. Hartsock's framework risks treating "women" as a unitary subject. Collins shows that women's standpoints are multiple, intersected, and partly conflicting. Her concept of "outsider within" knowledge, African-American domestic workers' access to white family life from a position of structural exclusion, is one of the most generative ideas in twentieth-century sociology. The outsider within sees, because of structural exclusion, what insiders cannot, and is seen by insiders, when seen at all, as a non-witness. Harding endorses the formulation directly when she names the subject of feminist knowledge as "the thinker whose consciousness is bifurcated, the outsider within" (Harding, 2004: 134). Harding also explicitly cites Collins's argument that "starting thought from the lives of poor and in some cases illiterate African-American women reveals important truths about the lives of intellectuals, both African-American and European-American, as well as about those women" (Harding, 2004: 130).

Second, the empirical productivity. The matrix of domination directs researchers to specific intersections where multiple systems intersect to produce distinctive forms of knowledge. Harding's catalogue of standpoint research sites (Harding, 2004: 129) gives the texture of this productivity: women who are holocaust survivors, Chicana cannery workers, older lesbians, African-American women in slavery, Japanese-American concentration-camp survivors. Each is an intersected location, and the knowledge each generates would be missed by a single-axis framework. The reference-man cases catalogued by Criado-Perez (crash-test dummies, PPE, office thermostats, pharmacology) all have a matrix dimension as well: the "average woman" implied as the deviation from the reference man is herself usually white, often Western, often middle-class, and the matrix asks what is missed when the deviation is generalised in turn.

Third, the integration of standpoint and politics. Collins's standpoint is articulated through Black feminist intellectual and political tradition. The production of standpoint knowledge is inseparable from the political organisation of marginalised communities. This recovers Hartsock's original insistence that "a standpoint is an achievement, won from political struggle". It also matches Harding's claim that "political struggle itself produced insight" (Harding, 2004: 6) and that "good politics" produces "good science" rather than substituting for it.

Three critical points are worth registering.

First, the matrix of domination is more powerful as a heuristic than as a precise theoretical apparatus. It directs attention productively but does not specify the mechanisms by which axes integrate. Subsequent work has provided more precision.

Second, the framework is sometimes accused of producing analyses so multi-axis that they become unfalsifiable. The reply is that intersectional claims, like single-axis claims, are subject to empirical test. The reference-man cases, disaggregated by race as well as gender, would generate testable predictions that single-axis fairness measures would miss.

Third, the framework is sometimes accused of being too tied to one national context. Collins's revised editions address this; the matrix is portable to other racialised societies by attention to local histories. The portability is part of what makes the framework a development of standpoint epistemology rather than a parochial instance of it.

For sociology today, Collins's framework is the most useful inheritance from the standpoint tradition. It retains the political grounding of Hartsock, the methodological reflexivity of Harding, the situated-knowledge insistence of Haraway, and adds the structural integration of multiple axes of oppression. It is, in short, standpoint epistemology brought up to date.

The lecture's connection is direct. hooks's oppositional gaze and Gilroy's Black Atlantic are intersectional standpoints in Collins's sense. The lecture's slogan that perspectives produce more objectivity is best read through Collins: multiple intersected perspectives produce more objectivity than either no perspective or single-axis perspectives. Collins's contribution to standpoint epistemology is therefore both critical and constructive. She criticised the single-axis tendency of the earlier tradition and built a richer framework in its place, a framework that remains the most useful tool we have for empirical sociology of inequality.
